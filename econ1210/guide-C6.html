Ch.6 Taxes and subsidies · Exam guide

← Start HereChapter note · Ch.6← Guide Ch.5Next: guide Ch.7

Every Ch.6 question from 2020 Spring to 2025 Spring Supplementary (173 unique questions, 185 parts with repeats) sorted into seven strategies. This chapter is predictable: five of the seven strategies are fixed calculations. Learn the cards and the ready answers, then drill.

0How Ch.6 is examined

  • Where: the second half of every midterm (often a block of 4–8 linked blanks on one background) and the early questions of the finals; 27 sittings 2020–2025 have Ch.6 questions.
  • Format (unchanged since 2020): online quiz. Numeric blanks to 2 decimal places, A/B choice blanks (tax or subsidy), MC, "how many statements are true", True/False. No marks shown.
  • Linked blanks: one background often carries 3–4 blanks. Keep unrounded values from blank to blank; rounding early changes the last digit.
  • No formula sheet. Memorise the box below.
Memorise for Ch.6
  • Wedge: tax \(P_b - P_s = t\); subsidy \(P_s - P_b = s\). Who it is "levied on" never matters.
  • \(Q_d = a - bP\), \(Q_s = c + dP\): \(\Delta Q = t\cdot\frac{bd}{b+d}\); buyers' share \(\frac{d}{b+d}\).
  • Revenue/spending \(= t \times Q_1\); DWL \(= \tfrac12 t\,\Delta Q\).
  • Tax: CS + PS falls by revenue + DWL. Subsidy: CS + PS rises by spending − DWL.
  • Elasticity with slope ΔP/ΔQ: \(e = \frac{1}{\text{slope}}\cdot\frac PQ\).
  • Design: \(t = \Delta Q/k\); \(t = \sqrt{2\,\text{cap}/k}\); quadratic for revenue/budget (\(k = \frac{bd}{b+d}\)).
  • Revenue-max tax = ½ kill tax; shop at revenue-max price ⇒ midpoint of a straight demand line.
  • Incidence: LESS elastic side bears more / gets more. DWL larger when MORE elastic.

1The seven strategies at a glance

Which strategy?
  1. A price/quantity table with a tax or subsidy → C6.1.
  2. Equations (or slopes + elasticity) and a given tax/subsidy, asking for prices, Q, revenue, DWL or ΔCS+ΔPS → C6.2.
  3. The tax/subsidy is the unknown, chosen to hit a target or respect a cap → C6.3; chosen to maximise revenue (government or a shop) → C6.4.
  4. Only ΔCS, ΔPS, DWL numbers, no curves → C6.5.
  5. Two groups of suppliers or buyers and a planner forcing a quantity → C6.6.
  6. Statements to judge, no numbers to compute → C6.7.

C6.1 Wedge on a schedule 50 parts · 42 unique

Looks likeA price / quantity-demanded / quantity-supplied table and "a tax (subsidy) of $t per unit": buyers' price, sellers' price, new Q, revenue or spending.

DoFind two rows exactly $t apart whose quantities match: Qd at the higher price = Qs at the lower price (tax), or Qs at the higher = Qd at the lower (subsidy).

WatchRevenue uses the NEW Q; a subsidy puts the SELLERS' price on top; uneven price steps → check pairs, don't count rows.

C6.2 Wedge with linear equations 33 parts · 33 unique

Looks likeEquations \(Q = a - bP\), \(Q = c + dP\) (or slopes + an elasticity) and a per-unit tax or subsidy: prices, ΔQ, revenue/spending, DWL, change in CS + PS.

DoSolve \(Q_d(P_b) = Q_s(P_b \mp t)\); revenue \(= tQ_1\); DWL \(= \tfrac12 t\,\Delta Q\); tax: CS + PS falls by revenue + DWL; subsidy: rises by spending − DWL.

WatchStated "slopes" are ΔP/ΔQ; buyers' share \(= d/(b+d)\); constant MC = flat supply, buyers pay all.

C6.3 Design the wedge to hit a target 27 parts · 27 unique

Looks like"We should suggest a tax/subsidy of ___" to reach a target ΔQ, price cut or % change, or "at most / lowest" under a DWL cap, a budget or a revenue floor.

DoTarget ΔQ: \(t = \Delta Q(\frac1b + \frac1d)\). DWL cap: \(t = \sqrt{2\,\text{cap}\,(b+d)/(bd)}\). Budget/revenue: quadratic in t.

WatchUse BOTH slopes; square root in the cap; smaller root for "lowest tax"; "+270%" means ×3.7.

C6.4 Revenue maximisation (midpoint rule) 15 parts · 15 unique

Looks likeA government picking the revenue-max tax (constant MC or upward supply), or a shop at its revenue-max price hit by a buyer tax or subsidy.

DoRevenue-max tax = ½ × kill tax. Shop: the point given is the midpoint, so demand is \(Q = 2Q_0 - (Q_0/P_0)P\); shift by the wedge, take the new midpoint.

WatchAt \(t^*\): Q halves, DWL = ½ revenue, CS + PS lost = ¾ \(t^*Q_0\). Price asked = seller's price.

C6.5 Welfare accounting identity 10 parts · 10 unique

Looks likeNo curves: ΔCS, ΔPS and DWL (and maybe the rate) are given; find revenue/spending or the before/after quantities.

DoTax: revenue = CS lost + PS lost − DWL. Subsidy: spending = CS gain + PS gain + DWL. Then \(Q_1 = \)revenue/t, \(\Delta Q = 2\,\text{DWL}/t\).

WatchDWL sign flips between tax and subsidy; \(Q_1\) is the post-policy quantity; the factor 2.

C6.6 Forced quantity with a kinked market curve 9 parts · 9 unique

Looks likeTwo supplier groups (or buyer groups) with P = … lines, market demand (supply); a planner forces a quantity, cuts it by 50%, or bans trade.

DoHorizontal sum (check which groups are active), equilibrium, then the area between the curves from \(Q^*\) to \(Q_0\); equivalent wedge = vertical gap at \(Q^*\).

WatchGap negative ⇒ subsidy; split the area at the kink; option letters A/B swap between papers.

C6.7 Incidence and elasticity reasoning 41 parts · 37 unique

Looks likeTrue/False and "how many are true" statements about who bears a tax, DWL and elasticity, price directions; MC on subsidy incidence.

DoApply the four core rules in the Ready-answer box; most statements are recycled with one word flipped.

Watch"More elastic side bears more" is the classic False; "it can be the case" → True if an extreme curve does it.

C6.1Wedge on a schedule

50 parts, 42 unique · newest first within each form · Note §4 Tables and equations

Do
  1. Find the equilibrium row (\(Q_d = Q_s\)).
  2. Tax t: scan down the buyers' column from the equilibrium price; for each higher price \(P_b\), read \(Q_d(P_b)\) and compare it with \(Q_s(P_b - t)\). Stop at the match.
  3. Subsidy s: the same with the sellers' price on top: \(Q_s(P_s) = Q_d(P_s - s)\).
  4. Read off what is asked: \(P_b\), \(P_s\), new Q, revenue/spending \(= t \times Q_{\text{new}}\).
Ready answer · schedule shortcut

When both columns change by fixed steps (e.g. −20 and +20 per $5), the wedge moves Q by the same amount per $ on each side, so the buyers' and sellers' price moves split the wedge in the ratio of the supply step to the demand step. Still verify the pair in the table.

Do
  1. Is it about who bears/gets the wedge? → the LESS elastic side bears more of a tax and gets more of a subsidy.
  2. About ΔQ or DWL? → larger when demand and/or supply is MORE elastic (tax or subsidy alike); zero if one side is perfectly inelastic.
  3. About surplus vs revenue/spending? → tax: CS + PS lost = revenue + DWL (lost > revenue). Subsidy: spending = CS + PS gained + DWL (spending > gain).

  5. "It can be the case …" → True if a perfectly elastic or perfectly inelastic curve makes it happen. "Always / never / any" → look for that extreme counterexample.
  6. MC on incidence: compare the buyers' price change with half the wedge.

Suppose the demand and supply of widgets are both linear. In particular, the supply curve goes through the origin. At the market equilibrium, demand is unit elastic. Consider a small tax levied on widgets, we may conclude that buyers will bear 29 (A. 0%; B. 50%; C. 100%; D. undetermined) of the tax burden.

Hint

Buyers' share \(= \dfrac{E_s}{E_s + |E_d|}\). A straight supply line through the origin has \(E_s = 1\) at every point.

Solution
  1. Supply through the origin: \(Q\) \(= kP\), so \(E_s\) \(= \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P}\cdot\frac{P}{Q}\) \(= k \cdot \frac{P}{kP}\) \(= 1\).
  2. Demand unit elastic: \(|E_d|\) \(= 1\).
  3. Buyers' share \(= 1/(1 + 1)\) \(= 50\%\).

Answer: B (50%)
