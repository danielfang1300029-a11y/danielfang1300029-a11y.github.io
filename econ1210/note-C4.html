Comparative statics: when demand and supply shift

Chapter 3 found where a market settles. This chapter asks what happens to that resting point when something in the world changes: a new technology, a rise in income, a foreign buyer, a pandemic. You will learn to predict which way price and quantity move, to read the cause back from what you observe, and to work out who gains and who loses.

1Why the equilibrium is the benchmark

Gains from trade are maximised at equilibrium

Start by recalling Chapter 3 with the world oil market: the supply and demand curves cross at an equilibrium price of $30 a barrel and an equilibrium quantity of 65 MBD (million barrels per day).

Recall from Chapter 3

Demand curve: for each price, how much buyers want to buy. Read the other way, its height at a quantity is the marginal benefit (willingness to pay) of the buyer of that unit. It slopes down.

Supply curve: for each price, how much sellers want to sell. Its height at a quantity is the marginal cost of the seller of that unit: the minimum price that seller must receive. It slopes up.

Equilibrium: the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no shortage and no surplus of goods.

Split all potential traders into four groups:

BuyersOn the demand curve to the left of 65: they value a barrel above $30, so they buy.
Non-buyersOn the demand curve to the right of 65: they value it below $30, so they stay out.
SellersOn the supply curve to the left of 65: their cost is below $30, so they sell.
Non-sellersOn the supply curve to the right of 65: their cost is above $30, so they stay out.

Equilibrium and total surplus

A free-market equilibrium gives two important results:

  • Goods are produced at the lowest possible cost: only the sellers with cost below the price produce.
  • Goods satisfy the highest-valued demands: only the buyers who value the good above the price get it.

Together these mean that total surplus — consumer surplus plus producer surplus, the total gain from trade — is as large as it can be. The market equilibrium quantity is socially optimal (best for society as a whole).

The two conditions

The equilibrium quantity is socially optimal only

  • when all relevant production costs are paid by the sellers (nobody else, such as neighbours breathing smoke, bears part of the cost), and
  • when all relevant benefits go to the buyers (nobody else enjoys part of the benefit for free).

Chapters 8 and 9 (externalities, public goods) study what goes wrong when these fail.

Try it: total surplus is largest at the equilibrium quantity
demand P = 100 − Qsupply P = 20 + Qsurplus createdsurplus lost

Each unit traded adds (buyer's value − seller's cost) = height of demand − height of supply. Up to Q = 40 that is positive; beyond 40 it is negative. Total surplus is largest, $1600, at the equilibrium Q = 40.

Ready answer · total surplus at any quantity \[ \begin{aligned} TS(Q) &= \text{area between demand} \\ &\quad \text{and supply from } 0 \text{ to } Q \end{aligned} \]

In words: total surplus = add up (willingness to pay − cost) over every unit actually traded. It peaks where the curves cross.

Comparative statics

Definitions

Comparative statics: comparing two equilibrium outcomes, before and after a change in some exogenous variable. "Static" because we compare two resting points, not the path between them.

Endogenous variables ("determined inside" the model): the price of good X and the quantity of good X. These are the variables shown on the graph.

Exogenous variables ("determined outside" the model): any of the demand shifters and any of the supply shifters — income, prices of other goods, technology, weather… These are not shown on the graph; when one changes, a curve moves.

In words: an exogenous change moves a curve; the curves then decide the new price and quantity.

The rest of this note follows the outline: changes in demand, changes in supply, applications.

2Changes in demand

An increase in demand

Increase in demand

An increase in demand means that consumers buy more at every price — or, equivalently, that they are willing to pay more for each quantity.

On the graph the whole demand curve shifts outwards: up and to the right.

The picture reads the shift both ways. At the same price of $25, buyers now want 80 units instead of 70 (the curve moved right: greater quantity at the same price). At the same quantity of 70, buyers would now pay $50 instead of $25 (the curve moved up: greater willingness to pay for the same quantity). These are two descriptions of one and the same shift.

What it does to the equilibrium

With supply unchanged, an increase in demand from \(D_0\) to \(D_1\) moves the equilibrium up along the supply curve: from \((Q_0, P_0)\) to a higher price \(P_1\) and a higher quantity \(Q_1\).

Why? At the old price \(P_0\) buyers now want more than sellers offer: a shortage. Buyers compete for the scarce units, the price rises, and the rising price draws out more supply (sellers move up their supply curve) while trimming some of the extra demand. The market settles where the new demand curve crosses the old supply curve.

A decrease in demand

Decrease in demand

A decrease in demand means consumers buy less at every price, or reduce the price they are willing to pay for a given quantity. The demand curve shifts inwards: down and to the left.

In the picture, at $25 buyers now want 70 units instead of 80, and for the same quantity they would pay less than $50. The equilibrium moves down along the supply curve to a lower price and a lower quantity.

Try it: shift demand, watch the equilibrium slide along supply
old demand D₀new demand D₁supply S

D₀ is Q = 100 − P, supply is Q = P − 20. A demand shift of +20 means buyers want 20 more units at every price. Both P and Q move in the same direction as the demand shift.

Rule · demand shifts

Demand ↑ ⇒ P ↑ and Q ↑. Demand ↓ ⇒ P ↓ and Q ↓.

In words: a demand shift moves price and quantity the same way as the shift.

3Demand shifters

A demand shifter is anything other than the good's own price that changes how much buyers want at each price. There are six.

1. Income

The effect of income depends on the kind of good:

Normal and inferior goods

Normal good: demand increases when income increases (and decreases when income falls). Most goods: restaurant meals, travel, new phones.

Inferior good: demand decreases when income increases (and increases when income falls). Goods people switch away from as they get richer: instant noodles, second-hand clothes, long bus rides instead of taxis.

"Inferior" is a technical word, not a judgement about quality. The same good can be normal for one person and inferior for another.

2. Population

As the population changes, the number of potential buyers changes, and so does demand. What happens to the demand for diapers if birth rates drop? Fewer babies, fewer buyers of diapers at every price: demand for diapers decreases (the curve shifts left).

3. Price of substitutes

Substitutes

Two goods are substitutes if a decrease in the price of one leads to a decrease in demand for the other (and vice versa). They do the same job, so buyers switch between them.

  • Tea when coffee becomes dearer: some coffee drinkers switch to tea, so demand for tea increases.
  • A trip to Hawaii when a trip to Mexico costs more: both are beach holidays, so demand for Hawaii trips increases.

4. Price of complements

Complements

Two goods are complements if a decrease in the price of one leads to an increase in demand for the other (and vice versa). They are used together.

  • Sport utility vehicles (SUVs) when petrol gets dearer: SUVs burn a lot of petrol, so owning one becomes more expensive to run. Demand for SUVs decreases.
Ready answer · price of a related good Y rises

Y a substitute of X ⇒ demand for X up. Y a complement of X ⇒ demand for X down.

In words: when a rival gets dearer you gain customers; when a partner gets dearer you lose them.

5. Expectations

An increase (decrease) in the price buyers expect in the future increases (decreases) demand now. Buyers time their spending to pay the lowest possible price.

If Nintendo suddenly announces large discounts on Switch consoles just before Christmas, what happens to sales now? Buyers expect a lower price later, so they wait: demand now decreases and current sales fall.

6. Tastes

Tastes and preferences are subjective and vary between consumers. But seasonal changes and social norms have predictable effects:

  • Ice-cream in summer: demand increases.
  • Face masks during the pandemic: wearing masks became expected and advised, so demand increased sharply.

New information works through tastes too: a report that salmon lowers cancer risk, or that walking is healthy, shifts demand for salmon up and for minibus rides down.

Exam favourite · falling income

"A decrease in the income level" raises demand for an inferior good. Many "how many of these events…" questions hinge on this.

4Shift or movement? Reading a demand relation

Change in quantity demanded vs change in demand

A change in quantity demanded happens only when the price of the good itself changes. It is a movement along a fixed demand curve.

A change in demand happens only when a demand shifter changes. It is a shift of the entire demand curve.

In words: own price → slide along the curve; anything else → move the whole curve.

Demand relations

Economists often write demand as one equation that includes the shifters:

\[ Q_x = a + b\,P_x + c\,P_y + d\,\text{Inc} + \dots \]

In words: quantity of X demanded = a constant + (effect of X's own price) + (effect of another good's price) + (effect of income) + … each multiplied by how strongly buyers respond.

  • A change in \(P_x\) moves the point along the curve: price rises from \(P_0\) to \(P_1\), quantity falls from \(Q_0\) to \(Q_1\). The own-price coefficient \(b\) is negative, which is why demand slopes down.
  • A change in \(P_y\) or in income changes the value of the whole right-hand side at every \(P_x\): the curve shifts from \(D_0\) to \(D_1\).

In words: only the direction matters. Ask: when this variable goes up, does \(Q_x\) go up or down?

Going further · powers of income

Papers sometimes write income as a power, e.g. \(+\,\text{income}^{-0.5}\). A negative power means \(1/\sqrt{\text{income}}\): it gets smaller as income grows. So \(+\,\text{income}^{-0.5}\) falls with income: the good is inferior, despite the plus sign.

Likewise \(-0.2 \times \text{income}^{-2}\): as income grows, \(\text{income}^{-2}\) shrinks, so we subtract less and the term rises: the good is normal. With \(\ln Q_x\) on the left, signs read the same way, because \(\ln Q_x\) and \(Q_x\) rise and fall together.

In words: check what happens to the whole term when income goes up — plug in income = 1 and income = 4 if unsure.

5Changes in supply

An increase in supply

Increase in supply

An increase in supply means sellers offer more at every price — or, equivalently, they are willing to sell the same quantity at a lower price. The supply curve shifts right and down.

Take the oil market again. At $50 a barrel sellers now offer 80 MBD instead of 20 (supply greater quantity at the same price). And the quantity they used to sell only at $50 they now sell at about $10 (willing to sell the same quantity at lower prices). Two readings of the same shift.

Direction words

An increase in supply moves the curve down (and right). Students who think "increase = up" get this wrong. Think "more at each price" (right) or "cheaper for each quantity" (down).

What it does to the equilibrium

With demand unchanged, an increase in supply moves the equilibrium down along the demand curve to a lower price and a higher quantity. At the old price there is now a surplus of goods; sellers cut prices to sell them, and the lower price persuades buyers to take more.

A decrease in supply

Decrease in supply

A decrease in supply means sellers offer less at every price, or need a higher price to sell the same quantity. The supply curve shifts left and up.

In the oil picture, at $10 sellers now offer 20 MBD instead of 80. The equilibrium moves up along the demand curve to a higher price and a lower quantity.

Try it: shift supply, watch the equilibrium slide along demand
demand Dold supply S₀new supply S₁

Same curves as §2. A supply shift moves price and quantity in opposite directions.

Rule · supply shifts

Supply ↑ ⇒ P ↓ and Q ↑. Supply ↓ ⇒ P ↑ and Q ↓.

In words: a supply shift moves quantity the same way as the shift and price the opposite way.

6Supply shifters

A supply shifter is anything other than the good's own price that changes how much sellers offer at each price. There are five. Behind all of them is one idea: the supply curve is the marginal cost curve, so anything that changes the cost of producing (including the opportunity cost) shifts it.

1. Technological innovations

A technological innovation makes sellers willing to offer more at a given price, or to sell a given quantity at a lower price. Technology improvements increase supply. Example from past papers: a new lobster trap that lowers the cost of catching lobsters.

2. Input prices

An input is anything used to make the good (materials, labour, fuel, machines). A decrease in an input price lowers marginal cost and increases supply (and vice versa).

  • Smartphones when chips get cheaper: supply of smartphones increases.
  • Electric cars when batteries get dearer: supply of electric cars decreases.

3. Expectations

If sellers expect a higher price in the future, they decrease current supply (and vice versa) — if the good can be stored. They hold stock back now to sell later at the higher price.

Compare with buyers (§3): an expected price rise raises demand today but lowers supply today. Both push today's price up.

4. Entry or exit of producers

As producers enter or leave a market, total supply changes. Entry (more sellers) increases supply; exit (fewer sellers) decreases it.

What happens to the supply of marijuana in California if it is legalised for recreational use? Legal firms can now enter openly: supply increases.

Imports work like entry: the curve "domestic supply plus imports" lies to the right of "domestic supply". At each price the market gets more (domestic plus foreign), and each quantity is available at a lower price.

Ready answer · adding sellers \[ Q^S_{\text{total}}(P) = Q^S_{\text{domestic}}(P) + Q^S_{\text{foreign}}(P) \]

In words: market supply = add up, at each price, what every group of sellers offers. A foreign seller who sells a fixed \(k\) units at any price just adds \(k\) at every price (§13).

5. Changes in other opportunity costs

Sellers use their land, machines and time where the profit is highest. If the price of an alternative good that uses the same inputs rises, producing this good now means giving up more profit elsewhere: its opportunity cost rises and its supply decreases (and vice versa). Sellers always chase the highest-profit goods.

An example: farmers can grow wheat or soybeans on the same land. Rising wheat prices reduce soybean supply. With low opportunity costs farmers offered 2,800 million bushels of soybeans at $5; with high opportunity costs they offer only 2,000 million at $5, and need $7 to supply the old quantity.

Going further · joint products work the other way

Some goods are produced together: making low-fat milk also produces cream; making wine also produces grapeseed oil (the by-product). Now a higher price of the by-product makes the whole process more profitable, so supply of the main product increases.

  • Alternative uses of the same input (wheat vs soybeans): other good dearer ⇒ this supply ↓.
  • Joint products (cream with low-fat milk): by-product dearer ⇒ this supply ↑.

An event that raises demand for the by-product (cheaper ice-cream production raises demand for cream) raises its price, and so raises supply of the main product.

Change in quantity supplied vs change in supply

Movement or shift?

A change in quantity supplied happens only when the price of the good itself changes: a movement along a fixed supply curve.

A change in supply happens only when a supply shifter changes: a shift of the entire supply curve.

In words: exactly as for demand — own price slides along, anything else shifts.

Ready answer · classify an event in three questions
  1. Is it the good's own price? Then it is a movement, never a shift. ("The price of wine rises" does not shift the supply of wine.)
  2. Does it change what buyers want (income, related goods' prices, expectations, tastes/information, population)? → demand. Does it change costs (inputs, technology, number of sellers, expectations, other uses of the inputs, by-product prices)? → supply.
  3. Which direction?

In words: own price → movement; buyer side → demand; cost side → supply; then the arrow. Strategy C4.2.

Movement along demand

Papers ask which events cause a "movement along the demand curve to the right". Demand itself does not move; the price falls because supply increased. So the answer is any event that increases supply (more sellers, a foreign country agreeing to sell extra at any price). Events that shift demand are wrong answers.

Definition traps from past papers
  • The supply curve shows the minimum price suppliers must receive to produce one more unit — not the maximum.
  • Producer surplus is the gain to sellers (price − cost, summed over units sold), not "extra units produced beyond what consumers demand" and not "the cost of the marginal seller".
  • Marginal benefit is the willingness to pay for one more unit, not willingness to pay minus price (that difference is the buyer's surplus on that unit).
  • Supply slopes up; whether a good is normal or inferior is about demand, never supply.
  • Going further A normal good's demand curve can never slope up. An inferior good's demand curve can in theory slope up (a so-called Giffen good: a very poor family spends so much on rice that when rice gets dearer they can no longer afford meat and buy even more rice). Papers mark "an inferior good's demand curve can be upward sloping" as True.

7Applications I: prediction

Remember the three uses of economic models from Chapter 1: prediction, inference and policy. Comparative statics delivers all three.

An oil shock

An oil shock is a sudden cut in oil supply (a war, an embargo, a cartel cutting output). The supply curve shifts left, so the prediction is: the price of oil increases and the quantity decreases. On the graph the supply curve moves up along an unchanged demand curve.

The price of oil, 1960–2005

Plot the real (inflation-adjusted) price of oil and label the big moves with the shift that caused them:

  • Supply shocks (supply shifts left, price jumps): the Yom Kippur war and Arab oil embargo (1973), the Iranian revolution (1979), the start of the Iran–Iraq war (1980). OPEC, the oil producers' cartel, was founded in 1960, and nationalisations of oil in many OPEC countries in the 1970s also cut supply.
  • Supply increases (price falls through the 1980s): non-OPEC production came to exceed OPEC's.
  • Negative demand shocks (demand shifts left, price falls): the East Asian financial crisis (1997).
  • Positive demand shock (demand shifts right, price rises): rapid growth in China and India in the 2000s.

Each label is a small supply–demand diagram: the model explains forty years of prices with a handful of shifts.

Practice predictions

Predict the impact of three events on the price and quantity of various kinds of meat, and on the price and quantity of wine, plus the effect of HKU's expansion on housing prices in the Western District. Here is the reasoning.

Fishing holiday (a summer ban on fishing)

Supply of fresh fish falls: fish P ↑, Q ↓. Other meats are substitutes, so demand for pork, beef and chicken rises: their P ↑, Q ↑. White wine, often drunk with fish, is a complement of fish; less fish eaten lowers demand for it: its P ↓, Q ↓.

Avian flu

Buyers fear chicken: demand for chicken falls. Farms also cull birds: supply of chicken falls. Chicken Q ↓ for sure, P uncertain (two shifts, see §10). Substitutes (pork, beef, fish): demand up, P ↑, Q ↑.

Mad-cow disease

Demand for beef falls: beef P ↓, Q ↓. Other meats gain demand: P ↑, Q ↑. Red wine, a complement of beef, loses demand: P ↓, Q ↓.

HKU expands, housing in the Western District

More students and staff want to live near the campus: demand for flats in the Western District rises, so rents and prices rise and more flats are rented. (If the expansion also uses land that could have held housing, supply falls too, pushing price up further.)

8Applications II: inference — reading the shift from the data

Inference runs the model backwards: we observe how price and quantity changed and ask which shift caused it.

Price up, quantity down

We observe the price rise from \(P_0\) to \(P_1\) while quantity falls from \(Q_0\) to \(Q_1\). Is it likely that a supply shock occurred?

  • A leftward supply shift along a fixed demand curve produces exactly this pattern: price up, quantity down. That is the simplest single cause.
  • But other combinations fit too. With two demand curves and two supply curves, supply may have fallen and demand risen a little. With steep demand curves, supply may have fallen and demand fallen too.
  • In every possible explanation supply decreased: a demand shift alone can never move P and Q in opposite directions.

Price up, quantity up

Now price and quantity both rose. The simplest cause is an increase in demand along a fixed supply curve. Combinations fit too: demand up together with supply up a little, or demand up together with supply down (with steep supply curves). In every explanation demand increased.

Rule · inference from (ΔP, ΔQ)

P and Q moved in the same direction ⇒ demand must have shifted that way (the only possible single cause is a demand shift).

P and Q moved in opposite directions ⇒ supply must have shifted (in the direction quantity moved).

In words: demand shifts move P and Q together; supply shifts pull them apart. Read the pattern, name the shift.

Try it: pick the new observed point; the tool says which way each curve must have moved
old demandold supplycurves through the new point

Old equilibrium E₀ = (40, 60) with demand P = 100 − Q and supply P = 20 + Q. A new demand curve must pass through the new point: if that point is to the right of (above) the old demand curve, demand increased. Likewise a point above the old supply curve means supply decreased. Try P₁ = 50 with Q₁ = 40: price fell with quantity unchanged, which needs supply up and demand down.

Reading exam tables

Papers disguise quantity: "rows on the shelf" at the supermarket or "trading volume" of bonds stand for quantity bought and sold. Bonds at $10 and 5 million units last week, $18 and 1 million this week: P up, Q down ⇒ decrease in supply.

9Applications III: policy and food aid

Lowering housing prices

Housing prices are too high. What can the government do to lower the price?

  • Increase the supply of land, or lower the entry barriers to housing development (faster approvals, fewer restrictions). Supply shifts right: price lower, quantity higher.
  • Decrease the demand for housing. How? Going further Examples: extra stamp duty on buyers who are not residents or who already own a flat, or tighter mortgage rules that make it harder to borrow. Demand shifts left: price lower, but quantity lower too.

Example: the impact of food aid

The question

An important determinant of the amount of grain harvested next year by Ethiopian farmers is the amount of seed planted this year. Given that Western nations have guaranteed to donate five hundred tons of grain next year, this year the Ethiopian farmers will:

  • A. plant more seeds as the food aid established a minimum price for grain.
  • B. plant more seeds as the farmers' confidence is restored.
  • C. plant the same amount of seeds as they would have without the food aid.
  • D. plant less seeds as the price of grain will be lower with the food aid.

Solve it two ways; both give the same answer.

View 1: demand for domestic grain fallsThe 500 donated tons feed people who would otherwise buy grain from local farmers. So the demand for domestically produced grain falls by 500 tons at every price: D shifts left to D′. Next year's price falls from P to P′ and local farmers' quantity from Q to Q′.
View 2: total supply risesCount the donation as supply: total supply of grain rises by 500 tons at every price (S′ = domestic + donation). Price falls from P to P′. Total grain rises to Q′, but domestic farmers, moving down their own supply curve, now supply only Q″ < Q.

Either way, farmers anticipate a lower market price next year, so they want to supply less next year, and they do that by planting fewer seeds this year. Answer: D.

Two views of 500 tons of aid give the same price and the same domestic output

Demand P = 100 − 0.05Q, domestic supply P = 0.05Q (Q in tons, P in dollars per ton). Without aid: Q = 1000, P = 50. With 500 tons of aid: P = 37.5 and domestic farmers grow 750 tons in both views.

Ready answer · expected future supply

Extra supply expected next year (aid, a big harvest abroad) ⇒ lower expected price ⇒ producers cut what they prepare now (seeds, stock, investment).

In words: sellers act today on the price they expect tomorrow.

10Applications IV: two shifts at once

The question

What happens to the equilibrium price and quantity in the fresh seafood market if both of these occur: (1) a scientific report says fish contains mercury, which is toxic to humans; (2) the price of diesel fuel falls significantly?

  1. Mercury report → buyers want less fish: demand decreases → pushes P ↓ and Q ↓.
  2. Cheaper diesel (fishing boats' fuel, an input) → supply increases → pushes P ↓ and Q ↑.
  3. Both push price down: price falls for sure. On quantity they disagree: it rises if the supply shift is bigger (right panel), falls if the demand shift is bigger (left panel). Quantity is uncertain.

Labour markets

In a job market the wage is the price and employment is the quantity. Workers supply labour; firms and the government demand it. Graduates emigrating = labour supply ↓; the government hiring more graduates = labour demand ↑ ⇒ wage ↑, employment uncertain.

11Applications V: rent control and taxi fares

Rental regulation

Take a market for two-bedroom rental flats in "Collegetown" (a university town): supply and demand are straight lines that cross at a rent of $1000 a month and 2 thousand flats. Reading the grid: supply passes through (1, 500) and (3, 1500); demand passes through (1, 1500) and (3, 500).

The city council worries that many students cannot afford $1000 and considers forbidding landlords to charge more than $500. A legal maximum price like this is called a price ceiling; Chapter 7 studies ceilings in detail.

Consequences: at $500 landlords offer only 1 thousand flats but students want 3 thousand. Excess demand (a shortage) of 2000 flats. Rent controls produce excess demand in the housing market.

And at the quantity actually available (1 thousand), the marginal student — the one who just misses out — would pay up to $1500, that is $1000 more than the controlled rent, to get a flat. That gap is money on the table, so landlords and tenants find ways around the rule:

  • key deposits — a large up-front payment just to receive the keys;
  • required furniture rental — the flat comes only with expensive furniture you must rent;
  • excessive damage deposits;
  • curtailed maintenance — the landlord stops repairing, lowering the real quality of what you rent;
  • apartment conversion — landlords turn flats into uses not covered by the control (sale flats, offices), shrinking the supply further.
Try it: a rent ceiling on the Collegetown market

Curves: supply P = 500Q, demand P = 2000 − 500Q (Q in thousands of flats, P in dollars a month). A ceiling at or above $1000 does nothing (it does not bind).

Better ways to help the poor

There are much more effective ways to help poor people (or students) than holding prices of flats and other goods artificially low. For example, income transfers: wage subsidies (the government tops up low wages) and job offerings. These raise what poor people can pay without creating a shortage.

Taxi regulations

Taxi fares are set by the government, and the number of taxi licences is also capped. Observations: at the regulated fare, taxis were in excess supply. Every day many taxis queue at the airport, some waiting several hours for a passenger; some drivers offer discounts.

One picture explains it. Because licences are capped, the supply curve rises at first and then turns vertical at the maximum number of taxis. The fare is fixed by law, so the market cannot adjust the price:

  • Economy in a recession (demand D₁ low): at the regulated fare drivers offer more rides than passengers want: excess supply — queues of empty taxis.
  • Economy in a boom (demand D₂ high): passengers want more rides than the capped fleet can offer: excess demand — hard to get a taxi.
Regulated taxi fare in a recession and in a boom

Illustration with invented numbers: supply of rides P = Q/2 up to the licence cap of 60, then vertical; regulated fare $25; D₁: Q = 80 − 2P, D₂: Q = 140 − 2P.

Ready answer · fixed price, shifting demand

When the price cannot move, a demand shift changes the size of the gap between quantity demanded and quantity supplied instead of the price.

In words: controlled price + low demand = queues of sellers; controlled price + high demand = queues of buyers.

12Chain reactions across linked markets Going further

The shifters already link markets: the price of a substitute or complement shifts demand, the price of an input shifts supply. Exam papers turn this into a chain: an event hits market X, X's price changes, and that price change becomes a shifter for market Y.

The four-step chain

shift in X → price change in X → shift in Y → price change in Y

In words: solve the first market completely, then carry its price (not its curve) into the second market as a shifter.

How the link works

Link between X and YX's price fallsX's price rises
Substitutes for consumers (tea/coffee, green/red minibuses, ferry/bus)demand for Y ↓ → \(P_Y\) ↓, \(Q_Y\) ↓demand for Y ↑ → \(P_Y\) ↑, \(Q_Y\) ↑
Complements for consumers (cars/petrol, salmon/wine)demand for Y ↑ → \(P_Y\) ↑, \(Q_Y\) ↑demand for Y ↓ → \(P_Y\) ↓, \(Q_Y\) ↓

Shared input. If X and Y are both made with input Z (say both use the same land or workers), the link runs through Z's market and hits Y's supply, not its demand:

  • Demand for X falls → less Z wanted → price of Z falls → supply of Y rises → \(P_Y\) ↓, \(Q_Y\) ↑.
  • Demand for X rises → price of Z rises → supply of Y falls → \(P_Y\) ↑, \(Q_Y\) ↓.
Try it: build the chain

Codes as in past papers: 1 = price up, 2 = price down, 3 = demand up, 4 = demand down, 5 = supply up, 6 = supply down.

The "(PY, QY) pair" question

Papers give \(P_Y\) and \(Q_Y\) now and four candidate new pairs. Work out the direction of both \(P_Y\) and \(Q_Y\), then pick the pair where both move strictly in those directions. A pair with an unchanged number is wrong (a shift always changes both). If no pair fits, answer "None of the above".

Movement in the first market

The Star Ferry raises its fare (a past-paper favourite). For the ferry itself this is a change in its own price: quantity demanded of ferry rides falls (a movement), but the demand for ferry rides does not change. Cross-harbour buses are a substitute: demand for buses rises, so bus fares and rides both rise and bus revenue rises. Bus supply does not change.

Currency markets

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. Utopians who want to travel to Japan must buy yen, so they demand yen, paying in Utopian dollars.

Ready answer · travel enthusiasm and exchange rates

Utopians less keen to travel to Japan ⇒ demand for yen ↓ ⇒ yen gets cheaper in Utopian dollars ⇒ one Utopian dollar buys more yen. So the rate quoted in yen per Utopian dollar rises.

More keen ⇒ the rate in yen per Utopian dollar falls.

In words: the demand curve is for the foreign currency, priced in your currency. If the quote is "foreign per home", flip the direction.

13Computing the new equilibrium Going further

So far the shifts were only drawn; about a quarter of all Chapter-4 exam parts ask you to compute them with straight-line curves. Everything below uses only simultaneous equations.

Step 0: one form for both curves

Papers write curves either as \(Q = a - bP\) ("quantity form") or as \(P = \alpha - \beta Q\) ("price form"). Before adding quantities, put both curves in quantity form. From \(P = 30 - 18.5Q\): \(Q = (30 - P)/18.5\).

Linear equilibrium

Demand \(Q = a - bP\), supply \(Q = c + dP\). Setting them equal:

\[ \begin{aligned} P^* &= \frac{a - c}{b + d}, \\ Q^* &= a - bP^*. \end{aligned} \]

In words: equilibrium price = (demand intercept − supply intercept) ÷ (sum of the two slopes); then read the quantity off either curve.

Adding a fixed quantity

Fixed-quantity shifts

An extra buyer of \(k\) units at any price (a foreign buyer, a government buyback): market demand becomes \(Q = a - bP + k\).

An extra seller of \(k\) units at any price (imports, foreign workers, food aid, traders dumping shares): market supply becomes \(Q = c + dP + k\). Withdrawing such a supply subtracts \(k\).

\[ P_{\text{new}} = \frac{a - c \pm k}{b + d} \]

In words: a fixed extra buyer adds \(k\) to the top; a fixed extra seller takes \(k\) off. Price moves by \(k/(b+d)\).

Who buys what after the shift

Local buyers still buy on their own (original) demand curve at the new price: \(a - bP_{\text{new}}\). The extra buyer takes \(k\). Domestic sellers still sell on the domestic supply curve: \(c + dP_{\text{new}}\). Keep these straight: surplus questions (§14) need them.

Try it: a fixed extra buyer or seller
local demand Q = 20 − Pdomestic supply Q = 2P − 4new market curve

Before: P = 8, Q = 12. A buyer of 3 units: \(23 - P = 2P - 4\) gives P = 9, total Q = 14, of which locals buy 11.

Worked example 1 · a foreign buyer

Demand \(Q = 20 - P\), supply \(Q = 2P - 4\). A foreign buyer agrees to buy 3 units at any price.

  1. Before: \(20 - P = 2P - 4\) ⇒ \(P = 8\), \(Q = 12\).
  2. After: \(23 - P = 2P - 4\) ⇒ \(3P = 27\) ⇒ \(P = 9\).
  3. Total quantity \(= 2(9) - 4 = 14\). Locals buy \(20 - 9 = 11\); the foreigner buys 3.

P = 9, Q = 14 (locals 11)

Reading the new curve at a given price

Papers ask: "on the new market demand curve, when the price is 25, the total quantity demanded is …". Do not solve for equilibrium; just evaluate. Local demand at that price (zero if the price is above the locals' highest willingness to pay) plus the extra buyer's quantity.

A buyer with a fixed budget

A buyer who spends a fixed amount \(M\) at any price buys \(M/P\) units: more when the price is low, fewer when it is high. So market demand becomes \(Q = (\text{local demand}) + M/P\). Papers pair this with a horizontal supply curve (sellers supply any amount at a fixed price \(\bar P\)), so the price cannot change.

Worked example 2 · fixed spending, flat supply

Demand \(P = 50 - 10Q\), supply \(P = 10\). A foreign country spends $60 on the good regardless of price.

  1. Before: \(50 - 10Q = 10\) ⇒ \(Q = 4\) at \(P = 10\).
  2. On the new demand curve at \(P = 20\): locals buy \((50 - 20)/10 = 3\); the foreigner buys \(60/20 = 3\). Total 6.
  3. New equilibrium: supply is flat at 10, so \(P = 10\). Locals buy 4, the foreigner \(60/10 = 6\): total 10.

Q = 10 at P = 10; locals still buy 4

Other shapes the papers use

  • A shifter in the equation (population, the price of Y): plug in the numbers; the change moves the intercept. Example: \(Q = \text{Pop} - 2P\) with supply \(Q = 2P\). Pop 60: \(P = 15\), \(Q = 30\). Pop 80: \(P = 20\), \(Q = 40\).
  • Imports allowed only above a price \(\bar p\): supply gets the extra \(k\) only at prices \(\ge \bar p\). Below \(\bar p\) the supply curve is the old one.
  • Small open economy: with unrestricted trade at a world price \(P_w\) that the country is too small to affect, the domestic price becomes \(P_w\). Local buyers buy \(Q_D(P_w)\), local producers sell \(Q_S(P_w)\), imports fill the gap.
  • Fixed stock (shares of a company): supply is a vertical line at the number of shares. Traders selling extra at any price add to it; a company buying back shares subtracts from it.
Worked example 3 · buyback to zero

Demand \(Q = 20 - P\), supply \(Q = 2P - 4\). The government wants the quantity in private hands to be zero, using a buyback (it buys units at the market price).

  1. Locals buy nothing only when the price reaches their highest willingness to pay: \(20 - P = 0\) ⇒ \(P = 20\).
  2. At \(P = 20\) sellers offer \(2(20) - 4 = 36\) units. The government must buy all of them.
  3. Spending \(= 20 \times 36 = 720\).

Buy at least 36 units, spend at least 720

Worked example 4 · small open economy

Same curves; the world price is 6.

  1. Domestic price becomes 6.
  2. Local buyers: \(20 - 6 = 14\). Local producers: \(2(6) - 4 = 8\). Imports \(= 14 - 8 = 6\).

P = 6; buyers 14, producers 8

Units

Price in thousand dollars × quantity in thousand units = million dollars. Price in dollars × quantity in millions = millions of dollars. Papers remind you with "(1 thousand) × (1 thousand) = 1 million".

Printed keys sometimes round down at a 5: for 2020 Fall Midterm Q25 the exact price is 99.975 (key 99.97) and in Q29 the exact lobbying amount is 1212.975 (key 1212.97). Give the exact value in the guide's working; either rounding should be accepted.

14Who gains, who loses Going further

A shift moves the price, and a price change moves money between buyers and sellers. Exam papers ask for the surpluses before and after, how much each group would spend lobbying, and how the government could compensate the losers.

Recall: consumer and producer surplus

Consumer surplus (CS): the area under the demand curve and above the price, out to the quantity bought. With demand \(Q = a - bP\) the curve hits the price axis at the choke price \(a/b\) (no one buys above it), so

\[ CS = \tfrac12 \times Q_D \times \left(\tfrac{a}{b} - P\right). \]

In words: consumer surplus = ½ × quantity bought × (highest willingness to pay − price).

Producer surplus (PS): the area above the supply curve and below the price. With supply \(Q = c + dP\):

\[ \begin{aligned} c \lt 0:&\;\; PS = \tfrac12\, Q_S \left(P - \tfrac{-c}{d}\right) \\ c \ge 0:&\;\; PS = \tfrac12\,(c + Q_S)\,P \end{aligned} \]

In words: if supply starts on the price axis, PS is a triangle (½ × quantity sold × (price − lowest price any seller accepts)). If supply has a positive quantity at price 0, PS is a trapezoid from price 0 up to the price.

Worked example 5 · a trapezoid producer surplus

Demand \(Q = 40 - P\), supply \(Q = 10 + 2P\).

  1. \(40 - P = 10 + 2P\) ⇒ \(P = 10\), \(Q = 30\).
  2. CS \(= \tfrac12 \times 30 \times (40 - 10) = 450\).
  3. Supply has 10 units at price 0: PS \(= \tfrac12 (10 + 30) \times 10 = 200\).

CS = 450, PS = 200

The groups and their curves

Use the right curve for each group
  • Local consumers: CS on the original local demand curve, at the new price. Never on market demand that includes foreign buyers, the government or new immigrants.
  • Local producers: PS on the domestic supply curve. Never on supply that includes imports, foreign workers or aid.
  • Labour markets: firms are the buyers (their surplus is the area under labour demand above the wage); workers are the sellers.
Lobbying and compensation

A group will spend up to its gain lobbying for a change, and up to its loss lobbying against it.

If the winners gain more than the losers lose, the government can tax the winners an amount \(T\) with

\[ \text{loser's loss} \le T \le \text{winner's gain} \]

and hand \(T\) to the losers. Both groups are then at least as well off as before.

In words: tax the winners at least enough to cover the losers' loss, but not more than the winners gained.

Worked example 6 · foreign buyer, continued

From worked example 1: demand \(Q = 20 - P\) (choke price 20), supply \(Q = 2P - 4\) (lowest price 2). Price rises 8 → 9.

  1. Before: CS \(= \tfrac12 \times 12 \times 12 = 72\); PS \(= \tfrac12 \times 12 \times 6 = 36\). Total 108.
  2. After: locals buy 11, CS \(= \tfrac12 \times 11 \times 11 = 60.5\). Producers sell 14, PS \(= \tfrac12 \times 14 \times 7 = 49\). Total to locals 109.5.
  3. Consumers lose 11.5 → they spend up to 11.5 lobbying against. Producers gain 13 → up to 13 lobbying for.
  4. Compensation: tax producers between 11.5 and 13 and give it to consumers.

Consumers −11.5, producers +13; tax producers 11.5 to 13

Buyback or ban?

If the government is set on driving the quantity in private hands to zero, sellers prefer a buyback (the government buys everything at the choke price: they keep producer surplus) to a ban (nothing sold: producer surplus zero). So the most sellers would spend lobbying for a buyback = their PS under the buyback. In worked example 3: \(\tfrac12 \times 36 \times (20 - 2) = 324\).

Population inflow

Using the earlier example \(Q = \text{Pop} - 2P\), supply \(Q = 2P\), Pop 60 → 80. The original 60 keep the demand curve \(Q = 60 - 2P\) (choke price 30). Price rises 15 → 20. Their CS falls from \(\tfrac12 \times 30 \times 15 = 225\) to \(\tfrac12 \times 20 \times 10 = 100\): they would pay up to 125 to lobby against the policy.

Stock buybacks (2021 Spring Midterm Q15): the key's "total paid" of 3.78 billion is the trapezoid under the demand curve between the old and new quantities, (51.46 + 56.57)/2 × 0.07, not 0.07 × 56.57 = 3.96. Read "paid in total" in such a question as buying the shares one by one up the demand curve.

Qualitative statements

  • A leftward shift in demand lowers price and quantity: producer surplus and total gains from trade both fall.
  • A leftward shift in supply lowers quantity: total gains from trade fall. But producer surplus need not fall — if buyers barely cut back when the price rises, the higher price can more than make up for selling less (Chapter 5 calls such demand inelastic).

15Summary

  • Equilibrium maximises total surplus (lowest-cost sellers, highest-value buyers), provided sellers bear all costs and buyers get all benefits.
  • Comparative statics compares equilibria before and after a change in an exogenous variable (a shifter not shown on the graph).
  • Demand shifters: income (normal/inferior), population, prices of substitutes and complements, expectations, tastes. Supply shifters: technology, input prices, expectations, entry/exit, opportunity costs of the inputs.
  • Own price → movement along the curve (change in quantity demanded/supplied). Anything else → shift (change in demand/supply).
  • Demand ↑ ⇒ P ↑, Q ↑. Supply ↑ ⇒ P ↓, Q ↑. Two shifts: the variable they agree on is known, the other is uncertain.
  • Inference: P and Q in the same direction ⇒ demand shifted; opposite directions ⇒ supply shifted.
  • Expected future supply (food aid) lowers the expected price, so producers prepare less now.
  • Price controls turn shifts into shortages or gluts instead of price changes (rent control, taxi fares).
  • Linked markets: carry the price change in X into Y as a shifter (substitutes, complements, shared inputs, currencies).
  • Numbers: fixed buyer adds \(k\) to demand, fixed seller adds \(k\) to supply, fixed budget adds \(M/P\). Local groups' surplus uses their own original curves; lobbying = gain or loss; compensation lies between the loser's loss and the winner's gain.

16Common mistakes

✗Saying a change in the good's own price shifts its demand or supply. ✓ It is a movement along the curve. ✗Treating an "increase in supply" as an upward shift. ✓ Supply up = right and down. ✗Forgetting that lower income raises demand for an inferior good. ✓ Check normal/inferior before the direction. ✗Reading \(+\,\text{income}^{-0.5}\) as "normal" because of the plus sign. ✓ The term shrinks as income grows: inferior. ✗Letting a supply-side event (technology, new firms) shift demand. ✓ Cost events shift supply only. ✗Guessing the "uncertain" variable in a two-shift question from which shift sounds bigger. ✓ Without sizes it is uncertain. ✗Linking a shared input through demand. ✓ A shared input links the supplies. ✗Using market demand (with the foreign buyer or immigrants) for local consumer surplus. ✓ Use the original local demand. ✗Using a triangle for PS when supply has a positive quantity at price 0. ✓ Use the trapezoid. ✗Adding \(k\) to a curve written as \(P = \alpha - \beta Q\). ✓ Convert to \(Q = \dots\) first, then add \(k\). ✗Mixing the directions of exchange-rate quotes. ✓ Decide what happens to the foreign currency's price, then flip if quoted "foreign per home".

17Find the mistake

Each "solution" has one error. Find it before opening.

1

Wineries make grapeseed oil as a by-product. "The price of wine rises, so the supply of wine increases."

Mistake

Wine's own price moves sellers along the supply curve (quantity supplied rises); supply itself does not shift.

2

Demand for chicken falls and the cost of chicken feed falls. "Both lower the price, so price falls and quantity falls."

Mistake

Price does fall, but the cheaper feed raises supply, which pushes quantity up while the demand fall pushes it down. Quantity is uncertain.

3

Demand \(P = 40 - 2Q\), supply \(Q = P - 10\). A foreign buyer takes 6 units at any price. "New demand \(P = 46 - 2Q\)."

Mistake

Add 6 to the quantity: \(Q = (40 - P)/2 + 6 = 26 - P/2\). Then \(26 - P/2 = P - 10\) gives \(P = 24\), \(Q = 14\). (The wrong curve \(P = 46 - 2Q\) adds 6 to the price and gives \(P = 22\).)

4

Same market; after the foreign buyer enters, "local consumer surplus = ½ × 14 × (40 − 24) = 112."

Mistake

Locals buy only \((40 - 24)/2 = 8\) units. Local CS \(= \tfrac12 \times 8 \times 16 = 64\).

19Concept checks

1

Bus fares rise. Does the demand for bus rides fall?

Answer

No. The quantity demanded falls (a movement along the curve). Demand would shift only if something other than the fare changed.

2

Shoppers expect phone prices to rise next month, and phone shops expect the same. What happens to today's price?

Answer

Demand today rises (buy before the rise) and supply today falls (shops hold stock back, if they can store it). Both push today's price up; quantity uncertain.

3

Price of a good falls while the quantity traded stays the same. What happened?

Answer

Supply increased and demand decreased by just enough that quantity did not change.

4

X and Y share an input. Demand for X rises. Which way do \(P_Y\) and \(Q_Y\) move?

Answer

The input gets dearer, supply of Y falls: \(P_Y\) ↑, \(Q_Y\) ↓.

5

Why can a rent ceiling below the equilibrium rent leave some students worse off even if they pay less rent?

Answer

Fewer flats are offered, so many students get none; those who do may face key deposits, forced furniture rental and poor maintenance, which raise the real price.

6

A foreign buyer enters a market. Can local producers fully compensate local consumers and still be better off?

Answer

Yes. Producers gain more than consumers lose (the extra trade with the foreigner adds surplus to locals), so a transfer between the two amounts works.
