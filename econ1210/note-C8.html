ECON1210 · Introductory Microeconomics · Chapter 8 of 12

Externalities

Markets weigh only the costs and benefits of the people who trade. When an activity spills costs or benefits onto bystanders, the market quantity is wrong: too much of harmful things and too little of helpful ones. This chapter shows how to measure the damage and three ways to fix it: Pigouvian taxes and subsidies, bargaining (the Coase theorem), and pollution permits.

0Where this sits

This chapter has three parts:

  1. Notion of externality: what a spill-over cost or benefit is, and why it makes the free-market quantity wrong (sections 1–4 below).
  2. Pigouvian taxes and subsidies: fixing the market by charging or paying exactly the spill-over (sections 5–7).
  3. The Coase theorem: when the people involved can fix it themselves by bargaining, plus taxes and tradable permits for pollution (sections 8–12).

You need Chapter 3 (demand, supply, consumer and producer surplus as triangles) and Chapter 6 (a per-unit tax or subsidy drives a wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive). Both are recapped when used. Maths: straight lines, simultaneous equations, areas of triangles and trapezoids.

1Recap: subsidies, surplus and the efficient market

Some subsidies have serious benefits: wage subsidies

A wage subsidy is a payment from the government for each worker hired (or each hour worked). It works exactly like the per-unit subsidy of Chapter 6, in the labour market: the "good" is labour, the buyers are firms (demand for labour) and the sellers are workers (supply of labour).

The numbers: without the subsidy the market wage is $10.50 and employment is \(Q_m\). With a $4 subsidy per hour, workers receive $12 while firms pay only $8; the government pays the $4 gap. Employment rises from \(Q_m\) to \(Q_s\).

A $4 wage subsidy drives a wedge between what workers get and what firms pay
supply of labourdemand for labourwelfare loss in the market

Wages are as stated above; the quantities (\(Q_m = 10\), \(Q_s = 11\), in, say, thousand workers) are invented so the picture can be drawn. Point a = no-subsidy equilibrium; b = what workers receive at \(Q_s\); d = what firms pay at \(Q_s\).

The verdict on wage subsidies:

  • It would still lead to a welfare loss in the market: the workers between \(Q_m\) and \(Q_s\) cost more to supply (up to $12) than they are worth to firms (down to $8). That is the orange triangle.
  • It would result in more low-skilled workers being employed.
  • The welfare loss could be offset by social benefits: less crime, less dependency on welfare payments, and so on. These benefits go to people outside the labour market — which is exactly what this chapter calls an external benefit. Once they are counted, the subsidy may raise total welfare.

Recall: a free market maximises total surplus

Recall the oil market: equilibrium price $30 a barrel, equilibrium quantity 65. Buyers to the left of 65 have willingness to pay above $30 and buy; sellers to the left have costs below $30 and sell. To the right are non-buyers and non-sellers.

Recap from Chapter 3

Consumer surplus (CS): the area between the demand curve and the price, up to the quantity traded — buyers' total gain.

Producer surplus (PS): the area between the price and the supply curve — sellers' total gain.

Total surplus = CS + PS = the area between demand and supply up to the quantity traded: the gains from trade.

Equilibrium in a free market gives two important results:

  • Goods are produced at the lowest possible cost — only the sellers with the lowest costs (those below the price) sell.
  • Goods satisfy the highest-valued demands — only the buyers who value the good most (those above the price) buy.

So total surplus is maximised in a free market, and the market equilibrium quantity is socially optimal. But the fine print matters for this whole chapter. That is true only

  • when all relevant production costs are incurred by sellers, and
  • when all relevant product benefits accrue to buyers (accrue = go to).

The demand curve in the oil market is labelled MBD: the demand curve measures the marginal benefit to the buyers. The supply curve measures the marginal cost to the sellers. If anyone else is affected, neither curve shows it.

2What is an externality?

Externality

Sometimes costs or benefits that result from an activity accrue to people not directly involved in the activity. These are called

  • external costs (negative externalities): harm to bystanders, for example noise, smoke, traffic jams;
  • external benefits (positive externalities): gains to bystanders, for example a neighbour's beautiful garden, vaccination that protects people around you;

— externalities for short. "External" means outside the transaction: nobody pays or is paid for it.

Example · overuse of antibiotics

Think of Staphylococcus aureus, a common bacterium that gets stronger the more antibiotics people use. Each time someone takes antibiotics (especially when they are not needed), the surviving bacteria are the tougher ones, and resistant strains spread to other people. The question: what are the costs to society of mass antibiotics (over)use? Answer: infections that no longer respond to medicine, for people who never took those pills. The patient weighs only their own benefit and cost; the cost to everyone else is external.

Analogy · Hong Kong

A minibus driver idling the engine at a terminus: he saves the trouble of restarting, while everyone on the pavement breathes the fumes. A neighbour who keeps the shared corridor clean: everyone on the floor benefits without paying. The first is an external cost, the second an external benefit.

3Violin practice: too little or too much

Positive externality: Ray and Sarah

Ray is a classical violinist. His neighbour Sarah is a fan of classical music, so for Sarah, Ray's playing creates a positive externality. If Ray practises according to his own benefits and costs, will the amount of time he plays be socially optimal (best for everyone, Ray and Sarah together)?

  • Time is perfectly divisible, so Ray keeps playing until his marginal benefit equals his marginal cost (the Chapter 1 rule MB = MC).
  • But Sarah also benefits from each extra minute. At Ray's chosen time, the total marginal benefit for society (Ray's MB + Sarah's MB) is greater than the marginal cost.
  • So one more minute would add more benefit than cost: if Ray ignores Sarah's benefit, he practises too little. The social optimum \(T^*\) is where MB to Ray + MB to Sarah = MC to Ray.

Negative externality: Eddy and Sarah

Eddy has just started learning violin, and Sarah hates hearing him. For Sarah, Eddy's playing is a negative externality.

  • Eddy plays until his MB equals his MC.
  • Each minute also costs Sarah something. At Eddy's chosen time, the total marginal cost for society (Eddy's MC + Sarah's MC) is greater than the marginal benefit.
  • So the last minutes cost more than they are worth: if Eddy ignores Sarah's cost, he practises too much. \(T^*\) is where MB to Eddy = MC to Eddy + MC to Sarah.

Invented numbers: MB to the player = 6 − 0.05T, MC to the player = 0.05T dollars per minute, so the player alone chooses 60 minutes. Sarah's value per minute is constant. The shaded triangle is the surplus lost by stopping at the private choice instead of \(T^*\).

Externalities and activity

Negative externalities ⇒ too much activity.
Positive externalities ⇒ too little activity.

In words: people ignore the effects they don't pay for (or get paid for), so they overdo the harmful things and underdo the helpful ones.

4Private, external and social: the vocabulary

Costs

Private cost: a cost paid by the consumer or producer in the market.
External cost: a cost paid by people other than the consumer or the producer trading in the market.
Social cost: the cost to everyone.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Social cost} &= \text{private cost} \\ &\quad + \text{external cost} \end{aligned} \]

In words: what society gives up = what the traders pay + what the bystanders suffer.

Benefits

Private benefit: a benefit received by the consumer or producer.
External benefit: a benefit received by people other than the consumer or producer trading in the market.
Social benefit: the benefit to everyone.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Social benefit} &= \text{private benefit} \\ &\quad + \text{external benefit} \end{aligned} \]

In words: what society gains = what the traders gain + what the bystanders gain.

The same holds unit by unit, which is how exam questions are written. Abbreviations you will meet:

ShortMeaningUsually drawn as
MPBmarginal private benefitthe demand curve
MPCmarginal private costthe supply curve
MEB / MECmarginal external benefit / cost: the spill-over from one more unita separate line, or a constant $ per unit
MSB (or SMB)marginal social benefit = MPB + MEBdemand shifted up by MEB
MSC (or SMC)marginal social cost = MPC + MECsupply shifted up by MEC

Which margin?

  • From society's perspective, we should keep expanding production as long as marginal social benefit is larger than marginal social cost.
  • When the decision is left completely to the market, the market expands production as long as marginal private benefit is larger than marginal private cost.
Market vs social optimum \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Market: } & MPB = MPC \\ \text{Society: } & MSB = MSC \end{aligned} \]

In words: the market stops where demand meets supply; society would stop where the full benefit meets the full cost. With an externality these are different quantities.

Social surplus

Social surplus (total economic welfare) \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Social surplus} &= \text{social benefit} \\ &\quad - \text{social cost} \\ &= CS + PS \\ &\quad + \text{everyone else's} \\ &\quad\;\; \text{surplus} \end{aligned} \]

In words: add up the gains of buyers, sellers and bystanders (bystanders' "surplus" is negative when they suffer an external cost). Government revenue from a tax counts as a gain and government spending on a subsidy as a loss, because taxpayers are "everyone else" too.

The socially efficient equilibrium is the price and quantity that maximise social surplus; the socially efficient quantity \(Q^*\) is the quantity that maximises social surplus.

A favourite True/False trap

With an externality the market equilibrium is still where demand = supply, i.e. where excess demand and excess supply are both zero. What moves is the social optimum. So "with external costs, the market equilibrium does not occur where excess demand and supply are zero" is false; "the social optimum does not occur there" is true. Strategy C8.6.

5External costs: overproduction and the Pigouvian tax

In markets with externalities, consumers and producers focus on the wrong margin (from society's perspective). When costs are underestimated (external costs left out), the market quantity is greater than the socially efficient quantity:

  • At the higher market output, the social costs of the last units (private + external) exceed the benefits to buyers.
  • From society's perspective we should reduce output.
  • A deadweight loss emerges, reducing social surplus. (Deadweight loss = surplus that could have existed but is lost; Chapter 6 met it under taxes.)

The picture

Draw demand (= marginal private benefit) and supply (= marginal private cost). Add the marginal external cost on top of supply to get the marginal social cost curve, which lies above supply by MEC at every quantity.

  • Market: demand meets supply at \(Q_{market}\), price \(P_{market}\).
  • Efficient: demand meets MSC at \(Q_{efficient}\), price \(P_{efficient}\) — less output, a higher price. (These notes call this \(Q_{efficient}\); exam papers say "socially efficient quantity".)
  • Deadweight loss: the triangle between MSC (above) and demand (below), from \(Q_{efficient}\) to \(Q_{market}\). Each of those units cost society more than it was worth to anyone. This is called overuse / overproduction / overconsumption.

The fix: a Pigouvian tax

Raise a per-unit tax step by step: each step shifts the supply curve up and moves the quantity closer to \(Q_{efficient}\). The right size is reached when supply + tax lies exactly on the MSC curve:

Pigouvian tax \[ \text{Tax per unit} = MEC \text{ at } Q_{efficient} \]

In words: charge each unit exactly the damage it does to others. Then supply + tax = marginal social cost, and the market lands on the efficient quantity by itself. Economists say the tax makes traders internalise the externality: they now feel it as their own cost.

Example with external costs

Antibiotics consumers ignore part of the cost of their consumption: the increased danger to others from more resistant bacteria. That increased danger is the external cost, so the market over-consumes antibiotics. A Pigouvian tax would raise the price each user faces by the extra danger they create.

External cost: market vs efficient quantity, DWL, and the tax that fixes it
demand = MPBsupply = MPCMSC = supply + MECdeadweight loss

Demand P = 100 − Q, supply P = 20 + Q, constant MEC. Tick the tax box: the DWL triangle disappears and a wedge opens between what buyers pay and what sellers keep.

Tax revenue is not a cost to society

The tax money moves from traders to the government; nothing is destroyed. It is part of social surplus (the same point holds for pollution taxes in §11). The only welfare question is whether the quantity is right.

6External benefits: underproduction and the Pigouvian subsidy

When consumers and producers ignore external benefits, they base their decisions on private benefits only. Benefits are underestimated, so the market quantity is lower than the socially efficient level:

  • At the lower market output, the social benefits of more units (private + external) exceed the cost of producing them.
  • From society's perspective we should expand output.
  • A deadweight loss emerges, reducing social surplus.

The picture

Add the marginal external benefit to demand to get the marginal social benefit curve, above demand by MEB. The market stops at \(Q_{market}\) (demand = supply); society wants \(Q_{efficient}\) where MSB = supply — more output. The deadweight loss is the triangle between MSB (above) and supply (below) from \(Q_{market}\) to \(Q_{efficient}\): units worth more to society than they cost, which never get made. This is called underuse / underproduction / underconsumption.

The fix: a Pigouvian subsidy

Pigouvian subsidy \[ \text{Subsidy per unit} = MEB \text{ at } Q_{efficient} \]

In words: pay each unit exactly the benefit it gives to others. Demand + subsidy = marginal social benefit, and the market moves to the efficient quantity. The subsidy costs the government subsidy × \(Q_{efficient}\).

External benefit: too little output, and the subsidy that fixes it
demand = MPBsupply = MPCMSB = demand + MEBdeadweight loss

Same demand P = 100 − Q and supply P = 20 + Q as above, now with a constant external benefit.

Example of external benefits · education

Education has external benefits to society (past papers list better health, lower crime, a cleaner environment). Left to the market, we end up with too little education. More education would increase social surplus, so government policy to encourage education is justified.

Careful: "encourage" means the right subsidy, not unlimited subsidy. Making university free pushes the quantity beyond \(Q_{efficient}\) and creates a new deadweight loss on the other side; past papers (2023 Summer Final Q32–Q35) ask exactly this.

Pigou's rule

When there is a negative externality, use a tax equal to the external cost to help achieve the socially optimal outcome. When there is a positive externality, use a subsidy equal to the external benefit. (Named after the English economist A. C. Pigou, 1877–1959.)

7Doing the numbers: the exam toolkit Going further

The pictures above are qualitative; the exam gives equations and wants exact numbers. Everything below follows from sections 5–6 plus Chapter 3 areas.

Ready answer · the six steps
  1. Put every curve in "P = …" form (price as a function of Q). If a paper writes "Q = 955 − 5P", solve for P: \(P = 191 - Q/5\).
  2. Market: demand = supply → \(Q_{market}\), \(P_{market}\).
  3. Social curves: MSB = demand + MEB, MSC = supply + MEC. If both an external benefit and an external cost exist, only the net spill-over MEB − MEC matters.
  4. Efficient: MSB = MSC → \(Q^*\).
  5. Welfare at any quantity \(Q_x\) = area between MSB and MSC from 0 to \(Q_x\) (a triangle or trapezoid when the lines are straight). DWL = welfare at \(Q^*\) − welfare at \(Q_x\) = the triangle between the social curves from \(Q^*\) to \(Q_x\).
  6. Pigouvian tax/subsidy = net spill-over evaluated at \(Q^*\) (= vertical gap between demand and supply at \(Q^*\)). Revenue or subsidy bill = per-unit amount × \(Q^*\).
Worked example

Demand: \(Q = 300 - 2P\). Supply: \(Q = 3P - 60\) (P in dollars, Q in thousand units). Each unit causes an external cost of $15.

  1. "P =" form. Demand \(P = 150 - Q/2\); supply \(P = 20 + Q/3\).
  2. Market. \(300 - 2P = 3P - 60\) gives \(P = 72\), \(Q = 156\).
  3. Social cost. \(MSC = 20 + Q/3 + 15 = 35 + Q/3\).
  4. Efficient. \(150 - Q/2 = 35 + Q/3\) gives \(115 = 5Q/6\), so \(Q^* = 138\).
  5. Welfare. MSB − MSC \(= 115 - \tfrac56 Q\), a line falling from 115 to 0 at \(Q^*\). Welfare at \(Q^*\) = \(\tfrac12 \times 138 \times 115 = 7935\). At the market, the extra 18 units each lose between 0 and \(\tfrac56 \cdot 156 - 115 = 15\): DWL = \(\tfrac12 \times 18 \times 15 = 135\), so welfare at the market = 7800 (thousand dollars).
  6. Check by pieces. CS = \(\tfrac12 \times 156 \times (150 - 72) = 6084\); PS = \(\tfrac12 \times 156 \times (72 - 20) = 4056\); external cost accrued = \(15 \times 156 = 2340\). CS + PS − external cost = 7800 ✓.
  7. Tax. MEC is constant, so tax = 15. At \(Q^* = 138\) buyers pay \(150 - 69 = 81\) (up 9) and sellers keep \(20 + 46 = 66\) (down 6). Consumers bear the larger share because demand is the less responsive side here (Chapter 6 incidence).

\(Q_{market} = 156\), \(Q^* = 138\), DWL = 135, tax = 15 per unit.

Inverting a social-benefit equation

Papers often write "\(Q = 200 - 0.9 \times SMB\)". Solve for SMB: \(SMB = (200 - Q)/0.9\). It is not \(200 - 0.9Q\). At \(Q = 86\): \(SMB = 114/0.9 = 126.67\).

Going further · four more exam facts
  • "Total external cost (or benefit) accrued" = area under the MEC (MEB) line from 0 to the quantity actually traded. Constant MEC: just MEC × Q.
  • Non-constant externality: the tax must equal MEC at \(Q^*\), not at the market quantity. A tax can still fix a non-constant externality.
  • Permits instead of a tax: selling exactly \(Q^*\) permits (licence plates, building permits) works like the tax: buyers bid the permit price up to the tax amount, and revenue = permit price × \(Q^*\). See §12.
  • Welfare can be negative. If a subsidy makes a good free, buyers take the quantity where demand hits P = 0, far past \(Q^*\); the loss from the extra units can exceed all the gains.

8Property rights: Smith's smokestack

The third part of this chapter asks: do we always need the government? Ronald Coase's answer: not if the people involved can bargain.

Smith can produce with or without a filter on his smokestack. Producing without a filter causes more smoke damage to his neighbour Jones:

With filterWithout filterDifference
Gains to Smith$200/week$245/week$45/week
Damage to Jones$35/week$85/week$50/week
Total surplus$165/week$160/week$5/week

Question: if Smith is not liable for smoke damage (the law does not make him pay for it) and the two can negotiate costlessly, will he install a filter?

The answer
  1. Efficiency. Total surplus is $5 higher with the filter: \(200 - 35 = 165 \gt 245 - 85 = 160\).
  2. Smith's side. The filter costs Smith \(245 - 200 = 45\) a week. Not being liable, he need not install it — but if Jones pays him at least $45, he will gladly do so.
  3. Jones's side. The filter saves Jones \(85 - 35 = 50\) a week. So Jones is willing to pay up to $50.
  4. Deal. Any payment between $45 and $50 makes both better off. The filter is installed — the efficient outcome — even though Smith is not liable.

If instead Smith were liable (he must pay for the damage), he would compare 200 − 35 = 165 with 245 − 85 = 160 himself and install the filter without any negotiation. Either way, the filter goes in.

Perpetrator or victim?

The traditional (pre-Coase) view: Smith is the perpetrator (the one doing the harm), Jones the victim. If it is Smith's smoke that damages Jones, why should Jones pay Smith to install a filter?

Coase's insight is that the harm is reciprocal: who "harms" whom depends on the starting point, i.e. on who holds the right.

Status quo: with filterSwitching to without filter gives Smith +$45 and costs Jones $50. Smith is the perpetrator, Jones the victim.
Status quo: without filterSwitching to with filter costs Smith $45 and gives Jones +$50. Now Jones's demand harms Smith: Jones is the "perpetrator", Smith the "victim".

In words: any externality is a conflict over the use of something both want (clean air vs cheap production). Economics asks which use is worth more, not who is to blame.

9The Coase theorem and the unobstructed view

The Coase theorem

If property rights are fully assigned and if people can negotiate costlessly with one another, they will always arrive at efficient solutions to problems caused by externalities.

In words: when it is clear who owns what and bargaining is free, the parties will trade their way to the outcome with the biggest total surplus. The law decides only who pays whom, not what happens.

Ronald Coase won the 1991 Nobel Prize in Economics. Optional further reading: Posner (1993), "Nobel Laureate Ronald Coase and Methodology", Journal of Economic Perspectives 7(4); and "Of Bees and Lighthouses", The Economist, 23 Feb 1991.

Terms

Property right: a legal right to use something (here: the right to pollute, or the right to clean air) — including the right to sell or give it up.
Liable: legally required to pay for damage you cause. "Not liable" = the right is with the polluter; "fully liable" = the right is with the victim.
Negotiation (transaction) costs: costs of reaching a deal — lawyers, time, finding and organising everyone affected.

Example: the right to an unobstructed view

James owns a house near the Peak with a commanding sunset view over the harbour. Chris buys the property below and is choosing between (a) a one-story house that leaves James's view intact and (b) a two-story house that blocks it completely.

One-storyTwo-story
Damage to James0100
Gain to Chris200280
Total surplus200180

Efficient outcome: one story (200 > 180). The second story gives Chris 80 but costs James 100.

Right with Chris, costless bargainingChris would build two stories, but James pays him between $80 and $100 to build one. One story.
Right with James, costless bargainingChris would need James's consent for two stories; he would pay at most 80, James wants at least 100. No deal. One story.

If negotiations are costless, it does not matter how we assign the property right — the efficient one-story house is built either way.

What if negotiations are very costly or impractical? Then nobody bargains and the right-holder simply does what is best for them:

  • Right to build goes to Chris → a two-story house is built (inefficient).
  • Right to an unobstructed view goes to James → a one-story house is built (efficient).

So when bargaining is costly, the assignment of rights matters, and the law should give the right to the party who would have ended up with it under free bargaining. Zoning laws (rules on what may be built where, such as height limits in parts of Hong Kong) are one way governments do this.

Ready answer · discrete Coase questions
  1. Total surplus of each option (gains − damages, or −costs − damages). Bigger total = efficient.
  2. Costless bargaining + clear rights → the efficient option, whoever holds the right.
  3. Side payment lies between the payer's loss from the switch and the receiver's gain from avoiding it.
  4. With a negotiation cost: start from what the right-holder would do alone. A switch happens only if the total surplus gain from switching is bigger than the negotiation cost. If the right-holder's own choice is already efficient, nobody negotiates and nobody pays lawyers.

In words: the efficient answer wins only when the gain from getting there covers the cost of bargaining. Strategy C8.3.

Try it: who holds the right, and how costly is bargaining?

Numbers as in the example above (Chris gains 200 or 280; James loses 0 or 100). The surplus gain from switching two-story → one-story is 20, so bargaining succeeds only if it costs less than $20.

10Continuous bargaining: MB and MEC curves Going further

The Coase examples so far are yes/no choices. Exam questions also use the violin story from §3 with curves: \(Q\) hours of noise, the noise-maker's marginal benefit MB, and the neighbour's marginal external cost MEC.

Ready answer · continuous bargaining
  1. No bargaining. Noise-maker holds the right → plays until MB = 0. Neighbour holds the right → \(Q = 0\).
  2. Costless bargaining. Either way \(Q^*\) where MB = MEC.
  3. Surpluses. Noise-maker: area under MB up to Q (± payments). Neighbour: minus the area under MEC up to Q (± payments).
  4. Bargaining ranges. Each side is at least as well off as with no deal, and at most gets the whole gain from moving to \(Q^*\). Gain = the triangle between MB and MEC from the no-deal point to \(Q^*\). So: lower bound = no-deal surplus; upper bound = no-deal surplus + gain.
  5. With a negotiation cost, the move happens only if the gain exceeds the cost.

In words: the right decides the starting point and so who pays; costless bargaining always ends at MB = MEC.

Loud music: MB = 10 − Q to Smith, MEC = Q to Jones
Smith's MBJones's MECgain from bargaining to Q*

Smith's right: no-deal Q = 10 (Smith +50, Jones −50). Jones's right: no-deal Q = 0 (both 0). Moving to Q* = 5 creates a gain of 25 in either case (green triangle).

Reading the numbers

Smith holds the right. At \(Q^* = 5\): Smith's benefit = area under MB from 0 to 5 = 37.5; Jones's cost = \(\tfrac12 \times 5 \times 5 = 12.5\); total 25 (vs 0 at the no-deal point, where 50 − 50 = 0). Jones pays Smith to play less. Smith ends between 50 and 75 (so the payment is between 12.5 and 37.5); Jones ends between −50 and −25.

Jones holds the right. Smith pays Jones to allow 5 hours. Smith ends between 0 and 25, Jones between 0 and 25. Same \(Q^*\), same total 25: the right does not matter for total welfare but matters for its distribution.

11Taxing pollution: firms X and Y

Two firms, X and Y, each have five production processes. Each process has a different daily cost and gives off a different amount of smoke:

Process (daily smoke)A (4 t)B (3 t)C (2 t)D (1 t)E (0 t)
Cost to firm X20029070013002100
Cost to firm Y5080140230325

If pollution is unregulated and the firms cannot negotiate with their victims, each firm uses A, the cheapest process: 4 tons each, 8 tons a day in total.

The city council wants to cut emissions by half (to 4 tons) and considers two options: (A) require each firm to halve its emissions; (B) set a tax of T on each ton of smoke emitted each day. How large must T be, and how do the total costs to society compare?

Option A: command each firm to cut by half

Each firm must move from A to C (2 tons each). Cost of the switch: X \(700 - 200 = 500\) a day; Y \(140 - 50 = 90\) a day. Total cost 590 a day.

Option B: a tax per ton

Switching to the next cleaner process cuts pollution by 1 ton a day and saves tax of T a day. If the cost of switching to the next process is less than or equal to T, the firm switches; otherwise not. So first turn the table into the cost of cutting each ton:

Cutting the …1st ton (A→B)2nd (B→C)3rd (C→D)4th (D→E)
Firm X90410600800
Firm Y30609095
  • T = 50: X's first cut costs 90 > 50, so X stays at A. Y's first cut costs 30 ≤ 50 (switch), its second 60 > 50 (stop): Y moves to B. Emissions 4 + 3 = 7 tons — not the desired halving.
  • T = 91: X cuts one ton (90 ≤ 91) → B. Y cuts 30, 60, 90 (all ≤ 91) but not 95 → D. Emissions 3 + 1 = 4 tons, as desired. Cost to X: \(290 - 200 = 90\); cost to Y: \(230 - 50 = 180\). Total 270 a day — 320 less than the command.
Why the tax is cheaper

Under a tax every firm cuts every ton that costs less than T to cut and keeps every ton that costs more. So the 4 tons that get cut are automatically the 4 cheapest tons to cut in the whole economy (30, 60, 90, 90). The command forced X to make its expensive 410 cut while Y's cheap 90 and 95 cuts went unused.

In words: a tax lets the firms that can cut cheaply do most of the cutting. Any T from 90 up to (but not including) 95 achieves exactly 4 tons; take 91.

Tax payments are not a social cost

The taxes the firms pay (here \(91 \times 4 = 364\) a day) are not included in the social cost of the tax option, because this money is not lost to society: it can be used to reduce whatever taxes would otherwise be levied on citizens. Social cost = the extra production costs only (270).

Going further · two exam twists

Efficient pollution level. If each ton does $D of damage to residents, society wants a ton emitted only if it is worth more than D to the firm. With D = 100 here: X keeps tons worth 800, 600, 410 (cuts the 90 one) → B; Y cuts all four (worth 95, 90, 60, 30) → E. Efficient total 3 tons. A tax equal to the damage gets there.

Tables to watch. Some papers give profits instead of costs (then the value of a ton = extra profit from the dirtier process), and some list the letters in reverse (A = 0 tons). Always rebuild the "value of each ton" row first.

12Pollution permits, and permits vs taxes

Same two firms, but now the government issues pollution permits: each permit allows its holder to emit 1 ton a day, 4 permits in total. Will the pollution generated by each firm depend on how the permits are allocated?

Each firm gets 2 permits
  1. With 2 permits each, both firms must use C (2 tons): X pays 700, Y pays 140.
  2. If X had one more permit it could move C → B: 1 more ton, saving $410.
  3. If Y gave up one permit it would move C → D: 1 less ton, costing $90.
  4. So X will pay Y anything between $90 and $410 for one permit, and both gain. Negotiation leads to the new allocation: 3 tons for X (process B), 1 ton for Y (process D).
  5. Any further trade? X's next extra ton would save only 90; Y's next cut would cost 95. No gain: trading stops.

Same result as the tax: X at B, Y at D, extra cost 270 a day (total production cost 520 vs 840 under the 2-and-2 command).

If all 4 permits had gone to X, or all to Y, trading would end at the same place: the firm that values a ton more buys it. The initial allocation decides who pays whom (Coase again), not where the pollution ends up.

Tradable allowances vs Pigouvian taxes

  • There is a close relationship between taxes and tradable allowances (permits) for internalising externalities.
  • A tax set equal to the external cost is equivalent to tradable allowances when the number of allowances is set equal to the efficient quantity. The tax fixes the price and lets the quantity follow; permits fix the quantity and let the price follow (the permit price ends up equal to the tax).
  • Problem: imperfect information. For a tax we need to know the optimal tax, i.e. the size of the externality. For permits we need to know the socially efficient quantity.
  • Solution: research + "trial and error" (set a tax or permit number, watch the result, adjust).
Going further · continuous abatement costs

Some papers give each firm's marginal cost of reduction as a line, e.g. \(MC_X = 10 + 2R_X\), \(MC_Y = 20 + R_Y\), with a required total reduction of 40 tons. Permits trade until the last ton cut costs the same at both firms:

\[ \begin{aligned} 10 + 2R_X &= 20 + R_Y \\ R_X + R_Y &= 40 \end{aligned} \]

Substituting \(R_Y = 40 - R_X\): \(3R_X = 50\), so \(R_X = 16.67\), \(R_Y = 23.33\), and both marginal costs = 43.33 (the permit price). Emissions = each firm's unregulated emissions minus its reduction.

In words: the cheapest way to cut a fixed total is to equalise the marginal cost of cutting across firms — the Chapter 1 allocation rule again.

13Summary

  • A free market maximises total surplus only if all costs fall on sellers and all benefits on buyers. Externalities are costs or benefits that land on bystanders.
  • Social = private + external, for costs and for benefits. The market sets MPB = MPC; society wants MSB = MSC.
  • Negative externality → too much output; positive → too little. Either way a deadweight-loss triangle appears between the social curves.
  • Pigouvian tax = MEC at the efficient quantity; Pigouvian subsidy = MEB at the efficient quantity. Tax revenue is a transfer, not a social cost.
  • Coase theorem: with clear property rights and costless negotiation, the parties reach the efficient outcome whoever holds the right; the right only decides who pays. With costly negotiation the assignment matters (zoning laws).
  • Continuous version: costless bargaining ends at MB = MEC; the right sets the no-deal point and the range of payments.
  • Least-cost pollution control: a uniform command is expensive; a tax or tradable permits make the cheapest cuts happen first. A tax equal to the external cost ≡ permits equal to the efficient quantity. Both need information (research + trial and error).

14Common mistakes

✗Using the demand curve instead of MSB (or supply instead of MSC) for welfare. ✓ Social welfare = area between the social curves. ✗Inverting "\(Q = a - b \cdot SMB\)" as \(SMB = a - bQ\). ✓ \(SMB = (a - Q)/b\). ✗Setting a non-constant tax at MEC of the market quantity. ✓ Evaluate MEC at \(Q^*\). ✗Treating an external benefit and an external cost separately. ✓ Use the net spill-over MEB − MEC; its sign decides tax vs subsidy. ✗Picking a price ceiling or floor to fix an externality. ✓ The answer is a tax or subsidy (or permits). ✗Counting tax revenue as a cost of the tax policy. ✓ Only extra production/abatement costs are social costs. ✗"Coase works whenever rights are clear." ✓ It also needs negligible negotiation costs. ✗Assuming lawyers are always hired. ✓ If the right-holder's own choice is already efficient, no one negotiates. ✗Reading a process table without rebuilding "cost of each ton". ✓ Difference the costs (or profits) first; watch reversed letters. ✗"Cut by two-thirds" read as "cut to two-thirds". ✓ From 12 tons: by ⅔ → 4 tons; to ⅔ → 8 tons. ✗Forgetting units: thousand units × thousand dollars. ✓ = million dollars.

15Find the mistake

Each "solution" below has one error. Find it before opening.

1

Demand P = 100 − Q, supply P = 20 + Q, MEC = 0.5Q. "The market quantity is 40, where MEC = 20, so the Pigouvian tax is $20."

Mistake

The tax must equal MEC at the efficient quantity. \(100 - Q = 20 + Q + 0.5Q\) gives \(Q^* = 32\), so tax = \(0.5 \times 32 = \$16\). Check: with a $16 tax, \(100 - Q = 36 + Q\) gives Q = 32 ✓. A $20 tax would overshoot to Q = 30.

2 · numbers from §11

"The tax option costs society 270 + 91 × 4 = 634 a day, more than the command's 590, so the command is cheaper."

Mistake

The 364 of tax is a transfer to the government, not a resource used up (§11). Social cost of the tax option = 270 < 590.

3 · numbers as in 2020 Spring Final

"\(Q = 200 - 0.9 \times SMB\)". "So SMB = 200 − 0.9Q, and at Q = 86, SMB = 122.6."

Mistake

Solve for SMB properly: \(0.9 \cdot SMB = 200 - Q\), so \(SMB = (200 - Q)/0.9\). At Q = 86, SMB = 114/0.9 = 126.67.

4 · numbers from §9

James holds the right to an unobstructed view; negotiating costs $30. "Since negotiation is costly, Coase fails, so Chris builds the inefficient two-story house."

Mistake

With James holding the right, the no-deal outcome is already one story, which is efficient. Nobody needs to negotiate. A costly negotiation only blocks a switch; it leaves the right-holder's choice in place.

P1 · external benefit with equations

Demand P = 90 − Q, supply P = 10 + Q, constant MEB = $20 per unit. Find the market quantity and price, the efficient quantity, the deadweight loss, the Pigouvian subsidy and what it costs the government.

Answer

P2 · Coase with a lawyer

A factory chooses technology A (gain to factory $300, damage to residents $100) or B (gain $260, damage $30). (a) Which is efficient? (b) Factory not liable, negotiation free: what happens and what payment range? (c) Factory not liable, residents must spend $35 on a lawyer to negotiate: what happens?

Hint

Totals: A 200, B 230. The switch A → B gains 30 in total.

Answer

(a) B. (b) Factory would pick A; residents pay between $40 (factory's loss) and $70 (their saving) for B → B. (c) Residents would pay at least 40 + 35 = 75 > 70 saved: no deal → A (the 30 gain doesn't cover the 35 cost).

P3 · tax range from a process table

Costs for 4, 3, 2, 1, 0 tons: firm X 100, 150, 260, 420, 700; firm Y 40, 110, 200, 320, 480. The government wants total emissions of 3 tons (from 8). What tax range works, which processes are chosen, and what is the extra cost to society?

Hint

Cut costs: X 50, 110, 160, 280; Y 70, 90, 120, 160. Five cuts are needed.

Answer

Cheapest five cuts: 50, 70, 90, 110, 120; the next costs 160. So 120 ≤ T < 160. X cuts 2 tons (2 left), Y cuts 3 (1 left): 3 tons. Extra cost = 50 + 70 + 90 + 110 + 120 = $440.

17Concept checks

1

A bakery's smell delights passers-by. Too much or too little baking, and what policy?

Answer

Positive externality → too little; a subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit at the efficient quantity.

2

With an external cost, does the market still clear (no excess demand or supply)?

Answer

Yes. The market equilibrium is still demand = supply; it is simply not the social optimum.

3

Coase: who should pay — the polluter or the victim?

Answer

Efficiency doesn't care: with costless bargaining the outcome is the same. The law (who holds the right) decides who pays whom.

4

Why can't a price ceiling fix an external cost?

Answer

A ceiling lowers the price sellers receive, so they supply even less and buyers face shortages — it doesn't put the external cost into anyone's decision. A tax does, by raising the price buyers pay above what sellers keep by exactly MEC.

5

Two firms must cut 10 tons in total. Firm X can cut up to 10 tons at $50 a ton; firm Y up to 10 tons at $200 a ton. Command "5 tons each" vs a tax of $100 a ton: costs?

Answer

Command: 5 × 50 + 5 × 200 = $1250. Tax $100: X cuts all 10 tons, Y none: 10 × 50 = $500. The tax gets the same cut for less.

6

Permits: does it matter who receives them first?

Answer

Not for where pollution ends up (with costless trading) — only for who pays whom.
