Ch.2 Comparative advantage · Exam guide

← Start HereChapter note · Ch.2Previous: guide Ch.1Next: guide Ch.3

Every Ch.2 question from 2020 Spring to 2025 Spring Supplementary (137 unique questions, 144 parts with repeats) sorted into six strategies. This chapter is predictable: almost every question is "compute two opportunity costs, then compare". Learn the cards and ready answers, then drill.

0How Ch.2 is examined

  • Where: right after the Ch.1 block (usually Q4–Q15) of almost every midterm and final; 27 sittings 2020–2025 have a Ch.2 question. Questions often come in a chain of 2–4 parts on one setup (a window, then a closed-economy target, then the open economy).
  • Format (unchanged since 2020): an online quiz. Fill-in numeric blanks, A/B direction blanks ("larger / smaller than ___"), multiple choice (who has the CA / AA, who makes what), "how many statements are true", True/False. No marks are shown on the papers.
  • Answers are exact. Enter 2 decimal places and carry full precision through a chain (a rounded 23.02 fed into the next part can move the last digit).
  • No formula sheet. Memorise the box below.
Memorise for Ch.2
  • OC of X = \(Y_{max}/X_{max}\) from outputs; = time for X ÷ time for Y from times or costs. Lower OC = comparative advantage; more output = absolute advantage.
  • PPC \(Y = a - kX\): k is the OC of X.
  • Terms-of-trade window: strictly between the two OCs. Bargaining: worst = own OC, best = partner's OC.
  • Low-hanging fruit: expand a good using the lowest-OC person first.
  • Bundle alone: \(n = 1/(r_x/X_{max} + r_y/Y_{max})\).
  • Open economy: make X iff OC of X < \(P_X/P_Y\). Then income \(I\), and \(Y = (I - P_X x)/P_Y\).
  • The logic fact (C2.1 ready answer).

Practice mode:

1The six strategies at a glance

Which strategy?
  1. Asked who has an advantage, or a threshold for one unknown capacity → C2.1.
  2. Asked about the terms of trade between the two people (a window, best/worst in a negotiation, or capacities from a window) → C2.2.
  3. Closed economy, a target amount of one good, max of the other → C2.3. If the goods must be consumed in a fixed ratio or bundle → C2.4.
  4. World prices given: asked what or how much to produce → C2.5; asked how much they can consume → C2.6.

C2.1 Who has the advantage 17 parts · 16 unique

Looks likeTwo people, two goods, outputs per day or minutes per unit; "who has the CA / AA", "X is smaller than ___", logic statements about Tom and Jerry.

DoOne OC per person for the SAME good; lower OC = CA. AA = more output (or less time). For an unknown X, write the two OCs and solve.

WatchTime-per-unit tables are NOT inverted; AA ≠ CA; one person can have AA in both goods but never CA in both.

C2.2 Terms-of-trade window and bargaining 29 parts · 28 unique

Looks like"Terms of trade must be larger than ___ and less than ___", "best terms the UK can reasonably ask for / worst it can accept", or the window given and capacities asked.

DoWindow = the two OCs in the asked units. Bargaining: worst = own OC, best = partner's OC. Reverse: the specialist in good 1 has the higher OC of good 2.

WatchUnits (crabs per lobster vs lobsters per crab); "best" can be the smaller number; match each window end to the right person.

C2.3 Low-hanging fruit on a joint PPC 32 parts · 30 unique

Looks likeClosed economy: "if they consume x of good 1, the most of good 2 is ___"; teams of 3–5 with a table; a kinked joint PPC with slopes only.

DoEveryone on good 2, then move people to good 1 in order of lowest OC of good 1 until the target is met; the last person splits.

WatchScale by hours worked; minutes-per-unit tables → units = minutes worked ÷ minutes per unit; rank by OC, never by speed.

C2.4 Fixed-ratio bundles: alone vs together 25 parts · 24 unique

Looks likeGarage services (1 clutch + 1 or 2 brakes), fruit baskets, a 3:1 consumption ratio, or a marriage choice in a closed economy.

DoAlone: \(n(r_x/X_{max} + r_y/Y_{max}) = 1\). Together: specialise by CA, the short good's maker splits; % more = (together ÷ separate − 1) × 100.

WatchBundle with 2 brakes; answer the good asked, not the bundle count; gains grow with the GAP between OCs.

C2.5 What to produce at world prices 15 parts · 15 unique

Looks likeSmall open economy with world prices or a world ToT: "Chris should produce ___", "together they will catch ___", "X is larger than ___".

DoMake good X iff your OC of X is below \(P_X/P_Y\) (equivalently, the good worth more per day). At a kink, the price ratio sits between the two slopes.

WatchCompare with \(P_X/P_Y\), not its inverse; both people can make the same good; the domestic window is not the world price.

C2.6 Consumption at world prices 26 parts · 24 unique

Looks like"Opened to trade at ___ … if they consume x of good 1, they can consume at most ___ of good 2", a consumption ratio, a kinked PPC with coordinates, or a marriage with world prices.

DoEarn: produce at the income-maximising point (per person, or best corner). Spend: \(Y = (I - P_X x)/P_Y\); ratio \(k\!:\!1\): \(c = I/(kP_X + P_Y)\).

WatchProduce what pays, not what you eat; a ToT "Y per X" means \(P_X/P_Y\); CPC = PPC only when closed.

C2.1Who has the advantage

17 parts, 16 unique · newest first within each form · Note §2 Comparative advantage

Do
  1. Write each person's opportunity cost of the same good (say X), in units of the other good Y.
    • Output per day / per hour: \(\text{OC of X} = Y_{max} / X_{max}\).
    • Minutes or man hours or cost per unit: \(\text{OC of X} = \text{time for X} / \text{time for Y}\) — no flip.
  2. Lower OC of X → CA in X; the other person has the CA in Y.
  3. Absolute advantage: compare output per day (or time per unit) of that good directly.
  4. Unknown X: write "OC₁ < OC₂", solve, and test one value to check the direction blank.
  5. Statement sets: translate each statement to "lower OC" or "more output", then apply the logic fact below.
Ready answer · the logic fact

If A has the absolute advantage in good 1 but B has the comparative advantage in good 1, then A must also have the absolute advantage in good 2.

Why: B gives up less of good 2 per unit of good 1 while making less good 1, so B must also make less good 2.

Statement formTruth
"X has a lower OC of the good X has the CA in"True (definition)
"The person without the CA in good 1 must have the CA in good 2"True
"The person with the CA but not the AA in good 1 may have the AA in good 2"False (logic fact)
"The person with the AA in a good can produce more of it per week"True (definition)
"Faster at both tasks → AA in both"True

Find the threshold for X 5 questions

2025 Spring Supp Q6marks not shownthreshold-X

In a day, Joshua can catch 19 kgs of crab, or 5 kgs of fish. In a day, Louise can catch 3 kgs of crab, or X kgs of fish. Joshua has the comparative advantage in catching fish and Louise has the comparative advantage in catching crab if and only if X is 06A (A. larger; B. smaller) than 06B.

Hint

Write each person's OC of one kg of fish in kg of crab, then ask when Joshua's is lower.

Solution
  1. Joshua: OC of 1 fish = 19 ÷ 5 = 3.8 crab.
  2. Louise: OC of 1 fish = 3 ÷ X crab.
  3. Joshua has the CA in fish iff \(3.8 \lt 3/X\), i.e. \(X \lt 3/3.8 = 0.789\).
  4. Then Louise automatically has the CA in crab.

Marker note. Check the direction with a test value: X = 0.5 gives Louise an OC of fish of 6, above 3.8 ✓.

Answer: B (smaller), 0.79

Suppose John can catch one fish in X minutes or one crab in 15 minutes. Mary can catch one fish in 6 minutes or one crab in 10 minutes. When X is smaller than 04A, John has an absolute advantage in catching fish. When X is smaller than 04B, John has a comparative advantage in catching fish.

C2.2Terms-of-trade window and bargaining

29 parts, 28 unique · newest first within each form · Note §3 Terms of trade

Do
  1. Compute the two OCs of the good named after "per" in the question's units (e.g. "lobsters per crab" → OC of a crab in lobsters).
  2. Window: lower OC to higher OC.
  3. Bargaining for country K: worst acceptable = K's own OC; best it can reasonably ask = partner's OC. (Check: K must gain at least zero, partner too.)
  4. Reverse: specialised totals = each specialist's max of their own good. The person with the CA in good 1 has the HIGHER OC of good 2 → that end of the window. Max of the other good = own max ÷ OC (or × OC for the other person).
Ready answer · bargaining

Quote everything as N per D. A country with the LOWER OC of D exports D: worst = own OC (low), best = partner's OC (high). A country with the HIGHER OC of D imports D: best = partner's OC (low), worst = own OC (high).

From the PPC equation \(Y = a - kX\): k is the OC of X in Y. From man hours: \(\text{OC of D} = \text{hours for D} / \text{hours for N}\).
