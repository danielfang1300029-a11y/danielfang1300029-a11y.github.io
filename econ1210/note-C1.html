Cost–benefit analysis

The one idea the whole course is built on: do something when what you gain from it beats what it costs you. This chapter turns that idea into three tools you will use in every later chapter: economic surplus, thinking at the margin, and opportunity cost.

0Where this sits

This chapter has three parts:

  1. Basic ideas: the cost–benefit principle, rationality, and how economists use models (sections 1–3 below).

1The cost–benefit principle and economic surplus

Start with two quick questions:

Question AYou can pick up a cheque for $500 at Disneyland. The entry ticket costs $400. Go?
Yes: you gain $500 and pay $400, so you are $100 better off.
Question BSame, but the cheque is only $100. Go?
No: you gain $100 and pay $400, so you are $300 worse off.

You answered both without effort. The cost–benefit principle just writes down what you did.

Definitions

Additional benefit \(B(x)\): everything you gain because you do activity \(x\), compared with not doing it, measured in dollars (how much you would be willing to pay for it).

Additional cost \(C(x)\): everything you give up because you do \(x\), compared with not doing it, also in dollars.

Rational: a decision-maker (a person, a firm, a society) who has goals and picks actions to reach them as well as possible. More in §2.

The cost–benefit principle

A rational decision-maker should do \(x\) if \(B(x) \gt C(x)\) and should not if \(B(x) \lt C(x)\).

In words: do it when what you gain is bigger than what you give up.

The same rule is often written with one number:

Economic surplus \[ ES(x) = B(x) - C(x) \]

In words: economic surplus = benefit − cost = how much better off the action makes you, in dollars.

Do \(x\) if \(ES(x) \gt 0\); don't if \(ES(x) \lt 0\). If \(ES(x) = 0\) you are indifferent: either choice is equally good.

In Question A, \(ES = 500 - 400 = 100 \gt 0\), so go. In Question B, \(ES = 100 - 400 = -300 \lt 0\), so stay home.

Try it: benefit vs cost

Start at Question A ($500 vs $400), then drag the benefit down to $100 for Question B.

Exam wording on ties

Past papers often ask "should she go if X is at least …" or "larger than or equal to …". At the boundary \(ES = 0\) and she is indifferent, so the papers include the boundary value. Copy the inequality word the question gives you; the number in the blank is the break-even value either way.

2Rationality and economic models

The rationality assumption

Economists assume people have goals and try to fulfil them as best they can. Some examples:

  • Firms maximise profit (revenue minus costs).
  • Consumers maximise their satisfaction from what they consume.
  • Governments maximise … — left open on purpose. A government's goal depends on who it serves. It might be total welfare of citizens, or votes, or something else. Later chapters (taxes, externalities, public goods) take it to be total economic surplus in the economy.

"Rational" does not mean selfish, clever or unemotional. It only means that choices are consistent with some goal. A person who gives money to charity because helping others makes them happy is rational.

Modelling a complex world

A model is a simplified description of how something works, used to reason about it. Economists need models to make inferences, predict outcomes and propose policies. If we tried to include every detail of the real world, the model would take forever to build and be too complicated to use.

The key is abstraction:

  1. Identify the most important elements and build them into the model.
  2. Confront the model with data (compare what it predicts with what really happens) and modify it to improve the fit.
Analogy

The MTR map shows stations as dots on straight coloured lines. It ignores the real curves of the tunnels, distances and street names. It is "unrealistic", yet it is exactly what you need to plan a journey. A good economic model is the same: it leaves things out on purpose.

What is a good model?

  • A model's assumptions (for example rationality) may leave out things some of us consider important.
  • The ultimate test of a model is how well it fits the data and how well it predicts — not how realistic its assumptions are.
  • Rationality has proved useful in fitting and predicting people's behaviour.
  • Besides, it is more realistic than you may think: most everyday choices (which bus to take, whether a sale is worth queuing for) really are made by weighing gains against costs.

Inference, prediction and policy

Rational individuals choose actions to maximise some goal, subject to their constraints (limits they cannot escape: money, time, rules). This gives economists three uses for the cost–benefit principle:

PredictionKnow the decision-maker's goal and constraints → predict what they will choose.
InferenceObserve what they choose → work out (guess) their goals and constraints.
PolicyChange their goals/preferences and/or their constraints → cause them to choose differently.

3Inference, prediction, policy: the "Sleep No More" example

"Sleep No More" is an immersive drama staged in New York and Shanghai: the audience walks freely through a building while the actors perform around them.

Case 1You are willing to pay $1200 to see it. Ticket price $800. Go?
Yes: \(ES = 1200 - 800 = 400 \gt 0\).
Case 2You are willing to pay $500. Ticket price $800. Go?
No: \(ES = 500 - 800 = -300 \lt 0\).
Willingness to pay (WTP)

The most you would pay for something rather than go without it. It is how economists measure benefit in dollars. A rational buyer buys when WTP ≥ price.

Inference

Observed: Jon pays $800 to see the drama. Here are three possible conclusions. Let us judge each:

  • "Jon is willing to pay more than $800." This is the economic inference. Strictly, we learn WTP ≥ $800 (if WTP were exactly $800 he would be indifferent and might still go).
  • "Jon has more than $800 in his bank account." This is about his constraint, not his preference: he could afford it (at least $800 available). Cost–benefit analysis is about the first kind of conclusion.
  • "We will have a better idea with more observations." True: each extra observed choice narrows the range his WTP can lie in.

What if Brandon did not go? Then (assuming nothing else stopped him) Brandon's WTP is below $800.

Prediction

  • Price $700: Jon's WTP is at least $800, which is more than $700. He will go.
  • Price $900: we cannot tell. We only know WTP ≥ $800; it could be above or below $900.

Policy

If we want more people to see the drama, we can:

  • Advertise — this raises people's WTP (changes preferences).
  • Reduce the ticket price — this changes the cost side (the constraint).

Either change can turn a negative ES into a positive one for some people.

Try it: each observation narrows someone's WTP

Filled dots = bought (WTP is at least that price); open dots = did not buy (WTP is below that price). The shaded band is every WTP still possible.

Ready answer · revealed preference

Bought at price \(p\) ⇒ WTP ≥ \(p\). Did not buy at price \(q\) ⇒ WTP \(\lt q\). Several observations: WTP lies between the highest price at which they bought and the lowest price at which they refused.

In words: a choice reveals which side of the price the valuation lies on. This is strategy C1.4.

4How many? The optimal quantity, one unit at a time

Many choices are not yes/no but "how much": how many scoops of ice-cream, how many hours to study, how many workers to hire.

Totals

\(TB(n)\) = total benefit of having \(n\) units. \(TC(n)\) = total cost of having \(n\) units.

The brute-force way: compute \(ES(n) = TB(n) - TC(n)\) for every \(n\), and pick the \(n\) with the biggest ES.

The easier way: turn the decision into a sequence of yes/no questions.

  1. Starting from zero scoops: should I have the 1st scoop?
  2. Starting from one scoop: should I have the 2nd?
  3. … Starting from \(n-1\) scoops: should I have the \(n\)-th?

I have the \(n\)-th scoop if its additional benefit is larger than its additional cost.

Thinking at the margin

Trade-offs are usually at the margin. "Marginal" means "additional".

Marginal benefit and marginal cost (whole units) \[ \begin{aligned} MB(n) &= TB(n) - TB(n-1) \\ MC(n) &= TC(n) - TC(n-1) \end{aligned} \]

In words: marginal benefit of the \(n\)-th unit = total benefit with \(n\) units minus total benefit with one fewer. Same for cost.

Marginal rule

Do the \(n\)-th unit if \(MB(n) \gt MC(n)\).

In words: take one more unit whenever that unit adds more benefit than cost.

When can you stop?

Assume:

  • MB is non-increasing: each extra unit adds no more benefit than the one before (the 5th scoop is less exciting than the 1st). Economists call this diminishing marginal benefit.
  • MC is non-decreasing: each extra unit costs at least as much as the one before.

Under these assumptions, you can stop as soon as you say no: once MB has fallen below MC it can never climb back above it.

Worked example · ice-cream at $10 a scoop

Total benefit of \(n\) scoops: 0, 30, 54, 72, 84, 90, 92 dollars for \(n = 0,\dots,6\). Each scoop costs $10, so \(TC(n) = 10n\) and \(MC = 10\) for every scoop.

The 4th scoop adds $12 > $10, so take it. The 5th adds only $6 < $10, so stop. Optimal: 4 scoops, ES = 84 − 40 = $44. Brute force agrees: the ES column peaks at 44 when \(n = 4\).

Marginal benefit bars vs the price line

Green bars are the scoops worth buying (MB ≥ price). Economic surplus = sum of (MB − price) over the green bars: the part of each green bar above the dashed line. The table above updates with the price.

Going further · why the assumptions matter

If MB could rise again (say the 3rd scoop unlocked a "free topping" deal), saying "no" to one unit would not mean "no" to all later ones, and you would have to go back to brute force (compare ES for every \(n\)). Exam questions always satisfy the assumptions, but check: if MB ever rises, stop and use brute force.

5The continuous case: MB = MC

Some goods are perfectly divisible (also called continuous): you can have any amount, not just whole units. Ice-cream measured in grams, hours of study, litres of water. For example, \(Q\) = grams of ice-cream.

Now the "one more unit" is a tiny amount \(d\) (say 0.001 g). Have a little more if

\[ TB(Q+d) - TB(Q) \gt TC(Q+d) - TC(Q). \]

To be accurate we want \(d\) to approach 0. But then both sides approach 0 and the comparison says nothing.

Solution: divide both sides by \(d\) first, then let \(d\) approach 0.

Marginal benefit and cost (divisible goods) \[ \begin{aligned} MB(Q) &= \lim_{d \to 0} \frac{TB(Q+d) - TB(Q)}{d} \\ MC(Q) &= \lim_{d \to 0} \frac{TC(Q+d) - TC(Q)}{d} \end{aligned} \]

In words: marginal benefit = extra benefit per extra unit, for a very small extra amount. It is the rate of change of total benefit — the slope of the TB graph at \(Q\).

"\(\lim_{d\to 0}\)" (read "the limit as \(d\) goes to 0") means: the value the fraction settles down to as \(d\) gets smaller and smaller.

This is consistent with the whole-unit case: with \(d = 1\) unit,

\[ \begin{aligned} MB(n) &= TB(n) - TB(n-1) \\ &= \frac{TB(n) - TB(n-1)}{1}. \end{aligned} \]

The rule becomes:

  • Have more than \(Q\) if \(MB(Q) \gt MC(Q)\).
  • Have less than \(Q\) if \(MB(Q) \lt MC(Q)\).
Optimal continuous quantity \[ MB(Q^*) = MC(Q^*) \]

In words: keep going until the last tiny bit adds exactly as much benefit as it costs. Any less, and one more bit is worth it; any more, and the last bit was not.

Going further · MB of a quadratic without calculus

Exam questions give total benefit as \(TB(n) = an - bn^2\) (for example \(60n - n^2\)). Its MB, by the recipe above and plain algebra:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{TB(Q+d) - TB(Q)}{d} \\ &= \frac{a(Q+d) - b(Q+d)^2 - aQ + bQ^2}{d} \\ &= \frac{ad - 2bQd - bd^2}{d} \\ &= a - 2bQ - bd. \end{aligned} \]

As \(d \to 0\) the last term vanishes:

\[ MB(Q) = a - 2bQ. \]

In words: for \(TB = an - bn^2\), MB starts at \(a\) and falls by \(2b\) per unit — twice as fast as the average benefit.

The average benefit is \(AB(Q) = TB(Q)/Q = a - bQ\) (benefit per unit, averaged over all units). Many questions print AB next to MB as a distractor.

Whole-number version (for questions that say "\(n\) can only be integers"):

\[ \begin{aligned} MB(n) &= TB(n) - TB(n-1) \\ &= a - b(2n-1). \end{aligned} \]

In words: the discrete MB of the \(n\)-th unit equals the formula MB evaluated half a unit earlier, at \(n - \tfrac12\).

TB = 60Q − Q²: choosing Q where MB meets the price
MB = 60 − 2QAB = 60 − Qprice (= MC)economic surplus

With price $20: MB = 60 − 2Q = 20 gives Q* = 20 and ES = 60·20 − 20² − 20·20 = $400 — the green triangle, ½ × 20 × (60 − 20) = 400.

Going further · surplus is an area

Each tiny unit adds MB − price to your surplus. Adding these up from 0 to \(Q^*\) gives the area between the MB line and the price line: a triangle when MB is a straight line. So \(ES = \tfrac12 \times Q^* \times (MB(0) - \text{price})\). Chapter 3 calls this area consumer surplus.

The AB trap

Setting AB = price instead (\(60 - Q = 20\)) gives \(Q = 40\) and \(ES = 60\cdot 40 - 40^2 - 20\cdot 40 = 0\). That is no accident: AB = price means total benefit = total cost, so the surplus is always exactly zero. Tick "show the AB trap" to see it for every price.

6Incentives: prisoner ships and piece rates

Reducing the death rate of prisoner transport

In 1787 the British government hired sea captains to ship convicted felons to Australia. The British had no love for the convicts: typically more than one third of the men died on the voyage, and the rest arrived beaten, starved and sick.

Understand the captain's decision. Treat "keep one more convict alive" as a marginal decision:

  • Should the captain keep alive the 1st convict? The 2nd? … The \(n\)-th?
  • Yes, if the benefit to the captain of one more surviving convict is higher than the cost (more food, water, space, care).

Marginal benefit before the policy change: close to zero. The captain's payment did not depend on how many convicts arrived alive, so keeping one more alive brought him nothing while costing food and care. MB ≈ 0 < MC, so the captain saved on care.

(Historically, captains were paid per convict taken on board. The point does not depend on that detail: what matters is that survival earned the captain nothing.)

Policy

Pay the captains per living convict on arrival. Now the marginal benefit of keeping one more convict alive equals the payment per head, which is far above the marginal cost of feeding him. Captains keep many more alive.

Result: the death rate fell from over 33% to less than 1%.

The captain's margin, before and after

Illustration with invented numbers: 100 convicts; the cost of keeping one more alive rises as the captain aims for more survivors. The captain keeps convicts alive up to where his marginal benefit (the payment) meets the marginal cost.

Lesson: incentives matter

Incentives are rewards and penalties that motivate behaviour.

  • People respond to incentives in predictable ways.
  • Self-interest is an important incentive in economics.

In words: to change what people do, change their marginal benefits or marginal costs.

Incentives in the garment and textile industry

From the 1960s to the 1980s, Hong Kong's garment and textile industry employed huge numbers of workers. If you were the boss, how would you pay them: (A) by hours of work, or (B) by pieces of completed garment?

Think it through, then open

Apply the marginal rule from the worker's side:

  • Paid by the hour: an extra garment finished in the same hour earns the worker nothing extra. Marginal benefit of extra effort ≈ 0, so effort is low unless supervised.
  • Paid by the piece (a piece rate): each extra garment earns the piece rate. Marginal benefit of extra effort = piece rate, so workers work faster.

Piece rates were in fact widespread in Hong Kong's factories, because output per worker was easy to count. Going further The catch: piece rates reward quantity, so bosses must check quality; and they suit jobs where one worker's output can be counted separately.

7Allocating a fixed resource

Now the total is fixed (four workers, 60 minutes, $90) and the question is how to split it among uses. Still a marginal question: where should the next unit go?

Gatherers in a strategy game

Picture a real-time strategy (RTS) computer game, where a player sends worker units ("gatherers") to collect gold from mines. We have four gatherers and two gold mines, East and West. Here is the average output per gatherer (gold per minute) at each mine:

Step 2 — hand out gatherers one at a time, each to the mine where he adds more:

  1. 1st gatherer: East would add 10, West would add 13 → West.
  2. 2nd: East's 1st adds 10, West's 2nd adds 11 → West.
  3. 3rd: East's 1st adds 10, West's 3rd adds 9 → East.
  4. 4th: East's 2nd adds 10, West's 3rd adds 9 → East.

Answer: 2 East, 2 West, for a total of \(20 + 24 = 44\) gold per minute.

Why averages mislead

West's average (10–13) is never below East's (10), which tempts you to send all four West. That gives only 40 gold per minute. Averages describe all the gatherers already there; the decision is about the next one.

The general rule

Allocating a fixed amount of resources

Whole units: give each additional unit to the activity with the highest marginal benefit.

Perfectly divisible resource: split it so that

\[ MB(A_1) = MB(A_2) = \dots = MB(A_n). \]

In words: at the best split, the last bit of resource is equally valuable in every use. If one use were higher, moving a little resource into it would raise the total.

This assumes no activity is always better than another. If one activity's MB stays above the other's even when it gets everything, the answer is a corner: put everything into that activity.

Splitting T units between two activities
MB₁(a) = 100 − 4aMB₂(b) = 80 − 2b, with b = T − a

The horizontal axis is \(a\), the amount given to activity 1; the rest \(b = T - a\) goes to activity 2, so MB₂ is drawn rising from left to right. Interior answer where the lines cross: \(100 - 4a = 80 - 2(T - a)\), so \(a = (20 + 2T)/6\). For \(T \le 5\) the lines never cross inside the range: corner, everything to activity 1.

Ready answer · two linear MBs, fixed total T

1. Corner check first. If \(MB_1(T) \ge MB_2(0)\), everything goes to activity 1 (and vice versa).
2. Otherwise solve \(MB_1(a) = MB_2(T - a)\) for \(a\); then \(b = T - a\).

In words: first ask whether one use beats the other even at its worst; if not, equalise the marginal benefits. Strategy C1.5.

Hand out units one at a time from a table

Three sites; numbers are marginal outputs (extra output from the 1st, 2nd, … worker at that site).

Exam tables give totals; first turn each column into marginal outputs (differences between rows), then hand out. This is strategy C1.6.

8Opportunity cost and sunk cost

Trade-offs are everywhere

For every choice, something is gained and something is lost.

Opportunity cost (first version)

The opportunity cost of a choice is the value of the opportunities lost by making it. It includes

  • monetary and non-monetary costs (money, but also time, effort, enjoyment given up), and
  • explicit and implicit costs. Explicit = money actually paid out (a ticket price). Implicit = value given up without any payment (the wage you would have earned in those hours).

What to include? Three Disneyland cases

In all three you spend $100 a day on meals.

CaseChequeTicket situationCorrect B and CPick it up?
1$450must buy a $400 ticketB = 450, C = 400Yes (ES = 50)
2$300you already own a non-refundable ticketB = 300, C = 0Yes (ES = 300)
3$300you already own a fully refundable ticketB = 300, C = 400No (ES = −100)
  • Meals ($100) are never included: you eat whether or not you go, so the decision does not change them.
  • Non-refundable ticket: the $400 is gone whatever you do. Using the ticket costs you nothing extra, so C = 0.
  • Refundable ticket: using it means giving up the $400 refund. So using it costs $400, exactly as if you had to buy it.
What to include

Do not mechanically include any benefit or cost that will not be affected by your decision, at the time you make the decision.

Sunk cost

A sunk cost is a cost that is beyond recovery at the moment the decision is made. Sunk costs are ignored, because no choice you make now can change them.

In words: ask of every dollar, "do I get it back, or avoid paying it, if I choose differently?" If no, it is sunk: leave it out.

Only the next best alternative counts

You won three free tickets for the same time: a Jay Chou concert; Disneyland, where you can pick up a $400 cheque; Ocean Park, where you can pick up a $500 cheque. None can be resold (ID checked at the entrance). No other costs or benefits.

What is the opportunity cost of seeing Jay Chou? If you go to the concert you give up both trips, but you could only ever have done one of them. The best you give up is Ocean Park, worth $500. So the opportunity cost is $500, not $400 + $500 = $900.

Opportunity cost (precise version)

The opportunity cost of an activity is the value of the next best alternative that must be forgone in order to undertake the activity.

Net value of the alternative

You won a free, non-refundable Jay Chou ticket. At the same time you could go to Disneyland to pick up a $450 cheque, but the Disneyland ticket costs $400. Opportunity cost of seeing Jay Chou: $450, $400 or $50?

$50. The alternative is worth \(450 - 400 = 50\) to you, its net value. Giving it up loses $50, not $450.

Ready answer · the opportunity-cost ledger \[ \begin{aligned} \text{OC of } A &= \text{cash you spend on } A \\ &\quad + \text{best net value} \\ &\quad\;\; \text{among the alternatives} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the opportunity cost of doing A = what A costs you in cash + the net value of the single best thing you could have done instead. Net value of an alternative = its benefit (pay, enjoyment) − its own costs (fares, tickets). Leave out anything you pay either way.

Then do A if (benefit of A) ≥ (OC of A). Strategy C1.1.

In the two Jay Chou examples above the concert ticket is free, so only the forgone alternative appears. When the activity itself costs money (a $290 ticket), past papers add that price to the forgone alternative: for example a $290 concert ticket, forgoing tutoring worth $315, has opportunity cost \(290 + 315 = 605\) (2022 Fall Midterm Q5).

Example · the "willing to work for free" wording

Papers often say someone "enjoys the job just enough that they are willing to do it for free". That means the job's enjoyment and unpleasantness cancel out, so its net value is simply its pay. If instead "she would be unwilling to do the job for less than $17", the job's net value is pay − 17: the job is unpleasant enough to cost her $17 of her own.

9Uncle Paul's sneakers: two ways to count

After an exchange in England you are about to return to Hong Kong. Air Jordan I sneakers sell for HK$1,800 a pair in Hong Kong stores and about HK$500 in England. You planned to bring a pair back and sell it for about HK$1,300. Then Uncle Paul asks you to bring a pair for him and promises to pay you the cost (HK$500). You value the chance of helping Uncle Paul at \(\$x\). You can bring back only one pair. How does your decision depend on \(x\)?

Calculation 1 Imagine you have not yet bought the sneakers.
Benefit of helping: \(x + 500 - 500 = x\) (your valuation, plus his $500, minus the $500 you pay for the pair).
Cost of helping: you lose the chance to buy and resell yourself, worth \(1300 - 500 = 800\).
ES \(= x - 800\).
Calculation 2 Imagine you are already back with the sneakers (the $500 is now spent either way: sunk).
Benefit of helping: \(x + 500\) (valuation + reimbursement).
Cost of helping: you lose the resale, worth $1,300.
ES \(= x + 500 - 1300 = x - 800\).
Answer

Help Uncle Paul if \(x \gt 800\).

Same decision, different bookkeeping

You may sort costs and benefits slightly differently, but if every cost and benefit is included properly you will get the same economic surplus and reach the same decision.

In words: there is more than one correct ledger; there is only one correct ES.

10Additional problems, solved

Allocation of time · same as 2020 Spring Final Q65

David has 60 minutes to split between video games and newspapers. Marginal benefits: video games \(MB_G = 795 - 5G\); newspapers \(MB_N = 670 - 4N\), where \(G\) and \(N\) are minutes. Time is perfectly divisible. How many minutes on video games?

  1. Corner check. All 60 minutes on games: \(MB_G(60) = 795 - 300 = 495\), less than \(MB_N(0) = 670\). All on newspapers: \(MB_N(60) = 430\), less than \(MB_G(0) = 795\). Neither corner, so the answer is interior.
  2. Equalise. With \(N = 60 - G\): \(795 - 5G = 670 - 4(60 - G) = 430 + 4G\).
  3. So \(365 = 9G\), giving \(G = 365/9 = 40.5556\) minutes (and \(N = 19.4444\)).
  4. Check: both MBs equal \(592.22\).

40.5556 minutes on video games.

Visits to the Peak · same as 2021 Fall Midterm Q4

Karen's total benefit of \(n\) visits is \(TB(n) = 1222\sqrt{n}\) dollars (4 visits: \(1222\sqrt4 = 2444\)). Each trip costs $159. Visits are whole numbers. How many trips?

  1. Whole numbers → use the discrete MB: \(MB(n) = 1222\sqrt{n} - 1222\sqrt{n-1}\). Each trip's MC is 159.
  2. MB falls as \(n\) rises (the square-root curve flattens), so take trips while \(MB(n) \ge 159\).
  3. \(MB(15) = 1222(\sqrt{15} - \sqrt{14}) = 160.48 \ge 159\): take the 15th.
  4. \(MB(16) = 1222(4 - \sqrt{15}) = 155.21 \lt 159\): stop.

15 trips (economic surplus \(1222\sqrt{15} - 15 \times 159 \approx \$2347.79\)).

Quick way to find where to look: \(MB(n) \approx 1222/(2\sqrt{n})\), which equals 159 near \(n \approx 14.8\). Then check the two whole numbers either side exactly.

11Summary

  • Cost–benefit principle: do \(x\) if \(B(x) \gt C(x)\), i.e. if economic surplus \(ES = B - C \gt 0\).
  • Rationality: people have goals and pursue them as best they can. Models abstract; they are judged by fit and prediction, not by realism of assumptions.
  • Inference / prediction / policy: choices reveal valuations; known goals predict choices; changing benefits or costs changes choices.
  • Marginal = additional. Whole units: \(MB(n) = TB(n) - TB(n-1)\). Divisible: MB = rate of change of TB. Do one more while MB ≥ MC; divisible optimum at \(MB = MC\).
  • With MB non-increasing and MC non-decreasing, stop at the first "no".
  • Incentives matter: change marginal benefits (pay per living convict, piece rates) and behaviour changes predictably.
  • Fixed resource: give each next unit to the highest MB; divisible: equalise MBs, unless one use always wins (corner).
  • Opportunity cost = value of the next best alternative forgone (net of its own costs); include implicit costs; ignore sunk costs and anything paid either way.
  • Different bookkeeping, same ES, same decision.

12Common mistakes

✗Adding up all forgone alternatives. ✓ Only the single best one counts. ✗Using an alternative's gross value. ✓ Subtract its own costs (fares, tickets, unpleasantness) first. ✗Counting costs paid either way (daily meals, rent you pay regardless, a non-refundable ticket). ✓ Leave them out. ✗Treating a refundable ticket as free to use. ✓ Using it costs its refund value. ✗Setting AB = price, or allocating by highest average. ✓ Always marginal. ✗For whole-number questions, plugging into the formula MB and rounding down. ✓ Use \(TB(n) - TB(n-1)\). ✗Rounding a divisible answer to a whole number. ✓ Keep the decimals the question asks for. ✗Solving \(MB_1 = MB_2\) without the corner check. ✓ Compare each use's MB at "everything" with the other's MB at "nothing" first. ✗Allocating a total-output table by totals. ✓ Difference each column into marginal outputs first. ✗Letting a fixed fee change the marginal choice. ✓ A lump-sum cost only decides "any at all vs none".

13Find the mistake

Each "solution" below has one error. Find it before opening.

1

Mia can go to a 3-hour concert (ticket $150). Instead she could tutor for 3 hours at $60/hour or babysit for $170; she would do either job for free. "Opportunity cost = 150 + 180 + 170 = $500."

Mistake

She could only do one job. The best alternative is tutoring (3 × 60 = $180), so OC = 150 + 180 = $330.

2

Tom's hotel costs $400 a night, non-refundable, whether or not he takes a 2-day trip during which he stays with a friend. "The trip saves no hotel money, but the $800 of hotel nights counts as a cost of staying home."

Mistake

The $800 is paid in both cases (non-refundable): it is sunk and appears on neither side.

3 · same numbers as 2024 Summer Midterm Q2

\(TB(n) = 82n - n^2\), whole scoops, price $30.5. "MB = 82 − 2n = 30.5 gives n = 25.75; you can't buy part of a scoop, so 25."

Mistake

For whole units use the discrete MB: \(MB(n) = TB(n) - TB(n-1) = 83 - 2n\). \(MB(26) = 31 \ge 30.5\) but \(MB(27) = 29 \lt 30.5\), so the answer is 26. (Rounding 25.75 to the nearest whole number happens to work for quadratics; rounding down does not.)

4

\(TB(Q) = 40Q - Q^2\), divisible, price $10. "AB = 40 − Q = 10, so Q = 30."

Mistake

Use MB, not AB: \(40 - 2Q = 10\) gives Q = 15 with ES = $225. At Q = 30 the ES is exactly 0.

14Practice

Every answer was checked by computation.

P1 · opportunity-cost ledger

Ivy can see a 4-hour play (ticket $150) that she values at $400. Instead she could tutor for 4 hours at $60/hour or babysit for $180 in total; she likes both jobs just enough to do them for free. Should she see the play, and what is its economic surplus?

Hint

Best alternative only; add the ticket price.

Answer

Tutoring is worth 4 × 60 = $240 > $180, so OC = 150 + 240 = $390. ES = 400 − 390 = $10 > 0: see the play.

P2 · whole-number marginal rule

\(TB(n) = 50n - 2n^2\) for whole \(n\); each unit costs $21. How many units, and what is the economic surplus?

Hint

\(MB(n) = TB(n) - TB(n-1) = 52 - 4n\).

Answer

\(52 - 4n \ge 21\) gives \(n \le 7.75\): 7 units. \(ES = TB(7) - 7 \times 21 = 252 - 147 = \$105\).

P3 · splitting a fixed total

10 hours to split: \(MB_1(a) = 90 - 6a\), \(MB_2(b) = 70 - 4b\), divisible. Find \(a\) and \(b\).

Hint

Corner check: \(MB_1(10) = 30\) vs \(MB_2(0) = 70\); \(MB_2(10) = 30\) vs \(MB_1(0) = 90\).

Answer

No corner. \(90 - 6a = 70 - 4(10 - a)\) gives \(60 = 10a\): a = 6, b = 4 (both MBs = 54).

15Concept checks

1

A refundable $120 ticket sits in your drawer. You value the show at $100. Go?

Answer

No. Using the ticket costs the $120 refund; ES = 100 − 120 = −20. Return it.

2

Same, but the ticket is non-refundable and cannot be resold.

Answer

Go. The $120 is sunk; ES = 100 − 0 = 100.

3

Someone buys 3 units when \(MB(n) = 30 - 5n\) (whole units). What can you say about the price \(P\)?

Answer

The 3rd was worth it: \(P \le MB(3) = 15\). The 4th was not: \(P \gt MB(4) = 10\). So \(10 \lt P \le 15\).

4

Mine A adds 20 gold per worker at every level. Mine B's 1st, 2nd, 3rd workers add 25, 18, 12. Where do 3 workers go?

Answer

1st to B (25 > 20), 2nd to A (20 > 18), 3rd to A (20 > 18). 2 at A, 1 at B, total 65.

5

Why did paying captains per convict embarked fail to keep convicts alive?

Answer

Payment did not depend on survival, so the marginal benefit to the captain of keeping one more alive was about zero, below the marginal cost of food and care.

6

When the economy booms and wages are high, does the opportunity cost of attending college rise or fall?

Answer

Rises: the best alternative (a job) pays more. Expect enrolment to fall, not rise. (This is a recurring True/False item.)
