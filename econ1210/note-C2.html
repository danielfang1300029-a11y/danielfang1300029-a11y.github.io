Comparative advantage and the power of trade

Why don't we each grow our own food and build our own houses? Because everyone ends up with more when each person does the jobs they give up least to do, and then trades. This chapter makes that precise with three tools: opportunity cost per person, the production possibilities curve, and the consumption possibilities curve.

1Why exchange at all? Absolute advantage

Start with a question: why do people exchange goods and services at all? Why don't we all grow our own food, paint our own houses, write our own books?

The answer starts from opportunity cost: the value of the next best alternative you give up. What is your opportunity cost of growing your own food? All the hours you could have spent studying, working at a job you are good at, or resting. We can all have more of every good and service if we specialise (concentrate) in the activities at which we are relatively most efficient. And to allow for such specialisation, exchange is necessary: a farmer who only grows rice must trade rice for everything else.

Paul the painter and Ron the roofer

Paul is a house painter and Ron is a roofer. Each wants his own house painted and its roof replaced. Paul also knows how to install roofing, and Ron knows how to paint. Should Paul roof his own house? Should Ron paint his own house? The time each needs for each job:

Painting (one house)Roofing (one house)
Paul300 hours400 hours
Ron200 hours100 hours
Absolute advantage

A person has an absolute advantage at a task if they can do it using fewer resources (here: fewer hours) than another person. Equivalently: in the same time they produce more of it.

In words: absolute advantage = raw productivity. Who is simply faster at this job?

Ron has an absolute advantage at both painting (200 < 300 hours) and roofing (100 < 400 hours). So should Ron do the roofing and painting for both houses? No. Even though Ron is faster at everything, Paul has a comparative advantage at painting: he is relatively more efficient at painting than Ron is. The next section makes "relatively" precise.

Analogy

A senior lawyer may type faster than her secretary. She still should not do her own typing: every hour typing is an hour not spent on legal work worth far more. Being better at something (absolute advantage) is not a reason to do it yourself. What matters is what you give up.

2Comparative advantage = lower opportunity cost

Definition

To have a comparative advantage at a task = to have a lower opportunity cost of performing it.

In words: you have the comparative advantage in X if, to make one more X, you give up less of the other good than anyone else would.

Computing each person's opportunity cost

For Paul, the opportunity cost of painting one house = the number of roofing jobs he could do in the same time. Paul takes 300 hours to paint and 400 hours to roof, so in the time it takes him to paint a house he could complete \(300/400 = 0.75\) roofing jobs.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{Paul's OC of painting} \\ &= \frac{300 \text{ h}}{400 \text{ h}} = 0.75 \text{ roofing jobs} \end{aligned} \]

Ron takes 200 hours to paint and 100 hours to roof, so in the time it takes him to paint a house he could complete 2 roofing jobs.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{Ron's OC of painting} \\ &= \frac{200 \text{ h}}{100 \text{ h}} = 2 \text{ roofing jobs} \end{aligned} \]

In words: when the data are time per job, opportunity cost of X = (time for one X) ÷ (time for one Y), measured in Y.

Turn each one upside down for the other good: Paul's OC of roofing = \(400/300 = 1.33\) painting jobs; Ron's = \(100/200 = 0.5\) painting jobs.

Conclusion: Paul has the comparative advantage at painting, because his opportunity cost of painting is lower than Ron's. So it makes sense for Paul to do both painting jobs and leave both roofing jobs to Ron.

Ready answer · two people, two goods

If A's OC of X is lower than B's, then automatically B's OC of Y is lower than A's (the OCs of Y are just the reciprocals). So one person cannot have the comparative advantage in both goods, but one person can have the absolute advantage in both (Ron does).

Logic fact the papers love: if A has the absolute advantage in good 1 but B has the comparative advantage in good 1, then A must also have the absolute advantage in good 2. (A is faster at good 1, yet gives up more of good 2 per unit of good 1, so A must be even faster at good 2.) Strategy C2.1.

How many hours does specialising save?

If each man does both of his own jobs: Paul spends \(300 + 400 = 700\) hours, Ron \(200 + 100 = 300\) hours.

If each specialises in his comparative advantage (Paul paints both houses, Ron roofs both): Paul spends \(2 \times 300 = 600\) hours, Ron \(2 \times 100 = 200\) hours. Each saves 100 hours.

Notice what would happen the other way round. If Ron did both paintings (400 h) and Paul both roofs (800 h), they would waste time compared with doing everything themselves. Specialising according to comparative advantage is what creates the gain.

3Terms of trade

Terms of trade (ToT)

The terms of trade is the rate at which one good is exchanged for the other: how many units of one good are given for one unit of the other.

Paul and Ron each have one painting job and one roofing job to do. Specialisation with trade saves them 100 hours each. In effect they swap one painting job for one roofing job: Paul paints Ron's house in exchange for Ron roofing Paul's house. So the terms of trade are 1 painting job per roofing job.

Which terms will both accept?

Will they agree to exchange if the ToT is 2.0 painting jobs per roofing job? And what is the range of ToT on which agreement is possible?

Think about what each man could do without trading:

  • Paul wants roofing. Roofing his own house would cost him 400 hours, the same time as \(400/300 = 1.33\) painting jobs. So he will pay at most 1.33 painting jobs for one roofing job.
  • Ron supplies roofing. One roof costs him 100 hours, the time for \(100/200 = 0.5\) painting jobs. So he needs at least 0.5 painting jobs for each roofing job.
The terms-of-trade window \[ \begin{aligned} &0.5 \lt \text{ToT} \lt 1.33 \\ &\text{(painting jobs per roofing job)} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the price of a roofing job must lie between the two people's opportunity costs of roofing. Below the lower one the seller loses; above the higher one the buyer loses.

At ToT = 2.0, Paul would paint two houses (600 hours) to get one roof he could do himself in 400 hours. Paul refuses.

How many hours does each save at ToT = 1.1?

One clean way to count, valuing every job in the hours it would take you:

  • Paul no longer roofs (saves 400 h) but paints 1.1 jobs for Ron (\(1.1 \times 300 = 330\) h). Saves \(400 - 330 = 70\) hours.
  • Ron no longer paints (saves 200 h), roofs Paul's house (100 h), and receives 0.1 of a painting job extra, worth \(0.1 \times 200 = 20\) h of his time. Saves \(200 - 100 + 20 = 120\) hours.

A higher price for roofing shifts the gain from Paul (the buyer) to Ron (the seller). At ToT = 1 the gain is split 100/100.

Hours saved by each man as the terms of trade change
Paul saves 400 − 300·ToTRon saves 200·ToT − 100

(hours valued at each man's own speed). Both lines are above zero only between 0.5 and 1.33: that is the window. At ToT = 2 Paul's line is at −200.

Ready answer · window and bargaining

Window: the ToT (units of Y per unit of X) must lie strictly between the two people's OCs of X. Always state it in the units the question asks for; if it asks for "X per unit of Y", use the reciprocals.

Bargaining for a country that exports X (it has the comparative advantage in X):

  • Worst ToT it can accept = its own OC of X (it gains nothing).
  • Best ToT it can reasonably ask for = the partner's OC of X (the partner gains nothing).

In words: an exporter needs to receive at least what the good cost it to make, and cannot get more than it would cost the buyer to make it themselves. Whether "best" is the bigger or smaller number depends on which good is counted per unit, so reason from "what do I export?" Strategy C2.2.

Example · reading the window backwards (2023 Spring Midterm Q7–Q8)

Cindy has the comparative advantage in tea, Veronica in cakes. Fully specialised they make 36 kg tea and 18 kg cakes per week. The window is 0.6 to 4.8 kg tea per kg cakes. Who has which OC?

  1. The window's ends are the two OCs of a cake (in tea). Veronica has the comparative advantage in cakes, so she has the lower one: 0.6. Cindy: 4.8.
  2. Specialised, Cindy makes all the tea (36 kg). Her OC of a cake is 4.8 tea, so her maximum cakes = \(36 / 4.8 = 7.5\) kg.
  3. Veronica makes all the cakes (18 kg); her maximum tea = \(18 \times 0.6 = 10.8\) kg.

Cindy: 7.5 kg cakes; Veronica: 10.8 kg tea.

4The principle of comparative advantage

Principle of comparative advantage

Everyone does best when each person (or country) concentrates on the activities in which he or she is relatively most efficient.

In words: do what you give up least to do, and trade for the rest.

  • Concentrating on what you are relatively most efficient at is called specialisation.
  • Specialisation provides a rationale for market exchange. It explains why nobody spends 10% of their time producing cars, 5% growing food, 25% building housing and 0.0001% on brain surgery.
  • By doing only the tasks at which we are relatively most efficient, we can produce vastly more than if each of us tried to be self-sufficient (making everything we consume ourselves).

Inferred from data

Yang Qi is a game designer (lead of Black Myth: Wukong) and J.K. Rowling is a writer. We do not know their opportunity costs, but their choices reveal them (Chapter 1's "inference"): it is very likely that Yang Qi has the comparative advantage in designing games and Rowling in writing. Specialisation is what made Black Myth: Wukong and Harry Potter possible.

Sources of comparative advantage

Individual levelInborn talent · education · training · experience
National levelNatural resources · culture · institutions (laws, legal system, financial markets)

So what can we do to change our comparative advantage? Of the sources listed, talent is fixed, but education, training and experience can be changed: you can build a comparative advantage by learning a skill and practising it. Countries change theirs by investing in education and building institutions (Hong Kong's shift from manufacturing to finance and trading services is an example).

5The production possibilities curve for one person

Production possibilities curve (PPC)

A graph that describes the maximum amount of one good that can be produced for every possible level of production of the other good.

Chris alone

Chris can produce 6 square yards per week (sq yd/wk) of shelter or 12 pounds per week (lb/wk) of food, or any mix in between by splitting his week. If he works \(s\) of the week on shelter and \(1 - s\) on food, he makes \(6s\) shelter and \(12(1-s)\) food. All such points lie on the straight line from (0 food, 6 shelter) to (12 food, 0 shelter):

\[ S = 6 - \tfrac12 F \]

In words: Chris's shelter = 6 minus half of his food. Each extra pound of food costs him half a square yard of shelter.

Slope = opportunity cost

The absolute value of the slope of the PPC is \(6/12 = 1/2\). For Chris this means that the opportunity cost of an additional pound of food each week is \(1/2\) sq yd/wk of shelter.

In words: steepness of the PPC = how much of the vertical-axis good you lose per extra unit of the horizontal-axis good.

Chris's PPC: attainable, efficient, unattainable
A (0, 6), B (12, 0), C (4, 4), D (8, 2): attainable and efficient (on the curve) · E (4, 2): attainable but inefficient (inside) · F (8, 4): unattainable (outside). Points are (food, shelter).
  • Attainable point: one Chris can produce (on or inside the PPC).
  • Efficient point: on the PPC, so more of one good is only possible by having less of the other. At E Chris could have more of both (for example C or D): some of his time is wasted.
  • Unattainable point: outside the PPC; impossible with his time and skill. F would need 4 + 8 × ½ = 8 sq yd of shelter-equivalent, but he has only 6.

Dana alone

Dana can produce 4 sq yd/wk of shelter or 4 lb/wk of food. Her PPC is the line from (0, 4) to (4, 0), \(S = 4 - F\). For Dana the opportunity cost of an additional pound of food is 1 sq yd/wk of shelter.

6Two, three and many people: the low-hanging-fruit principle

Who has the comparative advantage?

Chris's OC of a pound of food is \(\tfrac12\) sq yd of shelter; Dana's is 1. So Chris has the comparative advantage in food: his OC is only half of Dana's. By the same token, Dana has the comparative advantage in shelter (her OC of shelter is 1 lb of food; Chris's is 2).

The joint PPC is not a straight line

If Chris and Dana are the only two people in the economy, what can they produce together? A tempting wrong answer is a single straight line joining the totals (10 shelter, 16 food). That line ignores who makes what.

The right way: start with everyone on shelter (10 sq yd, 0 food). To get the first pounds of food, move the person with the lower OC of food, Chris. Each pound costs only \(\tfrac12\) sq yd, so the curve starts with a gentle slope of \(-\tfrac12\). When Chris is fully on food (12 lb, leaving Dana's 4 sq yd), more food can only come from Dana, at 1 sq yd per pound: the slope steepens to \(-1\) until (16, 0).

Joint PPC of Chris and Dana

Kinked line (0, 10) → (12, 4) → (16, 0), in (food, shelter). It is bowed outwards: gentle first, steep later.

In words: the economy's PPC is made of the individual PPCs placed end to end, cheapest producer first.

Dividing a target between them

Dana and Chris have decided to consume, jointly, 6 sq yd/wk of shelter and 8 lb/wk of food. How should they divide the work?

  1. Dana has the comparative advantage in shelter, but even full time she makes only 4 sq yd/wk.
  2. So Chris must produce the other 2 sq yd. He can make 6 in a week, so 2 sq yd takes him \(2/6 = 1/3\) of a week.
  3. That leaves \(2/3\) of a week for food: \(\tfrac23 \times 12 = 8\) lb, exactly what they want.

Dana works full time on shelter; Chris works 1/3 week on shelter and 2/3 week on food. The point (8, 6) is on the gentle segment of the joint PPC.

The low-hanging-fruit principle

In expanding the production of any good, employ the resources with the lower opportunity cost first.

In words: pick the easiest fruit first. To get more food, start with everyone on shelter and switch Chris first (food costs him ½); use Dana only after Chris's time is used up. To get more shelter, start with everyone on food and switch Dana first (shelter costs her 1 lb of food, Chris 2); use Chris only after Dana's time is used up.

Three people: George joins

George can produce 2 sq yd/wk of shelter or 1 lb/wk of food. Opportunity costs of one more unit of shelter (in food): Chris 2, Dana 1, George \(\tfrac12\). To expand shelter starting from all-food, use George first, then Dana, then Chris.

Read from the other end, the opportunity cost of one pound of food (in shelter): Chris \(\tfrac12\), Dana 1, George 2. Starting from all shelter (6 + 4 + 2 = 12 sq yd), food comes from Chris first (to 12 lb), then Dana (to 16 lb), then George (to 17 lb):

Joint PPC of Chris, Dana and George

(0, 12) → (12, 6) → (16, 2) → (17, 0), in (food, shelter). Slopes \(-\tfrac12\), \(-1\), \(-2\).

Whatever order the OCs are listed in, the curve always uses them cheapest-first.

Target: 14 lb food and 4 sq yd shelter. Food comes cheapest from Chris, so he makes 12 lb food and no shelter. The remaining 2 lb come from Dana, the next cheapest: half her week, leaving her 2 sq yd of shelter. George makes shelter only: 2 sq yd.

The point (14, 4) lies on the middle segment of the joint PPC (Dana is the one splitting her time).

Build the joint PPC, cheapest producer first

Defaults reproduce the Chris–Dana target (8 lb food → 6 sq yd shelter). Tick George and set F = 14 for the three-person target. Each segment is one person; its slope is that person's OC of food.

An economy with many workers

With thousands of workers, each with a slightly different opportunity cost, the kinks become so many and so small that the PPC looks like a smooth curve bowed outwards (for example, food against clothing). The same rule builds it: produce the first units of clothing using the resources that are relatively most efficient at clothing, and only then turn to those that are relatively less efficient. That is why each extra garment costs more food than the last: the PPC gets steeper.

Ready answer · closed economy with a target

"If they consume \(x\) of X, what is the most Y?"

  1. Find each person's OC of X. Rank from lowest.
  2. Start with everyone on Y. Move people to X in that order until \(x\) is reached; the last person moved splits their time.
  3. Y = everyone else's full Y + the splitter's leftover Y. For the splitter, leftover Y = max Y − OC × (their X).

With different working hours, first convert to output per day (minutes worked ÷ minutes per unit). For "let the k people with the lowest OC of X specialise in X", rank, assign, then add. Strategy C2.3.

7Economic growth: the PPC shifts outwards

Picture an original bowed PPC for coffee (vertical) and nuts (horizontal), both in thousands of lb/day, and a new PPC lying further out. Economic growth means the economy can produce more of everything: points that were unattainable become attainable.

Factors that shift the PPC outwards
  1. Increases in productive resources (labour or capital): investment in new factories and equipment; population growth.
  2. Improvements in knowledge and technology: increasing education; learning by doing (getting better at a task by doing it repeatedly).

In words: more inputs or better know-how → the whole curve moves out.

Specialisation feeds growth: someone who does one task all day learns by doing and gets faster, which pushes the PPC further out.

8Gains from specialisation

Case I: Tom and George, mechanics

Tom can replace 15 clutches per day or 10 sets of brakes, so his opportunity cost of a set of brakes is \(15/10 = 1.5\) clutches. George can replace 10 clutches or 15 sets of brakes: his OC of brakes is \(10/15 = 2/3\) clutch. Their garage sells a full service = 1 clutch replacement + 1 brake replacement. How many more full services can they do if they specialise?

No specialisation. George's PPC: \(C = 10 - \tfrac23 B\). For full services he needs equal numbers, \(C = B\):

\[ \begin{aligned} C &= 10 - \tfrac23 C \\ \tfrac53 C &= 10 \;\Rightarrow\; C = 6 \end{aligned} \]

Tom's PPC: \(C = 15 - \tfrac32 B\); with \(C = B\): \(\tfrac52 C = 15\), so \(C = 6\). Each does 6 full services: 12 in total.

Specialisation. Tom (comparative advantage in clutches) replaces 15 clutches; George replaces 15 sets of brakes. That makes 15 full services: a 25% increase (\(15/12 = 1.25\)). Their joint PPC runs (0, 25) → (15, 15) → (25, 0) in (brakes, clutches), and the specialised point (15, 15) is its kink.

Case II: Eric and Gary

Eric can replace 30 clutches or 6 sets of brakes per day (OC of brakes = 5 clutches). Gary can replace 6 clutches or 30 sets of brakes (OC = 0.2 clutch). The OCs differ far more than in Case I (5 vs 0.2, against 1.5 vs 2/3).

  • No specialisation: Eric: \(C = 30 - 5C\) gives \(C = 5\); Gary likewise 5. Total 10 full services.
  • Specialisation: Eric 30 clutches, Gary 30 sets of brakes: 30 full services. The joint PPC is (0, 36) → (30, 30) → (36, 0).
  • Gain: \(30/10 = 3\), i.e. 200% more.
Lesson

The gains from specialisation are larger when differences in opportunity cost are larger.

In words: two people who are good at different things gain a lot from teaming up; two people with the same strengths gain nothing.

Mirror-image mechanics: how the gain grows with the OC gap
joint PPCfull services: C = B

George is Tom's mirror image (his clutches = Tom's brakes and vice versa), as in both cases above. 15/10 gives Case I (25%); 30/6 gives Case II (200%); equal numbers give 0%.

Choosing a team member

When we form a team, it is better to choose a member who has different strengths from ours, so that their presence covers our weaknesses. In essence, we choose team members who let us gain the most from specialisation, and the gains are larger when the differences in opportunity cost are larger.

International trade

The same logic that leads individuals to specialise and exchange also leads nations to specialise and trade. As with individuals, each trading partner can benefit from exchange, even if one is more productive than the other in absolute terms (like Ron, who was faster at everything).

Ready answer · fixed-ratio bundles

A bundle uses \(m\) units of X and \(n\) units of Y (a full service: \(m = n = 1\); "1 clutch + 2 brakes": \(m = 1, n = 2\)).

Alone (straight PPC with maxima \(\bar X, \bar Y\)): the number of bundles \(k\) solves

\[ \frac{k m}{\bar X} + \frac{k n}{\bar Y} = 1 \]

In words: the share of the day spent on X plus the share spent on Y adds to one whole day.

Together: each specialises in their comparative-advantage good; whoever's good is short then switches part of their time (low-hanging fruit) until the bundle ratio is met.

"Percent more" \(= (\text{together} \div \text{separate} - 1) \times 100\). Strategy C2.4.

9Consumption possibilities and world prices

Closed economy: consume what you produce

Elizabeth and Dennis are the only two workers in Islandia, a small island nation. Their PPC (bananas vertical, tea horizontal, both lb/day) runs (0, 120) → E (100, 100) → (120, 0). In a closed economy (no trade with the outside world) consumption possibilities = production possibilities: they can only eat what they grow.

Reading the slopes: the first segment gives up only 20 lb of bananas for 100 lb of tea (0.2 banana per tea); the second gives up 100 bananas for 20 tea (5 bananas per tea).

Opening to world trade

On the world market, tea can be bought or sold at $2 per lb and bananas at $1 per lb. Islandia is so small that its trading does not change these prices. How does this change what Islandia can consume?

  1. Produce at E and sell everything: income \(= 2 \times 100 + 1 \times 100 = \$300\) per day.
  2. Spend the $300: at most 300 lb of bananas (point F) or at most 150 lb of tea (point G), or any combination on the line FG, such as 150 bananas + 75 tea or 100 + 100.

In an open economy (one that trades), consumption possibilities exceed production possibilities: the line FG lies outside the PPC everywhere except at E.

Consumption possibilities curve (CPC)

A graph that describes the maximum amount of one good that can be consumed for every possible level of consumption of the other good.

How to maximise consumption possibilities:

  1. Choose the production mix that generates the largest income.
  2. Spend all the income you earned.
\[ P_{T} \cdot T + P_{B} \cdot B = \text{income} \]

In words: the CPC is a budget line: spending on tea + spending on bananas = what you earned at the best production point.

Can we do better by producing differently?

The slope of the CPC is set by the price ratio (\(P_T / P_B = 2\) bananas per lb of tea). Among all lines with that slope, the CPC is the highest one that still touches the PPC. At $2/$1 that is the line through E: producing at (0, 120) earns only $120, at (120, 0) only $240, at E $300.

A different price: tea at $6

Now tea sells at $6 per lb and bananas at $1. Where is the CPC? Compare incomes at the three corners: (0, 120) earns $120; E earns \(600 + 100 = \$700\); (120, 0) earns \(6 \times 120 = \$720\). Best: produce only tea, 120 lb, income $720. The CPC runs from 720 lb of bananas (all income on bananas) to 120 lb of tea: a steep line through (120, 0).

Islandia's PPC and CPC at world prices
PPCCPC (budget line)

Defaults: tea at $2 (75 tea + 150 bananas). Set tea to $6 for the second price. Between $0.20 and $5 per lb the best production point is the kink E.

Ready answer · what to produce at world prices

World price ratio \(P_X / P_Y\) = how many Y one unit of X sells for. For each person:

  • own OC of X below \(P_X/P_Y\) → make X only;
  • own OC of X above \(P_X/P_Y\) → make Y only;
  • equal → indifferent.

In words: make X if selling it buys more Y than you could have made yourself. Equivalently, make whichever good earns more money per day.

If both OCs lie on the same side of the price ratio, both make the same good. The person with the comparative advantage does not automatically make "their" good. On a kinked PPC, production sits at the kink when the price ratio lies between the two slopes. Strategy C2.5.

Ready answer · consumption at world prices
  1. Produce to maximise income (rule above). Never produce the bundle you want to eat.
  2. Income = Σ quantity × world price.
  3. Consume \(x\) of X: \(Y = (\text{income} - P_X x) / P_Y\). With a ratio \(X = rY\): \(Y = \text{income} / (r P_X + P_Y)\).

World ToT "\(k\) units of Y per unit of X" means \(P_X/P_Y = k\): set \(P_Y = 1\), \(P_X = k\). Strategy C2.6.

Going further · two T/F favourites

"A country gains from trade only if it imports more than it exports": false. The gain comes from producing what you are relatively good at and swapping at better rates than your own OC. "In an open economy the PPC always equals the CPC": false (it does in a closed economy).

10What limits or encourages specialisation

Factors that may limit specialisation
Low population density: too few customers nearby to support a specialist (a village cannot support a full-time violin repairer).
Geographic isolation: distance and transport costs make exchange expensive.
Laws: trade barriers, licensing rules or bans that restrict exchange.
Customs: traditions about who may do which job.
Factors that may enhance specialisation
Urbanisation: many people close together, so even very narrow specialists find enough customers.
Differences in preferences: people who enjoy different tasks gain more from dividing the work.
Learning by doing: the more you do a task, the better you get, which widens the gap in opportunity costs.

11Summary

  • Opportunity cost drives exchange: we specialise in what we are relatively best at and trade for the rest.
  • Absolute advantage = fewer resources per unit (raw productivity). Comparative advantage = lower opportunity cost. One person can have the absolute advantage in both goods, but never the comparative advantage in both.
  • OC from time per unit: \(t_X / t_Y\). From output per period: \(\bar Y / \bar X\). From a PPC: the slope's size.
  • Terms of trade must lie between the two OCs; an exporter's worst acceptable ToT is its own OC, its best is the partner's OC.
  • PPC: maximum of one good for each amount of the other. On it = efficient; inside = inefficient; outside = unattainable. Slope = OC.
  • Low-hanging fruit: expand a good using the lowest-OC resource first. Joint PPCs are kinked and bowed outwards; with many workers, smooth and bowed.
  • The PPC shifts outwards with more resources and better knowledge or technology (economic growth).
  • Gains from specialisation are larger when OCs differ more (25% for Tom/George, 200% for Eric/Gary). Nations gain from trade for the same reasons.
  • CPC: produce where income at world prices is largest, then spend it all. Closed economy: CPC = PPC. Open economy: CPC lies outside the PPC.
  • Specialisation is limited by low density, isolation, laws and customs; encouraged by urbanisation, different preferences and learning by doing.

12Common mistakes

✗Calling the faster person the one with the comparative advantage. ✓ Comparative advantage is about opportunity cost, not speed. ✗Flipping the ratio with time-per-unit data (\(t_Y / t_X\)). ✓ Time or cost data: OC of X \(= t_X / t_Y\) directly. Output data: OC of X \(= \bar Y / \bar X\). ✗Comparing one person's OC of X with the other's OC of Y. ✓ Compare both people's OC of the same good. ✗Giving the ToT window in the wrong units. ✓ Check which good is "per unit"; take reciprocals if needed. ✗Joining the totals with one straight line for a joint PPC. ✓ Put individual PPCs end to end, lowest OC first: the joint PPC is kinked. ✗Forgetting different working hours ("Frank works 3 hours, Annie 4"). ✓ Scale each person's maxima first. ✗Treating "1 clutch + 2 brakes" as 1:1. ✓ Put the bundle's quantities into the time-share equation. ✗At world prices, producing the bundle you want to consume. ✓ Produce for maximum income, then buy what you want. ✗Comparing OC of X with \(P_Y/P_X\). ✓ OC of X (in Y) is compared with \(P_X/P_Y\) (Y per X). ✗Assuming each person makes "their" comparative-advantage good at world prices. ✓ If both OCs are above (or below) the price ratio, both make the same good.

13Find the mistake

Each "solution" below has one error. Find it before opening.

1 · same numbers as 2021 Spring Midterm Q9

Carla needs 925 hours per gallon of gin and 37 hours per gallon of juice. "Her OC of a gallon of gin is 37/925 = 0.04 gallons of juice."

Mistake

With time-per-unit data, do not flip: OC of gin \(= 925/37 = 25\) gallons of juice. (One gallon of gin takes as long as 25 gallons of juice.)

2 · same numbers as 2024 Spring Midterm Q9

Steven catches 315 lobsters or 70 crabs a day; Cynthia 120 lobsters or 60 crabs. "The ToT must lie between 0.22 and 0.5 lobsters per crab."

Mistake

0.22 and 0.5 are OCs of a lobster in crabs. Per crab: Steven's OC = 315/70 = 4.5 lobsters, Cynthia's = 120/60 = 2. Window: 2 to 4.5 lobsters per crab.

3 · same numbers as 2020 Fall Final Q10

Per hour: Eric 2 bells or 3.8 whistles; Flora 16 or 46.4; Glen 12 or 27.6; Howard 20 or 30. Two people must make bells only. "Howard and Flora: they make the most bells."

Mistake

That ranks by absolute productivity. OC of a bell (whistles): Eric 1.9, Flora 2.9, Glen 2.3, Howard 1.5. Lowest two: Howard and Eric (option C).

4

Islandia (PPC (0, 120) → (100, 100) → (120, 0)), tea $6 and bananas $1. The islanders want to eat mostly bananas. "So they should produce at (0, 120), bananas only."

Mistake

Production should maximise income: 120 tea earns $720, against $120 for all bananas. Produce tea only, then buy up to 720 lb of bananas.

14Practice

Every answer was checked by computation.

P1 · who has which advantage, and the window

Ann needs 10 minutes per hat and 5 minutes per tie. Ben needs 12 minutes per hat and 4 minutes per tie. Who has the absolute and the comparative advantage in each good? Within what range must the price of a hat (in ties) lie for both to gain?

Hint

Time data: OC of a hat = (minutes per hat) ÷ (minutes per tie).

Answer

Absolute: Ann in hats (10 < 12), Ben in ties (4 < 5). OC of a hat: Ann \(10/5 = 2\) ties, Ben \(12/4 = 3\) ties, so Ann has the comparative advantage in hats and Ben in ties. Window: between 2 and 3 ties per hat.

P2 · closed economy with a target

Kim can make 8 fish or 24 coconuts a day; Lee 6 fish or 12 coconuts. They want 10 fish a day in total. What is the most coconuts they can have?

Hint

OC of a fish: Kim 3 coconuts, Lee 2. Lee makes fish first.

Answer

Lee makes 6 fish (all day). Kim makes the other 4 fish, giving up \(4 \times 3 = 12\) coconuts: \(24 - 12 = 12\) coconuts. Maximum 12 coconuts.

P3 · open economy

Same Kim and Lee. The world price of fish is $5 and of coconuts $2. What does each produce? If they consume 10 fish, how many coconuts can they have?

Hint

Price ratio = 5/2 = 2.5 coconuts per fish. Compare with each OC.

Answer

Kim's OC 3 > 2.5 → coconuts only (24). Lee's OC 2 < 2.5 → fish only (6). Income \(= 24 \times 2 + 6 \times 5 = \$78\). Buying 10 fish costs $50, leaving $28 for 14 coconuts: 2 more than in the closed economy.

15Concept checks

1

Can one person have the comparative advantage in both goods? The absolute advantage in both?

Answer

Comparative: no. The OCs of the second good are the reciprocals, so a lower OC in one good means a higher OC in the other. Absolute: yes (Ron in §1).

2

A straight PPC runs from 8 shelter (vertical axis) to 4 food (horizontal axis). What is the OC of one unit of food?

Answer

2 units of shelter (the slope's size, 8/4).

3

Why is a two-person PPC bowed outwards rather than straight?

Answer

Low-hanging fruit: the first units of a good come from the person with the lower OC (gentle slope); later units must come from the higher-OC person (steeper slope).

4

Jo's OC of a crab is 3.5 lobsters and Dan's is 1.5. Will both gain if crabs trade at 3 lobsters each?

Answer

Yes. Dan sells crabs and gets 3 lobsters each, above his OC of 1.5. Jo buys a crab for 3 lobsters, below the 3.5 it would cost her to catch one. 3 lies inside the window (1.5, 3.5).

5

True or false: in expanding the production of any good, we should first use the resources with the highest opportunity cost.

Answer

False: lowest opportunity cost first (the low-hanging-fruit principle).

6

Two mechanics have identical skills. How much do they gain from specialising?

Answer

Nothing: their opportunity costs are equal, so there is no comparative advantage to exploit. Gains grow with the difference in OCs.

7

Can an economy consume a point outside its PPC?

Answer

Not if it is closed (CPC = PPC). If it trades at world prices, yes: the CPC lies outside the PPC except where they touch.

In a day, Ken can pick 112 apples, or 160 oranges. In a day, Harris can pick 80 apples, or 56 oranges.

Q12. If one fruit basket includes 1 apple(s) and 1 orange(s), if Ken and Harris work separately, they can prepare in total 12 fruit baskets in a day (can be non-integer).

Q13. Continue with the previous question. If Ken and Harris work together, they can prepare in total 13 PERCENT MORE fruit baskets than working separately in a day.

Hint

Alone: \(k/\bar A + k/\bar O = 1\). Together: Harris has the lower OC of apples, so he picks apples; Ken picks oranges and switches part of his day to apples until the counts match.
