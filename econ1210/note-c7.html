Price ceilings and floors

What happens when the government makes it illegal to charge more than a set price (a ceiling) or less than a set price (a floor)? This chapter follows the market step by step: who still trades, who is shut out, how buyers compete when price can't do the job, and how much economic surplus is lost along the way.

0Where this sits

This chapter has three parts:

  1. Price ceilings: when they bite, and their five effects (shortages, misallocation, welfare loss, bribery and queues, lower quality) — sections 1–7 below.
  2. Discussion: why do price controls ever get passed? — section 8.
  3. Price floors: their four effects, and the minimum wage compared with a wage subsidy — sections 9–12.

Section 13 adds the exam-only variations (government buying up a surplus, buybacks, a ceiling on an input). They go beyond the core material but appear on finals.

You need Chapter 3 (demand, supply, equilibrium, consumer and producer surplus as triangle areas) and, for section 12–13, the idea of a per-unit subsidy from Chapter 6. Maths: HKDSE Core only — straight lines, simultaneous equations, areas of triangles and trapezoids.

Quick reminders from Chapter 3

Demand curve: for each quantity, the most the next buyer is willing to pay (their willingness to pay, WTP). Supply curve: for each quantity, the lowest price the next seller would accept — their marginal cost. Equilibrium \((Q_e, P_e)\): where they cross.

Consumer surplus (CS) = total of (WTP − price paid) over buyers who buy. Producer surplus (PS) = total of (price received − cost) over units sold. Total economic surplus (also "gains from trade" or "social welfare") = CS + PS (+ anything else that is a transfer, not a loss).

One market for the whole note

Unless stated otherwise, the figures and worked examples use one simple market:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Demand: } P &= 100 - Q \\ \text{Supply: } P &= 20 + Q \end{aligned} \]

Setting \(100 - Q = 20 + Q\) gives \(Q_e = 40\), \(P_e = 60\). At equilibrium \(CS = \tfrac12 \times 40 \times (100-60) = 800\), \(PS = \tfrac12 \times 40 \times (60-20) = 800\), total surplus 1600.

1Price ceilings: when do they bite?

Price ceiling

A price ceiling is a maximum price allowed by law. Sellers may not charge more than the ceiling; prices cannot legally go higher.

Ceilings are often meant to protect buyers. Some examples:

  • Rent control — a legal maximum rent (New York, San Francisco, and many European cities have versions of it; Hong Kong had rent control on older flats until the 1990s).
  • Gasoline (petrol) price caps during energy crises.
  • Essential drugs such as insulin.
Effective vs ineffective

A price ceiling is effective (also called binding) only if it is set below the equilibrium price \(P_e\).

A ceiling at or above \(P_e\) is ineffective (non-binding): the market price \(P_e\) is already legal, so nothing changes.

In words: a "no higher than" rule only matters if the market wanted to go higher.

Try it: move the ceiling
Demand P = 100 − QSupply P = 20 + Qceiling

Drag the ceiling above $60: nothing happens. Below $60 a shortage opens between the quantity sellers offer and the quantity buyers want.

Always check first

Many exam questions set a control that does not bind, to catch students who calculate a shortage anyway (for example a ceiling of $85 when \(P_e = 60\), or a floor of $99 when \(P_e = 100\)). Step one is always: find \(P_e\), then compare.

2Five effects, starting with shortages

A ceiling below the market price creates five important effects:

  1. Shortages (this section)
  2. Misallocation of resources (§3)
  3. Welfare loss (§4)
  4. Bribery, wasteful lines and other costs of search (§5–6)
  5. Reduction in product quality (§7)

Shortages

When the price is held below the market price, buyers want more than sellers offer. Picture children scrambling for Easter eggs: too many hands, too few eggs.

Shortage (excess demand) \[ \text{Shortage} = Q_d(P_c) - Q_s(P_c) \]

In words: shortage = quantity buyers want at the controlled price − quantity sellers offer at the controlled price.

The lower the controlled price relative to the equilibrium price, the larger the shortage.

Quantity actually traded \[ Q_{\text{transacted}} = \min\{Q_s,\ Q_d\} \]

In words: a trade needs both a willing buyer and a willing seller, so the quantity traded is whichever side is smaller — the short side of the market. Under a binding ceiling that is the sellers: \(Q = Q_s(P_c)\).

Worked example · our market, ceiling $40
  1. Binding? \(40 \lt P_e = 60\): yes.
  2. Sellers: \(40 = 20 + Q\) gives \(Q_s = 20\).
  3. Buyers: \(40 = 100 - Q\) gives \(Q_d = 60\).
  4. Shortage \(= 60 - 20 = 40\). Quantity traded \(= \min\{20, 60\} = 20\), down from 40.

Long-run shortage

Draw two supply curves through the same equilibrium: a steep short-run supply and a flatter long-run supply. In the short run, landlords and producers cannot change much (the flats already exist), so quantity supplied falls only a little. Given time, they leave the market (convert flats to offices, stop building, sell to owner-occupiers), so quantity supplied falls further. The shortage therefore grows over time.

Tick "short-run supply" in the figure above: with supply \(P = 60 + 3(Q - 40)\) the shortage at $40 is \(60 - 33.33 = 26.67\); with the flatter long-run curve it is 40.

Ready answer · elasticity and shortage size

The more elastic (flatter) demand and supply are, the more \(Q_d\) rises and \(Q_s\) falls when the price is pushed down, so the bigger the shortage. A recurring True/False item: "rent control below the equilibrium rent gives a bigger excess demand when both demand and supply are more elastic" — True. Strategy C7.1.

3Misallocation of resources

In a free market the price does two jobs: it tells buyers how scarce the good is, and it rewards sellers for getting the good to whoever values it most. A ceiling switches both off:

  • Consumers who value the good the most are prevented from signalling their preference by offering sellers a higher price — offering more is illegal.
  • Producers have no incentive to supply the good to those who value it the most — every buyer pays the same ceiling price, so the seller gains nothing by choosing a high-value buyer.
  • Price controls distort signals and eliminate incentives, leading to a misallocation of resources across markets.
Misallocation

Units end up with people who value them less than others who go without. Example: under rent control, a retired couple keeps a large rent-controlled flat they barely need, while a family that would pay much more cannot find one.

Think of the demand curve in two parts. Near the top are the highest-valued uses: buyers whose WTP is above the price at \(Q_s\). Further down, between \(Q_s\) and \(Q_d\), are lower-valued uses, and beyond \(Q_d\) the least-valued uses (who don't want it even at the ceiling). With \(Q_d\) people wanting only \(Q_s\) units, nothing guarantees the units go to the top of the demand curve; some can go to the lower-valued uses, while higher-valued buyers miss out. Section 4 measures the cost.

4Welfare loss and deadweight loss

Why a ceiling reduces welfare
  • A ceiling below the market price makes the transacted quantity lower than the unregulated \(Q_e\).
  • At this lower \(Q\), the marginal willingness to pay is larger than the marginal cost: some buyer values one more unit above what it costs some seller to make it.
  • Society would gain by expanding quantity, but the ceiling stops these gains from trade from happening.

Before the ceiling, welfare = CS + PS: the triangle between demand and the market price (CS) plus the triangle between the market price and supply (PS).

Case 1: units go to the highest-value buyers

This is a big if: assume, optimistically, that the \(Q_s\) units somehow reach the buyers with the highest WTP. Then:

  • Producer surplus shrinks to the small triangle between the ceiling and supply, up to \(Q_s\).
  • Consumer surplus changes to the area between demand and the ceiling, up to \(Q_s\): a trapezoid (a triangle on top of a rectangle). Buyers lose the units beyond \(Q_s\) but gain the rectangle — they now pay less than before for the units they still get. CS may rise or fall overall.
  • The triangle between demand and supply from \(Q_s\) to \(Q_e\) is lost to society: nobody gets it.
Deadweight loss (DWL)

The deadweight loss is the economic surplus that disappears because trades worth making do not happen. Under a ceiling with ideal allocation:

\[ \begin{aligned} DWL &= \tfrac12 \times (Q_e - Q_s) \\ &\quad \times \big[P_d(Q_s) - P_c\big] \end{aligned} \]

In words: DWL = ½ × (units no longer traded) × (gap between the demand curve and the supply curve at the reduced quantity). Here \(P_d(Q_s)\) means the height of the demand curve at \(Q_s\), and the supply curve's height at \(Q_s\) is exactly the ceiling \(P_c\).

Worked example · our market, ceiling $40, ideal allocation
  1. \(Q_s = 20\); demand height there \(P_d(20) = 100 - 20 = 80\).
  2. \(PS = \tfrac12 \times 20 \times (40 - 20) = 200\) (was 800).
  3. \(CS = \) trapezoid from 0 to 20 between demand and $40: \(\tfrac12 \times 20 \times (100 - 80) + 20 \times (80 - 40) = 200 + 800 = 1000\) (was 800 — buyers as a group gain here).
  4. \(DWL = \tfrac12 \times (40 - 20) \times (80 - 40) = 400\).
  5. Check: \(1000 + 200 + 400 = 1600\), the original total.

Case 2: units go to the lowest-value buyers

Now the worst case: of the \(Q_d\) people who want the good at the ceiling, the \(Q_s\) units go to those with the lowest WTP (just above the ceiling) — the part of the demand curve from \(Q_d - Q_s\) to \(Q_d\).

  • PS is the same small triangle as before (sellers still sell \(Q_s\) at \(P_c\)).
  • Consumer surplus shrinks to a small triangle down near the ceiling.
  • The loss to society is now much bigger: the DWL triangle plus the whole value that high-WTP buyers would have got.
Worked example · our market, ceiling $40, worst allocation

60 buyers want the good; the 20 units go to those at positions 40–60 on the demand curve, with WTP from $60 down to $40. \(CS = \tfrac12 \times 20 \times (60 - 40) = 200\). Total surplus \(= 200 + 200 = 400\), so the loss is \(1600 - 400 = 1200\) instead of 400.

Ceiling: who gains, who loses, what is lost
CSPSlost to society

Using D: P = 100 − Q, S: P = 20 + Q. With the lowest-value allocation the CS triangle slides down the demand curve to the buyers just above the ceiling.

Exam convention

Exam questions say "assume all consumption is allocated to the consumers with the highest valuation" or ask for the "maximum possible consumer surplus" or the "minimum possible deadweight loss". All three mean Case 1. Use \(Q_s\), never \(Q_d\), for the quantity. Strategy C7.2.

Ready answer · areas under a binding ceiling \(P_c\) (ideal allocation)

Let \(a\) = demand's price intercept, \(e\) = supply's price intercept, \(Q_s\) = supply at \(P_c\), \(b = P_d(Q_s)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} PS &= \tfrac12 Q_s (P_c - e) \\ CS_{\max} &= \tfrac12 Q_s (a - b) + Q_s (b - P_c) \\ DWL &= \tfrac12 (Q_e - Q_s)(b - P_c) \end{aligned} \]

In words: PS is a triangle under the ceiling; maximum CS is a triangle on top of a rectangle; DWL is the triangle between the two curves from the new quantity to the old.

Going further · curves given as Q = …

Papers often write demand as \(Q = 200 - 1.8P\). The price intercept \(a\) is where \(Q = 0\): \(P = 200/1.8 = 111.11\). And \(b = P_d(Q_s)\) solves \(Q_s = 200 - 1.8b\). Turn every curve into "P = …" form first if you find areas easier that way.

5Allocation by bribery

With a shortage but a price that may not rise, buyers must compete in other ways: bribery, waiting in line, knowing the right people, and so on. This chapter studies the first two.

The most a buyer will pay to get the good

Some buyers are willing to pay just for the right to buy. The highest such payment a buyer would make is

\[ \text{max bribe} = WTP - P_c \]

In words: the most you'd pay for the right to buy = what the good is worth to you − the legal price you still have to pay.

Example

John is willing to pay $10. The regulated price is $3. The sales counter only sells to people holding a coupon. John would pay up to \(10 - 3 = \$7\) for a coupon. If he pays $7 to someone (a clerk, a middleman) to secure the good, that is bribery.

  • The total price of the good = legal price + bribe.
  • The bribes usually go to some individuals (clerks, officials, middlemen), not to the companies selling the goods.

Equilibrium bribe

At the ceiling of $3, only two units are supplied. Four buyers: John $10, Mary $8, Paul $6, Jessica $4. All can afford the good. Their maximum bribes are $7, $5, $3, $1. Suppose everyone can see the going bribe (the coupon price).

BribeWho pays it (max bribe ≥ bribe)WantedSuppliedResult
$1John, Mary, Paul, Jessica42shortage
$2John, Mary, Paul32still shortage
$4John, Mary22zero excess demand

Any bribe above $3 (Paul's maximum) and at most $5 (Mary's maximum) clears the market; take $4. The two units go to John and Mary — the two highest valuers.

Try it: raise the going bribe

Bars are each buyer's maximum bribe (WTP − $3). Buyers whose bar reaches the dashed bribe line still want the good.

Ready answer · discrete buyers, \(n\) units

List each buyer's WTP − \(P_c\), largest first. With \(n\) units, the equilibrium bribe must be strictly above the \((n+1)\)-th value (so that buyer drops out) and at most the \(n\)-th value. Exam blanks ask for the \((n+1)\)-th value: "the bribe has to be strictly higher than ___".

In words: the bribe has to price out everybody except the top \(n\) buyers. Strategy C7.3.

A competitive market for coupons

With a smooth demand curve, imagine a competitive market where the right to buy (a coupon) is traded. There are \(Q_s\) coupons (one per unit supplied). The demand for coupons is the part of the demand curve above the ceiling: a buyer with WTP \(v\) will pay up to \(v - P_c\) for a coupon.

Equilibrium bribe (continuous) \[ \text{bribe} = P_d(Q_s) - P_c \]

In words: go to the quantity sellers supply, read the height of the demand curve there, and subtract the ceiling. That height \(P_d(Q_s)\) is the full price buyers pay (legal price + bribe) — the price at which exactly \(Q_s\) units are wanted.

Total value of bribery = bribe × \(Q_s\): the rectangle between the ceiling and \(P_d(Q_s)\), from 0 to \(Q_s\).

The total price of the good is the legal price plus the bribe, and buyers with the highest willingness to pay get the good — the same buyers who would have got it in an unregulated market (the top \(Q_s\) of them).

Worked example · our market, ceiling $40
  1. \(Q_s = 20\); \(P_d(20) = 80\). Bribe \(= 80 - 40 = \$40\) per unit.
  2. Total bribes \(= 40 \times 20 = 800\) (paid to clerks/middlemen).
  3. Buyers' CS net of bribes \(= \tfrac12 \times 20 \times (100 - 80) = 200\): the triangle above the full price $80.
  4. PS \(= 200\) (unchanged: sellers still get only $40).

6Allocation by waiting in line

Buyers can also compete through how long they are willing to queue.

Example

John is willing to pay $10; the regulated price is $3. The most he would give up in waiting, in money terms, is \(10 - 3 = \$7\). If his wage is $1 per hour (an hour in line costs him $1 of earnings), he will wait up to 7 hours.

\[ \text{max hours} = \frac{WTP - P_c}{\text{wage per hour}} \]

In words: hours someone will queue = what the good is worth to them above the legal price ÷ what an hour of their time is worth.

Same four buyers, everyone's wage $1/hour, 2 units: John would wait 7 hours, Mary 5, Paul 3, Jessica 1. A 1-hour line: all four wait (shortage). A 2-hour line: three wait (still shortage). A 4-hour line: only John and Mary — zero excess demand.

Equilibrium wait

If all buyers have the same cost of time, the line grows until its money cost equals \(P_d(Q_s) - P_c\) — exactly the equilibrium bribe. The total price is the legal price plus the time cost, and again the highest-WTP buyers get the good.

Total value of time spent in line = (money cost of the wait) × \(Q_s\): the same rectangle as the total bribe.

Waiting versus bribery: the welfare difference

Both mechanisms produce a total price above the controlled price. But they are different:

  • Bribes are a transfer from buyers to whoever receives them. Nothing is destroyed: the receivers are people too, so society loses only the DWL triangle.
  • Time spent waiting is simply lost. Nobody receives it. Society loses the DWL triangle plus the whole rectangle of wasted time. Paying in time is much more wasteful.
Bribery vs waiting in line
CS (net)PSbribes (transfer)lost

Switching the mechanism does not move any boundary; it only decides whether the rectangle is a transfer (bribes) or a loss (waiting).

Ready answer · bribery vs waiting (same time cost for all) \[ \begin{aligned} b &= P_d(Q_s) - P_c \\ CS_{\text{net}} &= \tfrac12 Q_s \big(a - P_d(Q_s)\big) \\ \text{waiting's extra loss} &= b \times Q_s \end{aligned} \]

In words: net consumer surplus is the same under both; producer surplus is the same; so CS + PS is the same. Waiting is worse for society by exactly the total bribe. Strategy C7.3.

Trap: "sum of CS and PS"

Under bribery, the bribe receivers' gain is not in CS or PS. So "CS + PS in the market" is the same number under bribery and under waiting, even though total social welfare differs.

What if buyers have different time costs?

Now a buyer with a low wage (a student, a retiree) can out-queue a buyer with a high WTP but a high wage (a busy surgeon). Some buyers high on the demand curve, who would have been served in an unregulated market, give up, while a buyer below the equilibrium, who would get nothing in an unregulated market, gets one by waiting. So the good no longer goes to the highest-WTP buyers. The total value of wasted time can no longer be read off as one simple rectangle, because each person's hour is worth a different amount. Low-time-cost buyers win; allocation is worse than under bribery.

Going further · a Hong Kong picture

Think of the overnight queues for limited-edition sneakers, or for new public-housing flats when application windows open. Whenever a price is held below what buyers would pay, look for the queue, the ticket scalper (a bribe market) or the "knowing someone" — the shortage is being rationed somehow.

7Reduction in product quality

At the controlled price, sellers have more customers than goods.

  • In a free market this would be an opportunity to profit by raising prices.
  • But when prices are controlled, sellers cannot raise prices.
  • In the long run sellers respond in two ways: reduce quality and reduce service (landlords skip repairs; petrol stations stop washing windscreens).
Quality adjusts to eliminate shortages

Making a lower-quality good is cheaper, so supply increases (shifts right/down: sellers will offer more at each price). Buyers value the lower-quality good less, so demand decreases (shifts left/down). Both shifts shrink the gap at the ceiling. Quality keeps falling until the shortage is gone: the market clears at the ceiling price with a worse good.

Exam questions make this precise (strategy C7.5): diluting vodka by \(x\) cuts each seller's cost by \(x\) dollars and each buyer's WTP by \(2x\) dollars.

Ready answer · dilution that clears the shortage

New supply: every height falls by \(x\). New demand: every height falls by \(2x\). The price stays at \(P_c\). Choose \(x\) so the shortage is zero:

\[ Q_d(P_c + 2x) = Q_s(P_c + x) \]

In words: a buyer who now pays \(P_c\) for the weaker good is as well off as paying \(P_c + 2x\) for the original; a seller who gets \(P_c\) with costs cut by \(x\) is as happy as getting \(P_c + x\) for the original. Find \(x\), then \(Q\). Total surplus = area between the new demand and new supply from 0 to \(Q\).

Dilution: watch the shortage close

Our market with a $40 ceiling; dilution x lowers cost by x and WTP by 2x. At x = 40/3 ≈ 13.33 the shortage is zero.

Worked example · our market, ceiling $40
  1. New demand \(P = 100 - 2x - Q\): at $40, \(Q_d = 60 - 2x\).
  2. New supply \(P = 20 - x + Q\): at $40, \(Q_s = 20 + x\).
  3. \(60 - 2x = 20 + x\) gives \(x = 40/3 \approx 13.33\), \(Q = 33.33\).
  4. Surplus between new curves: gap at \(Q = 0\) is \((100 - 2x) - (20 - x) = 80 - x = 66.67\), shrinking to 0 at \(Q = 66.67\). Area from 0 to 33.33 \(= 33.33 \times 66.67 - 33.33^2 = 1111.11\).
  5. Compare: without dilution (ideal allocation) total surplus was \(1000 + 200 = 1200\). Dilution trades more units, but each is worth less; here society ends up worse off.

8Why do price controls ever get passed?

If ceilings cause shortages, misallocation, deadweight loss, queues and worse quality, why are they so common? This is a discussion question. Recall the 1979 US energy crisis: a price ceiling on gasoline was imposed, and long lines of cars formed at petrol stations (June 15, 1979) — the queues of §6 in real life.

Some answers to discuss
  • Buyers can gain. In §4's example, buyers who get the good gain the rectangle (they pay $20 less per unit). If they are well organised and numerous voters, a ceiling can be popular even though total surplus falls.
  • The gains are visible; the costs are hidden. Lower rents are easy to see. Flats that were never built, or repairs never made, are not.
  • Fairness. Many people feel that price rises in a crisis are unfair ("price gouging"), and prefer queues to high prices even at a cost.
  • Short-run vs long-run. The short-run shortage is small (§2); the large long-run costs arrive after the next election.

9Price floors and surpluses

Price floor

A price floor is a minimum price allowed by law. Floors are not as common as ceilings, but still important (minimum wages, farm price supports, minimum airfares in the past).

A floor is effective (binding) only if it is set above the equilibrium price. A floor at or below \(P_e\) changes nothing.

A binding floor has four common effects:

  1. Surpluses
  2. Welfare loss (§10)
  3. Wasteful increases in quality (§11)
  4. A misallocation of resources (§11)

Surplus

When the price is held above the market price, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded.

Surplus (excess supply) \[ \text{Surplus} = Q_s(P_f) - Q_d(P_f) \]

In words: surplus = what sellers want to sell at the floor − what buyers want to buy at the floor. (Careful: this "surplus" means unsold goods, not economic surplus.)

Quantity traded \(= \min\{Q_s, Q_d\} = Q_d(P_f)\): now the buyers are the short side.

Worked example · our market, floor $80

Binding (\(80 \gt 60\)). \(Q_d\): \(80 = 100 - Q\), so 20. \(Q_s\): \(80 = 20 + Q\), so 60. Surplus = 40 units unsold; quantity traded 20.

Try it: move the floor
CSPS (lowest-cost sellers)DWL

Below $60 the floor does nothing. Above $60 an unsold surplus appears and quantity traded falls to Qd.

Statements examiners love
  • "A floor raises quantity supplied" — true for \(Q_s\) (a movement along supply), but the quantity actually traded falls.
  • "A floor shifts demand left" — false. A price control never shifts a curve; it moves us along curves. "Demand decreases" (shift) ≠ "quantity demanded decreases" (movement).
  • "A floor always raises sellers' revenue" — false. Revenue \(= P_f \times Q_d(P_f)\); here \(80 \times 20 = 1600\) is less than \(60 \times 40 = 2400\). It depends on the elasticity of demand (Chapter 5).
  • "Lowering a binding floor slightly raises quantity traded" — true: \(Q_d\) rises as the floor falls.

10Welfare loss under a floor

Price controls reduce the gains from trade. Under a binding floor only \(Q_d(P_f)\) units are traded, fewer than \(Q_e\). Between \(Q_d\) and \(Q_e\), buyers' WTP is above sellers' cost, but those trades don't happen: the grey deadweight loss triangle. Its height is the gap between the floor (the demand curve's height at \(Q_d\)) and the supply curve's height at \(Q_d\) — the lowest price at which that seller is willing to sell.

Ready answer · areas under a binding floor \(P_f\) (lowest-cost sellers)

\(Q_d\) = demand at \(P_f\); \(s\) = supply height at \(Q_d\); \(a, e\) = intercepts.

\[ \begin{aligned} CS &= \tfrac12 Q_d (a - P_f) \\ PS_{\max} &= Q_d (P_f - s) + \tfrac12 Q_d (s - e) \\ DWL &= \tfrac12 (Q_e - Q_d)(P_f - s) \end{aligned} \]

In words: mirror image of the ceiling. CS is a triangle above the floor; the most sellers can get is a rectangle plus a triangle (if the cheapest sellers are the ones who sell); DWL is the triangle between the curves from the new quantity to the old.

Worked example · our market, floor $80

\(Q_d = 20\), \(s = 20 + 20 = 40\). \(CS = \tfrac12 \times 20 \times 20 = 200\). \(PS_{\max} = 20 \times 40 + \tfrac12 \times 20 \times 20 = 1000\). \(DWL = \tfrac12 \times 20 \times 40 = 400\). Check: \(200 + 1000 + 400 = 1600\).

Going further · "maximum possible" PS

The formula assumes the units are made by the lowest-cost sellers. If high-cost sellers produce instead (§11), PS is smaller and the loss is bigger, exactly like the lowest-value buyers case in §4. Exam questions say "assume production is automatically allocated to suppliers with the lowest cost". Strategy C7.2.

11Wasteful quality and misallocation under floors

Quality adjusts to eliminate surpluses

Sellers can't cut the price to win customers, so they compete by offering higher quality. Higher quality costs more, so supply decreases (shifts up/left). Buyers value the better good more, so demand increases (shifts up/right). Quality rises until the surplus disappears at the floor price.

Wasteful increases in quality

  • Higher quality raises costs and reduces seller profit.
  • Buyers get higher quality, but would prefer a lower price.
  • Price floors encourage sellers to waste resources: higher quality than buyers are willing to pay for.
Example

Think of a lavish airline meal: most flyers would rather have a lower price. When US airfares had legal minimums (until 1978), airlines competed with meals, legroom and service instead of fares — quality travellers valued at less than it cost.

Misallocation of resources

Price controls misallocate resources by:

  • Allowing high-cost firms to operate: at the high floor price, even inefficient sellers cover their costs and can get some of the limited sales.
  • Preventing low-cost firms from entry: a new efficient firm can't win customers by charging less, because charging less is illegal.

12Minimum wage vs wage subsidy

The minimum wage is the clearest example of a price floor in the United States and Hong Kong. In the labour market, workers supply labour and firms demand it; the price is the wage.

  • A minimum wage above the market wage can hurt low-skilled workers by reducing employment: firms hire fewer hours.
  • Some economists believe a better approach is to subsidise employers (the demanders of labour). This gives a higher wage and a higher level of employment.
The numbers

Market wage $10.50 at point a (quantity \(Q_m\)). Minimum wage $12: firms hire only \(Q_d\), workers offer \(Q_s\); employment falls from \(Q_m\) to \(Q_d\), and \(Q_s - Q_d\) is unemployment (a surplus of labour).

Wage subsidy of $4 per hour paid to firms: workers receive $12 (point b on supply), firms pay only $8 (point d on demand), and employment rises to \(Q_s\), above \(Q_m\). The $4 is the subsidy "wedge" between the two curves.

Here is one pair of straight lines that passes through all these points:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Demand: } Q &= 163 - 6w \\ \text{Supply: } Q &= 10w - 5 \end{aligned} \]

Check: at \(w = 10.5\), both give 100 hours, so \(Q_m = 100\). At $12: \(Q_d = 91\), \(Q_s = 115\), unemployment 24. With the subsidy, firms hiring 115 hours pay \(w = (163 - 115)/6 = 8\) and workers get 12: the wedge is $4, as above.

Demand for labourSupply of labourDWL

Hours in the horizontal axis, wage vertical. At $12: minimum wage gives employment 91 and DWL 10.8; the subsidy gives employment 115, costs taxpayers $460 and has DWL 30.

Going further · the subsidy is not free

The subsidy gives workers more (worker surplus 661.25 vs at most 632.45 under the minimum wage in this example) and firms more, but taxpayers pay \(4 \times 115 = 460\). Hours between \(Q_m = 100\) and 115 cost workers more than they are worth to firms, so the subsidy has its own deadweight loss, \(\tfrac12 \times 15 \times 4 = 30\) — here larger than the minimum wage's 10.8. Which is "better" depends on whether you care most about employment, workers' pay, or total surplus. Exam questions on this comparison use Chapter 6's subsidy wedge: strategy C7.7.

Ready answer · subsidy to hit a target price

Target the sellers' price \(P_s\) (workers' wage, farmers' price): \(Q = Q_s(P_s)\); buyers' price \(P_b\) from demand at that \(Q\); subsidy \(= P_s - P_b\).
Target the buyers' price \(P_b\): \(Q = Q_d(P_b)\); \(P_s\) from supply at that \(Q\); subsidy \(= P_s - P_b\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Spending} &= s \times Q \\ DWL &= \tfrac12 \times s \times (Q - Q_e) \end{aligned} \]

In words: fix the quantity from the side whose price is targeted, read the other side's price off its own curve, and the gap is the subsidy. Government pays the gap on every unit, including the new ones.

13Going furthers: price support, buybacks, input ceilings

These set-ups go beyond the core material but appear on finals. Each uses the tools above. All numbers below use our market (D: \(P = 100 - Q\), S: \(P = 20 + Q\)).

A · Price support with government purchase

The government promises sellers it will buy any unsold output at a support price \(P_g\) above \(P_e\). Sellers then sell \(Q_s(P_g)\) in total: buyers take \(Q_d(P_g)\) and the government buys the rest.

Worked example · support at $80
  1. \(Q_s = 60\), \(Q_d = 20\): government buys \(60 - 20 = 40\) units.
  2. Government cost \(= 80 \times 40 = 3200\) (plus storage if stated: storage \(c\) per unit adds \(40c\)).
  3. \(PS = \tfrac12 \times 60 \times (80 - 20) = 1800\) — all 60 units sell at $80.
  4. \(CS = \tfrac12 \times 20 \times (100 - 80) = 200\).

B · Which policy do sellers prefer? Lobbying

Suppose the government will raise sellers' price to $80 in one of three ways. Sellers' surplus under each:

PolicySellers sellPS
Price floor $80 (lowest-cost sellers)\(Q_d = 20\)1000
Price support at $80\(Q_s = 60\)1800
Subsidy so sellers get $80\(Q_s = 60\) (buyers pay $40, subsidy $40, cost 2400)1800
Lobbying rule

The most a group will spend lobbying for its favourite policy = its surplus under that policy − its surplus under the next-best policy it would otherwise get. Here sellers would spend up to \(1800 - 1000 = 800\) to avoid the floor.

In words: you'd pay up to the difference it makes to you.

C · Driving quantity to zero: buyback vs floor vs tax

For a harmful good (guns, drugs), the government may want community consumption to be zero.

  • Buyback: the government buys \(G\) units at whatever price, regardless of price. Market demand becomes private demand plus \(G\), added horizontally: \(Q = (100 - P) + G\). Private quantity is zero once the price reaches the choke price, the demand intercept, $100. So the government must buy everything supplied at $100: \(Q_s(100) = 80\) units, spending \(100 \times 80 = 8000\).
  • Price floor: at least $100 ("at least", because any floor above the choke price also works). Nobody buys; nothing is sold.
  • Per-unit tax (Chapter 6): at least \(100 - 20 = 80\) per unit, the gap between the demand intercept and the supply intercept.
Worked example · sellers' lobbying

Buyback: sellers sell 80 units at $100, \(PS = \tfrac12 \times 80 \times (100 - 20) = 3200\). Floor or tax: nothing is sold, \(PS = 0\). Sellers would spend up to 3200 lobbying for the buyback. Strategy C7.6.

2021 Fall Final Q34 (key 50625): the flag in the question bank wondered whether the key wrongly lets suppliers sell at the floor. Checked: \(\tfrac12 \times 225 \times (780 - 330) = 50625\) is exactly the suppliers' PS under the buyback (they sell \(Q_s(780) = 225\) million to the government), while floor and tax both give PS = 0. So the key follows the rule above and is correct.

D · A ceiling on an input

Instead of capping the price of the final good (gasoline, bubble tea), the government caps the price of an input (crude oil, boba), hoping to lower costs without a shortage of the final good. But an effective ceiling on the input means less input is supplied, so less output can be made.

Worked example · input ceiling

Each unit of output needs one unit of input. Input supply: \(Q_{in} = 2P_{in}\). An effective ceiling of $10 on the input means only 20 units of input are supplied.

  1. Output is capped at \(Q = 20\) (if a share, e.g. 10%, of the input goes to this good, multiply first).
  2. The output price is whatever clears the market for those 20 units: read it off demand, \(P = 100 - 20 = 80\). The price rises from $60.
  3. \(CS = \tfrac12 \times 20 \times (100 - 80) = 200\).
Ready answer · which policy maximises consumer surplus?
Policy (our market)CS
No policy800
Ceiling $40, ideal allocation1000
Ceiling $40, waiting in line200
Input ceiling (20 units)200

In words: compute CS for every option and pick the largest. The answer depends on the numbers: a mild ceiling with ideal allocation can beat no policy, but a deep ceiling usually cannot. Strategy C7.4.

E · Ceiling vs subsidy when demand grows

To get buyers' price to $40, a subsidy needs \(Q = Q_d(40) = 60\), sellers' price \(20 + 60 = 80\), subsidy $40, DWL \(= \tfrac12 \times 40 \times 20 = 400\) — the same DWL as the $40 ceiling here. But if demand later shifts right in parallel and the subsidy rate is kept, the subsidy's DWL stays the same (same wedge, same over-production gap), whereas a fixed ceiling becomes more binding and its DWL grows. Every subsidy has a positive DWL, so it is strictly worse for total surplus than no intervention. Strategy C7.7.

14Summary

  • Ceiling = legal maximum price; binds only below \(P_e\). Floor = legal minimum; binds only above \(P_e\). Non-binding controls change nothing.
  • Binding ceiling: shortage \(= Q_d - Q_s\); quantity traded \(= Q_s\). Binding floor: surplus \(= Q_s - Q_d\); quantity traded \(= Q_d\). In general \(Q = \min\{Q_s, Q_d\}\).
  • Shortages grow in the long run and with more elastic curves.
  • Ceilings stop price from signalling who values the good most: units can go to lower-value buyers (misallocation).
  • Welfare: even with ideal allocation there is a DWL triangle between the curves from the new \(Q\) to \(Q_e\); with bad allocation the loss is larger.
  • Buyers compete in other ways. Equilibrium bribe or money cost of waiting \(= P_d(Q_s) - P_c\). Bribes are transfers; waiting time is pure waste, so waiting loses an extra bribe × \(Q_s\). With different time costs, low-time-cost buyers win.
  • Ceilings → lower quality and service; floors → wasteful higher quality, high-cost firms survive, low-cost firms are kept out.
  • Minimum wage: higher wage, less employment, unemployment \(= Q_s - Q_d\). A wage subsidy can give the same wage with more employment, at a cost to taxpayers.
  • Going furthers: price support (government buys the surplus), buybacks to drive quantity to zero (shift demand right by \(G\); choke price), input ceilings (output price rises), lobbying = difference in surplus.

15Common mistakes

✗Computing a shortage for a ceiling above \(P_e\) (or a surplus for a floor below \(P_e\)). ✓ Check binding first; if not, \(Q = Q_e\). ✗Using \(Q_d\) as the traded quantity under a ceiling. ✓ Use the short side: \(Q_s\) for a ceiling, \(Q_d\) for a floor. ✗Taking the DWL height as \(P_e - P_c\). ✓ Height = demand height at \(Q_s\) − ceiling. ✗Drawing maximum CS as a triangle only. ✓ It is a triangle on top of a rectangle down to \(P_c\). ✗Multiplying the bribe by \(Q_d\). ✓ Only \(Q_s\) units are bought: total bribe = bribe × \(Q_s\). ✗Thinking waiting and bribery give different CS. ✓ Same net CS, same PS; waiting wastes the bribe rectangle. ✗Saying a price control shifts demand or supply. ✓ It moves us along the curves (only quality changes shift them). ✗Expecting an input ceiling to lower the output price. ✓ Less input → less output → higher price, read off demand. ✗Adding a buyback vertically. ✓ Add \(G\) units horizontally: \(Q = Q_d(P) + G\). ✗"The supply curve shows the maximum price sellers need." ✓ It shows the minimum price they must receive.

16Find the mistake

Each "solution" below has one error. All use our market D: \(P = 100 - Q\), S: \(P = 20 + Q\).

1

Ceiling $40, ideal allocation. "DWL = ½ × (60 − 40) × (60 − 40) = 200."

Mistake

The lost units are between \(Q_s = 20\) and \(Q_e = 40\), and the height is \(P_d(20) - 40 = 40\). \(DWL = \tfrac12 \times 20 \times 40 = 400\).

2

Floor $55. "Surplus = \(Q_s - Q_d\) = 35 − 45 = −10, so there is a shortage of 10."

Mistake

$55 is below \(P_e = 60\): the floor does not bind. The market stays at \(P = 60\), \(Q = 40\); no shortage, no surplus.

3

Ceiling $40, bribery. "Bribe = $40, so total bribes = 40 × 60 = 2400."

Mistake

Only \(Q_s = 20\) units are sold, so only 20 bribes are paid: 800.

4

WTPs $20, $15, $12, $8; ceiling $4; 2 units. "Bribe must be strictly higher than $11 (the 2nd buyer's 15 − 4)."

Mistake

Surpluses are 16, 11, 8, 4. The bribe must push out the 3rd buyer: strictly higher than $8, and at most $11.

P1 · ceiling, bribery, waiting

Demand \(Q = 120 - 2P\), supply \(Q = 2P - 40\). Ceiling $30. Find the shortage, the equilibrium bribe, the DWL (ideal allocation), and how much more society loses if the good is rationed by waiting (same time cost for everyone).

Hint

\(P_e = 40\). Find \(Q_s(30)\), then the demand price at that quantity.

Answer

\(Q_s = 20\), \(Q_d = 60\): shortage 40. Demand price at 20: \(20 = 120 - 2P\), \(P = 50\); bribe \(= 50 - 30 = 20\). \(Q_e = 40\); \(DWL = \tfrac12 \times 20 \times 20 = 200\). Waiting loses an extra \(20 \times 20 = 400\).

P2 · floor

Same market, price floor $50. Find the surplus, CS, maximum PS and DWL.

Hint

Demand intercept: \(Q = 0\) at \(P = 60\). Supply height at \(Q_d\): \(P = (Q + 40)/2\).

Answer

\(Q_d = 20\), \(Q_s = 60\): surplus 40. \(CS = \tfrac12 \times 20 \times 10 = 100\). Supply height at 20 is 30: \(PS_{\max} = 20 \times 20 + \tfrac12 \times 20 \times 10 = 500\). \(DWL = \tfrac12 \times 20 \times 20 = 200\). (Check: unregulated CS 400 + PS 400 = 800 = 100 + 500 + 200.)

P3 · discrete buyers

Five buyers value a good at $25, $20, $18, $12, $9. Ceiling $5; 3 units supplied; everyone earns $2 an hour. (a) The bribe must be strictly above what? (b) How long must the line be, at least? (c) At least how much more does society lose with waiting than with bribery?

Answer

Surpluses 20, 15, 13, 7, 4. (a) Strictly above $7 (at most $13). (b) More than \(7/2 = 3.5\) hours. (c) More than \(7 \times 3 = \$21\) of time is wasted, so at least $21.

18Concept checks

1

In a market with \(P_e = 60\), the government sets a ceiling of $70. What changes?

Answer

Nothing: the ceiling does not bind. So "after a ceiling, quantity traded must fall" is False.

2

Can a binding ceiling raise consumer surplus (ideal allocation)?

Answer

Yes, sometimes: buyers gain the rectangle (lower price on units still traded) but lose the units no longer traded. In our market CS rose from 800 to 1000 at $40; at a $25 ceiling it falls to \(\tfrac12 \cdot 5 \cdot 5 + 5 \cdot 70 = 362.5\). PS, by contrast, always falls.

3

Why does society lose more when a shortage is rationed by waiting than by bribery?

Answer

A bribe is a transfer: someone receives it. Time in a queue is received by nobody; it is destroyed.

4

A ceiling on crude oil is meant to make petrol cheaper. Does it?

Answer

No. If it binds, less oil is supplied, so less petrol can be made, and petrol's price rises along the demand curve.

5

Under a binding price floor, does quantity supplied increase? Does the quantity traded?

Answer

Quantity supplied \(Q_s\) increases (a movement up the supply curve), but the quantity traded falls to \(Q_d\).

6

Why might a ceiling bind more and more over time even if nothing else changes?

Answer

Supply is more elastic in the long run: sellers leave the market, so \(Q_s\) at the ceiling falls further and the shortage grows.
