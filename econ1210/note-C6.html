Taxes and subsidies

What happens to prices, quantities and everyone's surplus when the government takes a slice of every sale (a tax) or adds money to every sale (a subsidy)? The answer rests on two ideas: the wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers keep, and the rule that the less elastic side of the market carries more of the burden.

1Types of taxes

A tax is money the government collects, by law, from buyers or sellers. There are three kinds, distinguished by how the amount is worked out:

TypeHow much you payEffect on marginal decisionsExample
Lump-sum taxA fixed amount, regardless of circumstances (how much you sell, earn or buy)None. One more unit does not change the tax bill.Yearly licence fee to operate a business
Per-unit (excise) taxA fixed charge per unit soldDirectly raises the cost (or lowers the benefit) of every extra unitFuel tax of HK$6.82 per litre of leaded petrol
Sales (ad valorem) taxA fixed percentage of the value of the saleDirectly affects marginal cost/benefit, but the dollar amount varies with price and quantityStamp duties in Hong Kong (on property and share transactions)
Two words

Excise: a tax on a particular good (petrol, tobacco, alcohol), usually charged per unit. Ad valorem: Latin for “according to value”, so a percentage of the price.

Why does a lump-sum tax not affect marginal decisions? Recall from Chapter 1 §4: you produce one more unit when its marginal benefit beats its marginal cost. A licence fee is paid whether you sell 10 units or 10 000, so it adds nothing to the cost of the next unit. It can only change the yes/no decision “operate at all?”. A per-unit tax adds exactly the tax to the cost of every unit, so it changes how many units are worth selling.

In words: a tax changes behaviour at the margin only if the amount you pay depends on how much you do.

The rest of the chapter studies the per-unit tax, because it is the one that shifts curves.

2Who pays the tax?

A per-unit tax must be handed to the government by someone. The law may say “sellers pay” or “buyers pay”. The surprising lesson: it makes no difference who hands over the money. Market prices adjust so that the burden ends up shared in the same way either way.

Prices with a tax

Price paid by buyers \(P_b\): everything a buyer gives up per unit, tax included.

Price received by sellers \(P_s\): what a seller keeps per unit after the tax has gone to the government.

With a per-unit tax \(t\): \(P_b = P_s + t\).

In words: what buyers pay = what sellers keep + the tax.

A tax on sellers shifts supply up by the tax

Recall that the supply curve shows, for each quantity, the lowest price sellers will accept (their marginal cost). If sellers must hand $1 per unit to the government, they will only supply each unit if the price covers their old marginal cost plus $1. So every point of the supply curve moves up by exactly the tax.

  • The new equilibrium B is where the new (higher) supply curve meets the unchanged demand curve.
  • Buyers pay more than before: the price at B is above the old price at A.
  • Sellers receive less than before: they get the price at B minus the tax, which lies below the old price.
  • Quantity falls from \(Q_{\text{no tax}}\) to \(Q_{\text{with tax}}\).

The apple example

Start with supply alone: a $1 tax on apple sellers lifts the supply curve by $1 at every quantity (at 250 baskets, from $1 to $2; at 1250 baskets, from $3 to $4).

Now add demand. Without the tax the market is at point a: 700 baskets. With the tax it moves to point b: 500 baskets, buyers pay $2.65 and sellers receive $2.65 − $1 = $1.65. Point c (on the old supply line, directly above a) shows that the full $1 would only reach buyers if quantity did not change. It does change, so the price rises by less than the tax.

A tax on buyers shifts demand down by the tax

The demand curve shows, for each quantity, the most buyers will pay (their marginal benefit). If buyers must also hand $1 per unit to the government, the most they will pay the seller falls by $1. So the demand curve, measured in the price sellers receive, moves down by the tax.

In the apple market a $1 tax on buyers moves demand down by $1. The new equilibrium is at 500 baskets, sellers receive $1.65, and buyers pay $1.65 + $1 = $2.65. Same result as if the tax were levied on sellers. The price rises by less than the tax.

Who pays the tax?

Whether the law collects the tax from buyers or from sellers, the market ends up at the same quantity, the same buyer price and the same seller price. The statutory payer (who sends the cheque) does not decide the economic burden (whose prices change).

In words: who writes the cheque doesn't matter; how the prices move does.

3The tax “wedge”

Since it does not matter whether buyers or sellers are taxed, we can skip shifting curves and draw the tax as a simple wedge: a vertical gap of size \(t\) slotted between the demand and supply curves.

The wedge rule

Find the quantity \(Q_t\) at which the demand curve lies exactly \(t\) above the supply curve. Then

\[ \begin{aligned} P_b &= \text{demand price at } Q_t \\ P_s &= \text{supply price at } Q_t \\ P_b - P_s &= t \end{aligned} \]

In words: slide a vertical stick of length \(t\) into the gap between the curves from the left until it fits exactly. Its top touches demand at the buyers' price, its bottom touches supply at the sellers' price, and its position is the new quantity.

In the apple market the $1 wedge sits between B (on demand, buyers pay $2.65) and D (on supply, sellers receive $1.65), at the quantity demanded with the $1 tax. Point A is the old equilibrium. Without the labels, the wedge is all you need to draw.

Analogy

Think of a cross-harbour tunnel toll that the government keeps. Drivers and the bus company both feel the toll: fares go up a bit and the bus company keeps a bit less per trip. The toll is the wedge between what passengers pay and what the company keeps, whoever officially pays it at the booth.

4Using the wedge with numbers: tables and equations

The wedge rule above is stated with pictures. The exam gives numbers, either as a schedule (a table of prices with quantity demanded and supplied) or as linear equations. This section applies the rule to both.

From a schedule C6.1

  1. Find the no-tax equilibrium row, where quantity demanded = quantity supplied.
  2. Tax \(t\): look for two rows whose prices differ by exactly \(t\), with \(Q_d\) at the higher price equal to \(Q_s\) at the lower price. Higher price = \(P_b\), lower price = \(P_s\), common quantity = new \(Q\).
  3. Subsidy \(s\) (section 7): the same search, but now sellers get the higher price: \(Q_s(P_s) = Q_d(P_b)\) with \(P_s - P_b = s\).
  4. Tax revenue = \(t \times Q_{\text{new}}\); subsidy expenditure = \(s \times Q_{\text{new}}\).
Chocolate schedule (2025 Spring Supp, also 2020 Fall Final): slide the wedge

Highlighted cells: the buyers' row (quantity demanded) and the sellers' row (quantity supplied). They must show the same number. The table moves in $5 steps and quantities change by 20 per step on each side, so each $10 of wedge moves quantity 20 units.

Worked example · the chocolate schedule above

No tax: at $65 both columns read 140, so \(P_0 = 65\), \(Q_0 = 140\).

$20 tax: try \(P_b = 75\), \(P_s = 55\): \(Q_d(75) = 100\) and \(Q_s(55) = 100\). Match. Buyers pay $75, sellers keep $55, 100 units, revenue \(20 \times 100 = \$2000\). Buyers pay $10 more and sellers get $10 less: here the burden splits evenly.

$10 subsidy: try \(P_b = 60\), \(P_s = 70\): \(Q_d(60) = 160 = Q_s(70)\). New quantity 160, expenditure \(10 \times 160 = \$1600\).

Schedule traps

Revenue uses the new quantity, not 140. Some tables step by $2, $3 or $4, or have irregular quantity steps (potato chips, pizza): don't fit a straight line; just scan for the pair of rows. A subsidy moves the buyer price down and the seller price up.

From linear equations C6.2

Exam equations usually give quantity as a function of price: demand \(Q = a - bP\), supply \(Q = c + dP\). Here \(b\) and \(d\) say how many units quantity changes per $1 change in price.

Ready answer · per-unit tax with linear curves

The tax means buyers pay \(P_b\) and sellers get \(P_b - t\). Set demand at the buyers' price equal to supply at the sellers' price:

\[ a - bP_b = c + d(P_b - t) \]

In words: the amount buyers want at their price equals the amount sellers offer at their price.

Solving gives handy shortcuts:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta Q &= t \cdot \frac{bd}{b+d} \\ P_b - P_0 &= t \cdot \frac{d}{b+d} \\ P_0 - P_s &= t \cdot \frac{b}{b+d} \end{aligned} \]

In words: buyers' share of the tax = supply's responsiveness ÷ total responsiveness. The more strongly sellers react to price (big \(d\)), the more of the tax lands on buyers.

Worked example · diesel (2024 Summer Midterm Q31)

Demand \(Q = 25 - 2P\), supply \(Q = 3P - 4\), tax $2 per litre.

  1. No tax: \(25 - 2P = 3P - 4\) gives \(P_0 = 5.8\), \(Q_0 = 13.4\).
  2. With tax: \(25 - 2P_b = 3(P_b - 2) - 4\), so \(35 = 5P_b\), \(P_b = 7\), \(P_s = 5\), \(Q = 25 - 14 = 11\).
  3. Shortcut check: \(\Delta Q = 2 \times \tfrac{2 \times 3}{5} = 2.4\) (13.4 → 11). Buyers' share \(= \tfrac{3}{5}\): \(P_b\) rises by 1.2.
  4. Revenue \(= 2 \times 11 = 22\). Deadweight loss (section 6) \(= \tfrac12 \times 2 \times 2.4 = 2.4\).

Q = 11, DWL = 2.4

When the question gives slopes and an elasticity

Some papers describe curves by their slopes and the price elasticity of demand at equilibrium, and you must recover the equilibrium first. In these questions “slope” means the slope as drawn, \(\Delta P / \Delta Q\) (price on the vertical axis). The printed answers only work with that reading.

Recall from Chapter 5: elasticity \(= \dfrac{\Delta Q / Q}{\Delta P / P} = \dfrac{1}{\text{slope}} \cdot \dfrac{P}{Q}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} |e_d| &= \frac{1}{|\text{slope}_D|} \cdot \frac{P}{Q} \\ \text{so } P &= |e_d| \cdot |\text{slope}_D| \cdot Q \end{aligned} \]

In words: knowing any two of price, quantity and elasticity (plus the slope) gives you the third.

Worked example · widget (2020 Fall Midterm Q37–Q38)

Demand slope −1, supply slope 1, equilibrium price $50, elasticity of demand −0.6. Quantity: \(Q = P / (|e_d| \cdot |\text{slope}_D|) = 50 / 0.6 = 83.33\).

A $4.8 subsidy: with slopes in \(\Delta P/\Delta Q\), quantity changes by \(\Delta Q = s / (|\text{slope}_D| + \text{slope}_S) = 4.8 / 2 = 2.4\). Buyers' price falls by \(1 \times 2.4 = 2.4\) to $47.60; sellers' price rises by \(1 \times 2.4\) to $52.40.

Q₀ = 83.33; Pb = 47.60, Ps = 52.40

Two kinds of slope

Equation coefficients like the \(b\) in \(Q = a - bP\) are \(\Delta Q/\Delta P\). A stated “slope” is \(\Delta P/\Delta Q = 1/b\). Mixing them up flips the burden split. If a curve is given as \(P = A - BQ\), its slope is \(-B\) and \(b = 1/B\).

5Who bears the burden? It depends on elasticity

Tax burden (tax incidence)

The burden of a tax on buyers is how much the price they pay rises: \(P_b - P_0\). The burden on sellers is how much the price they keep falls: \(P_0 - P_s\). The two add up to the tax.

\[ (P_b - P_0) + (P_0 - P_s) = t \]

In words: buyers' share + sellers' share = the whole tax.

The incidence rule

Who pays the tax depends on the relative elasticities of supply and demand. The less elastic side of the market pays the greater share of a tax (bears more of the burden).

In words: whoever can't easily walk away gets stuck with the bill.

Recall: elastic means quantity reacts strongly to price (a flat curve); inelastic means quantity hardly reacts (a steep curve). A side that can easily switch to something else, or stop buying or selling, is elastic.

Demand more elastic than supplyDemand is flat, supply steep. The buyers' price b rises only a little above the old price a; the sellers' price d falls a lot. Sellers pay more of the tax.
Supply more elastic than demandSupply is flat, demand steep. Buyers' price rises a lot; sellers' price falls a little. Buyers pay more of the tax.

The luxury tax on boats

A pleasure boat seems like a good thing to tax: only the rich buy them. The US Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1990 put a 10% federal luxury tax on the retail sale of luxury goods such as pleasure boats priced above $100 000. Expected revenue: $9 billion. Reality:

  • Sales of boats fell 52.7%.
  • Net loss of 30 000 jobs (mainly boat-building workers).
  • The federal government paid out more than $7 million more in unemployment benefits to those workers than it collected in luxury-tax revenue.
  • The tax was repealed in 1993.

Why? Demand for yachts is very elastic: rich buyers can buy a boat abroad, buy a second-hand boat, or spend the money on a holiday home instead. Supply is less elastic: boat-yard workers and equipment cannot quickly switch to another industry. So the less elastic side (the sellers and their workers) carried most of the burden, and the large drop in quantity meant little revenue.

Who pays the cigarette tax?

Smokers are addicted, so their demand is very inelastic; tobacco firms can sell elsewhere or produce other products, so supply is relatively elastic. The less elastic side, the smokers, pays most of a cigarette tax: the shop price rises by nearly the full tax.

The burden formula

When demand and supply are straight lines (or for small taxes), the shares can be computed exactly. Let \(\alpha\) be the angle the demand curve makes with the horizontal and \(\beta\) the angle of the supply curve, so \(\tan\alpha = |\text{Slope}_d|\) and \(\tan\beta = \text{Slope}_s\) (slopes as drawn, \(\Delta P/\Delta Q\)).

\[ \begin{aligned} \%\ \text{burden on buyers} &= \frac{\tan\alpha}{\tan\alpha + \tan\beta} \\ &= \frac{|\text{Slope}_d|}{|\text{Slope}_d| + \text{Slope}_s} \\ &= \frac{1/|\eta_d|}{1/|\eta_d| + 1/\eta_s} \\ &= \frac{\eta_s}{\eta_s + |\eta_d|} \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} \%\ \text{burden on sellers} &= \frac{\tan\beta}{\tan\alpha + \tan\beta} \\ &= \frac{\text{Slope}_s}{|\text{Slope}_d| + \text{Slope}_s} \\ &= \frac{|\eta_d|}{\eta_s + |\eta_d|} \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\eta_d\) and \(\eta_s\) are the price elasticities of demand and supply at the original equilibrium. The third line comes from multiplying each slope by \(Q/P\): at the same point, \(|\text{Slope}_d| \cdot Q/P = 1/|\eta_d|\) and \(\text{Slope}_s \cdot Q/P = 1/\eta_s\).

In words: buyers' share = how steep demand is ÷ (steepness of demand + steepness of supply) = supply elasticity ÷ (sum of the two elasticities). The buyers' share is driven by how elastic the other side is.

Ready answer · equation form

For \(Q_d = a - bP\) and \(Q_s = c + dP\): buyers' share \(= \dfrac{d}{b+d}\), sellers' share \(= \dfrac{b}{b+d}\).

In words: each side's share is the other side's price coefficient over the total.

Quick check · 2024 Fall Midterm Q29

Linear supply through the origin has elasticity exactly 1 at every point (a 10% rise in price gives a 10% rise in quantity). If demand is unit elastic at equilibrium too, buyers' share \(= 1/(1+1) = 50\%\).

Reading the evidence backwards

A frequent multiple-choice item: “a $3.1 subsidy cut the buyers' price by $2.4. What can we conclude?” Buyers got \(2.4/3.1 \approx 77\%\) of it, more than half, so demand is the less elastic side. More than half to buyers ⇔ demand less elastic than supply.

6The effects of a tax: revenue and deadweight loss

Recall from Chapter 3: consumer surplus (CS) is the area under the demand curve and above the price buyers pay, the total of (willingness to pay − price) over all units bought. Producer surplus (PS) is the area above the supply curve and below the price sellers receive. With no tax, total economic surplus = CS + PS. In the apple market this is the big triangle up to 700 baskets.

With a tax the triangle is cut into four pieces:

  • Consumer surplus, consumers get this: the triangle above the buyer price ($2.65), up to the new quantity (500).
  • Producer surplus, producers get this: the triangle below the seller price ($1.65), up to 500.
  • Tax revenue, the government gets this: the rectangle between $2.65 and $1.65, 500 wide. Height × width \(= 1 \times 500 = \$500\).
  • Deadweight loss, no one gets this: the small triangle between the curves from 500 to 700.
Deadweight loss (DWL)

The fall in total surplus (CS + PS + government revenue) caused by a policy. It comes from trades that would have benefited both a buyer and a seller but no longer happen because of the tax.

DWL of a tax \[ DWL = \tfrac12 \times \text{decrease in quantity} \times \text{tax} \]

In words: the lost triangle has height = the tax and base = the units no longer traded.

In the apple market: \(DWL = \tfrac12 \times (700 - 500) \times 1 = \$100\).

Ready answer · the welfare accounting identity C6.5

For a tax with linear curves, everything CS and PS lose goes either to the government or nowhere:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{CS loss} + \text{PS loss} \\ &= \text{revenue} + DWL \end{aligned} \]

and \(\text{revenue} = t \times Q_1\), \(DWL = \tfrac12 \times t \times (Q_0 - Q_1)\).

In words: the private sector loses the revenue plus the deadweight loss; so it always loses more than the government collects.

Exam questions sometimes give only the losses and the DWL. Then revenue = CS loss + PS loss − DWL; \(Q_1 = \text{revenue}/t\); \(Q_0 - Q_1 = 2 \cdot DWL / t\).

Worked example

A $10 tax lowers CS by $700 and PS by $450; DWL is $150. Revenue \(= 700 + 450 - 150 = \$1000\). New quantity \(= 1000/10 = 100\). Quantity fell by \(2 \times 150 / 10 = 30\), so it was 130 before.

Deadweight loss and elasticity

  • Deadweight losses are larger the bigger the tax's impact on the quantity traded.
  • The impact on quantity is bigger when the demand curve is more elastic or the supply curve is more elastic.

In words: the more people walk away from the market because of the tax, the more good trades are lost.

From the shortcut in section 4: \(\Delta Q = t \cdot bd/(b+d)\), which grows when either \(b\) or \(d\) grows. If either side is perfectly inelastic (\(b = 0\) or \(d = 0\)), quantity does not change and DWL = 0. Even then the tax still moves prices: with perfectly inelastic demand, buyers pay the full tax.

Combining the two lessons: taxing goods with inelastic demand or supply raises revenue with little deadweight loss, but puts the burden on whichever side cannot escape.

Going further · zero revenue, big loss

A tax so high that nobody trades (a prohibitive tax) raises zero revenue but destroys the whole surplus triangle: the largest possible DWL. So “a tax that raises no revenue causes no deadweight loss” is false. And “any positive tax causes a positive DWL” is also false: not if one side is perfectly inelastic.

7Subsidies: a tax in reverse

Subsidy

A subsidy is a reverse tax: the government gives money to consumers (or producers) for each unit traded.

\[ \text{Subsidy} = P_s - P_b \]

In words: subsidy = price received by sellers − price paid by buyers. Sellers get more than buyers pay; the government fills the gap.

Three facts about subsidies:

  1. Who gets the subsidy does not depend on who receives the cheque from the government. Exactly like a tax: pay it to buyers or to sellers, the market ends at the same prices and quantity.
  2. Who benefits does depend on the relative elasticities of demand and supply.
  3. Subsidies must be paid for by taxpayers, and they create inefficient increases in trade (deadweight loss).

The subsidy wedge

A subsidy drives a wedge between the price received by sellers and the price paid by buyers, but now sellers are on top. Slide the wedge into the gap to the right of the equilibrium, where the supply curve lies above the demand curve, until the gap equals the subsidy.

In the apple market a $1 subsidy gives: sellers receive $2.40 (point b, on supply), buyers pay $1.40 (point d, on demand), at the quantity demanded with the subsidy, to the right of the old equilibrium a. If the subsidy is $1, the price buyers pay must be $1 less than the price sellers receive.

Ready answer · subsidy with linear curves \[ a - bP_b = c + d(P_b + s) \]

In words: the amount buyers want at their (lower) price equals the amount sellers offer at their (higher) price.

Shortcuts, for \(Q_d = a - bP\), \(Q_s = c + dP\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta Q &= s \cdot \frac{bd}{b+d} \quad (\text{an increase}) \\ P_0 - P_b &= s \cdot \frac{d}{b+d} \\ P_s - P_0 &= s \cdot \frac{b}{b+d} \end{aligned} \]

Who benefits more?

Picture a steep supply curve and a flatter demand curve, with both a tax wedge (to the left of the equilibrium) and a subsidy wedge (to the right). In both, the sellers' price moves a lot and the buyers' price a little.

Benefit rule

When demand is more elastic than supply, suppliers bear more of the burden of a tax and receive more of the benefit of a subsidy. In general: the less elastic side gets the larger share of a subsidy.

In words: whichever side is stuck carries more of a tax and pockets more of a subsidy.

The formulas are identical to the tax formulas:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{buyers' share of the subsidy} \\ &= \frac{|\text{Slope}_d|}{|\text{Slope}_d| + \text{Slope}_s} \\ &= \frac{\eta_s}{\eta_s + |\eta_d|} \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} &\text{sellers' share of the subsidy} \\ &= \frac{\text{Slope}_s}{|\text{Slope}_d| + \text{Slope}_s} \\ &= \frac{|\eta_d|}{\eta_s + |\eta_d|} \end{aligned} \]

In words: % of a subsidy buyers receive = % of a tax buyers would bear; same for sellers.

Worked example · legal services (2024 Spring Final Q16)

Demand \(Q = 53.3 - 8P\), supply \(Q = 2.3 + 2P\), equilibrium $5.10 and 12.5 thousand hours. Subsidy $10 per hour.

Buyers' share \(= d/(b+d) = 2/10\): buyers pay \(10 \times 0.2 = \$2\) less. Sellers' share \(= 8/10\): sellers receive $8 more. Demand (\(b = 8\)) is the more elastic side here, so sellers, the less elastic side, get most of it.

Buyers pay $2.00 less; sellers receive $8.00 more

8The effects of a subsidy

Build the subsidy picture step by step.

  1. No subsidy: CS above \(P_{\text{no sub}}\), PS below it, up to \(Q_{\text{no subsidy}}\). Welfare to society = CS + PS.
  2. Quantity traded under the subsidy: \(Q_{\text{sub}}\) is larger. Sellers receive a price above \(P_{\text{no sub}}\); buyers pay a price below it.
  3. Consumer surplus: the triangle under demand and above the (lower) buyer price, out to \(Q_{\text{sub}}\). It is bigger than before.
  4. Producer surplus: the triangle above supply and below the (higher) seller price, out to \(Q_{\text{sub}}\). Also bigger.
  5. Subsidy expenditure: the government pays the rectangle \(s \times Q_{\text{sub}}\), between the seller price and the buyer price.
  6. Deadweight loss: the rectangle overlaps both surplus triangles, except for a small triangle beyond \(Q_{\text{no sub}}\), between the supply curve (above) and the demand curve (below). That triangle is paid for but no one gets it.
Welfare with a subsidy \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Welfare} &= CS + PS \\ &\quad - \text{subsidy expenditure} \end{aligned} \] \[ DWL = \tfrac12 \times s \times (Q_{\text{sub}} - Q_{\text{no sub}}) \]

In words: society gains CS and PS but must pay for the subsidy; it ends up worse off by a triangle whose height is the subsidy and whose base is the extra units traded.

Why is the extra trade a loss? Each extra unit between \(Q_{\text{no sub}}\) and \(Q_{\text{sub}}\) costs more to produce (height of supply) than buyers value it (height of demand). The subsidy makes it worthwhile privately, but society spends more resources than the unit is worth.

Ready answer · subsidy accounting C6.5 \[ \begin{aligned} \text{expenditure} &= \text{CS gain} + \text{PS gain} \\ &\quad + DWL \end{aligned} \]

In words: the government spends more than buyers and sellers gain, by exactly the DWL. Compare the tax identity: there DWL is subtracted (revenue = losses − DWL); here it is added.

Then \(Q_{\text{sub}} = \text{expenditure}/s\) and \(Q_{\text{sub}} - Q_0 = 2 \cdot DWL/s\). The combined change in CS + PS \(= s \cdot Q_{\text{sub}} - DWL\).

Example · withdrawing a subsidy

Removing a subsidy works like imposing a tax from the subsidised position: the sellers' price falls, the buyers' price rises, quantity falls back. Buyers and sellers lose \(s \cdot Q_{\text{sub}} - DWL\), which is less than the expenditure the government saves (\(s \cdot Q_{\text{sub}}\)). Society gains the DWL back.

Does a subsidy raise or lower DWL with elasticity?

Exactly like a tax: the more elastic demand and/or supply, the bigger the change in quantity, and the bigger the deadweight loss. A subsidy with a perfectly inelastic side causes no extra trade and no DWL.

King Cotton and the deadweight loss of water subsidies

Farmers in California's Central Valley typically pay $20–$30 an acre-foot for water that costs $200–$500 an acre-foot to supply. (An acre-foot is the water that covers one acre of land one foot deep, about 1.2 million litres.) This is a huge subsidy to cotton growing. Who benefits most: (a) California cotton suppliers or (b) California cotton buyers?

Hint

Which is more elastic, demand or supply for California cotton?

Answer

(a) Suppliers. California cotton is sold on a world market where buyers can easily switch to cotton from elsewhere: demand for California cotton is very elastic. Supply (land, water rights, farms) is less elastic. Since supply is less elastic than demand, suppliers receive more of the benefit of any subsidy. Meanwhile the subsidy draws scarce water into a low-value crop in a dry state: a large deadweight loss.

9Wage subsidies versus the minimum wage

In the labour market, workers are the sellers (they supply hours of work) and firms are the buyers (they demand hours). The price is the wage.

The minimum wage is the clearest example of a price floor, a legal lowest price, in the United States and Hong Kong. What is the current minimum wage in Hong Kong? Hong Kong now reviews it every year: it rose to $42.1 per hour in May 2025 and to $43.1 per hour from 1 May 2026 (Labour Department), so check the current figure.

  • A minimum wage can hurt low-skilled workers by reducing employment.
  • Some economists believe a better approach is to subsidise employers (the demanders of labour). This leads to a higher wage and a higher level of employment.

Minimum wage reduces employment

Suppose the market wage is $10.50 (point a, employment \(Q_m\)). A minimum wage of $12 is set. At $12 firms hire only \(Q_d\) (point c on demand) while \(Q_s\) workers want jobs (point b on supply). Employment falls from \(Q_m\) to \(Q_d\); \(Q_s - Q_d\) workers are unemployed. Chapter 7 studies price floors in detail.

Wage subsidies increase employment

Instead, the government pays firms a $4 subsidy per hour worked. Workers receive $12 (point b), firms pay only $8 (point d): the $4 subsidy wedge. Employment rises from \(Q_m\) to \(Q_s\). The subsidy costs the government money, but increases employment (and reduces welfare payments to the unemployed).

labour demand (firms)labour supply (workers)

Illustration with invented quantities, matching the wages above: supply \(W = 10.5 + 0.05(Q - 100)\), demand \(W = 10.5 - (Q - 100)/12\), with Q in thousands of workers. Minimum wage $12: firms hire 82, 130 want work. $4 subsidy: 130 employed, workers get $12, firms pay $8.

Wage subsidies: pros and cons

  • Suggested by Edmund Phelps, winner of the Nobel Prize in Economics (2006).
  • It would be expensive, paid by taxpayers.
  • It would result in more low-skilled workers being employed.
  • The costs could be offset by social benefits: less crime, less dependency on welfare, and so on.

Then why minimum-wage legislation instead of an employment subsidy?

Think it through, then open

There is no single answer. Points worth making:

  • Who pays. A minimum wage costs the government nothing: firms and the unemployed bear the cost, and it does not appear in the budget. A subsidy is a visible line in the budget, paid for by taxes, which themselves cause deadweight loss (section 6).
  • Politics. Workers who keep their jobs see a pay rise they can point to; the workers who are never hired are invisible.
  • Targeting and abuse. A subsidy needs administration and checks (are those hours real? would the firm have hired anyway?).

Many countries combine the two, for example a minimum wage plus a wage top-up through the tax system for low-paid workers.

10Designing a tax or subsidy to hit a target

Going further · why this section exists

The sections above explain what a given tax does. About a quarter of the past-paper parts turn the question around: “what tax achieves this goal?”. No new economics is needed, just the section-4 formulas run backwards. All examples here use one market:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Demand: } Q &= 1200 - 20P \\ \text{Supply: } Q &= 30P - 300 \end{aligned} \]

Equilibrium: \(1200 - 20P = 30P - 300\), so \(P_0 = 30\), \(Q_0 = 600\). Here \(b = 20\), \(d = 30\).

The key number is how much quantity falls per $1 of tax:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\Delta Q}{t} &= \frac{bd}{b+d} \\ \text{so } t &= \Delta Q \left( \frac1b + \frac1d \right) \end{aligned} \]

In words: to remove \(\Delta Q\) units you must push the buyers' price up along demand (\(\Delta Q/b\)) and the sellers' price down along supply (\(\Delta Q/d\)); the tax is the sum of the two moves.

Here \(1/b + 1/d = 1/20 + 1/30 = 1/12\), so each $1 of tax removes 12 units.

Target a change in quantity C6.3

Example · cut quantity by 60

\(t = 60 \times \tfrac{1}{12} = \$5\). Check: \(Q_1 = 540\): buyers pay \((1200 - 540)/20 = 33\), sellers get \((540 + 300)/30 = 28\), gap $5. DWL \(= \tfrac12 \times 5 \times 60 = 150\).

A subsidy to raise quantity by 60 is also $5. A target given as a percentage (“raise quantity by 20%”) first becomes units (\(0.2 \times 600 = 120\)). A target buyer price (“buyers pay 10% less”) gives \(Q_1\) from demand, then read \(P_s\) off supply at \(Q_1\); the wedge is the gap.

Biggest tax (or subsidy) under a DWL cap

Since \(\Delta Q = t \cdot bd/(b+d)\),

\[ \begin{aligned} DWL &= \tfrac12 \, t \, \Delta Q = \tfrac12 \, t^2 \, \frac{bd}{b+d} \\ \text{so } t_{\max} &= \sqrt{2 \cdot DWL_{\max} \left( \tfrac1b + \tfrac1d \right)} \end{aligned} \]

In words: DWL grows with the square of the tax, so to find the largest allowed tax, take a square root.

Example · DWL at most 600

\(t_{\max} = \sqrt{2 \times 600 \times \tfrac1{12}} = \sqrt{100} = \$10\). Check: \(\Delta Q = 120\), \(DWL = \tfrac12 \times 10 \times 120 = 600\). ✓

Revenue floor or budget cap: a quadratic

Revenue with tax \(t\): \(R(t) = t \cdot (Q_0 - 12t) = t(600 - 12t)\). “Raise at least $5400, with the lowest possible tax”:

\[ \begin{aligned} 600t - 12t^2 &= 5400 \\ t^2 - 50t + 450 &= 0 \\ t &= \frac{50 \pm \sqrt{700}}{2} \end{aligned} \]

\(t = 11.77\) or \(38.23\). Both raise $5400; the question wants the smaller root, $11.77.

Budget cap on a subsidy: expenditure \(= s(600 + 12s) \le 8400\). Solve \(12s^2 + 600s - 8400 = 0\), i.e. \(s^2 + 50s - 700 = 0\): \(s = (-50 + \sqrt{5300})/2 = \$11.40\) at most. (The other root is negative.) Expenditure uses the new, larger quantity.

Revenue-maximising tax: the midpoint rule C6.4

Revenue \(t(600 - 12t)\) is zero at \(t = 0\) and at \(t = 50\) (the tax that kills the market). A hill-shaped quadratic peaks halfway between its zeros:

\[ t^* = \tfrac12 \times t_{\text{kill}} \]

In words: the revenue-maximising tax is half the tax that would shut the market down; it halves the quantity.

Example · revenue-max tax

\(t_{\text{kill}} = Q_0 \left(\tfrac1b + \tfrac1d\right) = 600/12 = 50\), so \(t^* = 25\). Then \(Q = 300\), revenue \(= 25 \times 300 = 7500\), DWL \(= \tfrac12 \times 25 \times 300 = 3750\), and CS + PS fall by \(7500 + 3750 = 11\,250\).

Ready answer · constant marginal cost (flat supply)

Demand \(P = A - BQ\), supply flat at \(MC\): the kill tax is \(A - MC\), so \(t^* = (A - MC)/2\). With \(Q = a - bP\): \(t^* = (a - b \cdot MC)/(2b)\). Buyers pay the whole tax; \(DWL = \tfrac12 \, t \, (b\,t)\).

In words: halfway between the cost and the highest price anyone would pay.

A seller maximising sales revenue, then a tax on buyers

Some questions describe a shop (or charity) that sets its price to maximise revenue on a straight-line demand curve. Revenue \(P \times Q\) is largest at the midpoint of the line, where elasticity is −1 (Chapter 5). So the observed price \(P_0\) and quantity \(Q_0\) pin down the whole line: the midpoint means the line hits the quantity axis at \(2Q_0\) and the price axis at \(2P_0\):

\[ Q = 2Q_0 - \frac{Q_0}{P_0} P \]
Example

A stall maximises revenue at $20 and sells 100 a day: \(Q = 200 - 5P\). A $4 tax is charged to buyers, so buyers react to \(P + 4\): \(Q = 200 - 5(P + 4) = 180 - 5P\). The new midpoint: \(Q = 90\), \(P = 18\). The stall sets $18 (buyers pay $22) and sells 10 fewer. In general quantity falls by \(b\,t/2\). A subsidy to buyers works the other way: price and quantity both rise.

11Forcing a quantity: kinked market curves C6.6

Going further · combines Chapter 3 and this chapter

A planner sometimes orders a quantity directly: “cut trade by 50%”, “ban imports”, “produce exactly 953 units”. Two questions follow: what surplus is lost, and what tax or subsidy would reach the same quantity? Often the market curve is built from two groups, so it has a kink.

Recall from Chapter 3: the market supply curve is the horizontal sum of the groups' curves (add the quantities at each price). A group only joins once the price reaches its intercept, which creates the kink.

  1. Build the market curve and find the equilibrium \(Q_0\). Check that the price is on the right side of the kink.
  2. The loss from forcing \(Q^*\) is the area between demand (above) and market supply (below) from \(Q^*\) to \(Q_0\). If the area spans the kink, split it into two pieces at the kink.
  3. The equivalent wedge is the vertical gap at \(Q^*\): demand price − supply price. Positive ⇒ a tax. Negative (when \(Q^* \gt Q_0\)) ⇒ a subsidy of that size.
  4. A ban (\(Q^* = 0\)) loses all the surplus; the smallest tax that achieves it is (demand intercept − lowest supply intercept).
Two supplier groups, one mandate
demand P = 100 − Qmarket supply (kinked)surplus lost

Group A: \(P = 10 + Q\); group B: \(P = 30 + 2Q\). Below $30 only A sells, so market supply is \(P = 10 + Q\) up to Q = 20. Above $30 both sell: \(Q = (P - 10) + (P - 30)/2\), i.e. \(P = (Q + 25)/1.5\). Equilibrium: P = $50, Q = 50.

Example · cut trade by 40% (Q* = 30)

The gap at 30 is \((100 - 30) - (30 + 25)/1.5 = 70 - 36.67 = 33.33\). The lost area is a triangle from 30 to 50: \(\tfrac12 \times 20 \times 33.33 = 333.33\). Equivalent policy: a tax of $33.33.

Cut to Q* = 10 instead: the area spans the kink. From 10 to 20 (only group A): gap goes from \(90 - 20 = 70\) to \(80 - 30 = 50\), area \(10 \times 60 = 600\). From 20 to 50: triangle \(\tfrac12 \times 30 \times 50 = 750\). Total 1350; equivalent tax $70.

12Summary

  • Lump-sum taxes don't affect marginal decisions; per-unit and sales taxes do.
  • A per-unit tax on sellers shifts supply up by \(t\); on buyers it shifts demand down by \(t\). Same outcome either way. Buyers pay more, sellers receive less, quantity falls, and the price rises by less than the tax.
  • Wedge: \(P_b - P_s = t\) at the new quantity. Subsidy: \(P_s - P_b = s\).
  • Incidence: the less elastic side bears more of a tax and gets more of a subsidy. Buyers' share \(= |\text{Slope}_d|/(|\text{Slope}_d| + \text{Slope}_s) = \eta_s/(\eta_s + |\eta_d|)\); with \(Q_d = a - bP\), \(Q_s = c + dP\) it is \(d/(b+d)\).
  • Tax: revenue \(= t Q_1\); \(DWL = \tfrac12 t (Q_0 - Q_1)\); CS + PS lose revenue + DWL.
  • Subsidy: expenditure \(= s Q_1\); \(DWL = \tfrac12 s (Q_1 - Q_0)\); CS + PS gain expenditure − DWL. Subsidies are paid by taxpayers and cause inefficient extra trade.
  • DWL is larger when demand or supply is more elastic; zero if either is perfectly inelastic.
  • Wage subsidies (Phelps) can raise both wages and employment, unlike a minimum wage, but cost taxpayers money.
  • Exam toolkit: \(t = \Delta Q (1/b + 1/d)\); \(t_{\max} = \sqrt{2 \cdot DWL (1/b + 1/d)}\); revenue-max tax = half the kill tax; revenue floors and budgets are quadratics (smaller root); forced quantities lose the area between the curves.

13Common mistakes

✗“The more elastic side bears more of the tax.” ✓ The less elastic side bears more (and gets more of a subsidy). ✗Thinking the side that hands over the money bears the tax. ✓ Statutory payer is irrelevant. ✗Revenue or expenditure with the old quantity. ✓ Use the new \(Q_1\). ✗Moving subsidy prices the wrong way. ✓ Buyers pay less, sellers receive more. ✗Burden share with the slopes swapped. ✓ Buyers' share \(= |\text{Slope}_d|/(\ldots)\) with slopes as drawn (ΔP/ΔQ), or \(d/(b+d)\) with equation coefficients. ✗Treating an equation coefficient (ΔQ/ΔP) as a “slope” (ΔP/ΔQ). ✓ Check which one the question means. ✗Thinking a subsidy has no deadweight loss because “everyone gains”. ✓ The government spends more than buyers and sellers gain. ✗Tax identity with the DWL added. ✓ Tax: revenue = losses − DWL. Subsidy: expenditure = gains + DWL. ✗\(t = \Delta Q / d\) (one curve only). ✓ \(t = \Delta Q (1/b + 1/d)\): both prices move. ✗Larger root of a revenue-floor quadratic. ✓ “Lowest tax” or “at most” ⇒ smaller positive root. ✗Forgetting the factor 2: \(\Delta Q = DWL/t\). ✓ \(\Delta Q = 2 \cdot DWL / t\). ✗Adding supply curves vertically. ✓ Market curves are horizontal sums; check which segment you are on.

14Find the mistake

Each “solution” has one error. Find it before opening.

1

No-tax equilibrium: 140 units at $65. A $20 tax gives 100 units at buyer price $75. “Tax revenue = 20 × 140 = $2800.”

Mistake

Revenue is collected only on units actually sold after the tax: \(20 \times 100 = \$2000\).

2

Demand slope −2, supply slope 1 (as drawn). A $6 tax. “Buyers' share = 1/(2 + 1) = 1/3, so buyers pay $2 more.”

Mistake

Buyers' share is \(|\text{Slope}_d|/(|\text{Slope}_d| + \text{Slope}_s) = 2/3\): steep demand means buyers can't escape. Buyers pay $4 more, sellers receive $2 less.

3

A subsidy raises CS by $500 and PS by $300; DWL is $100. “Expenditure = 500 + 300 − 100 = $700.”

Mistake

For a subsidy the DWL is added: the government pays for the gains and for the wasted triangle. Expenditure \(= 500 + 300 + 100 = \$900\).

4 · same market as section 10

“Raise at least $5400 with the lowest tax.” “\(t^2 - 50t + 450 = 0\) gives \(t = 38.23\).”

Mistake

That is the larger root. Both roots raise exactly $5400; the lowest tax is $11.77, with far less deadweight loss.

16Concept checks

1

A city doubles its yearly restaurant licence fee. Will restaurants change how many meals they serve per day?

Answer

No: a lump-sum fee does not change the cost of the next meal. It may only make some restaurants close altogether.

2

A $2 tax raises the price buyers pay by $1.50. Which side is less elastic?

Answer

Buyers bear 75% of the tax, more than half, so demand is less elastic than supply.

3

Demand for insulin is perfectly inelastic. A $5 tax is imposed. What happens to quantity, buyer price and DWL?

Answer

Quantity is unchanged; buyers pay the full $5 more; sellers' price is unchanged; DWL = 0.

4

Doubling the tax on cigarettes exactly doubles the revenue. What can you infer?

Answer

Quantity did not respond, so demand (or supply) might be perfectly inelastic. (A recurring True/False item.)

5

The government withdraws a $1 subsidy on petrol. Does the price sellers receive rise or fall?

Answer

Falls. Withdrawing a subsidy acts like a tax on the subsidised market: seller price down, buyer price up, quantity down.

6

“It can be the case that the price received by sellers rises by exactly the subsidy.” True or false?

Answer

True: if demand is perfectly elastic (flat), buyers' price can't move, so sellers get all of it. But “buyers' price increases by the subsidy” is always false: a subsidy never raises the buyers' price.

7

Supply is horizontal at marginal cost. A small rise in the tax raises revenue. Must demand be elastic there?

Answer

No. Tax revenue \(t \times Q\) is not the sellers' revenue \(P \times Q\), so the "elastic ⇒ revenue falls" rule from Chapter 5 does not apply to it. Example: demand \(P = 100 - Q\), MC = $20, so \(Q = 80 - t\) and revenue \(t(80 - t)\) rises until \(t = 40\). At \(t = 10\): price $30, \(Q = 70\), \(|e| = 30/70 \approx 0.43\), inelastic, yet a slightly higher tax still raises revenue. The statement is false.

Mixed-chapter items you will meet in Chapter-6 statement sets: “perfectly inelastic demand ⇒ a supply increase leaves quantity unchanged” (Chapter 4, comparative statics) and “how does a small shift change sellers' revenue?” (Chapter 5, elasticity and total revenue).

Evaluate whether the following statements are True or False.

A) It can be the case that the price received by sellers increases by exactly the per-unit subsidy rate. 30A

B) When the per-unit tax rate on cigarette is doubled, the tax revenue collected from cigarette is also doubled. We can infer that demand for cigarette might be perfectly inelastic. 30B

C) Commodity taxes that raise zero tax revenue can never cause any deadweight loss. 30C

Hint

“It can be the case” is true if any extreme curve (perfectly elastic or inelastic) makes it happen. Think of a prohibitive tax for C.

Answer

A: perfectly elastic demand ⇒ sellers get all of it. B: revenue doubles only if quantity doesn't fall. C: a prohibitive tax raises nothing and destroys all surplus.

A: T · B: T · C: F
