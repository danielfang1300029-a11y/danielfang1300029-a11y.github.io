Competitive supply

Where supply curves come from. A firm that cannot set its own price decides how much to produce by comparing price with marginal cost. In the long run, firms enter and exit until nobody earns more than a normal return. This chapter builds the supply curve from a firm's costs: first one firm, then a whole industry.

1The supply side and competitive firms sl. 3–6

Firms maximise profit sl. 3

On the supply side we study firms: any seller (a company, a shop, a farmer, a hotel) that is assumed to be profit-maximising.

Profit \[ \pi = TR - TC \]

In words: profit (written with the Greek letter \(\pi\), "pi") = total revenue − total cost.

  • Total revenue \(TR = P \times Q\): price per unit times the number of units sold.
  • Total cost \(TC\) includes explicit and implicit costs: money spent on inputs and set-up, and other opportunity costs (for example, the salary the owner gives up by running the firm). This is the same opportunity cost idea as in Chapter 1.

What can a firm do to make profit as large as possible? It depends on how much control it has over its price.

Market structure sl. 4

The handout places markets on a line by the seller's ability to control price:

Perfect competitionNo control over price. This chapter.
MonopolyPerfect control over price (one seller). Chapter 11.

Competitive firms sl. 5

Perfectly competitive market
  • The product is similar across sellers (one farmer's rice is as good as another's).
  • There are many potential sellers, each small relative to the size of the market.

The slide's pictures: an oil field, a huge crowd, a market stall piled with fruit. Think of the stalls in a wet market selling identical choi sum.

What price to set? sl. 6

A competitive firm has no control over price: the market sets it, where market demand meets market supply. The slide's example: the world oil market clears at $50 a barrel with 82,000,000 barrels traded. Your oil well is a tiny part of that.

  • Charge $51 and nobody buys from you: identical oil is available everywhere at $50.
  • Charge $49 and you throw money away: you could sell all you produce at $50.

So the demand curve for your oil is a horizontal line at the market price. Economists call such a curve perfectly elastic (Chapter 5): the quantity buyers want from you jumps from "everything" to "nothing" if you move the price at all. A firm in this position is called a price taker.

The market sets the price; your firm takes it

Top: the whole oil market (millions of barrels). Bottom: one small producer (thousands of barrels). Move market demand and the firm's horizontal demand line moves with the market price; nothing the firm does can move it. Starting point from the handout ($50, 82 million barrels); the curves are drawn for these notes.

Marginal revenue of a price taker

Marginal revenue \(MR\) = the extra revenue from selling one more unit. For a price taker every extra unit sells at the market price, so

\[ MR = P. \]

In words: for a competitive firm, one more unit sold always brings in exactly the market price.

2Factors of production; short run and long run sl. 7–9

Factor of production sl. 7

A factor of production is an input used to produce a good or service: workers, machines, buildings, land, flour and eggs. (The slide shows a worker folding fortune cookies at a tray.)

Fixed and variable factors sl. 8

A fixed factor is an input whose quantity cannot be changed within the time horizon considered (the factory you have leased for a year).

A variable factor is an input whose quantity can be changed within the time horizon (how many workers you call in today).

Short run and long run sl. 9

The long run is a time horizon in which there are no fixed factors: all factors are variable.

The short run is a time horizon in which some factors are fixed and cannot be changed.

In words: "short" and "long" are not a number of months. Short run = stuck with something; long run = long enough to change everything, including opening or closing the business.

Analogy

A cha chaan teng has signed a two-year lease. Tonight it can call in one more cook (variable factor) but cannot add tables (fixed factor). That is the short run. When the lease ends, the owner can move to a bigger shop, a smaller one, or close: the long run.

The costs follow the same split:

Fixed and variable cost sl. 11

Fixed cost \(FC\) = what is paid for the fixed factors. It is the same whatever the output, even at zero output.

Variable cost \(VC\) = what is paid for the variable factors. It rises with output.

\[ TC = FC + VC \]

In words: total cost = fixed cost + variable cost.

3Fortune cookies: diminishing returns and costs sl. 10–12

The handout's running example: Wong's Fortune Cookies. In the short run its kitchen and equipment are fixed; the only thing it can vary is the number of employees per day.

Marginal product sl. 10

Marginal product (MP)

The marginal product of the \(n\)-th worker is the extra output that worker adds:

\[ MP(n) = \text{output}(n) - \text{output}(n-1) \]

In words: marginal product = output with \(n\) workers minus output with one fewer. It is the difference between two rows, not output divided by workers.

Law of diminishing returns

When at least some factors of production are fixed, adding more and more of a variable factor eventually adds less and less output. Here marginal product starts to diminish after the 2nd worker (from the 3rd on).

In words: more cooks in the same small kitchen eventually get in each other's way. The law is about the short run, because something is fixed.

From production to costs sl. 11–12

Wong pays a fixed cost of $80 per day (rent of the kitchen and equipment) and a wage of $24 per day to each employee. So with \(n\) employees: \(FC = 80\), \(VC = 24n\), \(TC = 80 + 24n\).

Marginal cost per pack

Marginal cost \(MC\) = the extra cost of producing one more unit of output (one more pack). When one more worker costs $24 and adds MP packs, each of those extra packs costs

\[ MC = \frac{\Delta TC}{\Delta Q} = \frac{24}{MP} \]

In words: marginal cost per pack = extra cost ÷ extra packs. (\(\Delta\), "delta", means "change in".)

4How much to produce, and whom to hire sl. 13–19

Now suppose the wholesale price of a pack (after paying for flour, eggs and other materials) is $2.50 sl. 13. How many packs should Wong produce?

Exactly as in Chapter 1, split "how many?" into a sequence of yes/no questions sl. 14–15:

Units view sl. 14Should we produce the \(n\)-th unit? Yes if its marginal revenue ≥ its marginal cost.
Workers view sl. 15Should we hire the \(n\)-th employee? Yes if the marginal revenue from that employee ≥ the marginal cost of that employee.

Both lead to the same answer. Profit maximisation is about comparing extra revenue with extra cost at the margin.

Workers view: compare P × MP with the wage sl. 16

The \(n\)-th employee adds MP packs, each selling at $2.50, so she adds revenue \(2.50 \times MP\). She costs $24.

Hiring rule \[ \text{hire the } n\text{-th worker if } P \times MP(n) \ge W \]

In words: hire one more worker if the value of what she adds (price × her marginal product) is at least her wage \(W\).

Units view: compare P with MC per pack sl. 17

The marginal cost per pack from §3 is 0.6, 0.4, 0.8, 1.2, 1.6, 2.4, 4.0. Every pack produced by the first six workers costs at most $2.40 < $2.50, so it is worth making. The 7th worker's packs cost $4.00 each > $2.50. Produce 175 packs (6 employees): the same answer.

Why the two views agree

\(P \times MP \ge W\) ⟺ \(P \ge W / MP = MC\). Dividing both sides of the hiring rule by MP turns it into "price ≥ marginal cost per unit".

Fixed cost never enters the choice

The $80 fixed cost is paid whatever Wong does in the short run, so changing it moves every profit by the same amount and leaves the best number of workers unchanged. Past papers test this directly ("suppose the fixed cost has become $570 …"): same answer as before.

5The rule P = MC sl. 20–24

When the law of diminishing returns applies (some factors are fixed), marginal cost eventually rises as the firm expands output sl. 20. Then the best plan is: keep expanding as long as marginal cost is below price.

Profit-maximising output of a competitive firm sl. 20

If the variable input and output are perfectly divisible (any amount, not just whole units), the profit-maximising output \(Q^*\) satisfies

\[ P = MC(Q^*) \]

In words: produce until the last tiny unit costs exactly what it sells for. This is Chapter 1's \(MB = MC\) with the firm's marginal benefit equal to the price.

The slides build the picture in three steps:

  1. MC rises with production sl. 21: each extra unit gets more costly to produce (more equipment, more maintenance, overtime, crowded kitchen).
  2. MR is flat at the market price sl. 22: however much we sell, the next unit always sells for P. So \(MR = P\) is a horizontal line.
  3. Profit is maximised where P = MC sl. 23. To the left of that point MC < P: one more unit adds profit ("more profit" arrow). To the right MC > P: those units lose money ("less profit").

If the market price changes, so does the profit-maximising quantity sl. 24. A higher price line meets the rising MC curve further to the right, so the firm produces more. Tracing these points as the price moves is exactly how the firm's supply curve is built.

Price line meets the rising marginal cost curve
MC = 3q² − 20q + 40MR = Pgain from each unit up to q*

Written for these notes. The green area is the surplus from units whose MC is below the price. Units to the right of q* would each cost more than they earn.

Two crossings

A U-shaped MC curve can cross the price line twice: once on its falling part and once on its rising part. Only the crossing on the rising part maximises profit; at the falling crossing, the next units cost less than P, so you should keep going. In a formula question, take the larger root of \(MC = P\).

6The short-run shut-down condition sl. 25–32

It might seem that a competitive firm always does best in the short run by producing where \(P = MC\). But there is an exception sl. 25. Is it when fixed costs are very large?

Large fixed costs: still produce sl. 26–27

Wong's best profit at $2.50 was $213.50 with fixed cost $80. If the fixed cost had been any more than $293.50 per day (= 213.50 + 80), Wong would make a loss at every output. Take a fixed cost of $300:

  • In the short run the firm must pay its fixed cost anyway (the equipment lease is signed). A smaller loss beats a larger one, so it keeps producing.
  • In the long run, if conditions are expected to stay the same, it would leave the fortune-cookie business as soon as the lease expires.
So the answer to slide 25's question is no

Large fixed costs do not make a firm shut down in the short run. Fixed costs are sunk in the short run (Chapter 1): no decision today can change them, so they must not affect today's decision.

The real exception: price too low to cover variable cost sl. 28

Suppose the price falls so low that revenue is smaller than variable cost at every output. Then the firm should cease production for the time being:

  • By shutting down, loss = fixed cost (no revenue, no variable cost).
  • By remaining open, loss = fixed cost + (variable cost − revenue) > fixed cost.

Slide 29 asks: if the price of a pack is below $0.48, how much should Wong produce? Answer: nothing. Where does $0.48 come from sl. 30?

Why P < min AVC means shut down sl. 31

If \(P \lt AVC\) at every output \(Q\), multiply both sides by \(Q\):

\[ \begin{aligned} P \times Q &\lt AVC \times Q \\ TR &\lt VC \end{aligned} \]

In words: revenue does not even cover the variable cost at any output, so every unit produced adds to the loss. Shut down in the short run.

Short-run optimal output sl. 32
  1. If \(P \lt \min AVC\), set output to zero (shut down).
  2. Otherwise, choose \(Q\) such that \(P = MC(Q)\) (on the rising part of MC).

In words: first ask "can I cover my variable costs?"; if yes, produce where price equals marginal cost. The shut-down price is min AVC.

7MC, AVC and the short-run supply curve sl. 33–35

With a smooth (divisible) output the costs become curves. Throughout this section and the next we use one example firm, written for these notes:

\[ \begin{aligned} AVC &= q^2 - 10q + 40 \\ MC &= 3q^2 - 20q + 40 \\ FC &= 72 \end{aligned} \]

The exam always gives you the MC formula; you never have to derive it. Here \(VC = AVC \times q = q^3 - 10q^2 + 40q\).

MC and AVC sl. 33

The MC curve crosses the AVC curve at the minimum point of AVC.

  • When MC is below AVC, AVC is falling.
  • When MC is above AVC, AVC is rising.

In words: an average is pulled toward each new value. If the next unit costs less than the average so far, the average drops; if it costs more, the average rises.

Analogy · your GPA

Your cumulative GPA is 3.0. A new course graded 3.6 (above the average) pulls it up; one graded 2.4 pulls it down. The average stops falling and starts rising exactly when the new grades cross it. The new grade is the "marginal", the GPA is the "average".

Beyond the handouts · min AVC without calculus

Complete the square:

\[ \begin{aligned} AVC &= q^2 - 10q + 40 \\ &= (q - 5)^2 + 15 \end{aligned} \]

A square is never negative, so AVC is smallest at \(q = 5\), where \(AVC = 15\). Check: \(MC(5) = 75 - 100 + 40 = 15\), equal to AVC there, as slide 33 says.

In words: for \(AVC = q^2 - bq + c\), the minimum is at \(q = b/2\); plug it back in for the shut-down price.

Short-run supply sl. 34–35

Slide 34 draws several price lines \(MR = P\) at different heights. For each one the firm produces where it meets the rising MC, as long as the price is at least min AVC. Below min AVC it produces nothing.

Firm's short-run supply curve sl. 35

The firm's short-run supply curve is the part of its MC curve that lies above the AVC curve (from the minimum of AVC upwards). Below the shut-down price, quantity supplied is zero.

In words: the supply curve is the marginal cost curve, starting where price first covers average variable cost.

8The long run: profit, entry and exit sl. 36–41

In the long run sl. 37:

  • Fixed factors can be changed (no cost is fixed any more).
  • Firms can choose to enter (start producing) or exit (close down and leave) the industry.
  • Firms exit unprofitable industries and enter profitable ones.
Average cost and profit per unit

Average cost (also average total cost) is total cost per unit: \(AC = TC/Q = AVC + FC/Q\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \pi &= TR - TC \\ &= P \times Q - AC \times Q \\ &= (P - AC) \times Q \end{aligned} \]

In words: profit = (price − average cost) × quantity = profit per unit × number of units. On a graph it is a rectangle: height P − AC, width Q.

Profit in a graph sl. 38

The slide's firm faces \(P = \$50\). It produces where \(P = MC\), at \(Q = 8\), where its average cost is $25.75. Profit:

\[ \pi = (50 - 25.75) \times 8 = \$194. \]

On the graph, the rectangle runs from $25.75 up to $50 and from 0 to 8 units.

MC and AC sl. 39

The MC curve also crosses the AC curve at AC's minimum point. When MC is below AC, AC is falling; when MC is above AC, AC is rising (same averaging logic as for AVC).

AC lies above AVC by \(FC/Q\), a gap that shrinks as Q grows, so min AC sits to the right of min AVC and higher.

When to enter and exit sl. 40

  • A competitive firm is profitable when \(P \gt \min AC\) and unprofitable when \(P \lt \min AC\).
  • So in the long run firms enter industries where \(P \gt \min AC\) and exit industries where \(P \lt \min AC\).
  • At the in-between point \(P = \min AC\), profit is zero.
Two critical prices
PriceShort run (FC sunk)Long run (can leave)
\(P \ge \min AC\)produce at P = MC; profit ≥ 0stay (others may enter if P > min AC)

In words: shut-down price = min AVC (short run); exit price (break-even price) = min AC (long run).

Zero profit in the long run sl. 41

  • While firms earn positive profits, new firms enter. Market supply grows and the price is driven down.
  • While firms earn negative profits, existing firms exit. Market supply shrinks and the price is driven up.
  • In long-run equilibrium, \(P = \min AC\) and all remaining firms earn zero profit. Each firm produces the quantity at the bottom of its AC curve.

9Zero profit, economic profit and producer surplus sl. 42–45

Zero ("normal") profit sl. 42

Economists call zero economic profit normal profit: the firm covers all its costs, including enough to pay its labour and capital what they could earn elsewhere (their opportunity costs).

In words: zero profit is not failure. It means the owners earn exactly what their time and money would earn in their next best use.

Economic vs accounting profit sl. 43

Not all costs need a money payment.

  • An explicit cost requires a money outlay (wages, rent).
  • An implicit cost does not (the salary the owner gives up, the interest the owner's own savings could have earned).
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{economic } \pi &= TR - \text{explicit} \\ &\quad - \text{implicit} \\ \text{accounting } \pi &= TR - \text{explicit} \end{aligned} \]

In words: accountants subtract only the bills; economists also subtract what the owner gave up. Accounting profit ≥ economic profit.

Example · written for these notes

Ada runs a bubble-tea stall. Revenue $600,000 a year; rent, tea and wages $420,000. She quit a $200,000-a-year office job to do this. Accounting profit = 600,000 − 420,000 = $180,000. Economic profit = 180,000 − 200,000 = −$20,000: she would be better off back in the office.

How can everyone earn zero at once? sl. 44

Suppose there are two types of firm: high-cost and low-cost. Low-cost firms keep entering while they can profit, pushing the price down to their min AC. At that price the high-cost firms make losses and are driven out. Eventually only the firms with the lowest min AC survive, and they all earn zero economic profit.

That is why the exam always says "all firms (including potential entrants) are identical": then a single min AC sets the long-run price.

Profit vs producer surplus sl. 45

Producer surplus \[ \begin{aligned} PS &= TR - VC \\ \pi &= TR - TC \\ \pi &= PS - FC \end{aligned} \]

In words: producer surplus = revenue − variable cost; it is the producers' economic surplus in the market. Fixed costs are sunk, so they are left out of surplus. Profit subtracts the fixed cost as well.

Wong again · written for these notes

At $2.50 with 6 workers: \(PS = 437.50 - 144 = \$293.50\) and \(\pi = 293.50 - 80 = \$213.50\). That is where slide 26's $293.50 came from: the firm makes a loss at every output exactly when FC exceeds the biggest possible producer surplus.

Graphically (Chapter 3), producer surplus is the area above the supply (MC) curve and below the price, which is why surplus calculations in the market diagram never include fixed costs.

10Industry long-run supply curves sl. 46–49

So far: one firm. Now the whole industry. The shape of an industry's long-run supply curve depends on how firms' costs change as the industry's total output grows or shrinks sl. 47. There are three types:

1. Increasing-cost industryCosts rise as industry output grows. Long-run supply slopes upward.
2. Constant-cost industryCosts do not change with industry output. Long-run supply is flat (horizontal).
3. Decreasing-cost industryCosts fall as industry output grows. Long-run supply slopes downward. A rare case.

How to find a long-run supply curve sl. 49

We can never "see" a supply curve directly; we only see equilibrium points. The trick is a thought experiment: shift the demand curve (D1 → D2 → D3) while costs follow their rules, and record the new long-run equilibrium each time. The points (Q1, P1), (Q2, P2), (Q3, P3) trace out the supply curve. The slide's points rise from left to right, so that industry's supply slopes upward.

Short-run vs long-run industry supply

Short-run industry supply: the number of firms is fixed; add up the firms' MC-above-AVC curves sideways (Chapter 3's horizontal summation). It slopes upward.

Long-run industry supply: firms can enter and exit until profit is zero; it joins the long-run equilibrium points and can be flat, rising or falling.

11Constant-cost industries sl. 50–54

The handout draws two panels side by side: the market (all firms together) and a representative firm (one typical firm; all firms are alike).

  1. Long-run equilibrium sl. 50. Market supply S and demand D meet at price \(P_0\). In the firm panel, \(P_0\) is exactly at the bottom of the AC curve, where MC crosses it: zero profit.
  2. Demand increases from D0 to D1 sl. 51. In the short run the number of firms is fixed, so the market moves up along S to a higher price \(P_1\). Each firm moves up its MC curve, produces more, and now \(P_1 \gt AC\): the shaded rectangle is a positive short-run profit.
  3. Entry sl. 52. Profit attracts new firms. Market supply shifts right from S0 to S1. In a constant-cost industry the new firms do not change anybody's costs, so entry continues until the price is back down to \(P_0 = \min AC\). Each firm is back at its old output; the extra market output comes entirely from the new firms.
  4. Trace the long-run supply sl. 53–54. The two long-run equilibria are E0 = (Q0, P0) and E1 = (Q1, P0): same price, more quantity. Joining them gives a horizontal long-run supply curve at P0. The additional demand has no impact on the price of the inputs.

In words: in the short run existing firms absorb a demand rise by producing more at a higher price; in the long run new firms absorb it and the price returns to minimum average cost. A demand fall runs the same way in reverse (losses, exit, price back to P0).

"Willingness to pay rises by $10"

Exams describe a demand shift as "each consumer's willingness to pay increases by $10". That moves the demand curve up by 10: replace P by P − 10. For \(Q = 1800 - 4P\) the new demand is \(Q = 1800 - 4(P - 10) = 1840 - 4P\). It does not mean Q rises by 10.

12Increasing- and decreasing-cost industries sl. 55–59

Increasing-cost industries sl. 55–58

Start the same way: demand rises from D0 to D1, the price jumps to \(P_1\), and existing firms earn short-run profits sl. 55. New firms enter, so supply shifts from S0 to S1 sl. 56.

The difference: as the industry grows it bids up the price of an input it needs (land, skilled workers, factory space). Every firm's MC and AC curves shift up. Entry stops when the price equals the new, higher min AC, so the new long-run price is above \(P_0\) (the slide shows it just above).

The two long-run equilibria are E0 = (Q0, P0) and E1 = (Q1, P1) with \(P_1 \gt P_0\) sl. 57. Joining them gives an upward-sloping long-run supply curve sl. 58. The additional demand raises the price of the input.

Analogy

More coffee shops open in Sheung Wan. Ground-floor rents in the district rise, and every shop's costs rise with them. A busier coffee scene ends up with pricier coffee even after all the profits are competed away.

How the exam builds an increasing-cost industry

Past papers (strategy C10.5) make each firm's fixed cost depend on the number of firms, e.g. \(FC(n) = C \times n/100\) ("rent for factory space is higher when there are more firms"). More firms ⇒ higher FC ⇒ higher min AC ⇒ higher long-run price. See §13.

Decreasing-cost industries sl. 59

  • A decreasing-cost industry has lower costs as production expands.
  • Industry clusters help reduce costs as production increases: more specialised resources in one place make each other more efficient (specialist suppliers, a pool of trained workers, shared know-how).
  • Examples: Hollywood, California, the movie capital of the world; Silicon Valley, California, a high-tech cluster.

After a demand increase, the long-run price ends up below the original price: the long-run supply curve slopes down. Set the figure in §11 to "decreasing cost" to see it.

Comparison table for a demand INCREASE

In words: the short run always looks the same (price, output and profit all rise, no new firms). The long run always brings profit back to zero and more firms; the industry type decides only where the price settles. For a demand decrease, flip every arrow.

Increasing cost with a firm's minimum-AC output unchanged (curves shifted straight up) gives \(q_2 = q_0\); in the exam's FC(n) model, \(q\) itself changes. Always compute rather than assume.

13Exam toolkit: cost formulas and industries of identical firms Beyond the handouts

The handout teaches the ideas with tables and sketches. Past papers ask the same ideas with formulas. Everything here follows from §6–§12 plus Core algebra; learn it as a kit.

A. One firm with a cubic cost (AVC a U-shaped quadratic)

Typical data: \(AVC = q^2 - 14q + 149\), \(MC = 3q^2 - 28q + 149\), \(AC = AVC + 28/q\).

  1. Shut-down quantity and price: complete the square, \(AVC = (q - 7)^2 + 100\): min at \(q = 7\), shut-down price $100.
  2. Fixed cost: \(AC - AVC = FC/q = 28/q\), so \(FC = 28\).
  3. Quantity supplied at price P: solve \(MC = P\) and take the larger root (rising branch). At \(P = 149\): \(3q^2 - 28q = 0\), so \(q = 28/3 = 9.33\).
  4. Profit: \(\pi = (P - AVC)\,q - FC\).
Beyond the handouts · reading a cost-curve figure

When the paper shows a labelled MC/AVC/AC sketch: fixed cost \(= (AC - AVC) \times q\) at any quantity where both are labelled; output = where the price line meets the rising MC (if \(P \ge \min AVC\)); profit \(= (P - AVC)\,q - FC\). The falling branch of AVC may also cross the price line: ignore it.

B. One firm with a quadratic total cost

The workhorse \[ \begin{aligned} TC &= Aq^2 + Bq + F \\ MC &= 2Aq + B \\ AVC &= Aq + B \\ AC &= Aq + B + F/q \end{aligned} \]

In words: the MC of \(Aq^2\) is \(2Aq\) (same rule as Chapter 1's \(MB = a - 2bQ\)); the constant \(B\) passes straight through; \(F\) is fixed and has no marginal cost.

  • Shut-down price = min AVC. \(AVC = Aq + B\) is a rising line starting at \(B\), so the shut-down price is \(B\).
  • Firm supply: \(P = 2Aq + B\) ⟹ \(q = (P - B)/(2A)\) for \(P \ge B\).
  • Exit (break-even) price = min AC:
\[ \begin{aligned} q^* &= \sqrt{F/A} \\ \min AC &= B + 2\sqrt{AF} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the efficient scale is where the rising variable part \(Aq\) equals the falling fixed part \(F/q\); there average cost is \(B\) plus twice the square root of \(A \times F\).

Why, without calculus

For positive \(x, y\): \((\sqrt{x} - \sqrt{y})^2 \ge 0\) gives \(x + y \ge 2\sqrt{xy}\), with equality when \(x = y\). Put \(x = Aq\), \(y = F/q\):

\[ \begin{aligned} Aq + \frac{F}{q} &\ge 2\sqrt{Aq \cdot \tfrac{F}{q}} \\ &= 2\sqrt{AF}, \end{aligned} \]

with equality when \(Aq = F/q\), i.e. \(q = \sqrt{F/A}\). Check with slide 39: \(MC(q^*) = 2A\sqrt{F/A} + B = B + 2\sqrt{AF} = \min AC\). ✓

C. An industry of n identical firms

Short-run industry supply

Each firm supplies \(q = (P - B)/(2A)\). With \(n\) firms, add sideways:

\[ \begin{aligned} Q &= n\,\frac{P - B}{2A} \\ P &= B + \frac{2A}{n}\,Q \end{aligned} \]

In words: same vertical intercept \(B\) as one firm, but the slope is divided by the number of firms. Each firm then produces \(q = Q/n\).

Ready answer · the standard question chain
  1. Shut-down price = \(B\); exit price = \(B + 2\sqrt{AF}\).
  2. Long run: \(P = \min AC\); each firm makes \(q^* = \sqrt{F/A}\); market \(Q\) from demand at that price; \(n = Q/q^*\) (may be a decimal).
  3. Short run after a shock (n stays): SR supply \(P = B' + (2A/n)Q\) with the new cost numbers; intersect with the (new) demand; \(q = Q/n\); profit \(= Pq - TC(q)\) with the new \(F\).
  4. New long run: new \(\min AC = B' + 2\sqrt{AF'}\); \(Q\) from demand; \(n = Q/\sqrt{F'/A}\).
  5. Backwards: given LR \(n\) and demand, find \(P\) from SR supply ∩ demand; then \(q = Q/n = \sqrt{F/A}\) gives \(F = Aq^2\).
Worked example · written for these notes

\(TC = 50q^2 + 200q + 800\), demand \(P = 2000 - 2Q\), currently 100 firms.

  1. Shut-down price $200. Min AC at \(q^* = \sqrt{800/50} = 4\): \(\min AC = 200 + 2\sqrt{50 \times 800} = 600\).
  2. SR supply with 100 firms: \(P = 200 + (100/100)Q = 200 + Q\). With demand: \(2000 - 2Q = 200 + Q\), \(Q = 600\), \(P = 800\), each firm \(q = 6\).
  3. Profit per firm: \(800 \times 6 - (50 \cdot 36 + 200 \cdot 6 + 800) = 4800 - 3800 = \$1000 \gt 0\). Firms will enter.
  4. Long run: \(P = 600\), \(Q = (2000 - 600)/2 = 700\), \(n = 700/4 = 175\) firms, each with zero profit.
Try it: change the number of firms

The worked example above. Red: SR industry supply \(P = 200 + (100/n)Q\). Green dashed: min AC = $600. More firms flatten the supply curve and lower the price; at n = 175 the price hits $600 and profit is zero.

D. Constant-cost industry from given market curves

Typical data: SR supply \(Q = 2P - 1\), demand \(Q = 1800 - 4P\) (thousand units), currently in LR equilibrium.

  1. Equilibrium: \(2P - 1 = 1800 - 4P\) ⟹ \(P = 300.17\), \(Q = 599.33\). Because it is a LR equilibrium, this price is min AC.
  2. WTP up $100: new demand \(Q = 1800 - 4(P - 100)\). LR price stays 300.17, so \(Q = 999.33\).
  3. If 10 new firms supply the extra 400 (thousand), each firm makes \(q = 400/10 = 40\) at min AC; its average cost is 300.17 and its total cost is \(P \times q\).

E. Increasing cost: fixed cost depends on n

Typical data: \(MC = q\), \(TVC = 0.5q^2\), so \(AC = 0.5q + FC/q\). By the rule in B (with \(A = 0.5\)): \(q^* = \sqrt{2\,FC}\) and \(\min AC = 2\sqrt{0.5\,FC} = \sqrt{2\,FC} = q^*\). So in LR equilibrium price = each firm's output.

Worked example · written for these notes

Demand \(Q = 6000 - 60P\); LR price $30; \(FC(n) = C \times n/100\).

  1. \(q = P = 30\); \(Q = 6000 - 1800 = 4200\); \(n = 4200/30 = 140\).
  2. \(FC = q^2/2 = 450 = C \times 140/100\), so \(C = 321.43\).
  3. Demand changes and the industry settles with 200 firms: \(FC = 321.43 \times 2 = 642.86\), \(q = \sqrt{2 \times 642.86} = 35.86\), and \(P = 35.86\). More firms, higher price: an increasing-cost industry.

F. Different firms: a table of marginal costs

To produce a total of \(k\) units at least cost, use the \(k\) cheapest units across all firms (this is Chapter 1's "next unit to the best use", run in reverse). In a competitive market each firm produces every unit whose MC is at most the price, so a price that makes them produce exactly \(k\) units lies from the \(k\)-th cheapest MC up to (not including) the \((k+1)\)-th.

Example · written for these notes

Firm 1's MCs: 4, 6, 9, 13; Firm 2's: 5, 8, 12, 15. For 5 units: cheapest are 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 (Firm 1: 3 units, Firm 2: 2). Price from $9 up to (not including) $12.

14Summary

  • Competitive firm: similar product, many small sellers ⇒ price taker; demand for one firm's output is horizontal at the market price, so \(MR = P\).
  • Short run: some factors fixed; long run: none fixed, firms can enter or exit. \(TC = FC + VC\).
  • Marginal product = output(n) − output(n−1); with a fixed factor it eventually falls (diminishing returns), so MC rises. \(MC = W/MP\).
  • Hire while \(P \times MP \ge W\) ⟺ produce while \(P \ge MC\). Divisible output: \(P = MC\) on the rising part of MC.
  • Fixed costs never change the short-run choice. Shut down only if \(P \lt \min AVC\) (revenue cannot cover variable cost). Shut-down price = min AVC.
  • MC crosses AVC and AC at their minimum points. Firm's SR supply = MC above AVC.
  • \(\pi = (P - AC)Q\). Enter if \(P \gt \min AC\), exit if \(P \lt \min AC\); LR equilibrium: \(P = \min AC\), zero economic (normal) profit.
  • Economic profit subtracts implicit costs; accounting profit does not. \(\pi = PS - FC\), with \(PS = TR - VC\).
  • LR industry supply: constant cost → flat; increasing cost → up; decreasing cost (clusters) → down. Traced by shifting demand and recording LR equilibria.
  • After a demand rise: SR price, output and profit up with n fixed; LR profit back to 0, more firms, price back to / above / below \(P_0\) by industry type.

15Common mistakes

✗Taking MP as output ÷ workers. ✓ MP is the difference between consecutive rows. ✗Comparing MP with the wage. ✓ Compare \(P \times MP\) with the wage. ✗Changing the hiring or output choice when the fixed cost changes. ✓ Short-run choice is unchanged; only profit moves. ✗Using min AC as the shut-down price. ✓ Shut-down = min AVC (short run); exit = min AC (long run). ✗Taking the smaller root of \(MC = P\). ✓ Use the rising branch (larger root). ✗Profit = \((P - AVC)q\). ✓ Subtract the fixed cost too; keep the minus sign if it is a loss. ✗Industry SR supply slope = 2A. ✓ It is \(2A/n\). ✗"WTP up by $10" read as Q + 10. ✓ Replace P with P − 10 in the demand curve. ✗Constant cost: re-intersecting the new demand with the old SR supply for the LR answer. ✓ That is the SR answer; the LR price stays at min AC. ✗Thinking LR profit is positive after a demand rise. ✓ LR economic profit is always 0. ✗Rounding \(q^*\) before computing the price or n. ✓ Keep full precision (the 2025 key shows what goes wrong).

16Find the mistake

Each "solution" has one error. Find it before opening.

1 · written for these notes

Output with 1–4 workers: 50, 90, 120, 140. Price $2, wage $50. "With 4 workers the average product is 140/4 = 35 boxes, worth 2 × 35 = $70 ≥ $50 per worker, so hire all 4."

Mistake

Use the marginal product. The 4th worker adds only 140 − 120 = 20, worth $40 < $50, so do not hire him. The 3rd adds 30, worth $60 ≥ $50. Answer: 3 workers, 120 boxes.

2 · written for these notes

\(AVC = q^2 - 12q + 50\), \(AC = AVC + 40/q\). "The firm shuts down if the price is below min AC."

Mistake

Short-run shut-down uses min AVC: \((q - 6)^2 + 14\), so the firm shuts down below $14. Min AC is the long-run exit price.

3 · written for these notes

\(MC = 40q + 100\) for each of 50 identical firms. "Industry SR supply: P = 100 + 40Q."

Mistake

Each firm: \(q = (P - 100)/40\); industry \(Q = 50q\). So \(P = 100 + (40/50)Q = 100 + 0.8Q\).

4 · written for these notes

Constant-cost market in LR equilibrium at P = 20, demand \(Q = 500 - 10P\). WTP rises by $5. "New LR price = 25, because demand moved up by 5."

Mistake

Constant cost: the LR price returns to min AC = 20. New demand \(Q = 500 - 10(P - 5) = 550 - 10P\), so LR \(Q = 350\) (up from 300). The price rises only in the short run.

18Concept checks

Written for these notes.

1

A firm's rent doubles. Does its short-run output change?

Answer

No. Rent is a fixed cost: it lowers profit but changes neither MC nor AVC. (In the long run it raises min AC and may push the firm out.)

2

Price is above min AVC but below min AC. Short run? Long run?

Answer

Short run: produce at P = MC, making a loss smaller than the fixed cost. Long run: exit.

3

True or false: in a long-run competitive equilibrium, firms earn zero accounting profit.

Answer

False. They earn zero economic profit; accounting profit is positive by the size of the implicit costs (the owners' normal return).

4

Why does MC cut AVC at AVC's lowest point?

Answer

While the next unit costs less than the average, the average falls; once it costs more, the average rises. The switch happens exactly where MC = AVC.

5

Demand for a decreasing-cost good falls. Compare the new LR price with the old.

Answer

Higher. With a downward-sloping LR supply curve, a smaller industry has higher costs, so the LR price rises (and there are fewer firms).

6

With 40 identical firms, each with \(MC = 8q + 20\), what is the industry SR supply?

Answer

\(P = 20 + (8/40)Q = 20 + 0.2Q\) (for P above the shut-down price).

Q51. At the new long-run equilibrium, the price of X (P2) will be 51A P1, and be 51B P0. Individual firm's production (q2) will be 51C q1, and be 51D q0. Individual firm's profit (π2) will be 51E π1, and be 51F π0.

The paper prints "download-sloping"; read as downward-sloping.

Hint

Short run: n fixed, move down along supply and down each firm's MC. Long run: exit until P = min AC again; constant cost ⇒ back to the start.

Answer

SR: price falls, each firm cuts output along MC, profit becomes negative (π0 = 0). LR: firms exit, price rises back to min AC = P0, each firm back to q0, profit back to 0.

Q50: B, B, B · Q51: A, C, A, C, A, C
