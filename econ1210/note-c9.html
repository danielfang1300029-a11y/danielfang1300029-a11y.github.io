ECON1210 · Introductory Microeconomics · Chapter 9 of 12

Public goods and the tragedy of the commons

Some goods are hard to charge for, and some are not used up when one person enjoys them. Markets handle these goods badly. Public goods (street lights, fireworks) get too little supply, and shared resources (fish, pasture, roads) get overused. This chapter shows how to classify goods, find how much of a public good society should have, and fix overuse of a commons.

0Where this sits

This chapter has three parts:

  1. Classifying goods by two questions: can non-payers be kept out, and does one person's use leave less for others? (sections 1–2 below)
  2. Public goods: why markets under-provide them, how to find the efficient amount by adding everyone's marginal benefits, and how to get people to report their values honestly (sections 3–6).
  3. Common goods: the tragedy of the commons, and three fixes (property rights, permits, taxes) (sections 7–12).

The chapter builds on the marginal rule of Chapter 1 (do one more unit while its marginal benefit is at least its marginal cost) and on externalities from Chapter 8. Maths needed: straight lines, solving a linear equation, and adding up a short table.

1Excludability and rivalry

Why does the government provide street lights?

Street lights are almost always paid for by the government, not sold by companies. To see why, imagine you are a private company that has put up street lights and wants to charge for them. Someone walks down the street and refuses to pay. What can you do? Nothing: you cannot switch the light off for one passer-by while it shines for everyone else. There is no practical way to exclude her from the light.

Excludability

A good is non-excludable when people who don't pay cannot easily be prevented from using it.

A good is excludable when people who don't pay can easily be prevented from using it (a shop keeps the jeans until you pay; a cinema checks tickets).

Sort a list of goods by excludability:

Excludablejeans, cheeseburger, paper books, gasoline, cinemas, private parks, satellite television — and e-books and digital music (marked in red: see below).
Non-excludablenational defence, air in open space, lighthouse, timber in public land, bluefin tuna in the open ocean — and again e-books and digital music (red).

Notes on the tricky items:

  • Air and tuna: the qualifiers in open space and in the ocean matter. Bottled air or tuna in a supermarket can be excluded; air outdoors and fish in the open sea cannot. The same item can fall on either side depending on where and how it is supplied.
  • E-books and digital music sit on both sides. A seller can protect them with logins and copy protection (excludable), but once a file is copied and shared online, stopping non-payers is very hard (close to non-excludable).
  • Lighthouse: every ship nearby sees the light whether or not its owner paid.
  • Timber in public land: anyone can walk in and cut a tree.

Would you want to exclude anyone?

Now suppose you run the street lights to make society as well off as possible. Should you stop anyone from using the light? No: one more person walking under the light does not make it dimmer for anyone else. Letting them use it adds benefit and costs nothing. That idea is rivalry.

Rivalry

A good is rival when one person's use of it reduces the ability of another person to use the same good (if I eat the cheeseburger, you can't).

A good is non-rival when one person's use does not reduce another person's ability to use it (my watching a fireworks show doesn't stop you watching it).

In words: rivalry asks "does my use leave less for you?" Excludability asks "can you be stopped if you don't pay?"

Now sort the same goods by rivalry:

Rivaljeans, cheeseburger, paper books, bluefin tuna, gasoline, timber in public land.
Non-rivalnational defence, air in open space, lighthouse, e-books, digital music, satellite television.

Cinemas and private parks are left out of that sort. They are non-rival while there is spare room, but become rival once they are crowded (your seat is one I can't use). Economists call this congestible. Going further

A paper book is rival (only one person can read that copy at a time). An e-book is non-rival (my download doesn't stop yours).

Answer: why the government provides street lights
  • A private provider has little incentive to provide them, because they are non-excludable: it cannot collect payment from users.
  • It is socially efficient to let everyone who values them use them, because they are non-rival: an extra user costs nothing.

Ready answer · the 2 × 2 box

Rival + excludable = private good. Rival + non-excludable = common resource. Non-rival + excludable = club good. Non-rival + non-excludable = public good.

In words: public goods and common resources are both open to everyone; the difference is whether using them uses them up. Strategy C9.1.

What markets do with each type:

  • Private goods: markets work well (Chapters 3–6).
  • Public goods: under-provided, because nobody can be made to pay (§3).
  • Common resources: over-used, because nobody can be stopped and each user leaves less for others (§7).

Club goods

Club good

Excludable but non-rival. Netflix is the standard example: you need an account (excludable), but your streaming a film doesn't stop anyone else streaming it (non-rival).

  • Excludable → markets can provide these goods, because the seller can charge.
  • Non-rival → but markets do not provide them at the socially efficient level. Once the show exists, an extra viewer costs nothing, so efficiency would let everyone who values it watch. A price shuts out people who value it above zero but below the price.
  • New ways to profit from non-rival goods without exclusion: YouTube lets anyone watch for free and earns money from the advertisements shown alongside.
Try it: classify a good

Ask the two questions in order: rival? excludable? Then read the box. Items and answers follow the sorts above.

3Public goods and the free-rider problem

Public good

A good that is non-excludable and non-rival.

  • Non-excludable → people have no incentive to pay (they get it anyway), so firms have no incentive to provide it.
  • Non-rival → even if a market did provide it, charging a price would keep out people whose use costs nothing, so market provision would not be socially efficient.

In words: leave a public good to private markets and problems arise: too little of it gets made.

Example: the security device

Jones, Smith and Patrick are the only three residents of a village. A security device would alert all of them to trespassers on their properties. It costs $100. It is worth $20 to Jones, $50 to Smith and $60 to Patrick.

  • Would anyone buy it alone? No. For each person the cost ($100) is higher than their own value ($20, $50, $60).
  • Is buying it together efficient? Yes. The device is non-rival, so all three enjoy it at once. Total value \(20 + 50 + 60 = 130 \gt 100\) = cost.
  • How much should each pay? This is left open. Any split works if each pays at most their own value and the payments add up to $100 (for example Jones $10, Smith $40, Patrick $50). But agreeing on one split is hard, which leads to the next point.
Challenges facing private provision
  • Communication costs: getting everyone together to agree takes time and effort.
  • Bargaining problems: people argue about who should pay how much.
  • The free-rider problem (below).

It is often impractical for private citizens to negotiate a joint purchase, especially when large numbers of people are involved.

Free riders

Free rider

A free rider enjoys the benefits of a public good without paying a share of the costs.

  • Free riders can disrupt market efficiency. When many people are involved, each person's contribution is an insignificant part of the total required.
  • So each has an incentive to hold back, hoping that others will pay.
  • With enough free riders, public goods will be under-provided.
Security device again, now costing $40

Values unchanged: Jones $20, Smith $50, Patrick $60. Now Smith and Patrick could each afford it alone (50 > 40, 60 > 40). Yet each may hold off, hoping the other will install it: if Patrick installs it, Smith enjoys a $50 benefit for free. If both wait, nobody installs it, even though one installation would make the village $130 − $40 = $90 better off.

Analogy

A group project where everyone shares the grade. Each member is tempted to let the others do the work. If everyone thinks that way, the project doesn't get done.

The role of government

  • Left to private markets, public goods are under-provided → a reason for government provision.
  • Political agreement must be reached on how public purchases are financed, often using tax revenue. Taxes are compulsory, so nobody can free-ride on the payment.
  • Hong Kong examples: the High Island Reservoir and the Hill Road Flyover.
  • Which leaves one question: how much of a public good should the government provide? That is the next section.

4The efficient quantity: add marginal benefits vertically

Marginal social benefit and cost

Marginal social benefit (MSB): the extra benefit to the whole society (everyone added up) from one more unit.

Marginal social cost (MSC): the extra cost to society of producing one more unit.

Socially efficient quantity \[ MSB(Q_{\text{efficient}}) = MSC(Q_{\text{efficient}}) \]

In words: provide units as long as the extra unit is worth at least what it costs to society as a whole. Ideally the government provides this quantity, which maximises total social surplus (point A, where the falling MSB curve crosses the rising MSC curve).

Where does the MSB curve come from?

Social benefit = the benefit enjoyed by everyone in society. For a public good, every person consumes the same quantity: one fireworks show is watched by John and Steve together. So the key insight is:

Vertical aggregation \[ MSB(Q) = MB_1(Q) + MB_2(Q) + \dots \]

In words: at each quantity, the value to society of one more unit = the sum of what each person would pay for that extra unit. On the graph you stack the curves on top of each other (add heights), which is why it is called vertical summation.

If someone's MB formula turns negative, use 0 for that person instead: nobody gets negative value from more fireworks here, they just stop caring.

Vertical vs horizontal

For a private good (Chapter 3) you add demand curves horizontally: at each price, add the quantities, because each buyer gets their own units. For a public good you add vertically: at each quantity, add the prices (willingness to pay), because everyone shares the same units.

Worked example · John and Steve's fireworks, with a kink

\(MB_{\text{John}} = 10 - Q\) and \(MB_{\text{Steve}} = 5 - Q\), where \(Q\) is the number of fireworks.

  1. At \(Q = 1\): John would pay 9, Steve 4, so \(MSB(1) = 4 + 9 = 13\).
  2. Steve's MB hits zero at \(Q = 5\). Beyond that, only John cares.
  3. So \[ MSB = \begin{cases} 15 - 2Q & \text{if } Q \le 5 \\ 10 - Q & \text{if } 5 \lt Q \le 10 \end{cases} \]
  4. Three key points: A = (0, 15) on the vertical axis; the kink B = (5, 5); C = (10, 0) where John's MB reaches zero.
  5. MC constant at $11. The height at the kink is 5, below 11, so the MC line cuts the upper (steep) part: \(15 - 2Q = 11\), giving \(Q^* = 2\).

In words: left of the kink both people want more, so their slopes add (steeper line); right of the kink only one person still wants more.

A case with no kink

\(MB_{\text{John}} = 15 - 1.5Q\), \(MB_{\text{Steve}} = 10 - Q\). Both reach zero at \(Q = 10\), so there is no kink: \(MSB = 25 - 2.5Q\) all the way. At \(Q = 1\): \(13.5 + 9 = 22.5\).

Ready answer · two linear MBs \(a_1 - b_1 Q\), \(a_2 - b_2 Q\)
  1. A = \(a_1 + a_2\).
  2. Choke points \(a_1/b_1\) and \(a_2/b_2\). The smaller one is the kink's x-coordinate; the kink's height is the other person's MB there.
  3. C = the larger choke point.
  4. Optimum: if MC is above the kink height, solve \((a_1 + a_2) - (b_1 + b_2)Q = MC\); if below, solve the remaining person's line = MC.

In words: find the kink first, then check which piece the MC line hits. Strategy C9.2.

Exercise: optimal streetlight provision

Worked example · streetlights

Ten residents. Six are each willing to pay $30 for each additional streetlight, three are each willing to pay $90, and one is willing to pay $80. The marginal cost of the \(x\)-th streetlight is \(10x\). What is the socially efficient number?

  1. Each resident's MB is constant (the same for every extra light). Stack them: \[ \begin{aligned} MSB &= 6(30) + 3(90) + 1(80) \\ &= 530. \end{aligned} \]
  2. \(MSC = 10x\).
  3. \(MSB = MSC\): \(530 = 10x\), so \(x = 53\).

In words: when everyone's value per unit is constant, the MSB is a flat line at (number of people × value each), added over the groups. Strategy C9.3.

Exam twists on this form

Groups given as fractions ("three-eighths of 2000 citizens") → turn them into head-counts first. An extra cost per unit (an environmental cost) → add it to MC. MC with an intercept (\(10000 + 47100Q\)) → keep the intercept.

5Challenges facing government provision

Forced riders

One quantity is chosen for everyone, but people want different amounts. Some want more, some want less, some want none at all.

Forced rider

Someone who pays a share of the costs of a public good (through taxation) but who does not enjoy the benefits.

In the John and Steve example, Steve's MB is zero beyond 5 fireworks. If MC is low enough that the efficient quantity \(Q_e\) is above 5 (say MC = 3, so \(10 - Q = 3\) and \(Q_e = 7\)), the extra fireworks are worth nothing to Steve. If his taxes help pay for them, he is a forced rider for those units.

How does the government learn people's values?

  • In practice, the government doesn't know how much each person values the good.
  • If it simply asks, will citizens report their marginal benefit truthfully? Not if their answer affects what they pay: then people under-report to pay less (free-riding again). And if the answer costs them nothing (marginal private cost = 0), they may over-report to get more of the good.

6The VCG mechanism and donations

The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism is a solution to the free-rider problem. A mechanism is a set of rules: what people report, and what happens and who pays as a result.

  • VCG ensures truth-telling: everyone's best move is to report their true value.
  • With truthful reports we learn the total valuation of the public good,
  • and so we can decide on the optimal amount to provide.
VCG rules for the security device

Cost $100; true values Jones $20, Smith $50, Patrick $60.

  1. Each person reports a valuation.
  2. If the sum of reported valuations is more than $100, the device is installed.
  3. If it is installed, each person pays \(\$100 - \) (sum of the others' reports).

Why nobody lies

  • If my value \(\lt 100 - \) (others' reports), I don't want it installed: paying my share would cost more than it is worth to me.
  • If my value \(\gt 100 - \) (others' reports), I want it installed.
  • In both cases, reporting my true value produces the outcome I want.
  • And my payment doesn't depend on my own report at all — only on the others'. So exaggerating or understating can't lower my payment; it can only change whether the device gets installed, and only in the wrong direction for me.
The outcome with truthful reports

(Jones is poor; Smith and Patrick are rich.) Reports sum to 130 > 100, so yes, install.

  • Jones pays \(100 - (50 + 60) = -10\): he receives $10.
  • Smith pays \(100 - (20 + 60) = 20\).
  • Patrick pays \(100 - (20 + 50) = 30\).

Payments total \(-10 + 20 + 30 = 40\). The rest of the cost, $60, is financed by other tax revenue (unrelated to the reports).

Why VCG works

Individual incentives line up with society's. Each person's payoff (if installed) is

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{own value} \\ &\quad - (\text{cost} - \text{others' values}) \\ &= \text{total value} - \text{total cost}. \end{aligned} \]

In words: under VCG each person's gain equals society's net gain, so wanting the best for yourself means wanting the best for society. Here everyone's payoff is \(130 - 100 = 30\).

Try it: can lying ever help you under VCG?

The other two report truthfully. The line shows your payoff for every possible report; the dot is your current report. No report beats telling the truth (the dashed line marks your true value).

Extra · the catch

VCG usually doesn't collect enough to cover the cost (here only $40 of $100), so the shortfall must come from general taxes. Real governments rarely use it for big projects; they rely on votes, surveys and cost–benefit studies instead.

Donations

Another way public goods get provided: voluntary donations. Some environmental NGOs are funded by donations, as are churches and temples. People feel good about contributing to a public good (economists call this the warm glow), and to encourage giving, donors are sometimes given life-long honours (a building named after them, a plaque).

7Common resources and the tragedy of the commons

Back to the 2 × 2 box: the remaining type is the top-right corner.

Common resource

A good that is non-excludable but rival.

  • Consumers cannot be excluded from using it.
  • But when someone uses it, there is less for everyone else.

So there is a strong incentive to use the resource before others do.

Tragedy of the commons

The tendency for an un-owned common resource to be overused and under-maintained. ("Commons" was the old English word for village pasture that every villager could graze animals on.)

Example: fish. Fish in public waters are not owned, so it is hard to stop anyone fishing (non-excludable). When one person catches a fish, fewer fish are left for everyone else (rival). So each person has an incentive to fish before others.

8Raising cattle: individual choice vs the social optimum

A village has 5 residents, each with $100 of savings. Each can either

  1. buy a government bond for $100 that pays 11% interest: $11 a year, for sure; or
  2. buy a year-old cow for $100, graze it on the commons for a year, then sell it as a two-year-old.

The more cattle share the grass, the thinner each one grows and the lower its selling price:

Gain per cattle = selling price − $100 purchase price. Gain of the herd = number × gain per cattle. Social marginal profit = how much the herd's gain rises when one more animal is added.

Individual decisions

The bond pays $11, so the opportunity cost of investing in a cow is $11. A villager sends a cow if and only if the gain per cow is larger than $11.

  • With 4 cattle each earns $12 > $11, so the 4th villager joins.
  • A 5th would earn $10 < $11, so the 5th buys the bond.

Individually: 4 cattle. Village income = \(11 + 4 \times 12 = \$59\).

The social optimum

Now ask what maximises the village's total income. Send another cow only if the value of the herd rises by more than $11:

  • 2nd cow: herd gain rises from 20 to 32, by $12 > $11 → send.
  • 3rd cow: rises from 32 to 42, by $10 < $11 → don't.

Socially: 2 cattle. Village income = \(32 + 3 \times 11 = \$65\), which beats $59.

Why individuals overgraze

No individual takes into account that an extra cow eats grass that would otherwise have gone to the cattle already there. The 3rd cow earns its owner $14, but it knocks $2 off each of the other two cows: a $4 loss to the neighbours. To the village it adds only \(14 - 4 = 10\).

Key takeaway: the tragedy of the commons is a type of negative externality (a cost one person's action imposes on others who have no say, Chapter 8).

The two rules side by side \[ \begin{aligned} &\text{Individual: enter while} \\ &\quad \text{average payoff} \ge \text{outside option} \\ &\text{Society: add users while} \\ &\quad \text{marginal payoff} \ge \text{outside option} \end{aligned} \]

In words: each newcomer looks at what they will earn (the average across users). Society looks at how much the total rises (the marginal), which is lower because the newcomer drags down everyone else.

Going further · the general recipe used in past papers

Let \(p(n)\) be each user's payoff when \(n\) people use the resource, and \(o\) the outside option (bond interest, a wage, the tram, a private clinic). \(n\) must be a whole number.

  1. Free-entry equilibrium: the largest \(n\) with \(p(n) \ge o\).
  2. Social optimum: maximise total income \(n\,p(n) + (N - n)\,o\) for \(N\) people. Equivalently, add the \(n\)-th user while \[ n\,p(n) - (n-1)\,p(n-1) \ge o. \]
  3. Marginal external cost of the \(n\)-th user = the loss it imposes on the other \(n - 1\): \[ MEC(n) = (n-1)\,[\,p(n-1) - p(n)\,]. \]

In words: private payoff − external cost = social marginal payoff. For the 3rd cow: \(14 - 2 \times (16 - 14) = 10\).

When \(p(n) = a - bn\), the marginal payoff is \(a - b(2n - 1)\): it falls twice as fast as the average, just like MB vs AB in Chapter 1.

Worked example · congestion on a hill road

12 residents commute down a hill. The tram always takes 21 minutes. If \(n\) residents drive, each driver takes \(3n + 2\) minutes. Time is worth $10 a minute to everyone. (This "doctors on the hill" form appears on many past papers, strategy C9.4.)

  1. Individually: drive while \(3n + 2 \le 21\), i.e. \(n \le 6.33\). So 6 drive.
  2. Socially: minimise total time \(n(3n + 2) + (12 - n) \times 21\). The \(n\)-th driver adds \(6n - 1\) minutes of driving time (its own trip plus the delay to the others) and saves one tram trip of 21 minutes. Add drivers while \(6n - 1 \le 21\), i.e. \(n \le 3.67\). So 3 drive.
  3. Total time: 246 minutes with 6 drivers, 222 minutes with 3. A policy that achieves the optimum saves 24 minutes × $10 = $240 a day.

Cost is time here, so "payoff" is minus the time. Same logic, with the inequalities flipped.

9More tragedies of the commons

TreesEach tree cutter knows a tree left standing this year will be bigger, and so more valuable, next year. But he also knows that if he doesn't cut it now, someone else will.
BlackberriesEach picker knows the berries would taste better after another week of ripening. But berries not eaten today may not be there next week.
Southern bluefin tunaData from the Commission for the Conservation of Southern Bluefin Tuna show the annual catch falling from roughly 80 thousand tonnes around 1960 to under 10 thousand tonnes by the 1990s: the stock was fished down.
PollutionClean air and water are a commons. Each polluter has no incentive to take into account the cost his pollution imposes on others.
WhalesSeveral species were hunted to near extinction because international property laws were too weak to restrain each hunter's incentive to kill whales.

The last point is general: one continuing source of inefficiency in modern economies is resources that no single nation's property laws and regulations can govern (the open ocean, the atmosphere).

Exam carry-over from Chapter 8 · strategy C9.6

A few Chapter-9 finals mix in externality questions in the pollution setting:

  • Integer abatement. If treating \(n\) tons of wastewater costs \(n^2\), the \(k\)-th ton costs \(k^2 - (k-1)^2 = 2k - 1\) to treat. Unregulated, the factory treats nothing (treating is pure cost to it). Socially, treat the \(k\)-th ton while \(2k - 1 \le\) damage per ton. A per-ton tax on discharge must be at least the treatment cost of the last ton that should be treated.
  • Negotiation costs. Of the listed situations, the cheapest to negotiate is always "sellers and buyers in a perfectly competitive market": prices coordinate them, with no bargaining at all. Two-party deals (a car accident, a pay rise) and group decisions (roommates, juries, a whole city) all involve bargaining.

10Solution 1: property rights

There are three solutions: property rights, permits and taxation.

The difference is ownership

Chicken: owned by farmers, and not endangered. Tuna ("chicken of the sea"): unowned, and endangered. A farmer who owns chickens has every reason to keep breeding them; nobody owns the tuna in the ocean.

Property rights

Property rights are legal rules saying who owns a resource and may use it, sell it or keep others out. Clearly defined property rights are one way to solve the tragedy of the commons: an owner bears the full cost of overuse, so they use the resource carefully.

Example: sharing soft drinks

Once a week, Smith buys a six-pack of cola for his two children and puts it in the fridge. All six cans are always gone on the first day. Jones also buys a six-pack for his two children, but tells them each may drink no more than three. Why does the cola last much longer at Jones's house?

  • Smith's house: each child knows that a can not drunk now will probably be drunk by the sibling. So each has an incentive to drink fast, before the other gets to it.
  • Jones's house: each child's share is fixed at three cans. Property rights are assigned, so the reason to rush is gone, and the children can drink at a slower, more enjoyable pace.

The same idea, owned versions of the earlier tragedies:

  • Tree cutting: privately owned trees are not cut down too early: the owner waits for them to grow.
  • Blackberries: people don't pick blackberries too soon from their own backyard garden.
  • Private pool: people don't dump toxic waste into their own swimming pool.

11Solution 2: permits and licences

Issuing grazing permits

The village sells 2 permits for cattle-grazing at price \(X\) each, aiming for the social optimum of 2 cattle. Villagers decide individually. What should \(X\) be?

A villager now nets (gain per cattle − \(X\)) and buys a permit only if that beats the bond: gain − \(X \gt 11\).

We need the 2nd cow to enter (\(16 - X \gt 11\), so \(X \lt 5\)) and the 3rd not to (\(14 - X \le 11\), so \(X \ge 3\)).

A permit price between 3 and 5 gives exactly two cattle.

You may also see \(3 \lt X \le 5\). Under the strict rule (enter if and only if gain − X > 11), \(X = 5\) would leave the 2nd villager exactly indifferent and \(X = 3\) would leave the 3rd indifferent, so the strict reading is \(3 \le X \lt 5\). The two end points are indifference points; past papers word the boundaries in different ways ("greater than … and less than …", "at least … at most …"), but the two numbers are always the same.

Fishing licences

Fishing licences limit how much fish can be caught. New Zealand introduced Individual Transferable Quotas (ITQs) in 1986: each holder may catch a fixed amount and can sell that right. ITQs are like tradable pollution permits (Chapter 8), but for catching fish. New Zealand's annual catch rose from under 100 thousand tonnes around 1980 to around 600 thousand tonnes in the 2000s after ITQs began: the fish stocks are thriving.

12Solution 3: taxation

A tragedy of the commons is a negative externality, and the standard cure for a negative externality is a Pigouvian tax (a tax equal to the external cost, Chapter 8).

Taxing the income from cattle

Same village, but now a 25% tax on the profit from cattle. Villagers choose between the $11 bond (untaxed) and a cow.

Going further · how past papers ask about corrective policies

A per-user fee, toll, licence or tax \(F\) cuts each user's payoff to \(p(n) - F\). A subsidy \(S\) to the outside option raises \(o\) to \(o + S\). Both have the same effect on who enters.

  • New equilibrium for a given fee: largest \(n\) with \(p(n) - F \ge o\). Convert money to minutes (or minutes to money) first in time-cost questions.
  • Fee window for the optimum \(n^*\): the \(n^*\)-th user must still enter and the \((n^* + 1)\)-th must not: \[ \begin{aligned} p(n^*{+}1) - o &\lt F \\ &\le p(n^*) - o. \end{aligned} \] A subsidy window has the same two numbers.
  • Most a policy is worth: total income at the optimum − total income at the free-entry equilibrium. Fees and taxes collected are transfers, so they are not subtracted.

In words: the fee must be big enough to stop the first unwanted user, but not so big that it stops the last wanted one; and the policy is worth at most the income it rescues. Strategy C9.5.

13Summary

  • Excludable: non-payers can be kept out. Rival: my use leaves less for you.
  • Four types: private (rival, excludable), common resource (rival, non-excludable), club (non-rival, excludable), public (non-rival, non-excludable).
  • Public goods are under-provided by markets: free riders, communication costs, bargaining problems. Governments step in, usually funded by taxes.
  • Efficient quantity: \(MSB = MSC\), with \(MSB\) = vertical sum of individual MBs (each MB floored at 0). Two linear MBs give a kinked MSB; check which piece the MC hits.
  • Forced riders pay through taxes for units they don't value. Asking people their values invites lying; the VCG mechanism (pay cost − others' reports) makes truth-telling best, because each person's payoff equals total value − total cost.
  • Common resources are over-used (tragedy of the commons): each user ignores the cost they impose on the others, which is a negative externality.
  • Individuals enter while the average payoff ≥ the outside option; society wants users only while the marginal payoff ≥ the outside option. Free entry gives too many users.
  • Fixes: property rights (ownership), permits/licences/ITQs (limit numbers), taxes or fees (make users pay for the harm). A well-set fee or tax restores the optimum, and the revenue is a transfer, not a loss.

14Common mistakes

✗Mixing up common resources and public goods. ✓ Both are non-excludable; only the common resource is rival. ✗Calling Netflix or cable TV a public good. ✓ Excludable + non-rival = club good. ✗Adding public-good MBs horizontally (adding quantities). ✓ Add vertically: at each Q, add the willingness to pay. ✗Keeping a negative MB in the sum past someone's choke point. ✓ Use 0 for that person. ✗Solving on the steep piece without checking the kink. ✓ If MC is below the kink height, use the single remaining MB line. ✗Using one person's WTP as the social benefit. ✓ Multiply by the number of people in each group and add. ✗Finding the social optimum of a commons with "average payoff = outside option". ✓ That is the free-entry equilibrium; society uses the marginal payoff. ✗Rounding the user count up, or rounding a rounded number again. ✓ Users are whole numbers: for the equilibrium take the largest \(n\) that still satisfies the condition; for the optimum compare neighbouring whole numbers. ✗Forgetting the non-users when computing total income. ✓ Total = users' payoffs + (others × outside option). ✗Subtracting fee or tax revenue when valuing a policy. ✓ Revenue is a transfer; only the implementation cost is a real cost. ✗Comparing a dollar toll with minutes. ✓ Convert using the value of time first.

15Find the mistake

Each "solution" below has one error. Find it before opening.

1

\(MB_1 = 20 - 2Q\), \(MB_2 = 30 - Q\) for a public good. "At \(Q = 12\): \(MSB = (20 - 24) + (30 - 12) = -4 + 18 = 14\)."

Mistake

Person 1's MB is 0 beyond \(Q = 10\), not negative. \(MSB(12) = 0 + 18 = \mathbf{18}\).

2

John \(MB = 10 - Q\), Steve \(MB = 5 - Q\), fireworks. "Add the curves: \(Q = (10 - P) + (5 - P) = 15 - 2P\)."

Mistake

That adds quantities (horizontal summation, for private goods). Fireworks are non-rival, so add the prices: \(MSB = 15 - 2Q\) for \(Q \le 5\) and \(10 - Q\) for \(5 \lt Q \le 10\).

3

20 villagers, commons payoff \(p(n) = 40 - 2n\), outside option $12. "Socially optimal number: \(40 - 2n = 12\), so \(n = 14\)."

Mistake

That is the free-entry equilibrium (average payoff = outside option). Society adds users while the marginal payoff \(42 - 4n \ge 12\), so \(n \le 7.5\): 7 users.

4

Same commons. A fee of $13 per user gets the village to 7 users. "Value of the policy = income at 7 users (338) − fees collected (7 × 13 = 91) − income at 14 users (240) = 7."

Mistake

The $91 of fees stays in the village; it is a transfer. The policy is worth \(338 - 240 = \mathbf{\$98}\), so it pays to implement if it costs less than that.

P1 · kinked MSB

Two neighbours share a garden fountain (diameter \(Q\) metres): \(MB_A = 60 - 2Q\), \(MB_B = 30 - 3Q\), both floored at 0. (a) Find A, the kink B and C. (b) Find the efficient \(Q\) if MC = $20 per metre. (c) If MC = $50 per metre.

Hint

B chokes at \(Q = 10\); the kink height is A's MB there.

Answer

(a) A = (0, 90); B = (10, 40); C = (30, 0). (b) 20 < 40, so the flat piece: \(60 - 2Q = 20\), Q = 20. (c) 50 > 40, so the steep piece: \(90 - 5Q = 50\), Q = 8.

P2 · many payers

A town of 500: 400 residents would each pay $15 per extra kilometre of cycle track, 100 would each pay $40. The marginal cost of the \(y\)-th kilometre is \(200 + 50y\). Efficient length?

Hint

MSB is constant: add the groups.

Answer

\(MSB = 400(15) + 100(40) = 10000\). \(10000 = 200 + 50y\) gives \(y = 196\) km.

P3 · a fishing commons

10 fishers share a bay. If \(n\) fish there, each earns \(50 - 4n\) dollars a day; anyone else earns $18 on land. (a) How many fish under free entry? (b) Socially? (c) A licence fee \(T\) achieves (b) for which \(T\)? (d) Most a licence scheme is worth per day?

Hint

Marginal payoff of the \(n\)-th fisher: \(54 - 8n\).

Answer

(a) \(50 - 4n \ge 18\): 8. (b) \(54 - 8n \ge 18\), \(n \le 4.5\): 4. (c) 5th must stay out: \(T \gt 30 - 18 = 12\); 4th must still enter: \(T \le 34 - 18 = 16\). So \(12 \lt T \le 16\). (d) \(4(34) + 6(18) = 244\) vs \(8(18) + 2(18) = 180\): $64.

17Concept checks

1

The New Year fireworks over Victoria Harbour, watched from the public waterfront. Which type of good?

Answer

Public good: one more viewer doesn't spoil anyone's view (non-rival), and nobody on the waterfront can be stopped from watching (non-excludable).

2

A public road is a public good at 3 a.m. but a common resource at rush hour. Why?

Answer

It is non-excludable either way. At 3 a.m. one more car slows nobody (non-rival); at rush hour every extra car slows the others (rival).

3

Each of \(n\) users of a commons earns \(30 - 3n\). What external cost does the 5th user impose on the others?

Answer

Each of the other 4 loses $3: \(MEC = 4 \times 3 = \$12\).

4

Under the VCG rules of §6, Smith reports $90 instead of his true $50. What happens to his payment?

Answer

Nothing: he still pays \(100 - (20 + 60) = 20\), because his payment depends only on the others' reports. The device is installed either way, so lying gains him nothing.

5

Why does Jones's "three cans each" rule make the cola last longer?

Answer

It gives each child property rights over three cans. A can left in the fridge is no longer at risk of being drunk by the sibling, so there is no reason to rush.

6

True or false: a 25% tax on cattle profit makes the village poorer because villagers pay tax.

Answer

False. The tax collected stays in the village. Income rises from $59 to $65 because overgrazing stops.
