Supply, demand and market equilibrium

Why does a lobster cost $6 and not $4 or $8? This chapter builds the two curves that answer almost every "what happens to the price" question in the course. Demand records what buyers will pay and supply records what sellers will accept. Where the two cross, the market settles at the point that makes the total gain from trade as large as possible.

1Markets: gains from exchange and who gets them

A basis for exchange

Paul is a house painter whose roof needs replacing. Ron is a roofer whose house needs painting. Each can also do the other's job. The time each needs:

Painting a houseRoofing a house
Paul300 hrs400 hrs
Ron200 hrs100 hrs

Should Paul roof his own house, and should Ron paint his own?

  • Doing it yourself: Paul spends 400 hours roofing his house and Ron spends 200 hours painting his.
  • Exchanging: Paul paints Ron's house (300 hours) and Ron roofs Paul's (100 hours). Paul saves 400 − 300 = 100 hours and Ron saves 200 − 100 = 100 hours.

The swap was one painting job for one roofing job, so the terms of trade (how much of one thing is exchanged for one unit of the other) were 1 painting per roofing. Ron is faster at both jobs, yet both men gain. This is comparative advantage, the topic of Chapter 2. That leaves the question this chapter answers: what determines the terms of trade?

Gains from trade, and how they are split

John is willing to pay $10 for an apple. Mary can produce one at a cost of $4. If they trade, together they gain \(10 - 4 = \$6\). Trade creates a gain; the question is how to split it. At a price of $7, John gains $3 and Mary gains $3. At $9, John gains $1 and Mary $5. The price decides the split.

The split is all about bargaining power

Bargaining power is the ability to push the price your way. It depends on how many people are on each side:

BuyersSellersWho has the powerMarket structure
OneManyThe single buyer (very much); sellers negligibleMonopsony (one buyer)
ManyOneThe single seller (very much); buyers negligibleMonopoly (one seller, Chapter 11)
ManyManyNobody: each side negligiblePerfect competition

When we know we have negligible bargaining power, we have no choice but to take the price as given. A person or firm who takes the market price as given is called a price taker.

Markets and prices

Market

The market for any good or service consists of all buyers and sellers of that good or service, both actual (trading now) and potential (who would trade at some price).

Think of Shau Kei Wan Market or Sai Kung Pier Market: many fishmongers, many shoppers. Supply (the sellers) and demand (the buyers) jointly determine the market price, but each participant is too small to affect it on their own. One shopper who refuses to buy squid does not move the squid price. This chapter studies these perfectly competitive markets, using a running example: the market for lobsters in Portland, Maine, on 20 July 2004.

2The demand curve

Demand curve

The demand curve shows the relationship between the price of a good and the amount buyers are willing and able to purchase at that price (the quantity demanded). Economists draw it with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis.

The lobster demand curve is a straight line through (1, 10) and (4, 4), with quantity in thousands of lobsters per day. As an equation:

\[ P = 12 - 2Q \quad (Q \text{ in 1000s per day}) \]

In words: every extra 1000 lobsters bought a day corresponds to a price $2 lower.

A demand curve can be read in two directions, and exam questions use both.

Horizontal readingStart from a price and go across to the curve: how much buyers want at that price. At $4 a lobster, buyers together want 4000 a day.
Vertical readingStart from a quantity and go up to the curve: the most the marginal buyer (the next buyer in line) would pay for one more unit. At 4000 a day, the next lobster is worth at most $4 to anyone not yet served.
Read the lobster demand curve both ways

The lobster demand curve, P = 12 − 2Q, drawn between 1000 and 5000 lobsters a day.

Ready answer · reading a linear demand

Written as \(Q = a - bP\): plug in a price to get the quantity demanded. Set \(Q = 0\) to get the choke price \(a/b\): the highest willingness to pay of anyone in the market, the price at which demand is choked off to zero. Example: \(Q = 260 - 50P\) gives a choke price of \(260/50 = 5.20\).

Written as \(P = c - dQ\): plug in a quantity to get the marginal buyer's willingness to pay; the choke price is \(c\).

In words: the curve's top end, where it meets the price axis, is the most anyone would pay.

Why do demand curves slope downwards?

  1. Buyers value the good differently. At a high price only those with a high willingness to pay buy. At a lower price those with a lower willingness to pay join in.
  2. Each buyer buys less as the price rises, for two reasons:
    • Substitution effect: when the good becomes more expensive, people switch to substitutes (goods that do the same job, such as crab instead of lobster).
    • Income effect: when the good becomes more expensive, people can't afford as much of it. The same money buys fewer units, so they are effectively poorer.

3Consumer surplus

Consumer surplus (CS)

Consumer surplus is the buyer's gain from exchange: the economic surplus (Chapter 1) that buying gives the buyer.

\[ \text{CS of one buyer} = \text{WTP} - \text{price paid} \]

In words: consumer surplus = the most you would have paid − what you actually paid.

Total consumer surplus is the sum of the consumer surplus of all buyers.

Example · the concert ticket

Your roommate bought a concert ticket for $600. She would have been willing to pay $1,000. Her consumer surplus: (a) $600 (b) $400 (c) $1600 (d) $1400?

Answer

\(1000 - 600 = \$400\): (b).

The same idea applies to sellers. In a past-paper version, Maggie lists her textbook at $211 but would accept anything over $59, and Brian pays $134. Maggie's gain is a producer surplus (§6) of \(134 - 59 = \$75\). The $211 she asked for is irrelevant.

Eight buyers, one unit each

A hypothetical market has 8 potential buyers, each wanting at most one unit. Their reservation prices (another name for willingness to pay: the highest price at which they would still buy) are $20, $18, $16, … down to $6. Lined up from highest to lowest, they form a staircase demand curve. Each step is one buyer, its height is that buyer's WTP, and its width is one unit.

At a price of $12, the buyers with WTP of at least $12 buy. Their surpluses are \(20 - 12 = 8\), \(18 - 12 = 6\), \(16 - 12 = 4\), \(14 - 12 = 2\), plus 0 for the $12 buyer, who is indifferent:

\[ \text{Total CS} = 8 + 6 + 4 + 2 = \$20 \]
Staircase demand: consumer surplus is the part of each step above the price

The eight buyers (WTP 20, 18, …, 6). Green bars are buyers who buy at this price.

Consumer surplus as an area

With many buyers the steps become a smooth line, and total consumer surplus becomes the area below the demand curve and above the price, up to the quantity bought.

Worked example · cashews at $8 a pound

The demand for cashews runs in a straight line from $12 (at quantity 0) to quantity 12,000 pounds a day (at price 0): \(P = 12 - Q\), with \(Q\) in thousands of pounds. How much do buyers gain at a price of $8?

  1. Quantity bought: \(8 = 12 - Q\), so \(Q = 4\), i.e. 4000 pounds a day.
  2. The CS area is a right-angled triangle with vertical side \(h = 12 - 8 = \$4\) per pound and horizontal side \(b = 4000\) pounds a day.
  3. \(CS = \tfrac12 b h = \tfrac12 \times 4000 \times 4\).

CS = $8,000 per day

Consumer surplus with a straight demand line \[ CS = \tfrac12 \times Q \times (\text{choke price} - P) \]

In words: half × quantity bought × the gap between the top of the demand curve and the price.

For a price change, the change in CS is the trapezium between the two prices: \(\tfrac12 \times (Q_{\text{old}} + Q_{\text{new}}) \times (\text{price drop})\).

4Market demand: add quantities, not prices

Given two individual demand curves, how do we get the market demand curve (the demand of everyone together)?

Horizontal summation

Fix a price and add the quantities demanded. Repeat with other prices.

\[ Q_{\text{market}}(P) = Q_A(P) + Q_B(P) \]

In words: at each price, market quantity = what A wants + what B wants. We add sideways (horizontally) because the quantities are on the horizontal axis.

Take two buyers. Buyer A's demand runs from price 100 down to quantity 1000; buyer B's from price 50 down to quantity 500. As equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} A&: P = 100 - 0.1Q_A \;\Leftrightarrow\; Q_A = 1000 - 10P \\ B&: P = 50 - 0.1Q_B \;\Leftrightarrow\; Q_B = 500 - 10P \end{aligned} \]

At a price of 30, A wants 700 and B wants 200, so the market wants 900.

Above a price of 50, B buys nothing. B's quantity is zero, not negative. So the market curve has two pieces:

\[ Q_{\text{market}} = \begin{cases} 1000 - 10P, & 50 \le P \le 100 \\ 1500 - 20P, & 0 \le P \lt 50 \end{cases} \]

The pieces meet at a kink at \(P = 50\), \(Q = 500\). There the line bends and becomes flatter, because from that price down two buyers respond to each price cut instead of one.

Build the market demand: slide the price, add the two quantities
Demand ADemand BMarket demand (sum)

The two buyers' curves. Drag the price above 50 and B's dot disappears: B drops out of the market, so the market curve is just A's.

Two classic errors

Adding prices. \(P = (100 - 0.1Q) + (50 - 0.1Q) = 150 - 0.2Q\) is meaningless: it would say the two buyers together pay 150 for one unit. Always rewrite each curve as \(Q = \dots\) and add the quantities.

Negative quantities. At \(P = 60\), B's formula gives \(500 - 600 = -100\). B simply buys 0, so the market quantity is \(400\), not 300.

The three things exams ask about a kinked market demand

Ready answer · two linear demands

1. The kink points. Top point A: the higher choke price (100, at \(Q = 0\)). Kink B: at the lower choke price (50), with \(Q\) = the other group's quantity there (500). Bottom point C: the sum of both quantities at \(P = 0\) (1500).

2. Quantity at a price. Add only the groups whose choke price is above that price.

3. Willingness to pay of the marginal unit at a total quantity. First find which piece that quantity is on by comparing it with the kink quantity, then read that piece vertically. At total \(Q = 300\) (below 500): A alone, \(P = 100 - 0.1 \times 300 = 70\). At \(Q = 900\): both, \(P = (1500 - 900)/20 = 30\).

In words: find the kink first; every question is then about which side of the kink you are on. Strategy C3.2.

Worked example · consumer surplus with two groups

Same two buyers, price 30. Total consumer surplus?

  1. Each active group gets its own triangle. A: \(\tfrac12 \times 700 \times (100 - 30) = 24\,500\).
  2. B: \(\tfrac12 \times 200 \times (50 - 30) = 2\,000\).
  3. Add: \(24\,500 + 2\,000\).

CS = 26,500

The tempting single triangle \(\tfrac12 \times 900 \times (100 - 30) = 31\,500\) is wrong: it pretends the market curve is one straight line from the top, but it bends at the kink. Adding each group's own triangle is quicker and always right.

5The supply curve

Supply curve

The supply curve shows the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity supplied: how much sellers are willing and able to sell at that price.

The lobster supply curve is a straight line through (2, 4) and (5, 10), again with quantity in thousands a day:

\[ P = 2Q \quad (Q \text{ in 1000s per day}) \]

In words: each extra 1000 lobsters a day needs the price to be $2 higher.

Horizontal readingAt $4 a lobster, sellers wish to sell 2000 a day: how much suppliers are willing and able to sell at that price.
Vertical readingIf sellers are selling 2000 a day, the marginal cost (Chapter 1: the extra cost of one more unit) of a lobster is $4. This is the minimum price at which sellers will sell one more unit.
Read the lobster supply curve both ways

The lobster supply curve, P = 2Q, drawn between 1000 and 5000 lobsters a day.

Why is the supply curve upward sloping?

  • Sellers' costs differ. At a low price only the lowest-cost suppliers produce and sell. At a high price the higher-cost suppliers join in.
  • For one seller, marginal cost rises with output. This is the low-hanging fruit principle from Chapter 2: use your cheapest resources first (the fruit you can reach), so each extra unit needs dearer resources (a ladder, overtime, worse fishing grounds).
Ready answer · reading a linear supply

Supply \(P = c + dQ\): the intercept \(c\) is the lowest price at which anyone sells (the cheapest seller's cost). Below \(c\), nothing is supplied. Written as \(Q = \dots\): plug a price in. Excess demand at an imposed price \(= Q_d - Q_s\) (when positive).

In words: the bottom end of the supply line is the cheapest seller; below that price nobody sells.

6Producer surplus

Producer surplus (PS)

Producer surplus is the seller's gain from exchange:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{PS}_{\text{one seller}} &= \text{price received} \\ &\quad - \text{minimum acceptable price} \end{aligned} \]

In words: producer surplus = what you got − the least you would have accepted (your reservation price, which for a producer is the marginal cost).

Total producer surplus is the sum over all sellers. Graphically it is the area above the supply curve and below the price.

Worked example · oil at $40 a barrel

The supply curve of oil starts at the origin and passes through (60, $40), with quantity in MBD (millions of barrels per day). What is total producer surplus at $40?

  1. Quantity supplied at $40: 60 MBD.
  2. The PS area is the triangle between the price line and the supply curve: width 60, height \(40 - 0 = 40\).
  3. \(PS = \tfrac12 \times 60 \times 40 = 1200\).

PS = $1,200 million per day (since quantity is in millions of barrels)

Producer surplus: area above supply, below price

The oil supply curve, P = (2/3)Q. Each tiny barrel adds price − its marginal cost; adding them all gives the shaded area.

Producer surplus with a straight supply line \[ PS = \tfrac12 \times Q \times (P - \text{supply intercept}) \]

In words: half × quantity sold × the gap between the price and the bottom of the supply curve.

7Market supply

Market supply is built exactly like market demand: fix a price, add the quantities supplied; repeat with other prices.

\[ Q^S_{\text{market}}(P) = Q^S_F(P) + Q^S_G(P) \]

In words: at each price, market supply = what F sells + what G sells, counting only sellers for whom that price is high enough.

Take two sellers: F's supply starts at the origin and reaches (1000, 100); G's starts at price 50 and reaches (500, 100).

\[ \begin{aligned} F&: P = 0.1Q_F \;\Leftrightarrow\; Q_F = 10P \\ G&: P = 50 + 0.1Q_G \;\Leftrightarrow\; Q_G = 10P - 500 \end{aligned} \]

G's formula applies only for \(P \ge 50\); below that G sells nothing.

At price 60: F sells 600, G sells 100, the market 700. Below 50, G sells nothing, so

\[ Q^S_{\text{market}} = \begin{cases} 10P, & 0 \le P \le 50 \\ 20P - 500, & P \gt 50 \end{cases} \]

The kink is at \(P = 50\), \(Q = 500\). Above it the market supply is flatter: two sellers respond to each price rise.

Build the market supply: slide the price, add the two quantities
Supply FSupply GMarket supply (sum)

The two sellers' curves. Below 50, G is not willing to sell at all.

Ready answer · two linear supplies

Quantity at a price: add the groups whose intercept is below that price.
Marginal cost of the \(Q\)-th unit: compare \(Q\) with the kink quantity first. The 300th unit (below 500): F alone, \(MC = 0.1 \times 300 = 30\). The 1000th unit: both, \(1000 = 20P - 500\), so \(MC = 75\).
Producer surplus at a price: add each active seller's own triangle. At 60: \(\tfrac12 \times 600 \times 60 + \tfrac12 \times 100 \times (60 - 50) = 18\,500\).

In words: same three moves as for demand, with "intercept" in place of "choke price". Strategy C3.2.

8Market curves from tables of whole units Going further

The eight-buyer staircase in §3 had one unit per buyer. Exams extend it: each buyer may want several units, and a table gives each buyer's marginal willingness to pay for the 1st, 2nd, 3rd … unit (or each seller's marginal cost of the 1st, 2nd … unit). The rule is the same horizontal summation, done one unit at a time.

Pool, then sort

Market demand: put every entry of every buyer into one list and sort it from highest to lowest. The \(k\)-th number is the marginal willingness to pay for the \(k\)-th unit in the market.

Market supply: pool every marginal cost and sort from lowest to highest. The \(k\)-th number is the marginal cost of the \(k\)-th unit.

At a price \(P\): each buyer buys every unit worth at least \(P\) to them; each seller sells every unit costing at most \(P\).

In words: the market serves the keenest buyers' units first and uses the cheapest sellers' units first, whoever they belong to.

Bars are the pooled values sorted from highest to lowest (blue = Ann, orange = Ben). The market's 5th unit is worth $5 (Ben's 3rd unit), not the "row 5" of anything.

The row trap

"The 5th unit in the market" is not found by reading across row 5 of the table: that row is each person's own 5th unit. Pool everything and count down the sorted list.

Ready answer · price range for exactly k units (supply)

With pooled marginal costs sorted from lowest to highest, \(c_1 \le c_2 \le \dots\), exactly \(k\) units are supplied when

\[ c_k \le P \lt c_{k+1}. \]

In words: the price must cover the \(k\)-th cheapest unit but not the next one. Strategy C3.3.

9Market equilibrium

Why do diamonds cost more than water?

Water keeps us alive; diamonds are ornaments. Yet diamonds cost far more. This old puzzle, the diamond–water paradox, motivates the idea that a price comes from supply and demand together, not from usefulness alone.

Going further · the answer, at the margin

Price reflects the value of the marginal unit (Chapter 1). Water is so plentiful that we use it even for washing cars, so the last litre is worth very little to the marginal user. Diamonds are scarce and costly to mine, so few are sold, and the marginal buyer still values the last one highly. The total benefit of water is enormous, but the price reflects only the value of the last unit.

Curves as sets of "satisfied" price–quantity pairs

Both curves can be restated in a way that makes equilibrium easy to define:

Demand curveThe set of all price–quantity pairs at which buyers are satisfied: at that price they can buy exactly the amount they want.
Supply curveThe set of all price–quantity pairs at which sellers are satisfied: at that price they can sell exactly the amount they want.
Market equilibrium

Equilibrium occurs at the price–quantity pair at which both buyers and sellers are satisfied: where the two curves cross. At that price \(Q_s = Q_d\): the amount consumers want to buy is matched exactly by the amount producers want to sell.

\[ Q_d(P^*) = Q_s(P^*) \]

In words: the equilibrium price \(P^*\) is the one price at which the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers want. The common quantity is the equilibrium quantity \(Q^*\).

Worked example · the lobster market

Demand \(P = 12 - 2Q\), supply \(P = 2Q\) (\(Q\) in thousands a day).

  1. Both are written as \(P = \dots\), so set them equal: \(12 - 2Q = 2Q\).
  2. \(12 = 4Q\), so \(Q^* = 3\): 3000 lobsters a day.
  3. \(P^* = 2 \times 3 = \$6\).
  4. Surpluses: \(CS = \tfrac12 \times 3 \times (12 - 6) = 9\) and \(PS = \tfrac12 \times 3 \times (6 - 0) = 9\), in thousands of dollars.

P* = $6, Q* = 3000 a day; CS = PS = $9,000 a day

The surplus figures extend the straight lines to the axes, which is what exam questions do.

Ready answer · one demand, one supply

1. Put both curves in the same form (both \(P = \dots\) or both \(Q = \dots\)); invert one if needed.
2. Set them equal and solve for \(Q^*\) (or \(P^*\)); substitute back for the other.
3. \(CS = \tfrac12 Q^*(\text{choke} - P^*)\), \(PS = \tfrac12 Q^*(P^* - \text{supply intercept})\).

In words: same form, equate, then two triangles. Keep full precision and round only at the end. Strategy C3.1.

Equilibrium with a kinked curve: guess, solve, check Going further

When one side of the market is the sum of two groups (§4, §7), its curve has two pieces. You must find which piece the other curve crosses.

Worked example

Buyers A: \(P = 120 - Q_A\); buyers B: \(P = 60 - Q_B\). Supply: \(Q = 2P\). Find the equilibrium and total consumer surplus.

  1. Quantities: \(Q_A = 120 - P\) and \(Q_B = 60 - P\). B buys only when \(P \lt 60\) (the kink price).
  2. Guess both groups buy: \((120 - P) + (60 - P) = 2P\) gives \(180 = 4P\), \(P = 45\).
  3. Check: is \(45 \lt 60\)? Yes, so B really is buying and the guess holds. \(Q^* = 2 \times 45 = 90\).
  4. CS: A buys 75 with triangle \(\tfrac12 \times 75 \times 75 = 2812.5\); B buys 15 with \(\tfrac12 \times 15 \times 15 = 112.5\).

P* = 45, Q* = 90, CS = 2925

Same buyers, but supply \(Q = P/2\): both groups would give \(180 - 2P = P/2\), so \(P = 72\). The check fails: at 72, above B's choke price of 60, B buys nothing. Redo with A only: \(120 - P = P/2\) gives \(P^* = 80\), \(Q^* = 40\).

Ready answer · equilibrium with summed curves

Guess the piece (usually "both groups active"), solve, then check the price against the kink. If it fails, solve on the other piece. For surplus, add each active group's own triangle at \(P^*\).

In words: a solution only counts if every group you included really trades at that price. Strategy C3.4.

10Out of equilibrium: excess supply, excess demand, the trading locus

Excess supply and excess demand

Excess supply (also called a surplus): the price is above its equilibrium value, so \(Q_s \gt Q_d\). Excess supply \(= Q_s - Q_d\).

Excess demand (also called a shortage): the price is below its equilibrium value, so \(Q_d \gt Q_s\). Excess demand \(= Q_d - Q_s\).

In words: excess supply = the unsold amount; excess demand = the amount people want but can't get.

In the lobster market: at $8, buyers want 2000 and sellers offer 4000, an excess supply of 2000. At $4, buyers want 4000 and sellers offer 2000, an excess demand of 2000.

Above equilibriumSellers are not selling as much as they want. The impulse of a dissatisfied seller is to cut her price. The price falls.
Below equilibriumBuyers cannot get the quantities they want. Some dissatisfied buyers offer slightly higher prices. The price rises.

At the equilibrium price of $6, excess demand and excess supply are both exactly zero. Nobody has a reason to change what they are doing.

The trading locus

When the price is not the equilibrium price, the amount actually traded is limited by the short side of the market. Above equilibrium, buyers limit it (you can't sell more than people buy). Below equilibrium, sellers limit it. So

\[ Q_{\text{traded}} = \min(Q_d, Q_s). \]

In words: the quantity traded is the smaller of what buyers want and what sellers offer. On the graph this is the inverted-V shaped trading locus: the supply curve below \(P^*\) and the demand curve above it, peaking at the equilibrium.

Move the price: who is unhappy, and which way does the price go?

The lobster market (quantities in thousands a day). Start at $8, then try $4 and $6.

True/False favourites

The standard false statements swap who acts. "Under excess demand, unsatisfied sellers drive the price down" is false: it is unsatisfied buyers who bid it up. "Under excess supply, unsatisfied buyers drive the price up" is false: it is unsatisfied sellers who cut it. Also false: "at equilibrium, all potential buyers (or sellers) trade". Only those with WTP ≥ \(P^*\) buy and only those with cost ≤ \(P^*\) sell. Strategy C3.6.

11Efficiency: gains from trade are maximised at equilibrium

Too little: unexploited gains from trade

Take an oil market with equilibrium price $30 and quantity 65 MBD. Suppose only 24 MBD were traded. At \(Q = 24\), the demand curve says the marginal buyer values a barrel at $57, while the supply curve says the marginal seller can produce one for $15. A buyer valuing a barrel more than a seller's cost has not been matched. Every barrel between 24 and 65 is worth more to some buyer than it costs some seller. These are unexploited gains from trade: the red triangle between the curves from 24 to 65.

It is John and Mary's apple again: John values it at $10, Mary's cost is $4. This gain from exchange of $6 is also called the total economic surplus of the exchange. If the trade is not allowed to happen, the $6 is lost.

Too much: wasteful trades

Now suppose 95 MBD were traded. Beyond 65, each barrel costs the seller more than the buyer values it: at 95, cost $50 against value $15. The red triangle between the curves from 65 to 95 is the value of wasted resources.

In apple terms: John values an apple at $4 and Mary's cost is $10. If they are forced to trade, their gain is \(4 - 10 = -\$6\). If the trade is allowed not to happen, they avoid that loss.

Just right

At the equilibrium quantity there are no unexploited gains from trade and no wasteful trades. Total surplus, the consumer surplus triangle plus the producer surplus triangle, is as large as it can be.

"The invisible hand"

At the equilibrium price, the market sorts people automatically. On the demand curve, everyone to the left of \(Q^*\) (WTP above \(P^*\)) is a buyer and everyone to the right is a non-buyer. On the supply curve, everyone to the left (cost below \(P^*\)) is a seller and everyone to the right is a non-seller. No planner chooses who buys or sells; the price does it. Adam Smith called this the invisible hand.

Equilibrium and total surplus

A free-market equilibrium gives two results:

  • Goods are produced at the lowest possible cost (only the cheapest sellers sell).
  • Goods satisfy the highest-valued demands (only the keenest buyers buy).

So total surplus (CS + PS) is maximised, and the market equilibrium quantity is socially optimal (best for society as a whole). The condition: all relevant production costs are borne by the sellers, and all relevant benefits go to the buyers. When they are not, as with pollution, Chapter 8 (externalities) shows that the market gets it wrong.

\[ \text{Total surplus} = CS + PS \]

In words: the total gain from trade = buyers' gain + sellers' gain; it equals the area between the demand and supply curves up to the quantity traded.

Equilibrium Q* = 40, P* = 60, total surplus 1600. Below 40: lost gains (a triangle to the left of Q*). Above 40: wasted resources (a triangle to the right). Assumes the units that are traded go to the keenest buyers and cheapest sellers.

Deadweight loss (welfare loss)

The deadweight loss (DWL, also "welfare loss") of a quantity \(q\) is the total surplus lost compared with the free-market equilibrium: the area between the demand and supply curves from \(q\) to \(Q^*\), whichever side \(q\) is on.

\[ DWL = \tfrac12 \times |Q^* - q| \times \big|P_D(q) - P_S(q)\big| \]

In words: for straight curves, the loss is a triangle: half × how far quantity is from equilibrium × the gap between the curves at the forced quantity.

Worked example · cuts, bans and over-production

Demand \(P = 100 - Q\), supply \(P = 20 + Q\). So \(Q^* = 40\), \(P^* = 60\), \(CS = PS = \tfrac12 \times 40 \times 40 = 800\), total surplus 1600.

  1. Cut by 50% to \(q = 20\): at 20, demand price 80, supply price 40. \(DWL = \tfrac12 \times 20 \times (80 - 40) = 400\).
  2. Forced up to \(q = 60\): at 60, supply price 80, demand price 40. \(DWL = \tfrac12 \times 20 \times (80 - 40) = 400\).
  3. Complete ban (\(q = 0\)): the whole total surplus is lost, \(DWL = 1600\).

400 · 400 · 1600

If one curve is kinked and the forced quantity lies on the other side of the kink, the area is not one triangle. Split it at the kink, or compute total surplus at \(q\) by adding each group's area and subtract it from total surplus at \(Q^*\). Strategy C3.5.

Going further · a licence cap

Past papers also cap the number of licences (for example, for accountants) below \(Q^*\). The cap \(q\) is a ceiling on quantity. Buyers compete for the scarce units, so the price rises to the demand price at the cap. With the curves above and a cap of 20:

  • Price = \(100 - 20 = 80\).
  • \(CS = \tfrac12 \times 20 \times (100 - 80) = 200\).
  • PS = the area between the price 80 and the supply curve from 0 to 20 (a rectangle minus a triangle): \(20 \times (80 - 20) - \tfrac12 \times 20 \times 20 = 1000\). This assumes the licences go to the lowest-cost sellers.
  • DWL \(= 1600 - 200 - 1000 = 400\): the same triangle as the 50% cut.

Sellers can end up better off (1000 > 800), which is why a professional body might lobby for fewer licences. Chapter 7 develops price and quantity controls fully.

12Summary

  • Trade creates gains (Paul and Ron, John and Mary). How they are split depends on bargaining power. With many buyers and many sellers (perfect competition), everyone is a price taker.
  • Demand curve: price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal. Read horizontally for the quantity demanded at a price; vertically for the marginal buyer's WTP at a quantity. It slopes down because buyers' values differ and because of substitution and income effects.
  • Supply curve: read horizontally for the quantity supplied; vertically for the marginal cost (the minimum acceptable price). It slopes up because costs differ and because of low-hanging fruit.
  • CS = WTP − price = the area under demand above the price. PS = price − minimum acceptable price = the area above supply below the price.
  • Market curves = horizontal sums: add quantities at each price, counting only groups that trade at that price. The result is kinked at the second group's intercept. For table data, pool all entries and sort.
  • Equilibrium: \(Q_d = Q_s\); both sides are satisfied. With kinked curves, check that the solution lies on the piece you used.
  • Out of equilibrium: excess supply makes sellers cut the price; excess demand makes buyers bid it up. The quantity traded is \(\min(Q_d, Q_s)\) (the trading locus).
  • Efficiency: the equilibrium maximises total surplus (CS + PS), provided sellers bear all the costs and buyers receive all the benefits. Any other quantity creates a deadweight loss: unexploited gains (too little) or wasted resources (too much).

13Common mistakes

✗Adding demand curves vertically (adding prices). ✓ Rewrite as \(Q = \dots\) and add quantities. ✗Counting a negative quantity for a group whose choke price is below the current price. ✓ That group buys 0. ✗Setting \(P = a - bQ\) equal to \(Q = c + dP\). ✓ Put both curves in the same form first. ✗Solving a kinked equilibrium without checking the price against the kink. ✓ Always check; if it fails, use the other piece. ✗One big triangle for CS under a kinked demand. ✓ One triangle per active group, then add. ✗Reading a total quantity off the wrong piece (marginal WTP of the 35th unit on the two-group piece). ✓ Compare with the kink quantity first. ✗Reading "the 5th unit" across row 5 of a table. ✓ Pool and sort all entries. ✗Excess demand as \(Q_s - Q_d\). ✓ \(Q_d - Q_s\) (positive when the price is too low). ✗Using the asking or listing price in a seller's surplus. ✓ Agreed price − reservation price. ✗Rounding \(Q^*\) to 2 decimals before computing surplus. ✓ Keep full precision and round only the final answer.

14Find the mistake

Each "solution" has one error. Find it before opening.

1

Buyers A: \(P = 100 - 0.1Q\), B: \(P = 50 - 0.1Q\). "Market demand: \(P = 150 - 0.2Q\)."

Mistake

That adds prices. Add quantities: \(Q = (1000 - 10P) + (500 - 10P) = 1500 - 20P\) for \(P \lt 50\), and \(Q = 1000 - 10P\) for \(50 \le P \le 100\).

2

Same buyers, price 60. "Quantity demanded = (1000 − 600) + (500 − 600) = 300."

Mistake

B's choke price is 50, so at 60 B buys 0, not −100. Market quantity = 400.

3

Buyers A: \(P = 120 - Q_A\), B: \(P = 60 - Q_B\); supply \(Q = P/2\). "\(180 - 2P = P/2\), so \(P^* = 72\), \(Q^* = 36\)."

Mistake

At 72, B (choke price 60) buys nothing, so the two-group piece is wrong. A alone: \(120 - P = P/2\) gives \(P^* = 80\), \(Q^* = 40\).

4

A and B as in 1, price 30. "CS = ½ × 900 × (100 − 30) = 31,500."

Mistake

The market demand bends at \(Q = 500\), so the area is not one triangle. Per group: \(\tfrac12 \times 700 \times 70 + \tfrac12 \times 200 \times 20 = \mathbf{26\,500}\).

Buyers A: \(Q_A = 80 - P\); buyers B: \(Q_B = 40 - 2P\). Supply \(Q = 2P\). Find the equilibrium and consumer surplus.

Hint

B's choke price is 20. Try both groups, then check the price.

Both: \(120 - 3P = 2P\) gives \(P = 24 \gt 20\), which fails. A only: \(80 - P = 2P\) gives \(P^* = 26.67\), \(Q^* = 53.33\). CS (A only) \(= \tfrac12 \times 53.33 \times (80 - 26.67) = \mathbf{1422.22}\).

P3 · discrete market supply

Supplier 1's marginal costs for units 1–4: 2, 4, 7, 9. Supplier 2's: 3, 5, 6, 10. For what prices are exactly 5 units supplied? At a price of 6.5, how many does each supply?

Hint

Pool and sort: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10.

Answer

The 5th lowest is 6 and the 6th is 7, so \(6 \le P \lt 7\). At 6.5: supplier 1 sells 2 (costs 2, 4); supplier 2 sells 3 (costs 3, 5, 6).

16Concept checks

1

Kit lists his old bike at $300 but would sell for anything above $120. He sells it for $200. What is his surplus, and is it consumer or producer surplus?

Answer

Producer surplus = 200 − 120 = $80. The $300 asking price plays no role.

2

The price is above equilibrium. Who is dissatisfied, and what do they do?

Answer

Sellers: they can't sell all they want (excess supply), so they cut prices until the excess disappears.

3

Why is the market demand curve flatter below a kink?

Answer

Below the kink two groups buy, so a $1 price cut raises quantity by both groups' responses added together.

4

True or false: in equilibrium every potential buyer buys the good.

Answer

False. Only buyers with WTP at least \(P^*\) buy; the rest are non-buyers.

5

A government bans a good completely. What is the deadweight loss?

Answer

The whole total surplus (CS + PS) at the free-market equilibrium, since no trade happens at all.

6

A forced quantity above \(Q^*\) also creates a deadweight loss. Where does the loss come from, when more is being traded?

Answer

Each unit beyond \(Q^*\) costs sellers more to produce than any remaining buyer values it. These are wasteful trades, so total surplus falls.
