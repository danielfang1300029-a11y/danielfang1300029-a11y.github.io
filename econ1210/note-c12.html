Price discrimination

A single-price monopolist leaves money on the table: some buyers would pay more than the price, and others would buy at a price that still covers cost. Price discrimination is the set of tricks a monopolist uses to pick up that money: personal prices, coupons and other hurdles, separate prices for separate groups, cheap printers with expensive ink, and bundles.

0Where this sits

This chapter has two parts:

  1. Monopoly price discrimination in three degrees: first-degree (personalised pricing, §3), second-degree (buyers sort themselves, §4) and third-degree (market segmentation, §5–§6).
  2. Other monopoly pricing strategies: tying (§7) and bundling (§8).

This chapter builds on Chapter 11 (monopoly): a firm that is the only seller, faces the whole downward-sloping market demand curve, and picks its quantity where marginal revenue (MR, the extra revenue from one more unit) equals marginal cost (MC, the extra cost of one more unit). 1Why a single price wastes surplus

The inefficiency of uniform pricing

A uniform price (or single price) means every buyer pays the same price. A single-price monopolist produces where \(MR = MC\), at quantity \(Q^*\), and charges the price \(P^*\) that the demand curve gives at \(Q^*\). The diagram splits the area under the demand curve into three pieces:

The three areas

Consumer surplus (CS): the total of (willingness to pay − price paid) over all buyers. On the graph: the triangle between the demand curve and the price line, up to \(Q^*\).

Producer surplus (PS): the total of (price received − marginal cost) over all units sold. On the graph: the area between the price line and the MC curve, up to \(Q^*\). Fixed costs are not subtracted, so PS = profit + fixed cost.

Deadweight loss (DWL): surplus that nobody gets. These are the units between \(Q^*\) and the efficient quantity \(Q_{eff}\) (where demand meets MC). Buyers would pay more than they cost to make, but they are not produced.

Single-price monopoly: P = 100 − Q
demandMR = 100 − 2QMCCSPSDWL

With MC = 20: MR = 100 − 2Q = 20 gives Q* = 40 and P* = 60. CS = ½·40·40 = 800, PS = 40·40 = 1600, DWL = ½·(80 − 40)·(60 − 20) = 800. The efficient quantity is 80, where demand meets MC.

Can a monopolist do better?

The DWL units are buyers who would pay more than marginal cost. The monopolist would gladly expand production if it could do so without having to sell current output at a lower price. Under a single price it cannot: to sell one more unit it must cut the price for everyone, and that cut on the units already sold is why MR lies below the demand curve.

So the monopolist wants to charge high-value buyers a high price and low-value buyers a low price. Two key requirements:

  • Ability to distinguish consumers: know, or be able to guess, who will pay more.
  • Ability to prevent resale (arbitrage). Arbitrage means buying where a good is cheap and reselling it where it is dear. If low-price buyers can resell to high-price buyers, the high price collapses.

Definition and examples

Price discrimination

The practice of charging different prices to different buyers for the same product.

Some examples:

  • E-commerce platforms that show different prices based on browsing behaviour.
  • Student discounts (an Octopus student fare, cheaper cinema tickets with a student card).
  • Quantity discounts (lower price per unit when you buy more).
  • Digital subscriptions with standard and premium tiers.

2Ram's bowls: the single-price benchmark

Ram is a monopolist in carved wooden bowls in his village, a minor tourist stop in Northern India. Each day 8 tourists visit his shop, and each has a different reservation price for his standard bowl.

Reservation price

The highest price a buyer will pay for one unit: the same as willingness to pay (WTP). The buyer buys if price ≤ reservation price (the papers usually say an indifferent buyer buys).

VisitorABCDEFGH
Reservation price ($)161412108642

The demand curve: lining the visitors up from highest to lowest gives a staircase. At a price of $16 one bowl sells; at $14, two; … at $2, eight. Each step is one buyer.

How many bowls with one price?

Ram must charge the same price to all buyers. He can make as many bowls as he likes at a cost of $3 each (so MC = 3), and bowls are sold only in whole numbers. To sell \(n\) bowls he must set the price at the \(n\)-th highest reservation price. His marginal revenue from the \(n\)-th bowl is

\[ \begin{aligned} MR(n) &= TR(n) - TR(n-1) \\ TR(n) &= n \times P_n \end{aligned} \]

In words: the marginal revenue of a bowl = total revenue selling that many bowls minus total revenue selling one fewer. It is less than the price because the price drops for the earlier buyers too.

Ram's staircase demand and the single-price search

Bars = reservation prices (the demand staircase). To sell n bowls the price must fall to the n-th bar. Orange = Ram's margin above MC on each bowl sold (producer surplus), blue = buyers' surplus, red = bowls worth making that are not sold (deadweight loss).

The reasoning: expand while \(MR \gt MC = 3\). MR of the 1st bowl is 16, the 2nd 12, the 3rd 8, the 4th 4, the 5th 0. The 4th bowl adds 4 > 3; the 5th adds 0 < 3. Sell 4 bowls at $10.

Profit

Total revenue = 10 × 4 = $40/day. Total cost = 3 × 4 = $12/day. Profit = $28/day.

Is 4 bowls efficient?

No. Visitors A–G are all willing to pay more than the $3 cost of a bowl, so it is efficient that each gets one. Visitor H ($2) is not, and should not. The socially efficient number is 7 bowls/day. Under the single price, E, F and G miss out; the lost surplus is \((8-3)+(6-3)+(4-3) = 9\) dollars a day.

Ready answer · single price from a reservation-price table

Try each reservation price as the price \(P\). Profit \(= (\text{number with WTP} \ge P) \times (P - MC) - FC\). Pick the largest. This is the same as "expand while MR > MC". Strategy C12.1.

3First-degree (perfect) price discrimination

Now suppose Ram is a shrewd judge of human nature. After a moment's conversation he can discover each visitor's reservation price, and he charges each visitor exactly that.

Ram with personal prices

Ram sells to visitors A–G, each paying his own reservation price. He will not sell to H, whose $2 is below the $3 cost.

Total revenue = 16 + 14 + 12 + 10 + 8 + 6 + 4 = $70/day. Total cost = 7 × 3 = $21/day. Profit = $49/day (up from $28).

First-degree price discrimination

The seller charges each consumer their own willingness to pay. Also called perfect price discrimination. A monopolist who can do this is a perfectly discriminating monopolist.

Efficiency

  • For a perfectly discriminating monopolist there is no efficiency loss: DWL = 0.
  • All buyers who are willing to pay a price high enough to cover marginal cost are served.
  • In practice, however, virtually no seller knows each buyer's reservation price. Price discrimination does occur, but it is imperfect.

Why efficient? Selling one more unit no longer forces a price cut on the others, so the extra revenue from a unit is just that buyer's own WTP. In other words, MR = the demand curve (MR does not lie below demand). The firm keeps selling until WTP falls to MC: exactly the efficient quantity. But the whole surplus goes to the seller.

Perfect PD in one line \[ \begin{aligned} Q &= Q_{eff}\ (\text{demand} = MC) \\ CS &= 0,\quad DWL = 0 \\ PS &= \text{all of the total surplus} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the perfect discriminator produces the efficient amount but takes every dollar of surplus for itself. Efficient is not the same as fair.

Same market, two regimes: P = 100 − Q, MC = 20

Under perfect PD the firm sells 80 units and its PS is the whole triangle between demand and MC: ½·80·80 = 3200. Under one price it sells 40 (PS 1600, CS 800, DWL 800).

Going further · continuous curves and rising MC

Exams also give perfect PD with formulas, e.g. demand \(P = a - bQ\) and an upward-sloping MC \(= cQ\). Then: find \(Q\) where demand = MC; CS = 0 and DWL = 0; PS = the triangle between the demand line and the MC line from 0 to that \(Q\) (not a rectangle, because MC is not flat). Example 2023 Summer Final Q63: \(P = 130 - 1.5Q\), \(MC = 2Q\) gives \(Q = 130/3.5\) and PS \(= \tfrac12 \times \tfrac{130}{3.5} \times 130 \approx 2414.29\).

4Second-degree discrimination: hurdles and self-selection

Second-degree price discrimination

If the monopolist cannot directly observe buyers' willingness to pay, it can construct a menu of options and let buyers self-select: each buyer picks the option meant for their type.

Common methods:

  • Quantity discounts: lower price per unit for bulk purchases (a 6-pack of drinks costs less per can).
  • Multi-part tariffs: a membership fee plus a usage fee (a gym joining fee + per-class fee).
  • Vertical differentiation: different quality at different prices (economy vs business class).
  • Hurdles: coupons, promo codes and similar. A hurdle is a small hassle that gives a discount only to buyers willing to go through it. Price-sensitive buyers jump; buyers who do not care much about price don't bother.

Ram uses coupons

Ram does not know each buyer's reservation price. But he knows that all buyers with a reservation price above $9 never use discount coupons, and those below $9 use them whenever available. If he puts coupons in a tourist magazine, coupon users pay a discount price; others pay the regular list price. What list price and discount price maximise profit?

The coupon splits his market into two submarkets, and in each he expands output as long as \(MR \gt MC\):

Rebate coupons

A rebate coupon gives money back only if you mail the coupon to the manufacturer (for example, a $1.00 rebate voucher). The hurdle is the hassle of mailing it. Only people for whom $1 matters bother, which is exactly the group the firm wants to give a lower price.

Ready answer · coupon (hurdle) questions

1. Split buyers at the cut-off ("above $X never use coupons").
2. Run the single-price search separately in each submarket (§2).
3. If no coupon user values the good at MC or more, there is no discount sale.
4. "Discount" = list price − discount price. Read whether the blank asks for the discount price or the amount off. Strategy C12.1.

5Third-degree discrimination: segmented markets

Third-degree price discrimination

Segment the market into several submarkets (groups the firm can tell apart) and charge each its own price.

  • If the demand curves in the submarkets are different, it is more profitable to set different prices than a uniform price across all submarkets. (§6 shows the exception: "different" curves that share the same top price.)
  • Arbitrage may limit the firm's ability to set different prices.
  • Examples of submarkets: students, senior citizens, civil servants; specific geographical locations; timing of purchase (lunch vs dinner menus).

EU and UK

Picture two markets, the EU with a steep demand curve D1 and the UK with a flatter one D2. With a constant MC and no fixed cost (so MC = AC, average cost), the firm acts as a separate single-price monopolist in each market: in each, it sets its own MR = MC. The steeper, less price-sensitive EU gets the higher price \(P_{EU}\); the UK gets \(P_{UK}\). Each market has its own CS triangle, PS rectangle and DWL triangle.

Going further · the price formula for a linear group demand

Exams write each group's demand as \(Q = a - bP\). Its choke price (the price at which nobody buys) is \(a/b\). Rewriting it as \(P = \tfrac{a}{b} - \tfrac{Q}{b}\), Chapter 11's rule (MR falls twice as fast as demand) gives \(MR = \tfrac{a}{b} - \tfrac{2Q}{b}\). Setting \(MR = MC\):

\[ \begin{aligned} Q &= \frac{a - b\,MC}{2} \\ P &= \frac{a/b + MC}{2} \end{aligned} \]

In words: a monopoly price on a straight-line demand sits exactly halfway between the choke price and marginal cost; the quantity is half of what would sell at a price equal to MC.

Two shortcuts for straight-line demand with constant MC: CS = DWL = half of the profit in that market; and the efficient quantity is twice the monopoly quantity.

Arbitrage and the price gap Going further

If the EU price is $60 and the UK price $45, a trader could buy in the UK and resell in the EU. Each unit earns the price gap ($15) minus the cost of moving it. So:

Ready answer · arbitrage cost \[ \begin{aligned} &\text{arbitrage unprofitable when} \\ &\text{cost per unit} \ge P_{high} - P_{low} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the price gap is the lowest transport (arbitrage) cost that prevents arbitrage. If moving goods costs more than the gap, the firm can treat the markets as completely separate.

And the two prices are equal exactly when the two choke prices \(a/b\) are equal (with the same MC). Solve "\(a_A/b_A = Y/b_B\)" for an unknown intercept \(Y\). Strategy C12.3.

6Comparison with a uniform price

What if the firm must charge one price to both markets?

The combined demand curve

Add the two demand curves horizontally: at each price, total quantity = EU quantity + UK quantity. Above the UK choke price only the EU buys, so the combined curve D follows D1; below it, both buy, and D is flatter. The combined curve has a kink at the UK choke price.

The matching MR curve

Each straight piece of D has its own MR line (twice as steep). So MR comes in two pieces with a jump at the kink's quantity: at the kink, MR leaps up from the steep piece's MR to the flat piece's MR, because below the kink price a price cut wins a whole new group of buyers.

Three cases

Case 1: exit the UK marketMC is high: it crosses only the first (EU-only) piece of MR. The single price is above the UK choke price, so the UK buys nothing.
Case 2: serve both marketsMC is low: it crosses only the second piece. The single price is below the UK choke price and both markets buy.
Case 3: ???MC lies in the range of the MR jump, so MR = MC at two quantities. Compute the profit at both candidates and keep the larger one.

Same EU and UK as §5. The kink is at P = 70, Q = 7.5. MR jumps from 40 (EU piece) to 66.67 (combined piece). MC below 40: case 2. MC above 66.67: case 1. MC between: case 3. With these curves, serving both wins for MC below 60 (e.g. MC = 50: profit 306.25 vs 156.25), and exiting the UK wins above 60 (e.g. MC = 63: 85.6 vs 60.1).

Worked comparison at MC = 20
  1. Dual price: EU \(P = (100 + 20)/2 = 60\); UK \(P = (70 + 20)/2 = 45\). Profit 400 + 1250 = 1650. CS 200 + 625 = 825.
  2. Single price: below $70 the combined demand is \(Q = 165 - 2.25P\), choke \(165/2.25 = 73.33\). Price \((73.33 + 20)/2 = 46.67\), which is below 70, so both buy (case 2). \(Q = 165 - 2.25 \times 46.67 = 60\). Profit \(60 \times 26.67 = 1600\). CS 355.56 + 544.44 = 900.
  3. Efficient total surplus (price = MC in both): 800 + 2500 = 3300. DWL single = 3300 − 1600 − 900 = 800; DWL dual = 3300 − 1650 − 825 = 825.

Discrimination: profit +50, CS −75, DWL +25.

Remarks
  • The monopoly firm benefits from price discrimination (it can always choose to set equal prices, so it can't do worse).
  • Consumer surplus may increase or decrease.
  • The deadweight loss may increase or decrease. (In case 1, discrimination opens up the UK market and both CS and efficiency can rise.)
The proportional-demand trap

Exams often give \(Q_s = 1000 - 95P\) and \(Q_n = \tfrac{1000}{4} - \tfrac{95}{4}P\): different slopes but the same choke price \(1000/95\). Then the formula gives the same price to both groups, the dual-price and single-price regimes coincide, and every "difference" (profit gain, CS difference) is 0.00. The printed zeros are correct. "Different demand curves" therefore does not mean discrimination is always more profitable.

Ready answer · single vs dual price (strategy C12.2)

1. Dual: each group \(P = (\text{choke} + MC)/2\).
2. Single: add the demands below the lower choke price, apply the same formula to the sum, and check that the price really is below that lower choke (otherwise use the high-value group alone, and compare).
3. Compare profits, CS triangles and DWL = efficient surplus − actual.
4. A group will lobby for the regime that gives it the lower price, and will pay up to its CS difference. A regulator who removes the single-price monopoly's inefficiency gains at most the single-price DWL.

7Tying

Tying

A form of price discrimination in which one good, the base good, is tied to a second good, the variable good. Firms are not just selling individual goods but a package: the printer only works with the maker's ink.

The printer problem: a printer maker would like to charge customers different prices for printing. It knows that high-volume users are less price-sensitive and willing to pay more for printing than low-volume users. Best scenario: charge high-volume users more. But it is hard to tell who is a high-volume user from anything the firm can see when the printer is sold.

The solution: price the base good (printer) low and the variable good (ink) high.

  • Consumers reveal their willingness to pay through how much of the variable good they buy.
  • The firm effectively charges each consumer a different total price, based on usage.
  • Printing a lot? You have a higher willingness to pay, and you pay more in ink.

Other examples: coffee-capsule machines and capsules, razor handles and blades, game consoles and games.

Exam wording

Tying sets the base good low (possibly below cost) and the variable good high (above cost). A statement with it the other way round is false (2022 Spring Final Q55(i)).

8Bundling

Bundling

Requiring products to be purchased together in a bundle (package). Firms use bundling when they have more information on the demand for the bundle than for the individual parts.

Word and Excel

Word$100$40
Excel$20$90
Office (Word + Excel)$120$130

Selling separately (marginal cost zero):

  • Word at $40: both buy, profit $80. Word at $100: only Amanda buys, profit $100. → Word at $100.
  • Excel at $20: both buy, profit $40. Excel at $90: only Yvonne buys, profit $90. → Excel at $90.
  • Total: $190.

Bundling into Office:

  • Office at $130: only Yvonne buys, profit $130.
  • Office at $120: both buy, profit $240. → Office at $120.

By bundling, Microsoft raises profit from $190 to $240. Why it works: the two buyers' tastes are negatively correlated (Amanda loves Word, Yvonne loves Excel). Adding up each buyer's values evens them out ($120 vs $130), so one bundle price captures almost all of both buyers' WTP.

For each good: profit = max{2 × (lower WTP − MC), (higher WTP − MC)}. Add the goods. Bundle: same rule on each buyer's summed WTP, with cost per bundle = 2 × MC + packaging. 9Rationing coupons and "hunger marketing" Going further

Eight past-paper parts (2023 Fall Final, 2025 Spring Supp) ask about a brand that deliberately sets its price too low to create queues ("hunger marketing"), then hands out coupons giving the right to buy. The topic combines the price-ceiling logic of Chapter 7 with this chapter's monopoly and perfect-PD ideas.

Set-up and terms

The firm fixes the price at \(P_0\) and sells only \(Q_s\) units. A shortage is the gap between the quantity demanded at \(P_0\) and the quantity offered: \(Q_d(P_0) - Q_s\). A rationing coupon gives its holder the right to buy one unit at \(P_0\).

What is a coupon worth? If coupons can be traded in a competitive market, the \(Q_s\) coupons end up with the buyers who value the good most. The last of them values the good at the demand price at \(Q_s\), call it \(P_d(Q_s)\). A coupon saves you from paying that, so

\[ \text{coupon price} = P_d(Q_s) - P_0. \]

In words: a coupon is worth the gap between what the marginal buyer would pay for the good and the fixed low price.

Selling the coupons. Someone who controls the coupons faces a demand curve for coupons: \(v(Q) = P_d(Q) - P_0\). If each coupon sold costs a commission \(c\), then:

  • One price (a monopoly on coupons): maximise \(Q \times (v(Q) - c)\), i.e. set MR of the coupon demand equal to \(c\). On a straight line, the quantity is half the quantity at which \(v(Q) = c\).
  • Perfect PD on coupons: issue coupons until \(v(Q) = c\); the takings are the triangle \(\tfrac12 \times Q \times (v(0) - c)\).
Coupons for a capped product: Q = 300 − 3P, price fixed at $20

At P₀ = 20, quantity demanded is 240. With 120 units offered the shortage is 120, the marginal holder values the good at (300 − 120)/3 = 60, so a coupon trades at 60 − 20 = $40. The shaded band is what all coupons are worth together.

Selling the coupons with a $20 commission

Coupon demand: \(v(Q) = \tfrac{300 - Q}{3} - 20 = 80 - \tfrac{Q}{3}\). Net of the commission: \(60 - \tfrac{Q}{3}\).

  1. One price: MR of \(60 - Q/3\) is \(60 - 2Q/3 = 0\) → \(Q = 90\) coupons, price \(v(90) = 50\), takings \(90 \times (50 - 20) = 2700\).
  2. Perfect PD: issue until \(60 - Q/3 = 0\) → \(Q = 180\), takings \(\tfrac12 \times 180 \times 60 = 5400\).
The papers use rounded prices such as 28.182 or 16.667. Keep the paper's numbers exactly; that is why some printed answers look like 2999.92 instead of 3000. Strategy C12.5.

10Summary

  • Price discrimination = different prices to different buyers for the same product. It needs (1) a way to tell buyers apart and (2) a way to stop resale (arbitrage).
  • Single price: MR < price, so the monopolist stops short of the efficient quantity, leaving DWL.
  • First degree (perfect): each buyer pays own WTP; Q is efficient, DWL = 0, CS = 0, the firm takes all surplus.
  • Second degree: a menu or hurdle (coupons, quantity discounts, multi-part tariffs, quality tiers) lets buyers sort themselves. Solve each submarket as its own single-price problem.
  • Third degree: separate prices for identifiable groups; each \(P = (\text{choke} + MC)/2\). Firm gains; CS and DWL may go either way. Same choke price → same price. Price gap = lowest arbitrage cost that blocks arbitrage.
  • Uniform price vs segments: combined demand is kinked, MR jumps; three cases (exit the small market, serve both, or compare two candidates).
  • Tying: base good cheap, variable good dear; usage reveals WTP.
  • Bundling: profitable when buyers' values are negatively correlated.

11Common mistakes

✗Subtracting the fixed cost from producer surplus. ✓ PS = revenue − variable cost; profit = PS − FC. ✗Serving coupon users whose WTP is below MC. ✓ If none covers MC, there is no discount sale. ✗Writing the discount price when the blank asks for the amount off (or vice versa). ✓ Discount = list − discount price. ✗Using \(a\) instead of \(a/b\) as the choke price of \(Q = a - bP\). ✓ Choke = \(a/b\). ✗Using the summed demand at a price above the smaller group's choke price. ✓ Check the price; if invalid, price for the big group alone and compare. ✗"Different slopes, so different prices." ✓ Same choke price → same price, gain 0. ✗Calling the price gap the maximum arbitrage cost that prevents arbitrage. ✓ It is the minimum. ✗Counting MC once per bundle. ✓ A bundle uses two items: 2 × MC + packaging. ✗"Perfect PD causes more welfare loss than a single price." ✓ It causes none. ✗Treating a rising MC as flat in perfect-PD surplus. ✓ PS is the area between demand and MC.

12Find the mistake

Each "solution" has one error. Find it before opening.

1

Six buyers value a good at 90, 80, 70, 60, 50, 40; MC = 35, fixed cost 10. "Best single price 70, three buyers, so producer surplus = 3 × 35 − 10 = 95."

Mistake

95 is the profit. Producer surplus leaves out fixed cost: PS = 3 × (70 − 35) = 105.

2

Group A: \(Q = 100 - P\); group B: \(Q = 100 - 5P\); MC = 10; one price. "Sum: \(Q = 200 - 6P\), choke 33.33, so \(P = (33.33 + 10)/2 = 21.67\)."

Mistake

B's choke price is 20, so at 21.67 group B buys nothing and the summed line is invalid there. The best price on the summed range is at most 20 (profit 800). Selling to A alone: \(P = (100 + 10)/2 = 55\), profit \(45 \times 45 = 2025\). Price 55.

3 · same pattern as 2024 Summer Final Q61

\(Q_s = 1000 - 95P\), \(Q_n = 250 - 23.75P\), MC = 1. "The slopes differ, so dual pricing must raise profit."

Mistake

Both choke prices are \(1000/95 \approx 10.53\), so both groups get the same price, 5.76. Profit gain = 0.

4

Two buyers; bundle values 40 and 37; MC $4 per item, packaging $0.5. "Bundle at 37 to both: profit 2 × (37 − 4.5) = 65."

Mistake

A bundle contains two items: cost per bundle = 2 × 4 + 0.5 = 8.5. Profit = 2 × (37 − 8.5) = 57 (versus 40 − 8.5 = 31.5 selling to one buyer).

13Practice

Every answer was checked by computation.

P1 · reservation-price table

Six buyers with reservation prices 90, 80, 70, 60, 50, 40. MC = 35, fixed cost 10. (a) Best single price and profit? (b) Profit under perfect PD? (c) Buyers above $65 never use coupons, others always do. Best list and discount prices, and profit?

Hint

Try each WTP as the price. For (c), split the table at 65 and solve each half.

Answer

(a) Price 70: 3 × 35 − 10 = 95 (80 gives 90; 60 gives 100 − 10 = 90). (b) PS = 55 + 45 + 35 + 25 + 15 + 5 = 180, profit 170. (c) List 70 (PS 105); discount submarket 60/50/40: price 50 gives 2 × 15 = 30 (60 gives 25; 40 gives 15). List 70, discount 50, profit 125.

P2 · single vs dual price

Group A: \(Q = 200 - 4P\); group B: \(Q = 90 - 3P\); MC = 10. Find the dual prices, the single price, and the profit gain from discriminating.

Hint

Chokes are 50 and 30. Below 30 the summed demand is \(Q = 290 - 7P\).

Answer

Dual: \(P_A = 30\) (80 units, profit 1600), \(P_B = 20\) (30 units, profit 300); total 1900. Single: \(P = (290/7 + 10)/2 = 25.71 \le 30\), \(Q = 110\), profit 1728.57 (A alone would give 1600). Gain = 171.43.

P3 · bundling

Ann values tea at 30 and cake at 10; Ben values tea at 12 and cake at 25. MC = 0. Separate or bundle?

Answer

Separate: tea 30 (vs 24), cake 25 (vs 20) → 55. Bundle values 40 and 37: price 37 to both → 74. Bundle (74 > 55).

14Concept checks

1

Why is a perfectly discriminating monopolist efficient even though it is a monopoly?

Answer

Selling one more unit no longer lowers the price on other units, so MR equals the demand curve. It sells to every buyer whose WTP covers MC: the efficient quantity, DWL = 0. All surplus goes to the firm.

2

Groups with \(Q = 100 - 2P\) and \(Q = 50 - P\), same MC. Will third-degree PD give them different prices?

Answer

No. Both choke prices are 50, so \(P = (50 + MC)/2\) for both. Discrimination changes nothing.

3

A firm charges $90 in market X and $60 in market Y. Shipping costs $25 a unit. Can it keep these prices?

Answer

No. Arbitrageurs earn 30 − 25 = $5 a unit buying in Y and selling in X. Any shipping cost of $30 or more would block it.

4

Cheap coffee machine, expensive capsules that only fit it. Which strategy, and who pays more?

Answer

Tying: base good (machine) cheap, variable good (capsules) dear. Heavy users, who value coffee more, pay more in total.

5

Does third-degree price discrimination always raise consumer surplus? Always raise DWL?

Answer

Neither. The firm gains; CS and DWL may each increase or decrease. In the §6 example at MC = 20, CS fell by 75 and DWL rose by 25.

6

Why does a coupon have to be a hassle to work as price discrimination?

Answer

The hassle is what sorts buyers: only price-sensitive (low-WTP) buyers bother. If anyone could use it effortlessly, high-WTP buyers would use it too and it would be just a price cut.
