Elasticity and its applications

So far we could say which way a price or quantity moves. Elasticity says by how much. It is one number that measures how strongly buyers or sellers react to a price (or income) change. You will use it to predict revenue, to price a product and, in later chapters, to work out who really pays a tax.

0Where this sits

Elasticity is the tool that is used to measure how sensitive markets are. It matters for three reasons:

  • Heterogeneity across markets: why a price rise barely dents salt sales but wipes out sales of one brand of cola.
  • Pricing strategy when a seller can choose its price (the monopoly chapter, Chapter 11).
  • Effects of policy, for example who bears a tax (Chapter 6).

This note has four parts:

  1. Price elasticity of demand: meaning, what makes it big or small, how to compute it (sections 1–4).
  2. Expenditures and revenue: how elasticity tells you whether a price rise earns sellers more or less (sections 5–6).
  3. Other elasticities of demand: cross-price and income elasticity (section 7).
  4. Price elasticity of supply, with applications and quick predictions (sections 8–11).

You need Chapter 3–4 ideas (demand and supply curves, shifts versus movements) and HKDSE Core maths: percentages, straight lines, slopes. Logarithms appear in section 6; everything you need about them is explained there.

1What elasticity measures

From Chapter 3 we know the law of demand: when the price rises, the quantity demanded falls. That is a qualitative statement — it gives the direction, not the size. Elasticity answers the next question: by how much does quantity demanded fall when the price rises?

Elastic and inelastic demand

A demand curve is elastic when a rise in price reduces the quantity demanded a lot (and a fall in price raises it a lot).

It is inelastic when the same rise in price reduces quantity demanded just a little.

In words: "elastic" = stretchy = buyers react strongly; "inelastic" = stiff = buyers hardly react.

Draw two demand curves through the same point (quantity 80 at price $40). Raise the price to $50:

Same price increase, very different responses
elastic demandinelastic demand

At $50 the elastic curve gives 20 units and the inelastic curve gives 75 units, starting from 80 at $40. The flatter curve reacts more.

Elasticity is not slope

Price elasticity (in words)

Elasticity = the percentage change in quantity relative to the percentage change in price.

Slope compares raw changes (dollars per unit), so it depends on the units you measure in: the same demand for rice has a different slope in kilograms than in tonnes, or in HK$ than in US cents. Percentages have no units, so elasticity does not care how you measure. That is why economists use it to compare markets.

The one link between slope and elasticity

If two straight-line demand (or supply) curves pass through a common point, then at that point the flatter curve is more elastic.

In words: at the same starting price and quantity, the curve whose quantity moves further for a given price change is the more responsive one.

Exam trap

"Flatter is more elastic" holds only at a common point. On its own, a curve being steep or flat does not make it inelastic or elastic: a straight demand line has elastic and inelastic parts whatever its steepness (§4). Statements like "the supply curve is price inelastic when it is steep enough" are false (strategy C5.6).

2What makes demand elastic or inelastic

There are five determinants. The first is the fundamental one; the other four mostly work through it.

1 · Availability of substitutes (fundamental)

A substitute is another good that does the same job (Coke and Pepsi, MTR and bus).

  • Few substitutes: when the price rises, buyers have nowhere to go, so quantity hardly changes → inelastic.
  • Many substitutes: switching brands is easy → elastic.
Example · patent expiry

When the patent on a brand-name drug expires and 5 generic (unbranded copy) drugs come on the market, what happens to the elasticity of demand for the brand-name drug?

Answer

(a) It becomes more elastic. A patent is a legal right that stops others copying an invention for some years. Once it expires, five close substitutes appear, so a price rise on the brand drug now sends many buyers to the generics.

2 · Time horizon

Less time to adjust means lower elasticity. Over time consumers find substitutes (buy a more fuel-efficient car, move closer to work), so demand becomes more elastic in the long run.

3 · How the good is classified

The less specific (broader) the category, the fewer substitutes it has, so demand is less elastic. The narrower the category, the more elastic. Example: demand for lettuce is more elastic than for food (you can swap lettuce for spinach, but nothing replaces food).

4 · Necessity or luxury

For necessities (salt, rice, life-saving drugs) we hardly change the quantity when the price changes → inelastic. For luxuries (pleasure boats, fancy cars) we are more sensitive → elastic.

5 · Size of the purchase relative to the budget

When a good takes only a small share of your budget it "feels cheap" and you barely notice a price change → less elastic. When it takes a large share it "feels expensive" and you are more sensitive → more elastic. (Picture two pie charts: good X as a thin slice versus a big slice of the budget.)

Ready answer · ranking pairs of goods

The more elastic of a pair is the one that is: narrower / more specific (Wan Chai flats vs Hong Kong flats; Kit Kat vs chocolate bars; plates with "28" vs all licence plates), more of a luxury (fish vs salt; multivitamins vs life-saving drugs), a bigger budget share (cars vs coffee), or bought with more time to adjust (two-year rental vs two-day rental; coffee on a normal Sunday vs before a midterm).

In words: more substitutes, more time, narrower, more luxurious, more expensive → more elastic. Strategy C5.7.

3Computing the price elasticity of demand

Price elasticity of demand

Elasticity of demand = the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price:

\[ \eta = \frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta P} \]

In words: elasticity = how many percent quantity changes for each 1% change in price. \(\Delta\) (the Greek capital "delta") means "change in"; \(\eta\) (Greek "eta") is the symbol used here for elasticity. Many papers write \(\varepsilon\) instead — same thing.

Example · oil in the long run

The price of oil rises by 10% and, over several years, quantity demanded falls by 5%. Then

\[ \eta = \frac{-5\%}{10\%} = -0.5, \]

or 0.5 "in absolute terms".

The sign, and the three cases

Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, so the elasticity of demand is always negative. People often drop the minus sign and talk about the absolute value \(|\eta|\) (the size, ignoring the sign).

Inelastic: \(|\eta| \lt 1\)Quantity changes by a smaller percentage than price. Between −1 and 0.
Unit elastic: \(|\eta| = 1\)Quantity changes by the same percentage as price. Exactly −1.
Elastic: \(|\eta| \gt 1\)Quantity changes by a bigger percentage than price. Below −1 (−2, −3, …).
Sign convention in past papers

Numeric blanks expect the negative number (−1.50, not 1.50). A True/False statement "the price elasticity of demand ranges from 0 to infinity" is marked false: it ranges from 0 down to \(-\infty\). When a statement compares sizes ("more elastic"), compare absolute values.

Percentage change from a starting point

Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100%. So for a move from \((P_0, Q_0)\) to \((P_1, Q_1)\):

\[ \eta = \frac{(Q_1 - Q_0)/Q_0}{(P_1 - P_0)/P_0} \]

In words: the change in quantity as a fraction of the starting quantity, divided by the change in price as a fraction of the starting price.

Price upOil price 100 → 110; quantity 100 → 95 trillion barrels.
\(\eta = \dfrac{(95-100)/100}{(110-100)/100} = \dfrac{-5\%}{10\%} = -0.5\)
Price downSame two points, the other way: 110 → 100; quantity 95 → 100.
\(\eta = \dfrac{(100-95)/95}{(100-110)/110} = \dfrac{5.26\%}{-9.09\%} = -0.579\)

Same two points, two different answers — only because we changed which point we called "old". That is the base-point bias.

The midpoint formula

Midpoint (arc) elasticity \[ \begin{aligned} \%\Delta Q &= \frac{Q_1 - Q_0}{(Q_0 + Q_1)/2} \times 100\% \\ \%\Delta P &= \frac{P_1 - P_0}{(P_0 + P_1)/2} \times 100\% \\ \eta &= \frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta P} \end{aligned} \]

In words: measure each change as a percentage of the average of the two values, not of the starting value. Then the answer is the same whichever point you start from.

Worked example

When the price is $10, quantity demanded is 100. When the price rises to $20, quantity demanded is 90.

\[ \begin{aligned} \%\Delta Q &= \frac{90 - 100}{(100+90)/2} \\ &= \frac{-10}{95} = -10.53\% \\ \%\Delta P &= \frac{20-10}{(10+20)/2} \\ &= \frac{10}{15} = 66.67\% \\ \eta &= \frac{-10.53\%}{66.67\%} = -0.158 \end{aligned} \]

Cutting off the digits gives −0.15, but the exact value is −0.1579, which rounds to −0.16. In an exam blank, give the properly rounded value.

Try it: the three ways to compute an elasticity from two points

Starts at the worked-example numbers. Swap the two points and watch the "from point 0" and "from point 1" answers change while the midpoint answer stays put. Exam papers say "using the mid-point formula" whenever they want this version.

Exam use · which elasticity?

The midpoint formula also gives an income elasticity: replace price by income. 2024 Summer Midterm Q30: 21 cup noodles a month at income $14600, 29 at $9000:

\[ \begin{aligned} \%\Delta Q &= \tfrac{29-21}{25} = 32\% \\ \%\Delta I &= \tfrac{9000-14600}{11800} = -47.46\% \\ \eta_I &= -0.67 \end{aligned} \]

Negative: cup noodles are an inferior good for James (§7).

4Point elasticity and the straight-line demand curve

When the two points are very close, the original formula and the midpoint formula give almost the same number. Shrinking the change to a tiny one gives the elasticity at a single point.

Point formula \[ \eta \text{ at } (P_0, Q_0) = \frac{1}{\text{slope}} \times \frac{P_0}{Q_0} \]

Here slope = \(\Delta P / \Delta Q\), the slope of the curve as drawn, with price on the vertical axis.

In words: elasticity at a point = (how much quantity changes per $1 of price) × (price ÷ quantity at that point).

Why? Rearranging the definition:

\[ \begin{aligned} \eta &= \frac{\Delta Q / Q}{\Delta P / P} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \times \frac{P}{Q} \\ &= \frac{1}{\Delta P/\Delta Q} \times \frac{P}{Q}. \end{aligned} \]
Which "slope"? (an exam reading)

Slope here is \(\Delta P/\Delta Q\) (rise over run on the usual diagram). If a demand equation is written \(Q = a - bP\), then \(\Delta Q/\Delta P = -b\) and you use \(\eta = -b \times P/Q\) directly. When a past paper says "a demand curve with a slope of −4.5" it means \(\Delta P/\Delta Q = -4.5\) (graph slope); the keys are built on that reading. Example (2022 Spring Midterm Q31): slope −4.5 at \(P = 10, Q = 20\) gives \(\eta = \frac{1}{-4.5}\times\frac{10}{20} = -0.11\), inelastic.

Worked example

A straight-line demand hits the price axis at 20 and the quantity axis at 5. What is the elasticity when \(P = 8\)?

  1. Slope = −(vertical intercept)/(horizontal intercept) = −20/5 = −4.
  2. At \(P = 8\): the line is \(P = 20 - 4Q\), so \(Q = (20-8)/4 = 3\) (point A).
  3. \(\eta = \dfrac{1}{-4}\times\dfrac{8}{3} = -\dfrac{8}{12} = -\dfrac{2}{3}\).

\(\eta = -2/3\): inelastic at P = 8.

Elasticity changes along a straight line

Demand from price 6 (at Q = 0) to quantity 12 (at P = 0): slope \(= -6/12\).

  • At \(P = 4\): \(Q = 4\), so \(\eta = \frac{4}{4}\times\frac{1}{-6/12} = -2\) (elastic).
  • At \(P = 1\): \(Q = 10\), so \(\eta = \frac{1}{10}\times\frac{1}{-6/12} = -\frac{1}{5}\) (inelastic).

Observation: price elasticity is different at every point on a straight-line demand curve, even though the slope is the same everywhere — because \(P/Q\) changes.

Elasticity along a straight demand line
elastic part (above midpoint)inelastic part (below midpoint)

The line \(P = 6 - Q/2\) from the worked example. Midpoint (3, 6) is where \(\eta = -1\). Drag the price: at $4 you get −2, at $1 you get −0.2.

The general picture

For a straight demand line from price \(a\) (vertical intercept) to quantity \(b\) (horizontal intercept):

  • Above the midpoint (price above \(a/2\)): \(\eta \lt -1\), elastic.
  • At the midpoint \((b/2,\ a/2)\): \(\eta = -1\), unit elastic.
  • Below the midpoint: \(-1 \lt \eta \lt 0\), inelastic.

In words: high up the line, price is big and quantity small, so a $1 price change is a small percentage of price but a big percentage of quantity — elastic. Low down it is the reverse. As price rises along the line, \(|\eta|\) rises.

Two curves through a common point

D₁ runs from price 12 to quantity 6 (slope −12/6 = −2); D₂ runs from price 6 to quantity 12 (slope −6/12 = −½). Both pass through \((Q, P) = (4, 4)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{On } D_1:\ \eta &= \tfrac{4}{4}\times\tfrac{1}{-2} = -\tfrac12 \\ \text{On } D_2:\ \eta &= \tfrac{4}{4}\times\tfrac{1}{-1/2} = -2 \end{aligned} \]

Observation: if two demand curves share a point, the steeper one is less elastic at that common point (and the flatter one more elastic).

The two extremes

Perfectly elastic demandA horizontal line; elasticity \(= -\infty\). If the price rises a little, quantity demanded drops to zero; if it drops a little, quantity demanded increases a lot. (Slope 0, so 1/slope is "infinite".)
Perfectly inelastic demandA vertical line; elasticity \(= 0\). Quantity demanded does not respond to any change in price. (Slope infinite, so 1/slope = 0.)

5Elasticity, expenditure and revenue

Total revenue = total expenditure \[ \text{Revenue} = P \times Q \]

In words: sellers' revenue is the price per unit times the number of units sold. Every dollar a seller receives is a dollar a buyer spends, so sellers' revenue = consumers' total expenditure.

When the price changes, P and Q move in opposite directions along the demand curve, so whether \(P\times Q\) goes up or down depends on which moves by the bigger percentage — that is, on the elasticity.

Movie tickets

The demand for movie tickets is a straight line from $12 (nobody buys) to 600 tickets a day (price $0). With Q in hundreds of tickets: \(P = 12 - 2Q\).

Revenue is largest at the midpoint

On a straight-line demand curve, total revenue is maximised at the midpoint, where the elasticity is exactly −1.

In words: above the midpoint demand is elastic, so cutting the price raises revenue; below it demand is inelastic, so raising the price raises revenue. Both lead you to the midpoint.

Why: two effects of a price cut

Expenditure on shelter

Demand for shelter: \(Q = 16 - P\) (square yards per week). What happens to total expenditure when the price is cut from $12 to $10 per sq yd?

  • At $12: \(Q = 4\), expenditure \(= 12 \times 4 = 48\) (point E).
  • At $10: \(Q = 6\), expenditure \(= 10 \times 6 = 60\) (point G).
  • Loss from selling the original 4 units at $2 less: \(2 \times 4 = 8\) (the thin rectangle along the top).
  • Gain from the 2 extra units sold at $10: \(10 \times 2 = 20\) (the rectangle at the side).

Expenditure rises by 20 − 8 = $12, from $48 to $60.

General rule: when price goes down, total expenditure rises [falls] if the gain from selling additional units is larger [smaller] than the loss from selling existing units at the lower price.

In words: with elastic demand, revenue moves the same way as quantity; with inelastic demand, revenue moves the same way as price.

Example · why the war on drugs is hard to win

Demand for most illegal drugs is inelastic (addicted users have few substitutes). Prohibition makes supply scarcer, which pushes the price up. With inelastic demand, the quantity falls only a little while the price rises a lot, so dealers' total revenue (the rectangle P × Q) rises. The more effective the drug war, the richer and more powerful the dealers become. On the graph, the tall rectangle with prohibition stands against the shorter, wider rectangle without prohibition.

Inference · the fashion store

A fashionable clothing store raises its prices by 25%. What does that tell you about its estimate of the price elasticity of demand for its products: (a) elastic or (b) inelastic?

Answer

(b) Inelastic. A seller would only raise prices this much if it expected revenue to go up, which happens when demand is inelastic: customers attached to the brand keep buying. (Strictly a store cares about profit, not revenue, but selling fewer clothes also saves costs, which only strengthens the case.)

Going further · the revenue-maximising price on a linear demand

Write the demand as \(Q = a - bP\). It reaches zero at \(P = a/b\); its midpoint is half-way:

\[ P^* = \frac{a}{2b}, \qquad Q^* = \frac{a}{2}. \]

In words: the best price for revenue is half the price at which nobody buys; you then sell half the quantity you would sell at a zero price.

From two observations (past papers give two price–quantity pairs and say the demand is linear):

  1. \(b = -\Delta Q / \Delta P\) (how many fewer units per $1 price rise).
  2. \(a = Q_0 + bP_0\) (the quantity at a zero price).
  3. \(P^* = a/(2b)\), \(Q^* = a/2\).

Example (2022 Spring Midterm Q36): 1110 sets at $65, 1100 at $67. \(b = 10/2 = 5\), \(a = 1110 + 5\times 65 = 1435\), so \(P^* = 1435/10 = \$143.50\) and \(Q^* = 717.5\). The answer lies far outside the observed prices — that is fine; do not "clamp" it.

If income appears, as in \(Q = 1950 - 5P - 1.5I\) with \(I = 70\), first plug income in: \(a = 1950 - 105 = 1845\), so \(P^* = 1845/10 = \$184.50\). Strategy C5.2.

Going further · revenue after a curve shifts

The table above is about a price change along a demand curve, which is what happens when supply shifts. So:

  • Supply shifts: P and Q move in opposite directions; revenue follows the table. Supply increases (price falls) → revenue rises if demand is elastic, falls if inelastic.
  • Demand shifts: P and Q move in the same direction (along the supply curve), so revenue moves the same way as the shift, whatever the elasticity. Demand falls → revenue falls.

Example (2024 Spring Final Q12): demand elasticity −0.1, supply 1.8. A small increase in supply lowers revenue (inelastic demand); a small decrease in demand lowers revenue. Strategy C5.5.

6Curves with constant elasticity

A straight demand line has a different elasticity at every point. Some curves have the same elasticity everywhere.

Unit-elastic demand: \(PQ = \text{constant}\)

If the elasticity is −1 at every price–quantity pair, then every 1% price rise cuts quantity by exactly 1%, so spending \(P\times Q\) never changes. The curve is \(PQ = \text{constant}\), a rectangular hyperbola: a curve that bends towards both axes without touching them. It is not a straight line.

In words: a buyer who always spends the same total amount, whatever the price, has unit-elastic demand.

Constant elasticity: \(P^kQ = \text{constant}\)

The demand curve \(P^k Q = \text{constant}\) has elasticity \(-k\) at every point.

In words: the power on P (with Q on its own, power 1) is the size of the elasticity. \(P^2Q = 5\) has elasticity −2 everywhere; \(P^{1/2}Q = c\) has −½ everywhere.

Going further · logarithms in one box

The derivation uses ln, the natural logarithm (the logarithm to base \(e \approx 2.718\), on the "ln" key of your calculator). You need three facts:

  1. \(\ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y\) and \(\ln(x^k) = k\ln x\) (logs turn products into sums and powers into multipliers).
  2. For a small change, \(\Delta(\ln Q) \approx \Delta Q / Q\): a change in \(\ln Q\) is (approximately) the percentage change in Q, written as a decimal. Check: \(\ln 101 - \ln 100 = 0.00995 \approx 1\%\).
  3. So \(\dfrac{\Delta \ln Q}{\Delta \ln P} \approx \dfrac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta P} = \eta\).

Calculus writes the same derivation with the notation \(d\) ("a tiny change") instead of \(\Delta\); the meaning is the same.

The curves are derived this way:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\Delta\ln Q}{\Delta\ln P} = -k \;&\Rightarrow\; \ln Q = -k\ln P + c \\ &\Rightarrow\; \ln Q + k\ln P = c \\ &\Rightarrow\; \ln(QP^k) = c \\ &\Rightarrow\; QP^k = e^{c}. \end{aligned} \]

In words: "elasticity is −k everywhere" is the same statement as "ln Q is a straight line in ln P with slope −k", which is the same as \(QP^k\) = a constant. With \(k = 1\) this is the unit-elastic \(QP = \text{constant}\).

Constant-elasticity demand \(Q = 100/P^k\)

The shaded rectangle is revenue \(P\times Q = 100P^{1-k}\). With k = 1 it has the same area (100) at every price; with k above 1 (elastic) it shrinks as the price rises; below 1 (inelastic) it grows.

Estimating the elasticity from data

Collect many \((p, q)\) observations on a demand curve and fit the straight line

\[ \ln Q = \alpha + \beta \ln P + \text{error} \]

by regression (the statistical method for fitting the best straight line through scattered points; the "error" term \(\epsilon\) collects everything the line misses). The fitted slope \(\beta\) is an estimate of the price elasticity, since on a constant-elasticity curve \(\ln Q = -k\ln P + c\).

Log-linear demand in past papers

Ready answer · reading a log-linear demand

In \(\ln Q_x = k + \alpha\ln P_x + \beta\ln P_y + \gamma\ln I\) the coefficients are the elasticities: own-price \(\alpha\), cross-price \(\beta\), income \(\gamma\). No calculation needed; extra data like "\(Q_X = 150, P_X = 4\)" is a distraction.

If a variable appears not in logs, e.g. \(+0.2P_y^{-2}\), \(+1.4\,I^{-0.6}\) or \(+2.7/I\), you need only the sign of its elasticity: it is the sign of (coefficient × power). \(+0.2P_y^{-2}\): \(0.2\times(-2) \lt 0\) → complements. \(+2.7/I = 2.7I^{-1}\): negative → inferior. \(-2.7/I\): positive → normal.

In words: log term → read the number; non-log term → multiply coefficient by power and read the sign. Strategy C5.3 (signs explained in §7).

7Other elasticities of demand: cross-price and income

Cross-price elasticity of demand \[ \eta_{xy} = \frac{\Delta Q^d_x / Q^d_x}{\Delta P_y / P_y} \]

In words: the percentage change in the quantity of good x demanded, divided by the percentage change in the price of a different good y. It measures how sensitive demand for x is to y's price.

Substitutes: positiveA rise in the price of one brand of milk increases demand for other brands: price of y up, quantity of x up.
Complements: negativeComplements are goods used together. A rise in the price of milk decreases demand for Oreos: price of y up, quantity of x down.
Income elasticity of demand \[ \eta_I = \frac{\Delta Q^d_x / Q^d_x}{\Delta I / I} \]

In words: the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in income \(I\).

Normal, luxury and inferior goods
  • Normal goods: income elasticity positive (richer → buy more).
  • Luxury goods: income elasticity greater than 1 (spending on them rises faster than income).
  • Inferior goods: income elasticity negative (richer → buy less, e.g. instant noodles when you can afford restaurants).
Don't mix up

"Luxury" as a determinant of price elasticity (§2) and "luxury good" as income elasticity above 1 are related ideas but different measurements. Exam statements always say which elasticity they mean.

Elasticities from a linear demand function

Going further · the coefficient rule

Past papers give demand as a linear equation such as \(Q_A = a - 3P_A + 1.2P_B - 2.3I\). Each coefficient tells you how many units \(Q_A\) changes per one-unit change in that variable, holding the others fixed. So, by the same reasoning as the point formula (§4):

\[ \eta_X = (\text{coefficient of } X) \times \frac{X}{Q}. \]

In words: elasticity with respect to anything = (units of Q per unit of X) × (X ÷ Q at the point you are asked about). Use the coefficient of the variable you are changing: own price for own-price, \(P_B\) for cross-price, \(I\) for income. The coefficient's sign is the elasticity's sign.

Worked example · 2022 Fall Final Q17–Q18

\(Q_B = 289 - 2.4P_B + 0.5P_F + 1.5I\) (beef; \(P_F\) = price of fish). At one equilibrium \(Q_B = 566.9\), \(P_B = 69\), \(P_F = 77\). Find the income elasticity and the cross-price elasticity with respect to fish.

  1. Income is missing: \(566.9 = 289 - 2.4(69) + 0.5(77) + 1.5I\), so \(1.5I = 405\) and \(I = 270\).
  2. Income elasticity \(= 1.5 \times 270/566.9 = 0.71\) (positive: normal good).

8Price elasticity of supply

The law of supply says price and quantity supplied move together. How strongly?

Elastic and inelastic supply

A supply curve is elastic if a rise in price increases quantity supplied a lot (and a fall decreases it a lot); inelastic if a rise in price increases quantity supplied just a little.

For example, from $40 to $50, an inelastic supply goes from 80 to 85 units; an elastic supply from 80 to 170.

As for demand, elasticity ≠ slope, but where two straight supply curves share a point, the flatter one is more elastic there.

Determinants of supply elasticity

1 · How fast per-unit costs riseIf producing more is very expensive, supply is inelastic. If output can grow with little extra cost, supply is elastic.
2 · Time horizonRight after a price rise, producers can only use their current capacity (factories, machines) → inelastic. Over time they build capacity → elastic.
3 · Share of the market for inputsIf the industry can expand without pushing up the demand (and price) of its inputs (materials, workers), supply is elastic. If expanding drives input prices up a lot, supply is inelastic.
4 · Geographic scopeThe wider the market, the less elastic its supply; the narrower, the more elastic. A small area can easily draw in more goods from outside it — like the narrow "good category" for demand.
Exam favourite

"The supply of firearms in Washington state is less price elastic than the supply in the whole United States" — false: the narrower region has the more elastic supply (guns flow in from other states). Likewise Coca-Cola (easily produced more) has more elastic supply than ivory.

Computing supply elasticity

Price elasticity of supply \[ \eta_s = \frac{\%\Delta Q^s}{\%\Delta P} \]

In words: percentage change in quantity supplied per percentage change in price. It is positive.

\(\eta_s \lt 1\): inelastic; \(\eta_s \gt 1\): elastic; \(\eta_s = 1\): unit elastic.

Two points given → midpoint formula, exactly as for demand. A small change around \((P_0, Q_0)\) → point formula \(\eta_s = \frac{1}{\text{slope}}\times\frac{P_0}{Q_0}\).

A supply curve whose elasticity falls

Straight supply line starting at price 4 on the vertical axis, through A \((Q, P) = (2, 8)\) and B \((3, 10)\). Slope = (10 − 8)/(3 − 2) = 2.

\[ \begin{aligned} A:\ \eta &= \tfrac{8}{2}\times\tfrac12 = 2 \\ B:\ \eta &= \tfrac{10}{3}\times\tfrac12 = \tfrac53 \end{aligned} \]

Observations: (1) elasticity is positive; (2) it is greater than 1 everywhere on a straight supply line with a positive vertical (price) intercept; (3) it falls as quantity increases (towards 1).

A supply curve with elasticity 1

Straight line through the origin, through A (12, 4) and B (15, 5); slope = 1/3.

\[ \begin{aligned} A:\ \eta &= \tfrac{4}{12}\times 3 = 1 \\ B:\ \eta &= \tfrac{5}{15}\times 3 = 1 \end{aligned} \]

The price elasticity of supply is always 1 at any point on a straight-line supply curve through the origin: P/Q equals the slope everywhere, so they cancel.

Where the line starts decides everything · \(Q = a + 2P\)

A = −8 is the worked-example line (price intercept 4, through (2, 8) and (3, 10)): η = 2 at P = 8. Set a = 0 for the line through the origin (η = 1 everywhere). Positive a puts the intercept on the quantity axis: η below 1 everywhere, rising towards 1 as the price rises.

Ready answer · elasticity along a straight supply line \(Q = a + bP\), \(b \gt 0\) \[ \eta_s = \frac{bP}{a + bP} \]
  • \(a = 0\) (through origin): \(\eta_s = 1\) everywhere.
  • \(a \lt 0\) (positive price intercept): \(\eta_s \gt 1\) everywhere, falling towards 1 as P rises.
  • \(a \gt 0\) (positive quantity intercept): \(\eta_s \lt 1\) everywhere, rising towards 1 as P rises. Never elastic.

In words: a straight supply line has no "elastic part and inelastic part" — it is all one side of 1, and the intercept tells you which side. Strategy C5.6; if you observe a demand shift with %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP > 1, supply is elastic, so \(a \lt 0\) (C5.5).

The extremes

Perfectly inelastic supplyWhat is the elasticity of supply of land in Central? Nobody can make more of it: the supply curve is vertical and the elasticity is 0 at every point.
Perfectly elastic supplyIf marginal cost is constant (lemonade at 14 cents a cup, however many cups), the supply curve is horizontal at 14 cents and the elasticity is infinite at every point.

9Applications: slave redemption, gun buybacks, short and long run

Does slave redemption create more slavery?

In Sudan, charities buy slaves from slave traders in order to free them — "redemption". Buying slaves adds to the demand for slaves. How will slave traders respond? It depends on the elasticity of supply.

Slave redemption under inelastic and elastic supply
supplydemand from slave ownersowners + redeemers

The curves are drawn through the points of the two panels described below.

  • Perfectly inelastic supply (left panel): 1,000 slaves exist whatever the price. Before redemption the price is $15 (point a). Redeemers' demand pushes it to $50 (b); at $50 owners only want 200 (c). 800 slaves are freed, and no new slaves are captured.
  • Elastic supply (right panel): the higher price ($15 → $30) makes traders capture more people: supply rises from 1,000 to 2,200 (+1,200). Redeemers buy 1,600, but owners still hold 600. Net freed: only 400 (1,000 → 600 in captivity), and 1,200 extra people were captured to be sold.
Lesson

The effectiveness of slave redemption depends on the elasticity of supply. It works best when supply is perfectly inelastic; the more elastic supply is, the more a higher price simply calls forth more supply.

Will a gun buyback reduce the guns on the street?

A city buys back old, low-quality guns. Take guns in Washington, D.C.: the supply of such guns to a local region is very elastic — horizontal at $84, because guns can flow in from anywhere else. Without the buyback, residents buy 1,000 (point a). The buyback adds demand; quantity traded rises to 6,000 (point b), but the extra 5,000 is just new supply shipped in to be sold to the buyback. The number of guns held on the street (1,000) and their price ($84) do not change.

Lesson

The effectiveness of the buyback depends on the elasticity of supply. A national or global buyback faces a wider market, whose supply is less elastic (determinant 4), so it has a better chance of actually reducing guns.

Short run and long run after a demand increase

A manufacturer makes an experimental computer chip that critics praise, so demand for it rises a lot (D₀ → D₁). In the short run supply is inelastic (steep): the market jumps from A to B — price up a lot, quantity up a little. In the long run new capacity comes in, supply is elastic (flatter), and the market settles at C — a smaller price rise and a bigger quantity rise than in the short run.

10Quick predictions with elasticities

Supply elasticity mattersA given increase in demand raises the market price less when supply is more elastic (A → C instead of A → B).
Demand elasticity mattersA given increase in demand raises the market price less when demand is more elastic.
Two quick formulas \[ \begin{aligned} \%\Delta P_{\text{demand shift}} &= \frac{\%\text{ change in demand}}{|\eta_d| + \eta_s} \\ \%\Delta P_{\text{supply shift}} &= -\frac{\%\text{ change in supply}}{|\eta_d| + \eta_s} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the price moves by the size of the shift divided by the sum of the two elasticities (both taken as positive numbers). Demand up → price up; supply up → price down.

"% change in demand (supply)" means the curve moves sideways by that percentage of quantity at every price. You can check the directions on a graph; the mathematics behind the formulas is optional.

Going further · the quantity change, and where the formula comes from

After a shift, the market slides along the curve that did not move. So:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{demand shift: } \%\Delta Q &= \eta_s \times \%\Delta P \\ \text{supply shift: } \%\Delta Q &= |\eta_d| \times |\%\Delta P| \end{aligned} \]

(with Q moving in the direction of the shift). Why the price formula works, for a demand shift of \(s\%\): at the new price, quantity demanded changes by \(s + \eta_d\,\%\Delta P\) and quantity supplied by \(\eta_s\,\%\Delta P\). Setting them equal, \(s = (\eta_s - \eta_d)\,\%\Delta P = (\eta_s + |\eta_d|)\,\%\Delta P\). These are approximations, good for small shifts.

Worked example · 2023 Fall Final Q6–Q7

Oil: price $66, quantity 10000 barrels/day; \(\eta_d = -0.3\), \(\eta_s = 0.71\). A discovery raises quantity supplied by 200 barrels/day at any price.

  1. Shift as a percentage: \(200/10000 = 2\%\) supply increase. (The $66 is not needed.)
  2. \(\%\Delta P = -2/(0.3 + 0.71) = -1.98\%\): price decreases by 1.98%.
  3. Supply shifted, so move along demand: \(\%\Delta Q = 0.3 \times 1.98 = 0.59\%\), quantity increases.

Price B (decrease) 1.98%; quantity A (increase) 0.59%

Rounding note: 2020 Spring Final Q52 (supply shifts 3%, \(\eta_s = 4.5\), \(\eta_d = -2.7\)) gives %ΔQ = 2.7 × 3/7.2 = 1.125 exactly; the printed key shows 1.12. Keep full precision and round once at the end; where a value ends in exactly 5, either neighbouring answer may have been accepted.

Going further · reading a shift backwards

If only one curve shifted and you see P and Q move the same way, demand shifted and the market moved along supply: \(\%\Delta Q/\%\Delta P\) is the supply elasticity. Opposite ways → supply shifted, and you learn the demand elasticity. 11Why are the air fares so different?

A round trip Kansas City → Honolulu → Kansas City costs a lot less than Honolulu → Kansas City → Honolulu, although passengers fly the same planes, burn the same fuel and get the same in-flight service. Why?

  • Someone starting in Kansas City and flying to Honolulu is probably a vacationer. They could equally go to Florida, Barbados or Cancún. Many substitutes → elastic demand → airlines compete fiercely with low fares. Big planes have economies of scale (cost per seat falls when more seats are filled), so carriers want to fill extra seats by targeting low prices at the price-sensitive vacationers.
  • Someone starting in Honolulu and flying to Kansas City is probably travelling for business or family: they are not shopping for a destination. Few substitutes → inelastic demand → higher fares stick.

Charging different prices to groups with different elasticities is price discrimination, the subject of Chapter 12.

12Summary

  • Price elasticity of demand \(\eta = \%\Delta Q/\%\Delta P\), always negative. \(|\eta| \gt 1\) elastic, \(=1\) unit, \(\lt 1\) inelastic.
  • Determinants: substitutes (fundamental), time, narrowness of category, necessity vs luxury, budget share.
  • Two observations: use the midpoint formula (average as base). One point: \(\eta = \frac{1}{\text{slope}}\cdot\frac{P}{Q}\), slope = \(\Delta P/\Delta Q\); for \(Q = a - bP\), \(\eta = -bP/Q\).
  • Straight demand line: elastic above the midpoint, unit at it, inelastic below; flatter = more elastic only at a common point. Horizontal = perfectly elastic, vertical = perfectly inelastic.
  • Revenue = P × Q. Elastic: price and revenue move oppositely; inelastic: together. Revenue is maximised at the midpoint of a linear demand.
  • Constant elasticity: \(P^kQ = \text{const}\) has \(\eta = -k\); \(PQ = \text{const}\) is unit elastic (a hyperbola). In log-linear demand the coefficients are the elasticities.
  • Cross-price: + substitutes, − complements. Income: + normal (above 1 luxury), − inferior.
  • Supply elasticity ≥ 0. Determinants: cost growth, time, input markets, geographic scope (wider = less elastic). Straight supply: through origin 1, price intercept above 1, quantity intercept below 1. Vertical 0, horizontal ∞.
  • Policies that work through prices (redemption, buybacks) depend on supply elasticity.
  • Quick predictions: \(\%\Delta P = \pm\,\text{shift}/(|\eta_d| + \eta_s)\); quantity moves along the curve that did not shift.

13Common mistakes

✗Measuring the percentage change from the starting point when the question says "mid-point formula". ✓ Divide by the average of the two values. ✗Dropping the minus sign in a numeric blank. ✓ Demand elasticities are entered as negative numbers. ✗Using 1/(coefficient) when demand is written \(Q = a - bP\). ✓ Then \(\Delta Q/\Delta P = -b\) and \(\eta = -bP/Q\); "1/slope" is for slope \(= \Delta P/\Delta Q\). ✗"Steep means inelastic." ✓ Steepness only compares two curves at a common point. ✗Calling a unit-elastic demand a straight line. ✓ It is \(PQ = \text{constant}\), a hyperbola. Only a supply line through the origin is straight and unit elastic. ✗Confusing fixed spending (−1) with fixed quantity (0) or fixed price (\(-\infty\)). ✗Using the elasticity of the curve that shifted to get %ΔQ. ✓ Use the curve that stayed put. ✗Forgetting to turn "350 barrels" into a percentage of the starting quantity. ✓ Shift % = change ÷ initial Q; the price is usually a distraction. ✗Using the own-price coefficient for a cross-price or income elasticity. ✓ Use the coefficient of the variable that changes. ✗Rounding \(c\) or an intermediate price before the last step. ✓ Keep full precision. ✗Thinking demand elasticity decides revenue after a demand shift. ✓ Revenue then moves with the shift.

14Find the mistake

Each "solution" has one error. Find it before opening.

1

Price rises from $8 to $12; quantity demanded falls from 50 to 30. "Midpoint elasticity = \(\frac{-20/50}{4/8} = -0.8\)."

Mistake

That uses the starting values as bases. Midpoint: \(\%\Delta Q = -20/40 = -50\%\), \(\%\Delta P = 4/10 = 40\%\), so \(\eta = \mathbf{-1.25}\) (elastic, not inelastic).

2

\(Q = 100 - 4P\). "At \(P = 10\), \(Q = 60\), slope = −4, so \(\eta = \frac{1}{-4}\times\frac{10}{60} = -0.04\)."

Mistake

−4 is \(\Delta Q/\Delta P\), not the graph slope \(\Delta P/\Delta Q\) (which is −¼). So \(\eta = -4 \times 10/60 = \mathbf{-0.67}\).

3 · numbers from 2024 Fall Final Q14–Q15

Oil: Q = 9000, \(\eta_d = -0.5\), \(\eta_s = 1.13\); demand rises by 315 barrels at every price. "%ΔP = 3.5/1.63 = 2.15%; %ΔQ = 0.5 × 2.15 = 1.07%."

Mistake

Demand shifted, so the market moves along supply: %ΔQ = 1.13 × 2.147 = 2.43%.

4

"Ken spends $50 on lunch every day whatever it costs, so his demand is perfectly inelastic: elasticity 0."

Mistake

Fixed spending, not fixed quantity: \(PQ = 50\) is unit elastic, −1. Perfectly inelastic would be "the same number of lunches whatever the price".

P1 · midpoint and revenue

When a bubble-tea shop charges $40, it sells 300 cups a day; at $60 it sells 200. Using the midpoint formula, find the price elasticity. Did revenue change?

Hint

Averages: quantity 250, price 50.

Answer

\(\%\Delta Q = -100/250 = -40\%\), \(\%\Delta P = 20/50 = 40\%\), so \(\eta = -1\). Revenue: 40 × 300 = 12000 and 60 × 200 = 12000 — unchanged, as unit elasticity predicts.

P2 · revenue-maximising price

Demand is linear. A stall sells 400 units at $10 and 360 at $12. Find the revenue-maximising price, quantity and revenue.

Hint

\(b = 40/2\); \(a = 400 + 10b\).

Answer

\(b = 20\), \(a = 600\), so \(Q = 600 - 20P\). \(P^* = 600/40 = \$15\), \(Q^* = 300\), revenue $4500.

P3 · quick prediction

\(\eta_d = -0.4\), \(\eta_s = 1.6\). A typhoon cuts supply by 6%. Predict %ΔP and %ΔQ.

Hint

Supply shift: divide by 0.4 + 1.6; then move along demand.

Answer

%ΔP = 6/2 = +3%; %ΔQ = 0.4 × 3 = −1.2%.

16Concept checks

1

A straight demand line runs from P = 20 to Q = 40. Is demand elastic at P = 12?

Answer

Yes: the midpoint price is 10 and 12 is above it. Check: Q = 40 − 2P = 16, \(\eta = -2 \times 12/16 = -1.5\).

2

Demand is inelastic. A bad harvest shifts supply left. Do farmers' total revenues rise or fall?

Answer

Rise: the price rises by a bigger percentage than quantity falls.

3

Which is more elastic everywhere: \(P^2Q = 5\) or \(P^3Q = 0.5\)?

Answer

\(P^3Q = 0.5\) (−3 versus −2). The constants do not matter.

4

\(\ln Q_x = 6 - 1.5\ln P_x + 0.3\ln P_y - 0.4\ln I\). Classify good x and the relation between x and y.

Answer

Income elasticity −0.4: inferior. Cross-price +0.3: substitutes. Own-price elasticity −1.5.

5

Supply is \(Q = 10 + 3P\). Is it elastic at any price?

Answer

No. Positive quantity intercept: \(\eta_s = 3P/(10+3P) \lt 1\) at every price, approaching 1 as P grows.

6

After a shift of one curve only, the price falls 4% and the quantity falls 2%. Which curve shifted, and what do you learn?

Answer

Same direction → demand shifted (left), and the market moved along supply: \(\eta_s = 2/4 = 0.5\), inelastic supply.
