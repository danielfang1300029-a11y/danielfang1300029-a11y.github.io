Monopoly

What changes when one seller faces the whole market demand curve: it picks its own price, sells less than is efficient, and creates a deadweight loss. This chapter shows how a monopoly chooses quantity and price, how big the loss is, and what governments can do about it with price ceilings, subsidies and patents.

1Market power and why monopolies exist

Markets differ in how much a single seller can control the price. Picture a line:

Perfect competitionMany small sellers of an identical good. Each one is a price taker: it has no control over price. (Chapter 10.)
MonopolyA single seller of a good with no close substitutes. It is a price maker: it has perfect control over price, limited only by what buyers will pay.

Real markets sit somewhere along this line.

Market power

Competitive forces tend to drive the price down to the marginal cost of production (the cost of making one more unit). Sometimes these forces are prevented from working.

Market power = the power to raise price above marginal cost without fear that other firms will enter the market.

In words: a firm has market power if it can charge more than its extra cost per unit and nobody can come in and undercut it.

Why do monopolies exist? Barriers to entry

A barrier to entry is anything that stops new firms from entering a market where the existing firm earns profits. There are four kinds:

BarrierWhat it meansExample
Legal barriersPatents and intellectual property: the law gives an inventor the sole right to sell the invention for a period (usually 20 years). Licences and franchises: the government lets only one firm operate.Pharmaceuticals (new medicines)
Natural monopolyEconomies of scale: cost per unit keeps falling as output grows, so one big firm is cheaper than several small ones (§7).Utilities (electricity, water, gas)
Control of essential resourcesOne firm owns the input everyone needs.Salt commission (historic state control of salt in China)
Network effectsA product is more valuable the more people use it, so users flock to the biggest one.Tech platforms (WhatsApp, WeChat)
Hong Kong analogy

For decades, CLP Power and HK Electric each supplied electricity in their own areas with no rivals. Building a second grid alongside would double the cost of cables and power stations: a natural monopoly. The government instead regulated their profits through a "Scheme of Control" — a phrase you will meet in exam questions (§8).

2Marginal revenue

Every firm, competitive or not, uses the same rule from Chapter 1:

Profit-maximising rule

Profit \(\pi = TR - TC\) (total revenue minus total cost) is maximised by producing where

\[ MR = MC. \]

In words: keep producing while one more unit adds more to revenue than to cost; stop where the two are equal.

Definitions

Total revenue \(TR = P \times Q\): money received from sales.

Marginal revenue MR = the change in total revenue from selling one additional unit. Example: if total revenue rises from $100 to $150 when the firm sells one more unit, MR = $50.

Marginal cost MC = the change in total cost from producing one additional unit.

For a competitive firm, MR is just the market price: selling one more unit brings in \(P\). For a monopoly it is less than the price, because the monopoly faces the whole downward-sloping demand curve: to sell one more unit it must lower the price, and the lower price applies to every unit, not just the new one.

The shortcut for straight-line demand

MR of a linear demand curve

For a straight-line demand curve, the marginal revenue curve is a straight line that starts at the same point on the vertical axis as demand but has twice the slope:

\[ P = a - bQ \quad\Longrightarrow\quad MR = a - 2bQ. \]

In words: same starting price, falls twice as fast. So MR hits zero halfway to where demand hits zero: at \(Q = a/2b\) instead of \(a/b\).

Three examples: demand reaching the quantity axis at 500 has MR reaching it at 250; demand reaching it at 8 has MR at 4; in general \(a/b\) and \(a/2b\).

Exam trap: demand written as Q = …

Papers often give demand as \(Q = x - yP\) (e.g. \(Q = 660 - 5P\)). The doubling rule works only on the form \(P = a - bQ\), so rearrange first: \(P = x/y - Q/y\), so \(a = x/y\), \(b = 1/y\). For \(Q = 660 - 5P\): \(P = 132 - 0.2Q\) and \(MR = 132 - 0.4Q\).

Going further · why "twice the slope"

\(TR = P \times Q = (a - bQ)Q = aQ - bQ^2\). This is exactly the quadratic total benefit of Ch.1 §5, whose marginal value we found without calculus: \(a - 2bQ\). The same algebra gives a useful second form:

\[ \begin{aligned} MR &= a - 2bQ \\ &= (a - bQ) - bQ \\ &= P - bQ. \end{aligned} \]

In words: MR = price of the extra unit − the price cut \(b\) applied to the \(Q\) units already sold. That is the green-minus-red picture above, made smooth.

Whole units only. If \(Q\) must be a whole number, the MR of the \(n\)-th unit is \(TR(n) - TR(n-1) = a - b(2n - 1)\). Check with the table: \(20 - 2(2 \times 3 - 1) = 10\). ✓

3Profit maximisation in two steps

The monopoly's two steps

1. Choose a quantity. General rule: expand quantity by 1 unit as long as \(MR(Q) \gt MC(Q)\). When quantity is perfectly divisible: choose \(Q\) where \(MR(Q) = MC(Q)\).

2. Choose a price. Charge the highest price you can get away with: the highest price consumers will pay for that quantity. Once you have \(Q\), go straight up to the demand curve and read the price there.

In words: MR tells you how much to sell; demand tells you what to charge.

The classic mistake

Reading the price off the MR curve at \(Q_m\) gives MC, not the monopoly price. The price is always read off demand.

A picture of the monopoly choice

Take a monopoly with constant MC = $0.50 (a simplification: real MC usually varies with output). MR crosses MC at the profit-maximising quantity, 80 units. Going up to demand gives the profit-maximising price, $12.50. At 80 units the average cost (cost per unit, including fixed cost) is $2.50.

Profit as a rectangle \[ \begin{aligned} \pi &= (P - AC) \times Q \\ &= (12.50 - 2.50) \times 80 = \$800. \end{aligned} \]

In words: profit = profit per unit (price minus average cost) × number of units. On the graph it is the rectangle between \(P\) and \(AC\), from 0 to \(Q\) — the shaded profit box in the figure below.

Monopoly choice: MR = MC, then up to demand
demand P = 24.5 − 0.15QMR = 24.5 − 0.3QMCAC = MC + FC/Qprofit

The demand line and FC = 160 are chosen to reproduce P = 12.50, Q = 80, AC = 2.50 and MC = 0.50. Move FC: the quantity and price do not move — only profit does.

Ready answer · linear demand, constant MC \[ \begin{aligned} Q_m &= \frac{a - MC}{2b} \\ P_m &= \frac{a + MC}{2} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the monopoly sells half the quantity a competitive market would (\(Q_e = (a - MC)/b\)), at a price halfway between the choke price \(a\) and MC. Strategy C11.1.

Profit \(= (P_m - MC)Q_m - FC\).

If MC rises with output (e.g. \(MC = 8Q + 30\)), there is no shortcut: solve \(MR = MC\) as two straight lines meeting, then read \(P\) off demand. Profit is then \(TR - TC\) using the total-cost formula given.

Fixed costs do not change the decision

A fixed cost (FC) is paid whatever the quantity: rent, a licence fee, the cost of laying pipes. It does not appear in MR or MC, so it changes neither \(Q_m\) nor \(P_m\). It lowers profit by exactly its own amount.

Producer surplus vs profit

Producer surplus (PS) = total revenue − variable cost = \((P - MC) \times Q\) when MC is constant. Profit = PS − FC.

In words: PS is what the firm earns over the cost of making the units; profit also subtracts the fixed cost. Two firms differing only in FC have the same Q, the same P, the same PS, and different profits. Strategy C11.2.

In the long run a firm can leave the market and stop paying the fixed cost. It stays only if profit ≥ 0, i.e. PS ≥ FC.

Whole-number quantities

Use the general rule of step 1 literally: sell the \(n\)-th unit while its MR, \(a - b(2n - 1)\), is at least MC. Price = \(a - bn\) at the last unit sold.

Worked example · strategy C11.4

\(P = 100 - 10Q\), whole units only, MC = $25.

  1. MR of the \(n\)-th unit: \(100 - 10(2n - 1) = 110 - 20n\).
  2. \(110 - 20n \ge 25\) gives \(n \le 4.25\): sell 4 units (MR of the 4th = 30 ≥ 25; of the 5th = 10 \(\lt\) 25).
  3. Price: \(100 - 10 \times 4 = \$60\).

The deadweight loss in the integer case is counted unit by unit in §15 P3.

4Elasticity and the monopoly markup

Competitive firmCannot mark up: it charges \(P = MC\) and earns zero (normal) profit in the long run.
Firm with monopoly powerCan mark up: it charges \(P \gt MC\) and earns positive (above-normal) profit.
Price elasticity of demand (recall Chapter 5)

\(\eta\) (Greek "eta") = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price. At a point on a straight line \(P = a - bQ\):

\[ \eta = \frac{1}{\text{slope}} \times \frac{P}{Q} = -\frac{1}{b} \cdot \frac{P}{Q}. \]

In words: elasticity at a point = (how much Q changes per $1 of price) × (price ÷ quantity). It is always negative for demand. Demand is elastic when \(|\eta| \gt 1\) and inelastic when \(|\eta| \lt 1\).

The markup formula \[ P = \frac{\eta}{1 + \eta}\,MC \]

or, equivalently,

\[ P = \Big(1 + \frac{1}{|\eta| - 1}\Big)MC \] \[ \text{Markup} = \frac{P - MC}{MC} = \frac{1}{|\eta| - 1} \]

In words: the markup is how far price sits above marginal cost, as a fraction of MC. The less elastic the demand at the chosen point (\(|\eta|\) close to 1), the bigger the markup.

Exams quote the markup as a percentage: \((P - MC)/MC \times 100\). It is over MC, not over the price.

Going further · where the formula comes from

From §2, \(MR = P - bQ\). Since \(\eta = -P/(bQ)\), we have \(bQ = -P/\eta\), so

\[ MR = P\Big(1 + \frac{1}{\eta}\Big). \]

Setting \(MR = MC\) and solving for \(P\) gives \(P = \frac{\eta}{1+\eta}MC\). Two consequences exams test:

  • If MC \(\gt 0\), MR must be positive at the optimum, so \(1 + 1/\eta \gt 0\), i.e. \(|\eta| \gt 1\). A monopoly with positive marginal cost always prices where demand is elastic. (True in 2025 Spring Supp Q25B.)
  • Check against the worked example of §9: \(\eta = -1.078\) gives \(P = \frac{-1.078}{-0.078} \times 6 \approx 83\). ✓

Inelastic demand → big markup

Picture two monopolies with the same MC. On the left, a steep demand curve (relatively inelastic): price \(P_i\) sits far above MC — a big markup. On the right, a flat demand curve (relatively elastic): price \(P_e\) is only a little above MC — a small markup. Buyers with few alternatives cannot walk away, so the monopoly can push the price further.

Steeper (less elastic) demand, bigger markup
demand P = a − (a/60)QMRMC = 10

All demand lines reach the quantity axis at 60; raising \(a\) makes demand steeper. At the default \(a = 50\): \(Q_m = 24\), \(P_m = 30\), \(\eta = -1.5\), markup \(= 1/(1.5 - 1) = 200\%\).

Exam statements (C11.7)

"A monopoly facing a relatively more inelastic demand curve is more likely to charge a higher price" — True. Swap in "elastic" and it is False (2022 Spring Final Q14(iv)).

5The cost of monopoly: deadweight loss

By charging a price above marginal cost, a firm with monopoly power reduces total surplus (the gains from trade to society):

  • It produces less output and charges a higher price than a competitive industry with the same costs.
  • There are dissatisfied consumers whose willingness to pay is larger than the marginal cost of production, yet they are not served. The surplus those trades would have created is lost: the deadweight loss (DWL).
Recall: surplus areas (Chapter 3)

Consumer surplus (CS) = area between demand and the price, up to the quantity sold. Producer surplus (PS) = area between the price and MC. Total surplus = CS + PS. The efficient quantity \(Q_e\) is where demand meets MC: every unit worth more to a buyer than it costs to make gets made.

Monopoly deadweight loss \[ \begin{aligned} DWL &= \tfrac12 (P_m - MC)(Q_e - Q_m) \\ &\text{(constant MC)} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the triangle between demand and MC, from the monopoly quantity to the efficient quantity. Height = monopoly price − MC; base = units not produced.

With rising MC the region is still a triangle: height \(P_m - MC(Q_m)\) (MC at the monopoly quantity), base \(Q_e - Q_m\). Strategy C11.3.

With linear demand and constant MC, \(Q_e = 2Q_m\): the monopoly produces exactly half the efficient amount.

Competition vs monopoly: who gets what
consumer surplusproducer surplusdeadweight loss

Numbers from the worked example (§9): \(P = 160 - 0.2Q\), MC = 6. Competition: \(Q_e = 770\), CS = 59290, no DWL. Monopoly: \(Q_m = 385\), \(P_m = 83\), CS = 14822.5, PS = 29645, DWL = 14822.5. The three monopoly areas add up to the competitive total.

The message in one line: competition maximises social surplus; monopolies do not. Part of the old consumer surplus becomes the monopolist's profit (a transfer, not a loss), and the triangle disappears altogether (a true loss).

6Corruption, and the case for monopoly: R&D and patents

Corruption and inefficiency

Many monopolies are created by corrupt politicians, who hand the sole right to a market to friends. An example: cloves (丁香, the aromatic flower buds of a tree) are used in clove cigarettes, which are popular in Indonesia. The Clove Marketing Board in Indonesia was made the monopolist controlling the clove market — a legal barrier created to benefit insiders, with the usual monopoly result of high prices and a deadweight loss.

Incentives for research and development

Would AIDS drugs be available if every drug producer were forced to be perfectly competitive?

  • Under perfect competition, profits are competed away, at least in the long run; any short-run profits are small because of keen competition.
  • Innovation often requires a lot of spending on research and development (R&D): years of lab work and clinical trials before a single pill is sold.
  • A meagre short-run profit and zero long-run profit do not give enough incentive to pay for that R&D. Copies would undercut the inventor the moment the drug works.
  • From society's perspective, the benefit of the R&D likely outweighs its cost. So we want it done.

The fix: give additional incentive by making the company that develops the drug a monopoly for an extended period — a patent. The hard question is "how long": longer protection means more incentive to invent, but more years of monopoly price and deadweight loss.

The patent trade-off

A patent accepts a deadweight loss today in exchange for inventions that would otherwise not exist at all.

In words: a monopoly on a drug is worse than a competitive market for that drug, but much better than no drug. (True/False favourite: "one reason the government issues patents is to give firms incentives to conduct R&D" — True.)

Patent buyouts

Monopoly often results in deadweight loss. Can we remove the loss without weakening the incentive to innovate? One proposal is a patent buyout:

  1. The government pays the patent holder the value of the patent (so the inventor is still rewarded, keeping the R&D incentive).
  2. It then lets other firms use the patent at a small cost, encouraging competition.
  3. Competition drives the price down towards MC, eliminating the deadweight loss.
Going further · the catch

The buyout money comes from taxes, and the government must judge what a patent is worth. Overpay and taxpayers lose; underpay and inventors invent less. That is why buyouts remain an open question rather than a settled policy.

7Natural monopoly and economies of scale

Natural monopoly

A natural monopoly exists when a single firm can supply the entire market at a lower cost than two or more firms could.

  • It can emerge when economies of scale (average cost falling as output rises) are present over the relevant range of output.
  • The largest firm produces at a lower cost per unit than smaller firms, so only one firm tends to survive.

In words: when the big fixed cost is spread over more units, each unit gets cheaper — so splitting the market between rivals just duplicates the fixed cost.

The usual cause: large fixed costs and a decreasing average cost over the relevant range of output. With constant MC and fixed cost FC, average cost is

\[ AC(Q) = MC + \frac{FC}{Q}, \]

In words: cost per unit = extra cost per unit + the fixed cost shared out over all units. The second part shrinks as \(Q\) grows, so AC falls for ever, approaching MC.

One firm vs two firms sharing the market
AC, one firm making all QAC, two firms each making Q/2MC = 20

Each firm has MC = $20 and a fixed cost of $1000. At Q = 50 one firm's AC is 20 + 1000/50 = $40; two firms making 25 each have AC = 20 + 1000/25 = $60.

Picture it: small competitive firms have high average costs, so even a competitive price \(P_c\) is high. A single monopoly with large economies of scale can charge a lower price \(P_m\) than the competitive firms, even after its markup — though it still produces less than the optimal quantity \(Q_{\text{optimal}}\) where demand meets its MC. That gap is why natural monopolies are regulated rather than broken up. In order, the quantities are \(Q_{\text{competitive}} \lt Q_{\text{monopoly}} \lt Q_{\text{optimal}}\).

8A price control on a monopoly can increase output

In Chapter 7 a price ceiling on a competitive market always cut the quantity traded. On a monopoly it can do the opposite.

Why output rises

Without regulation the monopoly picks \(P_m, Q_m\). Now the government sets a maximum price \(P_R\) below \(P_m\) (but above MC).

  • Up to the quantity buyers want at \(P_R\), every unit sells for exactly \(P_R\). Selling one more no longer forces a price cut on earlier units, so the new marginal revenue is flat at \(P_R\) — like a competitive firm's.
  • Beyond that quantity, the firm would have to drop below \(P_R\) to sell more, and MR jumps down to the old MR curve.
  • The firm sells while new MR ≥ MC, so it sells all the way to the demand curve at \(P_R\): \(Q_R = \) quantity demanded at \(P_R\), larger than \(Q_m\).
Monopoly under a ceiling \(MC \lt P_c \lt P_m\) \[ \begin{aligned} Q &= Q_d(P_c) = \frac{a - P_c}{b} \\ DWL &= \tfrac12 (P_c - MC)(Q_e - Q) \\ PS &= (P_c - MC)\,Q \end{aligned} \]

In words: the firm sells what buyers want at the ceiling. The deadweight loss shrinks to a small triangle below the ceiling. In the long run it stays only if PS ≥ fixed cost. Strategy C11.5.

The ideal ceiling, P = MC, and why it fails

The optimal price is \(P = MC\): output reaches the efficient quantity and the DWL disappears. But at that price the firm earns nothing over MC on each unit, so it cannot cover its fixed cost: it makes a loss equal to the fixed cost (the red rectangle at point b) and will exit the industry in the long run.

The lowest workable ceiling: P = AC

The lowest price that keeps the monopolist in the industry is where price equals average cost on the demand curve (point c). There the monopolist makes zero (normal) profit.

Ready answer · lowest (optimal) ceiling \[ \begin{aligned} a - bQ &= MC + \frac{FC}{Q} \\ bQ^2 - (a - MC)Q &+ FC = 0 \end{aligned} \]

In words: find where demand meets the average-cost curve. Solve the quadratic and take the larger root (more output, lower price); then the ceiling is \(P = a - bQ\) there. The smaller root is also P = AC, but at a high price — not the "lowest" ceiling.

Slide a ceiling down: quantity, DWL and the firm's profit
demandMR the firm facesMC = 6AC (purple dot: P = AC)DWLproducer surplus

Demand \(P = 160 - 0.2Q\), MC = 6. With fixed cost $15000 the lowest workable ceiling is about $28.88 (Q ≈ 655.6). Set the ceiling to 6 (= MC) to see the firm's loss; set it above 83 and the ceiling stops binding.

Going further · ceilings exams add

"Scheme of Control" at the average of \(P_m\) and MC: just a ceiling at \((P_m + MC)/2\); then use the box above.

Rising MC (e.g. \(MC = 0.6Q + 9\)): the flat new MR at \(P_c\) can hit the MC curve before it reaches demand. The firm sells the smaller of \(Q_d(P_c)\) and the quantity where \(MC = P_c\). (2024 Spring Final Q50: \(Q_d(25) = 40\) but \(MC = 25\) at \(Q = 26.67\), so 26.67.)

Ceiling at MC with fixed cost: the firm exits "as soon as possible" unless the government gives a lump-sum payment of at least the fixed cost (2022 Spring Final Q67: support ≥ FC = 10006).

9A worked example, solved in full

This is the same set of numbers as 2020 Fall Final Q60–65: past students met this example word for word on the final.

Setting

A monopolist faces demand \(P = 160 - 0.2Q\) and has a constant marginal cost of $6.

(a) Profit-maximising quantity and price
  1. \(MR = 160 - 0.4Q\) (twice the slope).
  2. \(MR = MC\): \(160 - 0.4Q = 6\), so \(Q_m = 154/0.4 = 385\).
  3. Up to demand: \(P_m = 160 - 0.2 \times 385 = 83\).

Q = 385, P = $83

(b) Deadweight loss without intervention
  1. Efficient quantity: demand = MC, \(160 - 0.2Q = 6\), so \(Q_e = 770\).
  2. \(DWL = \tfrac12 (83 - 6)(770 - 385) = \tfrac12 \times 77 \times 385\).

DWL = $14822.5

(c) Elasticity at the monopoly point \[ \begin{aligned} \eta &= \frac{1}{\text{slope}} \times \frac{P}{Q} \\ &= \frac{1}{-0.2} \times \frac{83}{385} = -1.078 \end{aligned} \]

Elastic (\(|\eta| \gt 1\)), as §4 predicts. Markup \(= (83 - 6)/6 = 1283.3\%\), and \(1/(|\eta| - 1) = 1/0.078 \approx 12.83\). ✓

(d) Price ceiling at $7
  1. Quantity: the firm sells what buyers want at $7: \(Q = (160 - 7)/0.2 = 765\).
  2. New DWL: \(\tfrac12 (7 - 6)(770 - 765) = 2.5\). (Down from 14822.5 — a decrease of 14820, which is how exams ask it.)
  3. Maximum fixed cost to stay in the long run = short-run producer surplus: \((7 - 6) \times 765 = 765\).

Q = 765, DWL = $2.5, stays if FC ≤ $765

(e) Fixed cost $29645: the optimal price ceiling
  1. Set average cost equal to demand: \(6 + 29645/Q = 160 - 0.2Q\).
  2. Multiply by \(Q\) and rearrange: \(0.2Q^2 - 154Q + 29645 = 0\).
  3. Discriminant: \(154^2 - 4(0.2)(29645) = 23716 - 23716 = 0\): one (double) root, \(Q = 154/0.4 = 385\).
  4. Ceiling \(= 160 - 0.2 \times 385 = 83\).

Optimal ceiling = $83

Why the root is double: $29645 is exactly the unregulated monopoly profit, \(77 \times 385\). With a fixed cost that large, the firm only just breaks even at its own monopoly price, so any lower ceiling drives it out — the "optimal" ceiling is \(P_m\) itself (2020 Fall Final Q64). With a smaller fixed cost, the quadratic has two roots: take the larger.

(f) Per-unit subsidy of $1 to the monopolist
  1. A subsidy of $1 per unit lowers the firm's effective MC to \(6 - 1 = 5\).
  2. \(160 - 0.4Q = 5\), so \(Q = 387.5\); price \(= 160 - 0.2 \times 387.5 = 82.5\).
  3. DWL is measured with the true MC (society still uses $6 of resources per unit): \(\tfrac12 (82.5 - 6)(770 - 387.5)\).

DWL = $14630.625 (only slightly below 14822.5)

The figure shades the DWL as the triangle between demand and the true MC line from 387.5 to 770; the effective-MC line sits $1 lower.

How big must a per-unit subsidy be?
demandMRtrue MC = 6effective MC = 6 − sDWL

On the §9 numbers. A $1 subsidy barely helps; it takes \(s = 154\) (\(= 0.2 \times 770\)) to push output to the efficient 770 and remove the DWL. Past the efficient quantity the subsidy causes over-production and a new DWL.

Ready answer · subsidy that eliminates the DWL (linear demand, constant MC) \[ s = b\,Q_e \]

In words: the subsidy must close the gap between price and MR at the efficient quantity, which is \(bQ_e\). Strategy C11.6.

10Other forms of regulation, other sources of market power

Besides price controls, there are other options:

Lump-sum subsidyA fixed payment, independent of output. It does not change MR or MC, so Q and P stay put; it only raises profit. Useful to keep a firm alive under a P = MC ceiling (§8).
Per-unit subsidyPaid for each unit. It lowers the effective MC, so Q rises and P falls (§9).
Public ownershipThe government runs the monopoly itself and can choose to price nearer MC, covering losses from taxes (e.g. MTR's early years, water supply).

The same logic covers licence fees: a yearly licence fee is a fixed cost, so the monopoly's Q and P do not change and profit falls by the fee (2023 Summer Final Q61–62).

Going further · subsidising consumers instead

If consumers get \(s\) per unit, they will pay up to \(s\) more, so the demand the firm faces shifts up: \(P = a + s - bQ\). Then \(MR = a + s - 2bQ\), and \(MR = MC\) is the same equation as \(a - 2bQ = MC - s\). Same quantity as a firm subsidy of the same size, so the same DWL (2024 Spring Final Q53 and Q54 both give 30.24). The market price is higher by \(s\) on the shifted curve, but buyers' net price is the same.

In every subsidy question, the DWL is measured with the true demand and the true MC.

PatentsGlaxoSmithKline's patent on Combivir (an AIDS drug)

"Innovation" power is temporary: the first mover has the market to itself until rivals catch up.

11A final important remark: a monopoly has no supply curve

A supply curve answers: "at each price, how much will the seller offer?" A competitive firm has one, because it takes the price as given and produces where \(P = MC\): its MC curve is its supply curve (Chapter 10).

Try the same with a monopoly. Fix a quantity \(Q^*\). Is there one price at which the monopoly is willing to supply exactly \(Q^*\)?

  • If the monopoly chooses \(Q^*\), it must be that \(MR = MC\) at \(Q^*\).
  • If the MR curve through that point is MR1, demand must be D1 (steep), and the price is \(P_1\).
  • If instead it is MR2, demand must be D2 (flat), and the price is \(P_2\).
  • The same quantity \(Q^*\) goes with different prices depending on demand.
Same Q*, same MC, different prices

\(MC = 10 + 2Q\), so MC = 30 at \(Q^* = 10\). D1: \(P = 90 - 3Q\), MR1 = 90 − 6Q = 30 at Q = 10, price $60. D2: \(P = 40 - 0.5Q\), MR2 = 40 − Q = 30 at Q = 10, price $35.

Conclusion

The price that goes with a given quantity depends on the shape of demand, so a monopoly has no independent price–quantity relationship describing its supply.

In words: a monopoly does not ask "what will I sell at this price?" — it picks the price itself, using demand. So "monopoly supply curve" is meaningless; draw MC, MR and demand instead.

12Summary

  • Market power = ability to price above MC without attracting entry. Monopolies rest on barriers to entry: legal (patents, licences), natural monopoly (economies of scale), control of essential resources, network effects.
  • MR \(\lt\) P for a monopoly: selling one more means cutting the price on every unit. Linear demand \(P = a - bQ\) → \(MR = a - 2bQ\). Rearrange \(Q = x - yP\) first.
  • Two steps: \(MR = MC\) gives \(Q_m\); go up to demand for \(P_m\). Whole units: sell while \(a - b(2n-1) \ge MC\).
  • Profit = \((P - AC)Q\) = PS − FC. Fixed costs and licence fees change profit, not Q, P or PS. Stay in the long run iff PS ≥ FC.
  • Markup \(= (P - MC)/MC = 1/(|\eta| - 1)\); \(P = \frac{\eta}{1+\eta}MC\). Less elastic demand → bigger markup. With MC \(\gt 0\) the monopoly prices on the elastic part.
  • DWL = triangle between demand and MC from \(Q_m\) to \(Q_e\) (where demand = MC). Patents accept this DWL to fund R&D; patent buyouts try to remove it.
  • Natural monopoly: big fixed cost, falling AC; one firm is cheapest.
  • Price ceiling between MC and \(P_m\): new MR is flat at the ceiling, output rises to \(Q_d(P_c)\), DWL falls. P = MC is efficient but the firm loses FC and exits; the lowest workable ceiling is P = AC (larger root).
  • Per-unit subsidy: effective MC = MC − s; firm-side and consumer-side subsidies give the same Q. Measure DWL with true demand and true MC. Removing the DWL needs \(s = bQ_e\).
  • A monopoly has no supply curve: the price for a given Q depends on demand.

13Common mistakes

✗Reading the price off MR. ✓ Q from MR = MC, price from demand. ✗Doubling the slope of \(Q = x - yP\) directly. ✓ Rewrite as \(P = x/y - Q/y\) first. ✗Calling the MR = MC quantity "efficient". ✓ Efficient is where demand = MC. ✗Thinking a higher fixed cost raises the price. ✓ FC changes profit only (and the long-run stay/exit decision). ✗Confusing profit with producer surplus. ✓ PS ignores fixed cost; profit = PS − FC. ✗Markup over price, or as a decimal when the blank says percent. ✓ \((P - MC)/MC \times 100\). ✗Under a ceiling, producing where the old MR equals the ceiling. ✓ Sell the quantity demanded at the ceiling (or where MC = ceiling if MC rises and binds first). ✗Giving the DWL level when the blank asks for the change. ✓ Change = old DWL − new DWL, with "decreases". ✗Taking the smaller root of the P = AC quadratic. ✓ Larger Q, lower price. ✗Measuring a subsidy's DWL against the effective MC or the shifted demand. ✓ True demand, true MC. ✗Using \(a - 2bQ\) when quantities must be whole numbers. ✓ Unit-by-unit MR \(a - b(2n-1)\); count lost units one by one for the DWL.

14Find the mistake

Each "solution" below has one error. Find it before opening.

1

\(P = 100 - 2Q\), MC = 20. "MR = 100 − 4Q = 20 gives Q = 20. The price is MR at Q = 20, which is $20."

Mistake

Price comes from demand, not MR: \(P = 100 - 2 \times 20 = \$60\). (MR at \(Q_m\) always equals MC — that is how \(Q_m\) was found.)

2

Demand \(Q = 90 - 3P\), MC = 6. "MR = 90 − 6Q = 6, so Q = 14."

Mistake

The doubling rule needs \(P\) on the left: \(P = 30 - Q/3\), so \(MR = 30 - \tfrac23 Q = 6\), giving Q = 36 and \(P = 30 - 12 = \$18\).

3

\(P = 160 - 0.2Q\), MC = 6, fixed cost $20000. Lowest ceiling the firm accepts in the long run: "\(0.2Q^2 - 154Q + 20000 = 0\) has roots Q ≈ 165.4 and Q ≈ 604.6; the first gives P ≈ $126.92."

Mistake

That is the smaller root, a high price. The lowest ceiling uses the larger root: \(Q \approx 604.6\), \(P \approx \$39.08\).

4 · §9 numbers

Ceiling at $7 in the §9 example. "The deadweight loss decreases by $2.5."

Mistake

$2.5 is the new DWL level. The decrease is \(14822.5 - 2.5 = \$14820\) (2020 Fall Final Q62).

Same firm. (a) A ceiling of $20: quantity, DWL before and after, and does the firm stay in the long run? (b) The lowest ceiling the firm accepts in the long run.

\(P = 100 - 10Q\), whole units only, MC = $25. Find the price and the deadweight loss.

MR of the \(n\)-th unit = \(110 - 20n\). The efficient last unit is the last one with WTP \(100 - 10n \ge 25\).

16Concept checks

1

A monopoly with MC = $5 sets its price where demand has elasticity −0.8. Is it maximising profit?

Answer

No. Where \(|\eta| \lt 1\), MR is negative; cutting output would raise revenue and save cost. A profit-maximising monopoly with MC \(\gt 0\) is always on the elastic part.

2

A monopoly's fixed cost rises by $100 a year. What happens to its price, quantity, producer surplus and profit?

Answer

Price, quantity and PS unchanged; profit falls by $100. (If profit turns negative, it exits in the long run.)

3

A price ceiling is set between MC and the monopoly price. Does output rise or fall, and why?

Answer

Rises. Up to the quantity demanded at the ceiling, extra units no longer force a price cut on earlier units, so MR is flat at the ceiling, above MC.

4

Why can the government not simply impose P = MC on a natural monopoly?

Answer

At P = MC the firm earns nothing towards its fixed cost: loss = FC, so it exits in the long run — unless given a lump-sum subsidy ≥ FC.

5

Compare a $5-per-unit subsidy to the monopoly with a $5-per-unit subsidy to its customers.

Answer

Same quantity and the same DWL: both lift MR relative to MC by $5 per unit.

6

Why can't we draw a supply curve for a monopoly?

Answer

The price it charges for a given quantity depends on the demand curve (through MR), not on a fixed price–quantity rule.
