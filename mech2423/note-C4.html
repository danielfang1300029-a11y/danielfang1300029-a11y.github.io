Fasteners and power transmission: threads, bolts, gears, bearings

How screw threads, bolts, gears and bearings are drawn (the conventions examiners plant mistakes in), how threads are named and graded, and the handful of gear formulas you can be asked to calculate. Catalogue material is covered in quick tables.

0What the test wants

Medium The drawing conventions here (threads, gears, bearings) feed the 20-mark find-the-mistakes question: a wrongly drawn thread is a classic planted error. The gear formulas and bolt grades are the most likely short calculations. You need to be able to:

  • Draw and check external, internal and assembled threads: which line is thick, which is thin, the ¾ circle, where the hatching stops (§5, §6, drills 1–4).
  • Draw a spur gear and a bearing by convention and spot hatching errors (§11, §14, drills 5–6).
  • Work the gear formulas from module and tooth number (§10), decode M16×2-4h-5H and 3/4-10-UNC-2A (§3), and turn grade 10.9 into strengths (§4).
  • Quote the rules of thumb: lead = starts × pitch, screw length 2–2.5× thickness, rivet Ø ≥ 3× thickness, hole spacing 2D/3D (§2, §7).

1Mechanical systems and the fastener list sl. 1–4, 87

Quick pass

Mechanical system sl. 2: a power unit (motor, engine) drives a transmission unit (gears, cams, bearings), which drives a motion unit (e.g. a 4-bar chain). A control unit (mechatronics) supervises all three. Everything sits on a mount/framework, joined by fasteners, inside a housing.

Fastener sl. 3: a hardware device that mechanically joins objects together. Common ones: a) nail, b) screw, c) pins, d) rivets, e) key and keyway, f) clip and retaining ring, g) clamp, h) hook and loop (the slide writes "hoop").

Summary sl. 87: mechanical power transmission is the transfer of energy from its source to another place using mechanical elements: shafts, shims and spacers, machine keys, bearings and balls, shaft collars.

The mechanical system sl. 2

2Screw basics: parts, thread forms, lead and pitch sl. 5–9

Must know
  • Major diameter: the largest diameter (thread crests of a bolt). Minor diameter: the smallest (roots of a bolt). Pitch P: distance from one thread to the next, measured along the axis.
  • Lead: how far the screw advances axially in one turn. Lead = number of starts × pitch.
  • Single thread: lead = pitch, higher strength. Multiple threads: lead > pitch, quick assembly.
  • Screws allow disassembly (unlike rivets or welds).

Terminology sl. 6. A bolt has a head, a plain shank and a threaded part; a small radius joins head and shank, and the runout is where the thread fades out. Thread length + grip length (the unthreaded part that passes through the clamped parts) = nominal length, measured from under the head. On the thread itself: the crest (top), root (bottom), flanks (sloping sides), thread angle (60° for metric) and depth of thread (crest to root). The slide also shows drive types: slot, Phillips, slotted-Phillips combo, square, Phillips-square combo, Phillips hex head, slotted hex, six-lobe.

Bolt and thread terminology sl. 6
Quick pass · thread forms (sl. 7) and screw types (sl. 9)

Thread forms: sharp V (60°), Unified (60°, crests/roots flat or rounded), Whitworth (55°, rounded), modified square (10° included), Acme (29°), B&S worm (29°), modified buttress (45°/7°), knuckle (rounded), Dardelet. Profiles only; no need to learn the dimensions.

Five screw categories: 1) wood, 2) sheet metal ★, 3) machine ★★★, 4) drywall, 5) lag. The stars are the slide's: machine screws (threads for a nut or tapped hole) matter most, then sheet-metal screws (fully threaded, pointed). Also shown: thread-cutting machine screws (cut their own thread).

"What are the concerns in a screw?" sl. 5 is a title-only slide (a discussion prompt). The concerns are answered in §3–§4: form, size, pitch, fit, grade, material, companions.

Lead and pitch: single and multiple starts (written for these notes)

Same pitch P in both. A double-start thread has two helices side by side, so one turn moves it 2P. The slide also shows coarse vs fine threads (fewer or more threads per inch) and right-hand vs left-hand threads.

Worked example

An M10×1.5 lead screw must move a nut 30 mm. How many turns with a single-start and with a double-start thread?

Single: lead = 1 × 1.5 = 1.5 mm, turns = 30 / 1.5 = 20. Double: lead = 2 × 1.5 = 3 mm, turns = 30 / 3 = 10. Faster assembly, but the single-start thread is stronger and less likely to loosen.

\[ \text{lead} = n \times P \]In words: the distance gained per turn is the number of starts times the pitch.

3Selecting a screw and reading thread designations sl. 10–18, 24

Must know
  • ISO metric: M16 × 2 - 4h - 5H = metric, nominal (major) Ø 16 mm, pitch 2 mm, tolerance class of this thread 4h, class of the mating thread 5H (both optional). If the pitch is left off (M16) it is the coarse pitch.
  • Lower-case letter = external thread (bolt), capital = internal (nut). ISO fits: 6H/6g medium (general engineering), 5H/4h close (accurate, needs good equipment), 7H/8g free (quick assembly, even dirty or slightly damaged).
  • Unified: 3/4 - 10 UNC - 2A = major Ø 3/4 in, 10 threads per inch, form UN, series C (coarse), class of fit 2, A = external (B = internal).
  • Form: UN (flat crest and root) or UNR (rounded root, external only). Series: UNC coarse, UNF fine, UNEF extra fine, constant pitch (e.g. 8UN). Class: 1 loose, 2 general commercial, 3 close.
Quick pass · selection criteria (sl. 11)

Coarse or fine · thread specification · size and strength · grade and strength · material · working companion (washers, nuts: §7).

Unified format sl. 13: Major Ø – threads per inch, Form, Series – Class of fit, Ext or Int. The slide's example callout on a tapped hole reads .5-13UNC-3B: ½ in major Ø, 13 threads per inch, Unified coarse, class 3 (close), B = internal.

4Strength grades: metric and SAE sl. 19–23

Must know
  • Metric grade a.b: tensile strength = a × 100 MPa; yield = 0.b × tensile.
  • 10.9 → tensile 1000 MPa, yield 0.9 × 1000 = 900 MPa. 8.8 → 800 and 640 MPa. 12.9 → 1200 and 1080 MPa.
  • Rough equivalents: 4.8 ≈ SAE 2, 8.8 ≈ SAE 5, 10.9 ≈ SAE 8. SAE heads are marked with radial lines (none, 3, 6).
  • 1 ksi = 6.895 MPa.

The two numbers are stamped on the bolt head sl. 22. Think of it as "hundreds of MPa, then the fraction that is safe before permanent stretch": in 10.9 the 10 is 1/100 of the tensile strength in MPa and the .9 is yield ÷ tensile.

Worked example

Grade 8.8: tensile = 8 × 100 = 800 MPa; yield = 0.8 × 800 = 640 MPa. Grade 12.9: tensile 1200 MPa, yield 0.9 × 1200 = 1080 MPa.

\[ \sigma_{UTS} = 100a \ \text{MPa} \] \[ \sigma_y = 0.b \times \sigma_{UTS} \]In words: the first number times 100 is the tensile strength; the decimal is the yield-to-tensile ratio.

5Screw threads in drawings ★ sl. 25–32

  • One rule covers all four rows: the edge you can actually see is thick (bolt crests, hole edge); the thread depth beyond it is thin.
  • Thread limit (end of useful thread): a thick line across.
  • In section, hatching runs up to the thick line (it "crosses the thread"). Tapped holes: drill deeper than the thread, 120° drill point. Hidden threads: all lines dashed.
  • Assembly in section: the external thread covers the internal thread; bolts and screws are not hatched.

A real thread is a helix: drawing every crest would take hours and say nothing more. So the drawing standard replaces it with two lines at the two diameters. Why is one thick and one thin? Look at a bolt from the side: its outline is the crests (major Ø), a real visible edge, so it gets the visible-edge line (thick). The roots (minor Ø) are only "where the thread bottoms out", so they get a thin line. A tapped hole is the opposite: what you see when you look into it is the drilled hole (minor Ø), drawn thick, while the thread cut into the wall goes out to the major Ø, drawn thin. Learn "visible edge thick, thread depth thin" and you never need the table.

External threads

External thread: the convention (written for these notes)

Side view: thick outline at the major Ø, thin lines at the minor Ø running to the chamfered end, thick line across at the thread limit. End view: thick full circle (major), thin ¾ circle (minor); the chamfer circle is left out. A quarter of the thin circle is left open so nobody mistakes it for a hidden or visible full circle (the handout leaves the gap in different quadrants; any one quadrant is fine).

Internal thread: the convention (written for these notes)

Section: thick lines at the hole (minor Ø), thin lines at the major Ø, a thick line across at the thread limit, and the drill carried deeper with a 120° point. The hatching goes right up to the thick hole edge, crossing the thin line. End view: thick full circle (minor), thin ¾ circle (major).

Through tapping and the rules in words sl. 30

The five rules on the slide: (1) tapping-drill depth > useful thread length; (2) drill point included angle 120°; (3) in section the hatching crosses the thread; (4) a gap is shown in the major-Ø circle; (5) note the thick and thin lines.

Assembled threads

When a screw sits in a tapped hole and the drawing is sectioned, two sets of thread lines would sit on top of each other. The rule sl. 31: external threads are shown covering internal threads. Over the engaged length you draw the screw's lines (thick major, thin minor) and stop the block's hatching at the screw's major Ø. Only where the hole goes deeper than the screw do the internal-thread lines appear. The screw itself is cut by the section plane, but bolts, screws, nuts, washers, shafts, keys and pins are never hatched along their length: hatching them adds nothing and hides the thread.

7Installation: washers, nuts, hole spacing, retaining rings, rivets sl. 35–45

Must know
  • Screw length = 2 to 2.5 × the thickness of material being fastened.
  • Hole location (D = hole Ø): centre at least 2D from any edge, holes at least 3D apart.
  • Bolt installation: grip length at least as long as the hole; no more than three washers; at least one thread showing past the nut.
  • Rivets: same material as the parts; rivet Ø ≥ 3 × thickness; pitch ≥ 3D, tail ≥ 1.5D.
Minimum hole distances sl. 37
Worked example

Two plates, 5 mm and 7 mm, are joined with M10 screws in Ø11 clearance holes. Screw length ≈ 2–2.5 × 12 = 24–30 mm. Hole centres ≥ 2 × 11 = 22 mm from each edge and ≥ 3 × 11 = 33 mm apart. If the plates were riveted instead and the thinner plate is 3 mm, the rivet Ø ≥ 3 × 3 = 9 mm.

Quick pass · companions, nut and washer types (sl. 36, 38–41)

Working companions (sl. 36): washers (plain washer spreads load; spring washer resists loosening) and nuts.

Bolt installation (sl. 38): grip length at least as long as the hole; castle or self-locking nut with a cotter pin; no more than three washers; at least one thread showing.

Nut types (sl. 39): hex, heavy hex, nylon-insert lock (Nylock), jam (thin), nylon-insert jam, wing (hand tightening), cap (domed), acorn (high domed cap), flange (built-in washer), tee (driven into wood), square, prevailing-torque lock, K-lock/Kep (attached tooth washer), coupling (long, joins two rods), slotted and castle (with cotter pin, prevent loosening).

Washer types (sl. 40): flat, fender (oversize, soft materials), finishing (oval-head screws), split lock (most common anti-loosening), external- and internal-tooth lock, square, dock (thick, large), ogee (cast iron, docks and wood construction).

Retaining ring (sl. 41): also called a snap ring or circlip; a metal fastener fitted into a groove on a shaft or in a bore/housing to hold an assembly in place. Shown: external and internal rings, bowed (preloading), radial, spiral rings.

Quick pass · rivets (sl. 42–45)

Types (sl. 43): solid head, high strength, blind (set from one side), semi-tubular, drive, flush, friction-lock, self-pierce. Arrangement (sl. 44): chain riveting (rows in line) or diagonal/zig-zag riveting.

Selection (sl. 45): pitch ≥ 3D; tail sticking out ≥ 1.5D before forming; formed head ≥ 1.5D wide and ≥ 0.5D high; countersunk solid rivets use a 100° countersink, and the top sheet must be thick enough (e.g. D = 1/8 in needs T ≥ 0.042 in; 3/32 → .036, 5/32 → .055, 3/16 → .070).

9Gear types and choosing one sl. 50–54, 62–65

Quick pass · selection (sl. 51–53)

Selection tree: linear ↔ rotational motion → gear and rack. Rotational → rotational: parallel shafts → spur, epicyclic; perpendicular shafts → bevel, worm, crown, hypoid.

Types pictured (sl. 52): spur, helical, double helical, screw, internal, spur rack, helical rack, face, straight bevel, spiral bevel, hypoid, Zerol, worm and worm gear, double enveloping, worm gear. Compound gear set (sl. 53): different gear pairs combined on shared shafts.

Quick pass · helical gears and shaft layouts (sl. 62–65)

Helical gear (sl. 62): the leading edges of the teeth are set at an angle to the axis, not parallel. It runs more smoothly and quietly than a spur gear. The slide asks "Why?": because each tooth engages gradually, starting at one end and spreading across the face, and several teeth share the load at once, instead of a whole tooth width hitting at once (this explanation is not on the slide).

Shafts (sl. 63): parallel: meshing teeth meet at a common tangent, can carry higher load. Non-parallel (crossed helical): only point contact between tooth surfaces, light load. Sl. 64 draws parallel shafts in external engagement (gear-pair outlines). Sl. 65 summary poster: spur = simple, efficient, parallel shafts; helical = smooth, quiet, high speed, parallel or crossed; bevel = intersecting shafts (often 90°); worm = high reduction, self-locking, non-intersecting perpendicular shafts.

The words first sl. 55–56. Two meshing gears behave like two discs rolling on each other without slipping; those imaginary discs are the pitch circles, and their diameter is the pitch circle diameter (PCD). The tooth sticks out beyond the pitch circle by the addendum (to the addendum or tip circle) and is cut in below it by the dedendum (to the dedendum or root circle). The dedendum is a little bigger than the addendum so the tip of one gear never touches the root of the other: the difference is the clearance. Working depth = how far two meshing teeth overlap (2 addenda); whole depth = addendum + dedendum. Circular pitch = tooth-to-tooth distance measured along the pitch circle; half of it is tooth, half is space (tooth thickness and width of space). Face width = length of the teeth along the axis. The smaller gear of a pair is the pinion; the larger is the wheel.

Gear terminology sl. 55–56

Module is the size of a tooth: M = PCD ÷ N, so a gear with 25 teeth on a 250 mm pitch circle has 10 mm of pitch diameter per tooth. Two gears can only mesh if they have the same module (so the same circular pitch πM) and the same pressure angle. Slide 59 asks "How to ensure two gears can match together?" without the answer on the slide; this is the standard answer.

Slide 57 defines diametral pitch as Pd = N ÷ PCD, which is just 1/M in teeth per mm. In US practice Pd is teeth per inch of PCD; if a question gives Pd in inches, convert with M = 25.4 / Pd.

Gear practice 1 (written for these notes). A pinion with 20 teeth drives a wheel with 60 teeth, module 4 mm. Find both PCDs, the centre distance, the gear ratio and both tip-circle diameters.
Hint

D = MN for each; centre distance is the average of the two PCDs.

Answer

D1 = 4 × 20 = 80 mm, D2 = 4 × 60 = 240 mm. Centre distance = (80 + 240)/2 = 160 mm (or 4 × 80/2). Ratio = 60/20 = 3. Tip circles: 80 + 8 = 88 mm and 240 + 8 = 248 mm.

Gear practice 2. A gear blank (tip circle) measures 132 mm and the gear has 31 teeth. Find the module, PCD, whole depth and root-circle diameter.
Hint

Tip Ø = MN + 2M = M(N + 2).

Answer

M = 132 / (31 + 2) = 4 mm. PCD = 4 × 31 = 124 mm. Whole depth = 2.25 × 4 = 9 mm. Root Ø = 124 − 2.5 × 4 = 114 mm (check: 132 − 2 × 9 = 114).

Gear practice 3. Two shafts 150 mm apart must be joined by spur gears of module 5 mm with a ratio of 2:1. How many teeth on each gear? What is the circular tooth thickness?
Hint

C = M(N1 + N2)/2 gives N1 + N2; the ratio splits it.

Answer

N1 + N2 = 2C/M = 2 × 150/5 = 60, and N2 = 2N1, so N1 = 20, N2 = 40 (PCDs 100 and 200 mm; check (100 + 200)/2 = 150). Tooth thickness = π × 5 / 2 = 7.85 mm.

Drawing every tooth is slow and adds nothing a machinist needs (the teeth are cut from the module and tooth count in a table on the drawing). So a single spur gear is drawn as a plain blank. The pitch circle is the one size that matters for meshing, so it stays, as a chain line (the same line type as a centre line, because it is a "reference" circle, not an edge). In section, the teeth are left white so the tooth zone stands out: hatch the body only, from the root line inwards.

12Cams sl. 66–70

Quick pass

Cam–follower system (sl. 67): gives unusual, irregular motions. The follower reciprocates with a definite cyclic motion set by the shape of the cam profile as the cam turns on its shaft (parts: follower, roller, cam, shaft).

Follower motion (sl. 68): oscillating or translating. Cam types: 1) radial cam: follower moves radially (perpendicular to the cam axis); 2) axial cam (cylindrical/barrel): follower moves parallel to the cam's axis.

Joint closure (sl. 69): 1) force closure: an external force, e.g. a spring, keeps cam and follower touching; 2) form closure: a groove or rib guides the follower so it cannot leave the profile.

Follower types (sl. 70): a) knife edge, b) roller, c) flat (plate), d) spherical (mushroom).

Cam and follower types sl. 68–70

13Bearings: types, loads, mounting sl. 71–77

Quick pass

Bearing (sl. 72): a device that permits constrained relative motion between two parts, usually rotation or linear movement. Families: plain, rolling-element, jewel, fluid, magnetic, flexure.

Loads (sl. 73): radial Fr (perpendicular to the shaft), axial or thrust Fa (along the shaft), or combined.

14Bearing conventional representation and designation ★ sl. 78–86

Must know
  • Rolling bearings are drawn detailed or simplified.
  • Detailed: shows the essentials (rings, balls or rollers). Rings may be hatched (thin lines); balls and rollers are never hatched.
  • Simplified: a square or rectangle with an element symbol inside; no hatching. All symbol lines are continuous wide (thick).
  • Element symbols: long straight line = axis of rolling element (no self-alignment); arc = self-aligning; short line = each row of elements; or circle = ball, wide rectangle = roller, narrow rectangle = needle.
  • In an assembly, the bearing gets an item number and is named in the item list, e.g. Deep groove ball bearing ISO 15-7200B (description, standard, size).

A bearing is a bought-in standard part, so like a bolt it is drawn by symbol: the drawing only needs to say which bearing and where. The slides' "bearings are not hatched" (sl. 78) means the rolling elements and the simplified symbol. In the detailed drawings on sl. 80 and 86 the rings are hatched while the balls are left white with their centre lines.

Slide 78's wording ("hatching can be shown as a continuous narrow line type (bearings are not hatched)") is ambiguous. These notes follow the slide drawings: rings hatched in detailed views, balls and rollers never, simplified views never.

15Spot the mistake (written for these notes)

Each drawing has 3 planted errors of the kind used in the 20-mark question. Circle each one and name the rule it breaks. Work through a fixed checklist: thick or thin? full or ¾ circle? where does the hatching stop? what should not be hatched at all?

Drill 1 · Written for these notes
A bolt with a threaded end, side view and end view. Find 3 errors in the thread representation.
Hint

It's an external thread: which diameter is the visible edge?

Answer
  1. End view circles swapped. External thread: the major Ø is a thick full circle and the minor Ø a thin ¾ circle. Here the small circle is thick and the big one is the open thin arc (that is the internal-thread pattern).
  2. Thread limit line missing. The end of the useful thread needs a thick line across the major Ø.
  3. Minor-Ø lines drawn thick. In side view the roots are thin lines; only the major Ø (outline) is thick.
Drill 2 · Written for these notes
A blind tapped hole in a block, sectioned, with its end view. Find 3 errors.
Hint

Check the hatching edge, the bottom of the hole, then the circles.

Answer
  1. Hatching stops at the thin major line. In section the hatching "crosses the thread": it runs up to the thick minor line (the drilled hole), leaving no white strip.
  2. Flat-bottomed hole. A tapping drill leaves a 120° cone point; the drill depth also needs to exceed the thread length (here it does, but the end shape is wrong).
  3. End view circles swapped. Internal thread: the hole (minor Ø) is the thick full circle and the major Ø the thin ¾ circle.
Drill 3 · Written for these notes
A screw in a tapped block, assembly drawing, block sectioned. Find 3 errors.
Hint

Which thread covers which in an assembly? Is a standard screw hatched?

Answer
  1. Screw hatched. Screws, bolts, nuts, washers, shafts, keys and pins are not hatched in a longitudinal section.
  2. Internal thread drawn over the screw. The block's hatching and thick lines run to the minor Ø along the engaged length, hiding the screw's thread. Rule: the external thread covers the internal thread, so draw the screw's thick major lines there and stop the hatching at them.
  3. Hole's thread limit missing. The tapped hole needs a thick line across at the end of its useful thread (where the thin major lines stop).
Drill 4 · Written for these notes
A hexagon bolt, washer and nut clamping two plates, sectioned through the axis. Find 3 errors.
Hint

Two neighbouring parts, one fastener, one nut end.

Answer
  1. Bolt hatched. Standard fasteners are left unhatched in section.
  2. Both plates hatched in the same direction. Adjacent parts in a section must be hatched in different directions (or spacing), otherwise they read as one piece.
  3. Bolt ends inside the nut. At least one thread must show past the nut (sl. 34 "a", sl. 38). Choose a longer bolt.
Drill 5 · Written for these notes
A spur gear: normal view and section. Find 3 errors in the conventional representation.
Hint

Normal view: which circles should exist, and in which line type? Section: what is never hatched?

Answer
  1. Pitch circle drawn thick continuous. It must be a thin chain line.
  2. Root circle drawn. In the normal view the root circle is omitted.
  3. Teeth hatched in the section. The tooth zone (root to tip) is left unhatched; hatch the body from the root line inward only.
Drill 6 · Written for these notes
A ball bearing on a shaft in a housing, detailed representation, sectioned. Find 3 errors.
Hint

Three things in this picture should never carry hatching or should differ from their neighbour.

Answer
  1. Balls hatched. Rolling elements are never hatched; show them as circles with centre lines.
  2. Shaft hatched. A solid shaft cut along its axis is not hatched.
  3. Outer ring hatched the same as the housing (same angle and spacing), so the two merge. Adjacent parts need different hatching.

16Common mistakes

✗Drawing the internal-thread pattern on a bolt (thick small circle, thin ¾ big circle) or the reverse. Ask: which edge can I actually see? ✗A full thin circle instead of a ¾ circle, or a ¾ circle drawn thick. ✗Leaving a white strip between the thin and thick thread lines in section: the hatching must reach the thick line. ✗Hatching bolts, nuts, washers, keys, shafts or bearing balls. ✗In an assembly, drawing the tapped hole's lines over the screw (internal covering external). ✗Tapped hole with a flat bottom or with the drill depth equal to the thread depth. ✗Gear: drawing the root circle, a solid pitch circle, or hatching the teeth. ✗Gear sums: using 2M instead of 2.5M for the root circle (it is two dedendums, 2 × 1.25M), or mixing up working depth (2M) and whole depth (2.25M). ✗Grade 10.9: yield is 0.9 × 1000 = 900 MPa, not 9 MPa or 90% of 1090. ✗Mixing up the case in ISO tolerance classes: capital H = internal (nut), small g/h = external (bolt). Unified: A external, B internal. ✓Final check on any fastener drawing: thick/thin, full/¾, hatching edge, unhatched standard parts, thread past the nut.

17Practice (written for these notes)

1. State the line type (thick, thin, chain, omitted) for: (a) the minor Ø of a bolt in end view; (b) the minor Ø of a tapped hole in side section; (c) a gear's root circle in normal view; (d) a gear's pitch line in section.
Hint

Visible edge thick, thread depth thin; gears: pitch is always chain.

Answer

(a) thin ¾ circle; (b) thick (it's the drilled hole edge); (c) omitted; (d) thin chain.

2. Decode M12 × 1.25 - 5H/4h and say what kind of fit it is and where you'd use it.
Hint

Capital before small: nut, then bolt.

Answer

Metric thread, major Ø 12 mm, pitch 1.25 mm (a fine pitch; coarse M12 is 1.75). Nut class 5H, bolt class 4h: the close fit, used where thread form and pitch must be accurate; needs high-quality production equipment.

3. A bolt is marked 8.8. Find its tensile and yield strengths in MPa, and explain what the two numbers mean.
Answer

Tensile = 8 × 100 = 800 MPa; yield = 0.8 × 800 = 640 MPa. The 8 is tensile ÷ 100; the .8 is yield ÷ tensile. It is roughly equivalent to SAE grade 5.

4. A triple-start thread has a pitch of 2 mm. What is its lead, and how many turns move the nut 36 mm? Why might a designer choose it over a single-start thread?
Answer

Lead = 3 × 2 = 6 mm; turns = 36/6 = 6. Multiple-start threads give quick assembly / fast travel; the single-start is stronger and self-holds better.

5. A spur gear has module 3 mm and 40 teeth. Find the PCD, tip-circle Ø, root-circle Ø, circular pitch and the range of face width.
Answer

PCD = 3 × 40 = 120 mm; tip = 120 + 6 = 126 mm; root = 120 − 7.5 = 112.5 mm; circular pitch = 3π = 9.42 mm; face width 9M–14M = 27–42 mm.

6. Two 4 mm steel plates are to be joined by bolts with Ø9 clearance holes, or alternatively by rivets. Give the bolt length range, minimum edge distance and hole spacing, and the minimum rivet diameter.
Answer

Total thickness 8 mm → bolt length 2–2.5 × 8 = 16–20 mm. Edge distance ≥ 2D = 18 mm; spacing ≥ 3D = 27 mm. Rivet Ø ≥ 3 × 4 = 12 mm, same material (steel) as the plates.
