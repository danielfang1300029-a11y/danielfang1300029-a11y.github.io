Projection: turning a 3D part into 2D views

How a solid object becomes a set of flat, lined-up views; where each view goes in first- and third-angle projection; which line type to use for every edge; and how to choose and simplify views.

High · 10 marks← Start HereExam: projectionExam: find the mistakesHandout: Chapter 03: Projection (Dr C K Chan, 71 slides)

0What the test wants

High This chapter is the whole of the 10-mark projection question. It also supplies the line-type errors in the 20-mark find-the-mistakes question. You need to be able to:

  • Given two views (often on a grid), draw the missing view in the stated projection, with correct hidden and centre lines (§7, §8).
  • Place views and recognise the symbol for 1st and 3rd angle. Spot views in the wrong place (§3, §4).
  • Use the right line type and the hidden-line and centre-line rules, and circle violations (§5, §6, §12).
  • Say how many views a part needs, which one should be the front, and when to draw tangent edges (§9, §10).

1Orthographic projection and the six views sl. 3–8

Must know
  • Orthographic projection: parallel lines of sight, all perpendicular to the drawing plane. Sizes come out true, so views can be measured. Perspective: lines of sight meet at the eye, so far things look smaller. Engineering drawings use orthographic.
  • Orthographic projection can give a pictorial (one 3D-looking view) or a multiview drawing (several flat views).
  • There are six principal views: front, top, bottom, right, left, rear. Never more than six.
  • Every view is drawn looking straight at one face, as if from infinitely far away.

To get a 2D drawing from a 3D object, you project it onto a flat plane: imagine lines running from every edge of the object onto a sheet of glass. Slide 3 splits projection into two families.

Two families of projection sl. 3

A: orthographic. Parallel edges stay parallel. B: perspective. Parallel edges converge, the way a camera sees them. Slide 4 shows the defining idea of orthographic projection: each point (x, y, z) is projected along the normal (perpendicular) to the drawing plane.

Pictorial or multiview sl. 5. One orthographic projection taken at a slant gives a pictorial (3D-looking) drawing. Taken square-on to the faces, it gives the separate flat views (top, front, right side) of a multiview drawing. Only the multiview shows every size true, so it's the one used for manufacturing.

The glass box sl. 6–8. Put the object inside a transparent box and project it onto each of the six walls. Unfolding the walls flat gives six views, in a fixed arrangement around the front view.

The six principal views, unfolded sl. 8

Rear · L side · Front · R side in a row, top above and bottom below the front. The labels show which size each pair of views shares: width between top, front and bottom; height across the row; depth between top and side views (marked "EQUAL").

2The standard three views and the 45° mitre line sl. 9

Must know
  • Standard three views: top, front and right. The front view is also called the central view: everything is placed relative to it.
  • Front and top share width (same columns). Front and side share height (same rows). Top and side share depth.
  • Width and height transfer with straight projectors. Depth turns a corner, so transfer it with a 45° mitre line (or dividers).
  • Views must line up exactly. A view shifted out of line is an error.

Shared sizes and the mitre line (third angle, written for these notes)

To place the mitre line, extend the top view's front edge to the right and the right view's front edge upwards. The 45° line goes through the point where they meet. Every depth then goes across, hits the line and drops straight down.

Analogy

Think of the three views as three photos taken by cameras on rails. The top and front cameras slide along the same horizontal rail, so widths match. The front and side cameras sit at the same height, so heights match. The 45° line is a mirror that turns the top camera's depth measurements through a right angle into the side camera's frame.

3First angle vs third angle: where each view goes sl. 10–16

Must know
View1st angle (ISO)3rd angle (US)
Top viewBelow the front viewAbove the front view
Right viewLeft of the front viewRight of the front view
Left viewRight of the front viewLeft of the front view
Bottom viewAbove the front viewBelow the front view
Where the projection plane isBehind the object (object between eye and plane)In front of the object (plane between eye and object)
  • The views themselves are identical in both systems. Only their positions swap.
  • 1st angle: each view lands on the side away from where you looked. 3rd angle: on the side you looked from.
  • Check for the top and side views: in 3rd angle the object's front edge faces towards the front view; in 1st angle it faces away.

How each system is made sl. 10–14

Where the projection planes sit sl. 11, 13

Top: 1st angle. You look through the object, and its shadow lands on a plane behind it, like a slide projector throwing an image onto a wall. Bottom: 3rd angle. You look through a glass plane in front of the object and trace what you see on the glass.

Unfolding the planes sl. 12, 14

Open each box flat, keeping the front plane still. In 1st angle the plane under the object swings down, so the top view ends up below the front view, and the plane on the right swings round to the left. In 3rd angle the planes open towards you, so the top view stays above and the right view stays on the right.

Slide 10 shows both side by side with the same L-shaped block. Red arrows mark the viewing directions, and each result carries its symbol: 1st angle (cone then circles) and 3rd angle (circles then cone).

Why "first" and "third"? sl. 15–16

A vertical plane and a horizontal plane crossing each other divide space into four quadrants (angles), numbered 1st to 4th going round. Put the object in the 1st quadrant (in front of the vertical plane, above the horizontal one) and you get first-angle projection. Put it in the 3rd quadrant (behind the vertical plane, below the horizontal one) and you get third-angle projection. Then hold the front view (central view) still and unfold the other planes flat. The 2nd and 4th quadrants aren't used, because the unfolded views would land on top of each other.

The four quadrants and the unfolding sl. 15, 16

The slide labels third angle as the U.S. system and first angle as ISO (used in Europe and China). Always read the symbol. Never assume.

The same part in both systems (written for these notes)

Below, the same part is drawn twice. Compare the views and you'll see they're identical. Only the arrangement and the symbol change.

Look at the rebate (the step on the front edge). In the top view it's always at the bottom edge, and in the right view always at the left edge, because those are the object's front. In 3rd angle those edges face the front view. In 1st angle they face away from it.

Trap

A view in the "right view" position must actually be the right view. In 3rd angle, a view to the left of the front can only be a left view. If it shows what you'd see from the right, it's in the wrong place. This is a favourite planted mistake.

4Projection symbols sl. 17–19

Must know
  • The symbol shows a truncated cone twice: side view (trapezium) and end view (two circles).
  • 1st angle: cone on the LEFT, circles on the RIGHT. 3rd angle: circles on the LEFT, cone on the RIGHT. The cone's small end points left in both.
  • Proportions: cone length \(d\), big end \(\varnothing d\), included angle 30°, big end to circle centre \(1\tfrac14 d\). Centre lines through both.
  • It goes in the title block. A symbol that disagrees with the layout is a mistake.
The two symbols, drawn to the slide 17 proportions
Proportions sl. 17

The 30° is the angle between the cone's two sloping sides. The small end isn't dimensioned on the slide. It follows from the 30°: small end \(\varnothing \approx d(1-2\tan 15^\circ) \approx 0.46\,d\), which is also the inner circle.

In words: the small-end diameter is the big-end diameter minus what the two 15° sides take away over the cone's length.

Why the symbols look the way they do sl. 18

The two circles are what you see looking at the cone from its small end, which is the left. You see the small flat end (inner circle) and the sloping side around it, out to the big end (outer circle). So the circles are the cone's left view, and the symbol is a tiny two-view drawing that obeys its own rule:

  • 1st angle: the left view goes on the right of the front view, so the circles are on the right.
  • 3rd angle: the left view goes on the left, so the circles are on the left.

Slide 18 shows the green cone between coloured planes. In 1st angle the trapezium and the circles land on planes behind the cone. In 3rd angle they land on planes in front of it.

5Line types and the hidden-line rules sl. 21–22, 28

Must know
  • Thick solid: visible edges. Thin dashed: hidden edges. Thin chain (long–short): centre lines and axes. Thin double-dashed chain: path of motion and alternative positions.
  • An edge you can see beats one you can't: if a visible and a hidden edge coincide, draw the visible one.
  • Hidden-line junctions: touch a visible line you end on (A); join corners and meeting points (B, D, E); leave a space when continuing a visible line (C); start a hidden arc on the centre line (F); don't cross a visible or another hidden line with a dash (G, H).
The four line types

Solid lines show the edges you can see along the line of sight. Dashed lines show the edges hidden behind material sl. 21. Slide 21 points out eight junctions (A to H) on a three-view drawing of a notched block with a drilled hole. Slide 22 then gives the correct and incorrect way to draw each one.

Where the junctions occur on a real drawing sl. 21

The eight junction rules, redrawn sl. 22

A · Hidden line ends on a visible lineNo space: the first dash touches the visible line.
B · Two hidden lines form a cornerJoin the corner: both dashes meet in an L.
C · Hidden line continues a visible line in the same directionLeave a space, so the end of the visible line stays clear.
D · Three hidden lines meet at one pointJoin: all three dashes reach the point.
E · Drill-point "V" at the bottom of a hidden holeJoin: the V meets the sides and its own tip.
F · Hidden arc running into hidden straight linesStart the arc exactly on the centre line (the tangent point).
G · Hidden line crosses a visible lineDo not cross: leave a gap where the visible line passes.
H · Two hidden lines crossDo not cross dash over dash: one line keeps its dash, the other leaves a gap.

The marks come from how a person draws dashes. Starting a hidden line exactly at the visible line it ends on (A) shows where it ends. A gap where a hidden line continues a visible one (C) shows where the visible edge stops. A dash over a solid line (G) would look like a little cross, a point that doesn't exist.

Rule H: the slide's "correct" picture has the horizontal hidden line leave a gap while the vertical one keeps its dash. Some textbooks state it the other way round. Either way, never draw dash crossing dash like a "+".

Double-dashed chain sl. 28

A thin double-dashed chain line (long dash, two short dashes) shows a path of motion and the virtual shape of a part at another position. Slide 28 shows a crank arm drawn solid in one position, with its pin end shown again in double-dashed chain at a second position, joined by an arc marked "PATH OF MOTION".

6Centre lines sl. 23–27

Must know
  • A centre line (thin chain) is drawn for axes of symmetrical parts and features (cylinders, drilled holes, bolt circles) and for the centre of every circle or arc.
  • On a circle: two centre lines whose short dashes cross at the centre, extending about 8 mm (3/8 in) past the edge.
  • In the side-on (longitudinal) view of a hole or cylinder: a single centre line along its axis, between the two hidden or visible lines.
  • Holes too small to break the line: draw the centre lines thin and solid.
  • Holes on a bolt circle: draw the bolt circle as a chain-line circle through the hole centres.

7Completing a missing view: the method

Must know
  1. Read the symbol, then name each given view and the missing one from its position.
  2. Box it: width from the front view's columns, height from its rows, depth from the other view (via the 45° line, or counting grid squares).
  3. Sketch the solid in your head: take each region of one view and find where it lines up in the other.
  4. Outline (thick), then every edge: draw it visible if nothing is in front of it, hidden if something is.
  5. Centre lines for every hole and arc. Then check: every line must line up with something in the other views.

When using depth, remember which edge of the top and side views is the object's front: the edge facing the front view in 3rd angle, the edge facing away in 1st angle. On a grid, counting squares replaces the 45° line.

Worked example 1: third angle, find the right view

Worked example

A part has a base 6 × 4 × 2 squares, an upright along the back (depth 2, rising to a height of 5) with a slot 2 wide and 1 deep cut into its top, and a vertical hole through the front of the base. Given the top and front views, draw the right view.

  1. Symbol: circles left → 3rd angle. Top is above the front, so the missing view to the right of the front is the right view. Its left edge is the object's front.
  2. Box: height 5 (front-view rows). Depth 4 (top view). Project heights straight across and depths through the 45° line.
  3. Outline: the base runs the full depth up to height 2. The upright sits at the back half (depth 2 to 4) up to 5. That gives an L: thick lines.
  4. Slot: it's cut into the middle of the upright's top (columns 2–4 of the front view), so from the right it's hidden behind the right-hand part of the upright. Its floor at height 4 is a hidden line across the upright.
  5. Hole: it's at depth 0.5–1.5 and runs the full base height, so it shows as two hidden lines from 0 to 2 at depths 0.5 and 1.5 with a centre line between them. They end on the base's top line, touching it (rule A).

Final answer, shaded. Check: the hidden line at height 4 lines up with the slot floor in the front view, and the hole lines line up with the circle in the top view through the 45° line.

Worked example 2: first angle, find the left view

Worked example

A base 6 × 4 × 2 squares has a full-height block (height 4) along its back half. The block's left top corner is chamfered (cut at 45°) by one square. A notch 1 wide and 1 deep is cut into the middle of the front edge, through the full base height. Given the front and top views, draw the left view.

  1. Symbol: cone left → 1st angle. The top view is below the front view. The empty box to the right of the front view therefore holds the view from the left. In 1st angle the object's front faces away from the front view, so in this left view the front is the right-hand edge, and in the top view it's the bottom edge.
  2. Box: height 4, depth 4. In 1st angle the 45° line runs down to the right (see the solution).
  3. Outline: the back half (left side of this view) is 4 high and the front half 2 high, giving an L.
  4. Chamfer: the sloping face looks towards the left, so you see it. It spans heights 3–4 across the back half, and its lower edge at height 3 is a visible line.
  5. Notch: its back wall is at depth 1, hidden behind the base's left end: a hidden vertical line from 0 to 2. Its side walls are faces parallel to this view, so they give no line.

Final answer, shaded, with depth transfer. In 1st angle the mitre goes down and to the right, because both the top view and the left view are measured from their far (front) edges.

Common slip

Drawing the 1st-angle left view mirrored, with the front edge next to the front view. Ask yourself "which side did I look from?". From the left, the object's front is on your right.

Slide 35: exercises 3 and 4 (third angle)
Slide 35 ③ Stepped profile with an undercut (3rd angle): draw the top view.
Hint

The right view is the profile, pushed through the full width. From above, list what's on top at each depth (the right view's left edge is the front): flat top, slope, the low step. Then find what's underneath.

Slide 35 ④ The puzzle (3rd angle): draw the right view.
Hint

Try only flat, square-cut faces first. You'll find that no arrangement gives the top view's line at depth 2 on the right-hand side without adding an extra line to the front view. What kind of face shows as an area in both the front and the top view?

Answer

The front half is a slab 2 high. The back-left quarter is a full-height block. The back-right quarter has a sloping face that rises from height 2 at depth 2 to height 4 at depth 3, then a flat top to the back. A face sloping from front to back is seen as an area in both the front and top views, so it needs no extra line in either. In the right view it shows as the visible diagonal from (2, 2) to (3, 4).

This exercise has more than one valid answer. Any surface curving smoothly from (depth 2, height 2) to (depth 3, height 4), concave or convex, gives the same two given views, and only the right view changes (a curve instead of the diagonal). The flat slope is the simplest answer. In a test, any consistent solution should score.

9Number and arrangement of views sl. 37–48

Must know
  • At most six views (the principal views).
  • = 3: normal. < 3: enough when they describe the object completely. > 3: for complicated objects, or when too many dimensions would crowd three views.
  • One view: a constant-thickness or round part plus a note (washer: one view + thickness; bushing; sphere: Ø).
  • Two views: cylindrical and conical parts, cams (one view shows the shape, the other the length).
  • Any three views may be used (e.g. bottom, front, left), as long as each sits in its correct place for the projection system.
Six-view arrangement: U.S. (third angle) vs ISO (first angle) sl. 37–38

U.S.: top above, bottom below, left on the left, right on the right, rear at the far left. ISO: bottom above, top below, right on the left, left on the right, rear at the far right.

Fewer than three views sl. 40–43

A sphere needs one view and "Ø". A cone needs two (circle + triangle), since "Ø" and H alone leave the shape unclear in one view.

Choosing three of the six (first angle) sl. 44–48

Slides 45–48 pick different sets of three from the first-angle cross: bottom + front + left; front + left + top; right + front + top; bottom + right + front. Each view keeps its first-angle position relative to the front.

10View selection, tangent edges and runouts sl. 50–63

Must know
  • Order: decide the object's orientation → choose the front (central) view → use the minimum number of views.
  • Draw the part in its natural position (as it sits or works, e.g. base down).
  • Front view = the most descriptive view. Choose views with the fewest hidden lines.
  • Tangent edge (where a flat face blends smoothly into a curve): normally no line. A line there tells the reader there's a sharp corner (no tangency).
  • Do show tangent edges for simple objects, for clarity or for an important feature. Don't for complicated objects, where fillet lines reduce clarity.
  • Runout: a short curved end used where a fillet meets a cylinder.
Choosing orientation and natural position sl. 50–51
Minimum views, fewest hidden lines sl. 52–53

Slide 52: the end view adds nothing the other two don't show, so it's crossed out. Slide 53: the front view should be the side where the features are visible, not the one where they're all dashed.

Tangency: no line means a smooth join sl. 54–55

Same views from the front, different parts. The side view's line (or lack of one) is the only clue to whether the round face blends in or steps.

When to show tangent edges sl. 56–61

Slides 57–60: for these simple shapes, an outline with no tangent-edge lines (crossed out) reads as a featureless blob, so the lines are added. Slide 61: conventional practice adds a line where a curved surface changes direction abruptly ("add line to show abrupt change of direction", "line needed", "add for clarity"). The strict true projection without it is labelled "poor practice" or "not clear". Slide 62: runouts. With no fillets, a sharp corner gives a line. With fillets, the line fades out in a small curved runout.

Slide 56 says to show tangent edges for simple objects. In the line-convention questions of this course, the safer default is: a smooth tangent join gets no line unless it's needed for clarity (slides 54–55). If a drawing shows a line where two surfaces blend smoothly, say it wrongly suggests a corner.

11Other variations and conventions sl. 65–71

Must know
  • Half view: a symmetrical part drawn only up to its centre line. Partial view: only the needed part, ended with a break line.
  • Radially arranged features (holes, ribs on a circle): in the side view, draw them revolved to their true distance from the centre, not in true projection.
  • Conventional breaks shorten long uniform parts. The break symbol shows the section: S-curve for solid round, double S for tubular, hatched for rectangular solid, zigzag for long parts.
  • Identical features in a series: draw one, and show the rest with ditto lines.
  • Alternative positions: the moving part's other position in double-dashed chain.

12Spot the mistake (written for these notes)

Each drawing has 2–4 planted errors of the kind used in the 20-mark question. Circle them, then name the rule each one breaks. Work from the symbol first.

Drill 1 · Written for these notes
A note on this sheet reads "THIRD ANGLE PROJECTION". The part is the rebated base with an upright and a vertical hole from §2. Find 3 errors.
Hint

Check placement against the symbol, then trace the hole through all three views.

Answer
  1. Right view on the wrong side. The view shows the rebate at its left edge, so it's the view from the right. In 3rd angle the right view goes to the right of the front view.
  2. Hidden lines of the hole missing in the front view. The hole runs through the base, behind the front face, so two dashed lines (with the centre line between them) are needed.
  3. No centre lines on the circle in the top view. Every circle needs two centre lines crossing at its centre, extending past the edge.
Drill 2 · Written for these notes
The base with the chamfered back block and front notch from worked example 2, drawn in first angle. Find 3 errors.
Hint

Is the layout 1st or 3rd angle? Does the symbol agree? Then check the chamfer and the notch in every view.

Answer
  1. Wrong projection symbol. The layout is first angle (top view below, left view on the right), but the symbol is the third-angle one (circles left).
  2. Wrong line type: the notch's back wall in the left view is drawn solid. It's behind the base's left end, so it must be a hidden (dashed) line.
  3. Missing visible line in the top view: the chamfer meets the top face along a line at width 1 across the back half. Every edge you can see must be drawn.
Drill 3 · Written for these notes
A bracket in third angle: a base with rounded front corners and a vertical hole, and a round-topped upright with a horizontal hole. Find 3 errors (one of them appears twice).
Hint

Look for a line where two surfaces blend smoothly. Then check every hole for a full set of centre lines.

Answer
  1. Tangent edges drawn on the base in the front view (twice). The rounded corners blend smoothly into the front face, so there's no edge. The two vertical lines suggest sharp corners that don't exist.
  2. Missing centre line in the longitudinal view: the upright's hole appears in the right view as two hidden lines, and its axis needs a centre line between them (as the base hole has).
  3. Centre lines too short on the top-view circle. They stop at the circle instead of extending past it (about 8 mm on a real drawing), and they don't cross with short dashes.
Drill 4 · Written for these notes
The rebated base from §2 again, now in first angle. Find 3 errors.
Hint

In first angle, which edge of the top view is the object's front? Zoom into the front view where the hidden lines meet the solid line.

Answer
  1. Top view drawn flipped. The rebate is drawn along the edge next to the front view. In first angle the object's front faces away from the front view, so the rebate belongs on the bottom edge of the top view (and the hole nearer the top). The right view (rebate on its far-left edge) agrees.
  2. Hidden lines cross the visible line of the rebate with a dash (rule G). Leave a gap where the solid line passes.
  3. Missing centre line: the hole's axis between the two hidden lines in the front view (longitudinal view of a hole).

13Common mistakes

✗Placing the top view by habit (above) when the symbol says 1st angle. Read the symbol first. ✗Drawing a side view mirrored, with the object's front on the wrong edge. 3rd angle: front towards the front view. 1st angle: away. ✗Missing hidden lines for holes and slots in the views where they're behind material. ✗Drawing an edge dashed when nothing is in front of it, or solid when something is. ✗No centre line in the side view of a hole, or centre lines that stop at the circle. ✗A line across a smooth (tangent) join, which suggests a corner that isn't there. ✗Views not lined up: the side view a little higher, the top view a little shifted. ✗Forgetting that a sloping face shows as an area in two views and a line in the third. ✓Final check: every line in each view lines up with a line or a corner in at least one other view.

14Practice (written for these notes)

1. A drawing has the bottom view above the front view and the left view to the right of it. Which projection is it, and which symbol should it carry?
Hint

Which system puts views on the side away from where you looked?

Answer

First angle (bottom above and left on the right are both "away from the viewer" placements). Symbol: cone on the left, circles on the right.

2. Third angle. Given the top and right views, draw the front view.
Hint

The right view's left edge is the object's front. The top view's L-shaped line shows a raised part at the back left. What's at the front left?

Answer

Outline: 2 high on the right half, 3 high on the left half. There's a visible line at height 2 across the left half: the front-left corner is cut down to 2, so from the front you see its top edge, and behind it the taller part's front face from 2 to 3.

3. In the front view, a hidden line runs down from a visible horizontal edge. Should its first dash touch the edge or start with a gap? And if a hidden line carries straight on from the end of a visible line?
Answer

Ending on a visible line: touch, no space (rule A). Continuing a visible line in the same direction: leave a space (rule C).

4. Name the line type for (a) the axis of a drilled hole seen side-on, (b) the second position of a swinging lever, (c) the back edge of a slot hidden by the front face.
Answer

(a) Thin chain (centre line). (b) Thin double-dashed chain. (c) Thin dashed (hidden line).

5. A plain washer has outside Ø 20, inside Ø 10, thickness 2. How many views does it need, and why?
Hint

Is any information missing from the circular view if you add a note?

Answer

One view (the two circles) plus a thickness note (THK 2), as slide 40 shows. The thickness is the only size the circular view can't show.

6. A flange has four holes on a 60 mm bolt circle. In the side view, one hole would appear in true projection only 21 mm from the axis. What should you draw instead?
Answer

Revolve the hole to the centre line and draw it at its true radius (30 mm from the axis): the conventional treatment of radially arranged features (slide 66). True projection is "poor practice" because it misleads about the hole's position.
