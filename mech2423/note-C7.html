Fit & tolerance: how much a size may vary

Limits and general tolerances, hole and shaft fits from the ISO tables, worst-case tolerance stack-up, and how to read a geometric tolerance frame. The chapter where the calculation marks are most likely to come from.

0What the test wants

Medium Not confirmed for the test. But if any calculation appears, it most likely comes from here (fits and stack-up). Tolerance notation can also be a planted error in the find-the-mistakes question.

You need to be able to:

  • Read ⌀30 H7/k6, look up the deviations (how far each size limit sits from the nominal 30) in the ISO 286 table and work out the max/min clearance or interference (gap or overlap between shaft and hole). Then name the fit type (§5, §6).
  • Do a worst-case stack-up (adding up the tolerances of parts in a row): signs, nominal gap, ± sum of tolerances, min and max gap. Then say whether a requirement is met and how to fix it (§7).
  • Read ISO 2768-mK and say what tolerance an untoleranced dimension gets (§4).
  • Name the 14 GD&T (geometric tolerance) symbols, say which need a datum (a reference surface or axis), and read a feature control frame (the boxed label that carries a geometric tolerance) aloud (§8–§11).

1Why tolerance, plus surface finish and welding symbols

1aWhy sizes need a tolerance

A product is made from many parts that must mate in set ways: concentric (sharing one centre), parallel, or touching.

Picture a car maker. It builds some parts on its own line and buys the gear, the bearing and the shaft from three subcontractors (outside firms making parts to order). The parts only assemble if every supplier works to agreed size limits.

The chain of reasoning:

  1. You can't make anything to an exact size.
  2. Exact sizes aren't needed anyway.
  3. Accuracy is expensive, and often not feasible.
  4. So how do you make sure the parts are interchangeable (any one fits any other without hand fitting)?
  5. Answer: a system of limits and fits (BS 4500:1969, a British standard) that keeps function and cost acceptable and stays within machine capability.

1cWhat each process can hold (quick pass)
Quick pass · machining finishes and tolerances

Parts start from a few basic stock forms, each with its own processes:

  • Bar: round (turning, grinding, milling, forging, cold forming); square; hexagonal; tube (blow moulding).
  • Sheet: metal (shearing, blanking, bending, deep drawing, spinning…); polymer (blow moulding, vacuum forming).
  • Plate: rolling, fabrication.
  • Powder: powder metallurgy; continuous extrusion.
  • Granules: injection moulding, continuous extrusion.

Why this matters: you can't ask for a tolerance tighter than the process can hold. If you do, the part needs an extra, more expensive process. For each shape and feature, the tables give the finish (N grade) and the ± tolerance each process can hold:

Look along the "flat surface" row: milling holds ±0.1 but grinding holds ±0.005, twenty times tighter. That gap between rough and fine processes is the whole point of the table.

The tables also cover:

  • thin hollow shapes: slush casting ±0.05–5.0
  • tubes: extrusion ±0.1, drawing ±0.1, centrifugal casting ±0.2, spinning ±0.2
  • thin-sheet curvature and openings: blanking (punching a shape out of sheet) ±0.02, chemical blanking ±0.005–0.01
  • cross-sections: turning ±0.05
  • square edges: fine blanking ±0.001–0.01
  • small holes: laser ±0.025, EDM (electrical discharge machining: sparks erode the metal) ±0.005, ECM (electrochemical machining: metal dissolved electrically) ±0.01
  • surface textures: knurling (rolling a grip pattern), grinding, belt grinding ±0.005
Don't memorise this: work it out

Pattern to remember: the gentler the material removal, the tighter the tolerance.

  • Fine removal (grinding, broaching, EDM, ultrasonic machining): about ±0.005 mm and N1–N7.
  • Cutting with a tool (turning ±0.05, milling and end milling about ±0.1).
  • Casting and fabrication are the loosest (±0.5–2 mm).

Know those three levels cold. Skip the rest of the table.

1dJoining and welding symbols (quick pass)
Quick pass · joining and welding symbols

Joining = welding or fastening (fasteners are Chapter 4). The welding families:

  • Gas: oxy-acetylene, air-acetylene, oxy-hydrogen.
  • Resistance: butt, spot, seam, projection.
  • Arc: carbon arc, metal arc, plasma arc, MIG, TIG.
  • Newer: electron beam, laser.
  • Solid state: friction, ultrasonic, diffusion, explosive.
Don't memorise this: work it out

Each family is named after where the heat comes from: a gas flame, electrical resistance where parts touch, an electric arc, a beam, or no melting at all (solid state: pressure, rubbing or a blast). The process names say which, e.g. "oxy-acetylene" is a gas, "spot" is resistance.

A welding symbol is an arrow-and-line label that tells the welder where to weld, which kind of weld, and which side of the joint. The arrow touches the joint. A horizontal reference line carries a small picture of the weld's cross-section.

The side rule is the part people get wrong. A weld picture below the line means weld on the side the arrow touches. Above the line means the far side.

  • Tolerance = the total amount a size is allowed to vary = upper limit − lower limit. It's always a positive number.
  • Why: nothing can be made to an exact size, and exact sizes aren't needed. Accuracy is expensive, so tolerances keep function and cost acceptable and stay within machine capability.
  • Tolerances give interchangeability: parts from different subcontractors (gear, bearing, shaft) fit together without hand fitting. The standard used here for limits and fits is BS 4500:1969.

2Dimensional tolerance and how to write it

2aTwo kinds of tolerance

Limits and fits means a planned gap (clearance) or overlap (interference) between two parts, such as a hole and the pin that goes in it. You get it by choosing tolerances for both parts.

Dimensional toleranceControls size and location: how big a feature is, and where it is.
Geometric toleranceControls shape: how flat, round, square (form, orientation…). Section 8 onwards.

Every functional dimension (a size that affects whether the part works or fits) needs a tolerance, e.g. X.XX ± 0.02. Leave it off and the assembly may not work.

2bVocabulary on one example

Take a length written 40 +0.05/−0.03. The 40 is what you're aiming for. The two small numbers say how far over and under you may go.

2cThree ways of writing the same band

There are three styles. They differ only in what number you write as the "main" size:

  • Bilateral: deviations go both ways from the size (one + and one −).
  • Unilateral: deviation goes in one direction only (the other is 0).
  • Limit dimensions: skip deviations and write the two limits, upper limit above lower limit.

2dGeneral (footnote) tolerance

Tolerancing every single dimension clutters the drawing. Instead, the title block (the information box in the drawing's corner) carries a general tolerance, also called a footnote tolerance. It covers every dimension that has no tolerance of its own.

In inch drawings the rule often depends on how many decimal places the dimension is written with. More decimals = tighter tolerance.

The red box enlarges the title block's "tolerances unless otherwise specified" note, found bottom left of the sheet. Read the left column: each extra X after the point is one more decimal place and a tighter tolerance.

So 2.50 (two places) means ±0.03, and 2.500 (three places) means ±0.005. That's why you must never add or drop decimal places casually. The metric equivalent is ISO 2768 (§4).

Remember from this section
  • Limits and fits: a designed-in clearance or interference between two parts (a hole and the insert), specified by tolerances.
  • Dimensional tolerance controls size and location. Geometric tolerance controls shape (form, orientation…).
  • Every functional dimension needs a tolerance (e.g. X.XX ± 0.02), or the assembly may not work.
  • Three ways to write it: unilateral (deviation in one direction only), bilateral (deviations both ways), limit dimensions (upper limit written above lower limit).
  • Instead of tolerancing every dimension, a general (footnote) tolerance in the title block covers all the untoleranced ones.

3Tolerance zone, MMC and LMC, and what tolerance costs

3aTolerance zones for size and for location

A tolerance creates a tolerance zone: a strip or area the real feature must land in. For a size, the feature's edge may be anywhere in the strip. For a location, the feature's centre may be anywhere in the area.

Size first. A step's edge is dimensioned 1.00 ± .02 from the left end. So the edge may sit anywhere from 0.98 to 1.02: a strip 0.04 wide.

Look at the red band on the right-hand copy. Its left side is the 0.98 limit and its right side the 1.02 limit. Any edge position inside the band passes.

Now location. A hole's centre is placed 1.50 ± .02 from the left edge and 1.50 ± .02 from the bottom edge. Each ± gives a strip; where the two strips cross is a small square. The centre may lie anywhere in it.

The red square is the zone, 0.04 × 0.04. Check its sides against the dimensions: 1.48 and 1.52 along the bottom, 1.48 and 1.52 up the left. The big circle is just the hole.

Why this matters later

The corners of that square are \(0.02\sqrt2 \approx 0.028\) from the true centre, but the edge midpoints are only 0.02 away.

So a hole can be further off-centre diagonally than straight across. That makes no functional sense. GD&T's position tolerance (§9) uses a round zone (⌀) instead, the same in every direction.

3bMMC and LMC

Every feature with a size has two extreme sizes. Name them by how much metal the part has:

  • MMC (maximum material condition): the limit with the most material. A shaft has most metal when it's biggest. A part with a hole has most metal when the hole is smallest.
  • LMC (least material condition): the opposite limit. Smallest shaft, largest hole.

Why care: a shaft and hole both at MMC is the tightest fit. Both at LMC is the loosest.

Which limit is MMC

Material condition is about the amount of metal. A big shaft has more metal. A small hole leaves more metal in the part. Orange: the material around the hole.

The same works for flat features. A tab sticks out, so it behaves like a shaft. A slot is a gap, so it behaves like a hole.

Tab and slot

Top left, the tab: MMC = .497 (upper), LMC = .495. Below it, the slot: MMC = .498 (lower), LMC = .502. Notice MMC is the upper limit for the tab but the lower one for the slot. On the right, a slot measured at .501 is inside .498–.502, so it's accepted.

Tightest assembly (both at MMC): clearance = .498 − .497 = .001. Loosest (both at LMC): .502 − .495 = .007.

3cTolerance level, cost and process capability

Choose the tolerance to suit the precision the factory normally achieves.

  • A smaller tolerance gives a better fit, but costs more.
  • A larger tolerance is more economical.
  • Show any special (tighter) tolerance individually on the dimension.

The process sets a floor. Machining, pressed metal and casting can each hold only so much. Ask for less than the process can do and you force an extra, slower operation.

Read the "typical tolerance" column. Turning, drilling and planing: ±0.05. Milling: ±0.1. Broaching, grinding, honing and lapping: ±0.005. The finish column (µm Ra) improves down the list, to 0.012–0.8 for lapping. So a ±0.01 dimension means a grinding operation, which adds a process step and cost.

Remember from this section
  • Tolerance zone: the space the tolerance creates. The feature's edge (size) or centre (location) may lie anywhere inside it.
  • MMC (maximum material condition): the size limit with the most material. Shaft MMC = largest shaft. Hole MMC = smallest hole.
  • LMC (least material condition): the opposite limit. Shaft LMC = smallest shaft, hole LMC = largest hole.
  • Tightest fit: both parts at MMC. Loosest fit: both at LMC.
  • Smaller tolerance: better fit but more expensive. Larger tolerance: more economical. The tolerance must be within what the process can achieve (turning about ±0.05, grinding about ±0.005).

4General tolerances: ISO 2768

4aWhat ISO 2768 is for

Most dimensions on a drawing have no ± written next to them. They still need some tolerance, or nobody knows when the part is wrong. ISO 2768 supplies one: it gives a tolerance to every dimension that has no individual tolerance. It's the metric version of the decimal-places note in §2.

It has two parts:

ISO 2768-1: sizesLinear and angular sizes. Four classes: f (fine), m (medium), c (coarse), v (very coarse).
ISO 2768-2: geometryStraightness, flatness, perpendicularity, symmetry, run-out (wobble when the part spins). Three classes: H (tight), K (moderate), L (loose).

The title block names one class from each part. "ISO 2768-mK" means untoleranced sizes follow class m and untoleranced geometry follows class K.

Don't memorise these tables: work them out
  • Class letters are just initials: f fine, m medium, c coarse, v very coarse. H, K, L go tight → loose in alphabet order.
  • Bigger part → bigger tolerance, always. If your answer shrinks as the length grows, you read the wrong row.
  • Neighbouring classes scale roughly: f ≈ half of m, c ≈ 2.5 × m, v ≈ 2 × c. In part 2, H ≈ half of K, L ≈ double K.
  • Know cold: the m row (0.1, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8) and run-out H/K/L = 0.1/0.2/0.5. Everything else: look it up, and say which table you used.

5★ Hole and shaft fits

5aWhat a fit is

Holes and shafts are everywhere in machines: bearings, gears, pins, bolts. How tight a shaft sits in its hole is called the fit. You set it by choosing the two tolerance zones.

  • Clearance fit: the shaft is always smaller than the hole. It slides or turns freely.
  • Interference fit: the shaft is always bigger. It has to be pressed in, and then it can't slip.
  • Transition fit: depending on the actual parts, it could be either.

Change the shaft size relative to the hole and you change how tight or loose they are. That's the class of fit.

First example

A shaft in a hole with nominal ⌀0.5. One diameter is given as limits .51/.47 and the other as .50/.49.

The two bands overlap. So depending on the actual parts there may be up to 0.02 clearance or up to 0.03 interference: a transition fit.

If the hole and shaft bands are the other way round, you get 0.03 clearance / 0.02 interference instead. Either way it's a transition fit.

5bThe three classes, drawn to scale

The clearest way to see a fit: draw both zones against the zero line (a horizontal line standing for the basic size). Then just ask whether the shaft zone is below, overlapping or above the hole zone.

Tolerance zones of the three ⌀30 examples, 1 µm to scale

Blue: hole zone. Red: shaft zone. Heights are deviations from ⌀30 in µm. Clearance: the shaft zone is entirely below the hole zone, so there's always a gap. Transition: the zones overlap. Interference: the shaft zone is entirely above.

Analogy

Think of clearance as relaxed and interference as forced. A clearance fit is a key in a lock that turns freely. An interference fit is a gear pressed onto a shaft so it can't slip. A transition fit is for locating parts accurately: sometimes it slides in, sometimes it needs a tap.

5cReading a fit symbol like 40 H8/f7

ISO writes a tolerance as a size plus a letter plus a number, e.g. 40 H8.

  • 40 is the basic size: the nominal size the limits are applied to.
  • The letter is the fundamental deviation: where the zone sits, i.e. how far its nearest edge is from the zero line.
  • The number is the IT grade (International Tolerance grade): how wide the zone is. Bigger number = wider zone = looser.
  • Capital letter = hole, lower case = shaft. A fit writes the hole first: 40 H8/f7.
Letter = where, number = how wide

Own diagram. Change the letter and the box moves up or down. Change the number and the box gets taller or shorter.

The same vocabulary on a real hole and shaft:

Top is a shaft, bottom a hole. The grey strip on each is its tolerance zone; its width is the IT number. The tiny gap between the basic-size line and the strip is the fundamental deviation (letter).

Upper and lower deviation are measured from the basic size. The fundamental deviation is the deviation nearest the basic size: it fixes where the zone starts.

(A) a hole, (B) a shaft, (C) the fit: hole tolerance / shaft tolerance. Note the letter and number together are called the tolerance grade. Tolerance symbol = position letter + IT grade. Internal part (hole): 35H7. External part (shaft): 35h7.

5dHole basis and shaft basis

To make a range of fits you hold one part fixed and vary the other.

Hole basis (H)Every hole is H: its minimum size = basic size (lower deviation 0). You pick the fit by choosing the shaft letter.
Shaft basis (h)Every shaft is h: its maximum size = basic size (upper deviation 0). You pick the fit by choosing the hole letter.

Hole basis is more common. Holes are made with fixed-size drills and reamers (tools that finish a hole to an exact size), while a shaft can be turned to any size. So it's cheaper to keep the hole standard and vary the shaft.

Left picture (basic hole system): the hole is fixed with its smallest size .500 = basic size. The shaft on its left is bigger, so it jams (interference); the shaft on its right is smaller, so it slides (clearance). Right picture (basic shaft system): the shaft is fixed, largest shaft .500, and the two holes vary instead.

5eThe alphabet map
Don't memorise this: work it out

Every letter is a position relative to the zero line. One picture gives you all of them:

Own diagram, positions not to scale. Holes: A–G above the zero line (A furthest), H touching it from above, JS straddling it equally (±), K onwards below. Shafts are the mirror image: a–g below, h touching from below, js straddling, k onwards above.

So with an H hole sitting just above the line:

  • shaft a–g is below the hole → clearance;
  • shaft h just touches → zero clearance at worst;
  • shafts k, n sit just above the line, overlapping the H zone → transition;
  • shafts p, s, u sit well above → interference.

Further along the alphabet = bigger shaft = tighter fit. Only learn that rule and the mirror; every letter follows.

Remember from this section
  • Fit = how tight a shaft sits in a hole, set by where the two tolerance zones lie. Clearance: shaft always smaller than hole. Interference: shaft always bigger (pressed in). Transition: could be either.
  • Basic size: the nominal size the limits are applied to (the "30" in ⌀30 H7/k6).
  • Hole basis: the minimum hole size = basic size (hole letter H, lower deviation 0). Shaft basis: the maximum shaft size = basic size (shaft letter h, upper deviation 0).
  • Symbol 40 H8: 40 = basic size, letter = fundamental deviation (where the zone sits), number = IT grade (how wide it is; bigger number = wider). Capital = hole, lower case = shaft. A fit: 40 H8/f7 (hole first).
  • Max clearance = largest hole − smallest shaft. Min clearance = smallest hole − largest shaft. A negative result is an interference.

6★ Reading the ISO 286 tables and the worked fits

6aWhy you need a table

A symbol like H7 doesn't tell you numbers by itself. The actual deviations depend on the size: a 7-grade zone on a ⌀100 shaft is wider than on a ⌀10 one. The ISO 286 tables list the deviations for every letter, grade and size range.

7★ Worst-case tolerance stack-up

7aThe idea

Put several parts in a row and their size errors add up. A stack-up works out how much a gap or overall length in the assembly can vary, given each part's tolerance.

Why care? A gap allows a certain motion (a slider, a bearing, a pin going in). The gap's size depends on several part dimensions at once. If the errors all go the wrong way together, the gap can vanish.

Worst case means: assume every part is at whichever limit is worst, all at the same time. If the design works then, it always works.

AnalysisFrom the part tolerances to the assembly tolerance. "Given these parts, how big can the gap get?"
SynthesisThe other way: share a required assembly tolerance out among the parts.

Each dimension has a min and a max. The question is how far the required dimension (e.g. a gap) can deviate.

Biggest and smallest gap

Own diagram. The slot width counts + (bigger slot, bigger gap). Each block counts − (bigger block, smaller gap). For the worst small gap, push every + dimension down and every − dimension up.

7bSigns: walk along the chain

Pick a direction along the chain of dimensions. A dimension running that way is +; one running back is −. The gap's nominal size is Σ(+) − Σ(−).

The tolerances are different: they always add, whatever the sign. An error in a − part still widens the range of the gap.

Sign convention

Look at the top arrow: V runs from the bolt head to the nut face, left to right, so it's +. The washers W, X, Y, Z and the GAP are measured along the bottom.

Walk from the head face to the nut face: V runs in the + direction. Coming back through the washers, W, X, Y and Z are −. So GAP = V − W − X − Y − Z.

7cMethod and formula
Method (use a table every time)
  1. Name the gap or length you want, and sketch the chain of dimensions from one side of it to the other.
  2. Write each dimension in a + or − column, with its tolerance in a third column.
  3. Nominal = Σ(+ column) − Σ(− column). Tolerance = Σ(tolerances).
  4. Answer as nominal ± tolerance, then max and min. Compare the min (or max) with the requirement.
\[\begin{aligned}C_{ASM} &= \textstyle\sum C_i \qquad T_{ASM} = \sum |T_i|\\ \textstyle\sum C_i - \sum |T_i| &\le C \le \textstyle\sum C_i + \sum |T_i|\end{aligned}\]

In words: the assembly's nominal is the signed sum of the nominals. Its worst-case tolerance is the plain sum of all the tolerances (e.g. a long rod made from three segments \(C_1 \pm T_1\), \(C_2 \pm T_2\), \(C_3 \pm T_3\) bonded end to end).

Unequal tolerances (e.g. +0.08/−0.06): work out the two limits separately.

  • Upper deviation of the gap = Σ(upper deviations of +) − Σ(lower deviations of −).
  • Lower deviation = Σ(lower of +) − Σ(upper of −).

8Geometric tolerance (GD&T): why it exists

8aThe problem ± can't solve

A ± tolerance controls size. It says nothing directly about shape: a face can be the right height on average and still be wavy, or a hole can be the right diameter but tilted.

GD&T (geometric dimensioning and tolerancing) fixes this. It's a set of standard symbols that control a feature's form (flatness, roundness…), orientation (squareness, parallelism…), location and run-out. Each one is chosen for how the part has to function.

Because the symbols are standard, they're a common language for designers, engineers and manufacturers. They're especially powerful for assemblies: toleranced this way, parts are sure to fit.

The standards: BS ISO 1101 (geometric tolerancing) and BS ISO 5459 (datums: the reference surfaces or axes that other features are measured from).

  2. Then with the conventional ± method: ⌀25 +0.10/0, ⌀35 0/−0.10, 20.00 ± 0.05, 70.00 ± 0.05, 100.00 ± 0.10, 60.00 ± 0.10, 30.00 ± 0.05, heights 10.00 ± 0.10.
  3. Then comes the question: what if the top surface must be flat within 0.02 mm?
8cThe flatness example

Without GD&T you have two bad options. Either tighten the height until the surface can't be wavy (expensive), or leave the height loose and accept any waviness (flatness uncontrolled).

Remember from this section
  • GD&T (geometric dimensioning and tolerancing) defines a part by how it functions, using standard symbols. It controls form/shape, plus orientation, location and run-out.
  • It's a common language for designers, engineers and manufacturers, and very powerful for assemblies, because toleranced parts are sure to fit.
  • Standards: BS ISO 1101 (geometric tolerancing) and BS ISO 5459 (datums).
  • If no form tolerance is specified for a feature, it's acceptable whatever its form variation, as long as the size is in limits.
  • Direct limit tolerance + form tolerance = overall tolerance. The form zone is smaller and floats inside the size zone.

Symbols are little pictures of the idea:

  • Form symbols draw the perfect shape: a line (straight), a circle (round), a plane seen at a slant (flat), a circle between two lines (a cylinder).
  • Orientation symbols draw the angle: ⊥, ∥, and a slanted angle.

How to remember the datum column:

Form compares a feature with a perfect version of itself (is it flat? round?), so there's nothing to refer to. Every other group compares the feature with something else: perpendicular to what, positioned from what, running out about which axis. That something is the datum. Profile can be either: a free shape, or a shape located from datums.

Tell the look-alikes apart: parallelogram = flatness, circle with two slanted lines = cylindricity (a circle "moving along"), two concentric circles = concentricity, three lines with a long middle one = symmetry, one arrow = circular run-out, two arrows on a base line = total run-out, open arc = line profile, closed arc = surface profile.

9bReading a feature control frame

A feature control frame is the row of boxes that carries one geometric tolerance. A leader arrow joins it to the feature it controls. Read it left to right, like a sentence:

  1. Symbol: which characteristic.
  2. Tolerance: ⌀ if the zone is cylindrical, then the value, then any modifier (a circled letter that changes the rule, e.g. Ⓜ).
  3. Datums, in order: primary (the part is set down on this first), secondary, tertiary.

The value is always the width of the zone the feature must stay inside. No ⌀ means the zone is the gap between two parallel lines or planes. With ⌀ it's a cylinder of that diameter.

Location (3: position, concentricity, symmetry): required. Run-out (2: circular, total): required. Profile (2: line, surface): optional.
  • Frame, read left to right: symbol | tolerance (⌀ if the zone is cylindrical, value, modifier) | datums in order primary, secondary, tertiary.
  • The tolerance value is the width of the zone (the distance between two lines or planes, or the diameter of a cylinder if ⌀).
  • Ⓜ = at MMC: bonus tolerance as the feature departs from MMC. Ⓛ = at LMC (keeps a minimum wall thickness). Ⓟ = projected tolerance zone (threaded holes for long studs).

10A full GD&T drawing, and general geometric tolerances

10aA full GD&T drawing

Here everything comes together on one plate. Before you read the pins, two ideas you'll meet:

  • A basic dimension is written in a box. It's the theoretically exact distance to where a feature should be. It has no ±, because the feature control frame says how far the real feature may stray.
  • A datum can be a centre plane: if the datum triangle sits on a dimension line instead of a surface, the datum is the middle of that width.

11Reading drills: what does this frame mean?

Say each frame aloud in the form: characteristic, zone shape and size, modifier, relative to which datums. Then say what kind of zone it is.

Drill 1. On the top face of a block whose bottom face is datum A:
Answer

Parallelism, 0.05, to datum A. The whole top face must lie between two parallel planes 0.05 apart that are parallel to datum plane A. It's an orientation tolerance, so a datum is required.

Drill 2. On a drilled hole ⌀10 +0.1/0:
Hint

What does ⌀ in front of the value do to the shape of the zone? What does Ⓜ give you when the hole is ⌀10.06?

Answer

Position, cylindrical zone ⌀0.2, at MMC, relative to A, B, C (in that priority). The hole's axis must lie inside a ⌀0.2 cylinder at its true position, located from datums A, B, C by basic dimensions. At MMC (⌀10.00) the zone is ⌀0.2. A hole made at ⌀10.06 gets 0.06 bonus, so ⌀0.26.

Drill 3. On a turned pin:
Answer

Circularity (roundness), 0.01. At every cross-section, the circle must lie between two concentric circles 0.01 apart (radially). It's a form tolerance: no datum.

Drill 4. On the side face of a bracket whose base is datum A:
Answer

Perpendicularity, 0.1, to datum A. The side face must lie between two parallel planes 0.1 apart that are at exactly 90° to datum A. No ⌀, so the zone is two planes, not a cylinder.

Drill 5. On the middle diameter of a shaft supported on two bearing journals A and B:
Answer

Circular run-out, 0.05, relative to the common axis A–B (one datum made from both journals). Spin the shaft about A–B: at each cross-section the dial indicator's total movement must not exceed 0.05.

Drill 6. Spot the mistake: on a top face
Answer

Flatness is a form tolerance, so it takes no datum. The "A" compartment is an error. If the face must be flat and parallel to A, use parallelism to A (which also limits flatness). A plain flatness frame has just two compartments.

12Common mistakes

✗Putting ⌀30 in the "30–40" column. ✓ Ranges are "over … up to and including", so ⌀30 is in 18–30. ✗Using table numbers as millimetres. ✓ They're µm: +21 means +0.021 mm. ✗Max clearance = largest hole − largest shaft. ✓ Pair opposite extremes: largest hole with smallest shaft. ✗Calling a fit "clearance" because the max clearance is positive. ✓ Check both results: one positive and one negative means transition. ✗Shaft MMC = smallest shaft. ✓ MMC = most material: largest shaft, smallest hole. ✗Subtracting tolerances for the − dimensions in a stack-up. ✓ Signs apply to nominals only. Worst-case tolerances always add. ✗Putting every dimension at its minimum to find the minimum gap. ✓ + dimensions at min, − dimensions at max. ✗Giving the stack-up answer as only "±0.4". ✓ State the nominal and both limits, then compare with the requirement and say yes or no. ✗A datum letter in a flatness, straightness, circularity or cylindricity frame. ✓ Form tolerances take no datum. ✗Writing H for a shaft or h for a hole. ✓ Capital = hole, lower case = shaft. Hole first in a fit: H7/k6. ✗Reading ISO 2768-mK as one class. ✓ Two classes: m for sizes (part 1), K for geometry (part 2).

13Practice (every answer checked in Python)

Use the ISO 286 values in §6. Work in mm to three decimals.

1. ⌀25 H7/h6. Find the limits of the hole and the shaft, the max and min clearance, and the type of fit.
Hint

Column 18–30. H7: +21/0. h6: 0/−13.

Answer

Hole 25.000–25.021. Shaft 24.987–25.000. Cmax = 25.021 − 24.987 = 0.034. Cmin = 25.000 − 25.000 = 0. Clearance fit (locational clearance: it can just slide in when both parts are at MMC).

2. ⌀40 H7/n6. Is it clearance, transition or interference? Give the extreme values.
Hint

Column 30–50. H7: +25/0. n6: +33/+17.

Answer

Hole 40.000–40.025. Shaft 40.017–40.033. Cmax = 40.025 − 40.017 = +0.008. Cmin = 40.000 − 40.033 = −0.033 (max interference 0.033). One of each, so transition.

3. A bush is pressed into a housing: ⌀60 H7/p6. Find the min and max interference.
Hint

Column 50–80. H7: +30/0. p6: +51/+32.

Answer

Hole 60.000–60.030. Shaft 60.032–60.051. Imin = 60.032 − 60.030 = 0.002. Imax = 60.051 − 60.000 = 0.051. Interference fit.

4. ⌀12 H7/k6: max clearance and max interference?
Answer

Column 10–18. H7 +18/0, k6 +12/+1. Hole 12.000–12.018. Shaft 12.001–12.012. Cmax = 12.018 − 12.001 = 0.017. Imax = 12.012 − 12.000 = 0.012. Transition.

5. A housing slot is 60 ± 0.1 long. Three parts sit end to end in it: 20 ± 0.05, 20 ± 0.05 and 19 ± 0.05. Find the end gap (nominal, tolerance, min, max).
Answer

Gap = 60 − 20 − 20 − 19 = 1. Tolerance = 0.1 + 3 × 0.05 = ±0.25. Min = 59.9 − 20.05 − 20.05 − 19.05 = 0.75. Max = 1.25.

6. A slot 40 ± 0.2 holds two blocks, 12 ± 0.1 and 27.5 ± 0.1. (a) Find the gap. (b) The gap must never be less than 0.2. Keeping the blocks' tolerances, what tolerance must the slot have?
Hint

Min gap = nominal − Σ tolerances. Set it equal to 0.2.

Answer

(a) Gap = 40 − 12 − 27.5 = 0.5 ± 0.4, so 0.1 to 0.9. The 0.1 minimum fails a 0.2 requirement. (b) Need Σ tol ≤ 0.5 − 0.2 = 0.3. The blocks use 0.2, so the slot may have at most ±0.1 (synthesis: sharing the assembly tolerance out among the parts).

7. Title block: ISO 2768-mK. What are the limits of an untoleranced 70 mm length, and the allowed flatness of a 150 mm face?
Answer

70 is in 30–120, class m: ±0.3, so 69.7 to 70.3. Flatness, K, 100–300: 0.4.

8. Name the group and say whether a datum is needed: (a) cylindricity, (b) angularity, (c) total run-out, (d) profile of a surface, (e) symmetry.
Answer

(a) Form, no datum. (b) Orientation, datum. (c) Run-out, datum. (d) Profile, optional. (e) Location, datum.
