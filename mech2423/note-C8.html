0What the test wants

From this chapter

  • Classify a process into one of the four families: subtractive, formative, additive, joining (§1).

1From drawing to process: the four families

1aRead the drawing before you pick a process

A drawing doesn't say "make me on a lathe". You work that out from what's on it. Take a real example: an adapter ring (a thick ring that connects two parts of different sizes) made of cast iron.

Its drawing has section A–A (the ring shown cut in half so you can see inside) plus an end view. The key items on it:

  • A bore (the accurate inner hole) of Ø100.00 +0.05/−0.01. It may be up to 0.05 mm bigger or 0.01 mm smaller than 100.
  • Outer diameters Ø125 and Ø155.
  • "6 Ø10 holes on 140 PCD": six Ø10 holes spaced round a pitch circle diameter (PCD: the circle through the hole centres) of 140.
  • Lengths 12.5, 30 and 50 ±0.05.
  • Geometric tolerance frames (boxes that limit shape, e.g. how parallel two faces are) such as parallelism 0.02 to datum A (the reference surface you measure from). Plus a surface-texture mark (a tick-shaped symbol giving the Ra).

Before choosing a process, read five things off the drawing:

  1. Features: what has to be made (bores, steps, holes, flanges: flat rims that stick out)?
  2. Material: here cast iron, which already says "cast first".
  3. Dimensions and tolerances: tight ones (±0.05, +0.05/−0.01) need machining. Casting alone can't hold sizes that close.
  4. Surface quality: the Ra value on the surface-texture symbol picks the finishing process (§3).
  5. How to make it: so, cast the ring near shape, then turn (cut it on a lathe while it spins) the bore and faces, and drill the six holes.
1bThe four families

There are hundreds of process names, but every one falls into one of four families.

  • Subtractive: start with a solid block or bar and cut material away. Turning, milling (a spinning cutter shaves the work) and drilling (a spinning drill makes a hole). Section 2.
  • Formative: push the material into shape without adding or removing any. Casting (pour liquid into a mould), forging (hammer or squeeze hot solid metal), injection moulding, bending. Sections 4 and 5.
  • Additive: build the part up layer by layer, i.e. 3D printing. Section 6.
  • Joining: combine separate parts into one assembly. Welding, fastening (bolts, screws), bonding (glue). Section 6.
The four families at a glance

Own diagram. Left to right: material cut off as chips (orange); the same amount of material squashed into a new shape; a new layer (orange) added on top; two parts held together at a joint (orange).

Don't memorise this: work it out

For any process, even one you've never heard of, ask: what happens to the amount of material?

  • Less at the end, chips come off → subtractive.
  • Same amount, new shape → formative.
  • The part grows from nothing → additive.
  • Several parts become one → joining.

Worth knowing cold: two or three examples per family, as in the table below.

2Machining (subtractive)

2aWhat spins, and what cuts

Machining means cutting chips off a workpiece (the part being made) with a hard tool. The machining processes differ in just two things: which one spins (the work or the tool), and what the cutting tool looks like.

Turning spins the work; milling and drilling spin the tool

Own diagram. Blue arrows: what spins. Black arrows: the feed (the slow movement that makes the cut advance). Only turning spins the work, which is why only turning makes round parts.

Three kinds of cutting tool:

  • Single-point tool: one sharp tip, like a chisel. Used in turning and boring.
  • Multi-point cutter: many teeth round a spinning body (milling cutter, drill).
  • Abrasive wheel: a wheel of hard grit. Each grain takes a tiny scratch, so the finish is very smooth. Used in grinding.
2bTurning

Done on a lathe: a machine that grips a bar and spins it. The tool feeds along the bar and cuts diameter D₁ down to D₂ over length L.

Key operations: turning (reduce the outside diameter), boring (enlarge a hole), facing (cut the flat end), grooving (cut a narrow ring-shaped slot), cut-off or parting (cut the finished part free), thread cutting.

All these operations follow the same rule: the work spins, and the tool's path decides the shape. Feed the tool along the bar and you get a cylinder. Feed it across the end and you get a flat face. Feed it at an angle and you get a cone.

Lathe operations

In every sketch the curved arrow on the left is the workpiece spinning, and "Feed" shows which way the tool moves. Compare the arrows:

  • Facing feeds across the end; taper turning feeds at an angle to make a cone; contour turning follows a curved path.
  • Forming: a tool ground to the final shape is pushed straight in. Chamfering: cuts a small 45° bevel on the edge.
  • Parting off feeds straight in until the part drops off. Threading: a pointed tool cuts a spiral groove.
  • Boring and drilling work inside (the hatched cut-away shows the hole). Knurling: a patterned roller presses a criss-cross grip into the surface.
2cMilling

A rotating multi-point cutter removes material from a workpiece clamped on a moving table. The work is held in a vise (a clamp).

Machine parts, for recognition only: head, cutter, vise, worktable, saddle, knee, column, base. The saddle and knee are the slides under the table that move it across and up and down.

Types: face, end, slot, side (peripheral), plain, profile, pocket, thread, helical, straddle and gang milling. The deduce box below shows how to read these names.

2dDrilling and boring

Drilling makes a circular hole on a drill press. The parts: a motor; a belt and stepped pulleys to change speed; a feed handle to push the drill down; a spindle (the spinning shaft); a chuck (the jaws that grip the drill); and a work table.

The hole family:

  • Drilling: makes the hole. Boring: enlarges it.
  • Reaming: an accurate, smooth finish to size.
  • Tapping: cuts an internal thread.
  • Spot facing, countersinking, counterboring: make a flat seat, a cone-shaped recess or a stepped recess at the hole's top, for a bolt head.

How these holes are noted on drawings is covered in Chapter 5.

Boring enlarges and accurately finishes an existing hole with a single-point tool. On a lathe, the tool goes inside the spinning part. Why bother? A drill wanders a little and leaves a rough wall. Boring follows a controlled path, so the hole ends up the right size, round and in the right place.

2eGrinding

Precision finishing of flat and cylindrical surfaces with an abrasive wheel. It removes very little material, so it comes last, after turning or milling.

A surface grinder has a wheel head, wheel, guard, a worktable that feeds the work back and forth, a saddle, a column and a bed. It comes in horizontal- and vertical-spindle versions.

2fRa: how rough a surface is

Even a "smooth" machined face has tiny peaks and valleys. Ra (average roughness) is the average height of those peaks and valleys, measured from the surface's mean line, in micrometres (µm).

Smaller Ra = smoother. Each step in the standard series (0.4, 0.8, 1.6, 3.2, 6.3, 12.5) doubles the roughness.

Don't memorise this: work it out
  • What spins? Only turning spins the work. That's why turning gives round, symmetric parts. Everything else spins the tool.
  • What tool? Turning uses one point. Milling and drilling use many teeth. Grinding uses grit.
  • Ra order: the more controlled the cut, the smoother. Rough to smooth: drilling (3.2–12.5) → milling (0.8–6.3) → turning (0.8–3.2) → grinding (0.05–0.8). Know cold: grinding is the only one that reaches 0.4.
  • Lathe and milling names say what they do. Facing makes a face. Grooving, threading and chamfering make a groove, thread or chamfer. Slot, pocket, profile and thread milling make a slot, pocket, outline or thread. Face milling cuts with the cutter's end face. Side (peripheral) milling cuts with its rim. Straddle milling uses two cutters either side of the work, and gang milling uses several cutters on one shaft. Don't learn the list; read the word.

3bSurfaces A, B, C: matching Ra to a process

Here's the idea. Each surface on a drawing has its own Ra and shape limits. You pick the cheapest process whose Ra range reaches the number on the drawing. If the first process can't get there, add a finishing step after it.

The example is a crank-like shaft:

  • Two journals A (the round parts that turn inside bearings) on the main axis.
  • Two web faces B (the flat sides of the two plates).
  • An offset pin C between the webs, on its own axis, like a crankshaft.
The crank-like shaft and the Ra chart beside it

Left: the A arrows point at the two journals on the main centre line. The B arrows point at the web faces. C points at the pin, which has its own, lower centre line. (D isn't used here.) Right: each hatched bar is the Ra range a process can reach. Bars further left are smoother. Notice that only the grinding-and-finer bars reach left of 0.4.

The ranges in that chart (µm), with what each process is:

  • Turning & milling 0.32–25. Boring 0.4–6.3.
  • Reaming / broaching (pulling a long toothed tool through or along the part) / hobbing (cutting gear teeth with a spinning toothed cutter): 0.4–3.2.
  • Surface and cylindrical grinding 0.063–5.
  • Honing (rubbing a hole with abrasive stones) 0.025–0.4. Lapping (rubbing with a fine abrasive paste) 0.012–0.16.
  • Polishing 0.04–0.16. Burnishing (pressing the peaks flat with a hard smooth roller) 0.04–0.8. Super finishing 0.016–0.32.

Why grind A in one setup? Every time you unclamp a part and clamp it again, it lands slightly off-centre. Grinding both journals without unclamping means they can't drift apart.

Don't memorise the chart: work it out

Learn the order, not the numbers. Rough to smooth: drill → turn or mill → bore or ream → grind → hone, lap, polish or superfinish. Gentler, slower processes give smoother surfaces.

The one threshold worth knowing cold is 0.4: at or below it, you need grinding or finer.

Rule of thumb

Ra 0.8 or rougher: turning or milling can do it. Ra 0.4 or finer: grinding (or honing/lapping for even finer). Drilled holes are rough (3.2–12.5); if a hole needs size and finish, ream or bore it.

4Formative: forging, casting, moulding

4aThe idea: move material, don't cut it

Formative processes redistribute material: nothing is added or cut away. The aim is a net shape (the final shape straight away) or a near-net shape (only light machining left).

Why bother? Cutting a big part from a solid block wastes most of the metal and takes hours. Casting or forging it close to shape, then machining only the accurate faces, is far cheaper.

4bForging

Forging shapes solid metal by plastic deformation (permanent bending and squashing) under compressive loading: squeezing or hammering.

Advantages:

  • High strength and fatigue resistance (fatigue: cracking after many repeated loads).
  • Grain flow follows the load paths. Metal has a grain like wood. Forging bends the grain round the part's shape, instead of cutting through it, so the part is stronger where it's loaded.
  • Near-net shape possible, and consistent in repeat production.

Products: connecting rods, crankshafts, fasteners, aircraft fittings (also wheels, shafts).

4cCasting

Casting pours liquid material into a mould cavity (a hollow the shape of the part) and lets it solidify. The types differ mainly in what the mould is made of.

Sand casting is suitable for steel or aluminium. The mould is sand packed round a pattern (a model of the part), in two boxes: the drag (lower box) and the cope (upper box).

A sand mould, cut in half

Own diagram. Orange = where molten metal goes. It's poured into the cup, runs down the sprue and along the runner into the cavity. The riser is a spare reservoir; the vent lets air and gas out. The line across the middle is where the two boxes split so the pattern can come out.

The steps:

  1. Put half the pattern in the drag, ram sand round it and strike it off level.
  2. Flip it, add the cope with a sprue pin (a peg that will leave the pouring channel).
  3. Ram sand into the cope.
  4. Separate the boxes; remove the pattern and the pin.
  5. Cut vents (small holes that let gas escape).
  6. Pour molten metal through the sprue (the vertical pouring channel). The riser (a spare pool of liquid metal) feeds the part as it shrinks while cooling, so the part doesn't end up with holes.

Investment casting gives fine detail and finish:

  1. Make wax patterns and attach them to a wax pattern tree (sprue, runners).
  2. Dip the tree in ceramic slurry and stucco to build a shell.
  3. Melt the wax out (dewax).
  4. Pour metal into the hollow ceramic shell.
  5. Break the shell off.

Die casting, hot chamber: molten metal is forced into a reusable steel die (a metal mould). The injection unit sits in the furnace, in the molten metal. That's why it's for low-melting alloys such as zinc. Its parts: clamping unit, die assembly, injection unit.

Don't memorise this: work it out

Tell the casting types apart by the mould:

  • Sand: broken up after every casting. Cheap, rough, any metal.
  • Investment: a ceramic shell round a wax model. Fine detail.
  • Die: permanent steel. Fast, for many parts in low-melting metal.

The casting design rules below all come from two facts: the pattern must come out of the sand, and the metal must flow and cool evenly.

4dDesigning a part to be cast

Draft (taper) allowance: a slight taper on all vertical surfaces of the pattern. It lets the pattern lift out of the sand without tearing the mould sides, and without heavy rapping (knocking it loose). No draft = damaged mould. Think of a cake tin: sloped sides let the cake slide out.

Corners and radii, good:

  • Generous radii, uniform wall thickness, smooth flow.

Corners and radii, bad:

  • Sharp radii: cause stress concentration (stress piles up at a sharp corner, so cracks start there).
  • Inside/outside radius mismatch and uneven walls: cause uneven flow.
  • Sharp outside corner with an inside radius: thick metal at the corner cools last, causing shrinkage stress, voids (holes inside) and sinks (dents on the surface).

Cast part drawings: a pillow-block bearing housing (a block that holds a shaft bearing and bolts to a base) and a valve body. Both are cast to near shape (with fillets, the rounded inside corners, and draft), then the bores, faces and holes are machined.

4eInjection moulding

Injection moulding is the most common and most economical process for high-volume production of plastics. The mould is expensive, but each part takes seconds.

  1. Plastic pellets drop from the hopper (a funnel) into a heated barrel, where heaters melt them.
  2. A reciprocating screw (one that turns to mix and slides forward to push) injects the melt through the nozzle into a clamped mould.

Products: phone casings, plugs, pens.

Remember from this section
  • Formative makes a net or near-net shape by redistributing material, not adding or removing it. Net shape = final shape straight away; near-net = only light machining left.
  • Forging shapes solid metal by plastic deformation under compressive loading (squeezing or hammering).
  • Casting pours liquid material into a mould cavity of the desired shape and lets it solidify.
  • Injection moulding: most common and most economical for high-volume production (plastics).

5Sheet metal

5aWhat it is

Sheet-metal parts are lightweight and thin-walled, cut and formed from sheet stock, like folding a paper model. It suits small batches and mass production.

The flow:

Finished drawing→Develop flat pattern→Cut blank and holes→Form→Inspect

The flat pattern is the part unfolded flat. The blank is the flat piece cut to that shape before bending.

Three processes:

  • Cutting: shearing sheets, perimeters, holes.
  • Bending: straining the sheet around a straight axis.
  • Drawing: stretching it into a convex or concave shape, e.g. a can. (Nothing to do with a technical drawing.)

Advantages: high strength, good dimensional accuracy, good surface finish, and economical mass production for large quantities.

5bBending and the neutral plane

Bend a sheet and the two faces change length. The inside face is squashed shorter (compressed). The outside face is pulled longer (stretched).

Somewhere between them is a layer that does neither: the neutral plane. It's the same length bent or flat. So to find how long the flat blank must be, measure along the neutral plane. Use the inside or outside face instead and the bent part comes out the wrong size.

What happens inside a bend

Own diagram. Green inside face: shortened. Red outside face: lengthened. Blue dashed neutral plane: same length as before bending. It sits a fraction K of the thickness t in from the inside face (here K = 0.4), not exactly in the middle.

Where exactly is the neutral plane? A distance y = K·t from the inside surface. K (the K-factor) is that fraction of the thickness. Here K = 0.4.

The bend allowance BA is the length of neutral plane inside the bend: an arc of radius \(R + Kt\).

\[ BA = \theta\,(R + K t) \]

In words: bend angle in radians times the radius of the neutral plane (inside radius plus K times thickness).

\(BA = \tfrac{\pi}{2}(2 + 0.4\times 2) = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\times 2.8 = 4.398\) mm.

Straight legs (outside sizes minus R + t = 4): \(30-4 = 26\) and \(40-4 = 36\). Flat length \(= 26 + 36 + 4.398 = 66.398 \approx 66.4\) mm, as on the drawing.

5cCutting and bending methods
  • Cutting: like machining, with large shear (sliding force) at the tool. The stages: the punch (the upper cutting tool) touches → plastic deformation → penetration (leaves a smooth cut band) → fracture starts at the opposite cutting edges.
  • V-bending: low production. Done on a press brake (a long press that pushes the sheet into a V-shaped die), using simple, inexpensive V-dies.
  • Edge bending: high production. Needs a pressure pad to hold the sheet flat while the edge is bent, so the dies are more complicated and costly.
Remember from this section
  • Lightweight, thin-walled parts, cut and formed from sheet stock (like a paper model). Good for small batches and mass production.
  • Flow: finished drawing → develop flat pattern → cut blank and holes → form → inspect.
  • Processes: cutting (shearing sheets, perimeters, holes) · bending (straining around a straight axis) · drawing (forming into convex or concave shapes, e.g. a can).
  • Bending: metal inside the neutral plane is compressed, metal outside is stretched. The neutral plane keeps its length, so the flat length is measured along it.
  • Advantages: high strength, good dimensional accuracy, good surface finish, economical mass production for large quantities.

6Additive and joining

6aAdditive manufacturing (AM): what and where

Additive manufacturing (AM, 3D printing) builds a part layer by layer from a computer model.

Uses: medical, automotive, sports, wearables, aerospace (GE LEAP fuel nozzle), furniture, food, fashion; tooling, shoe cleats, custom airway stents.

The process chain:

  1. 3D CAD model.
  2. Convert to STL/OBJ (file formats that describe the surface as lots of small triangles).
  3. Check and prepare.
  4. Build (SLA, SLS, FDM).
  5. Post-process: UV curing (hardening resin with ultraviolet light), ultrasonic cleaning, chemical.

Versus subtractive: subtractive goes block → part + lots of waste. Additive goes powder → part + little waste.

The 7 AM methods (ASTM F42, the US standards committee for AM), from low to high energy:

Don't memorise this: work it out

Every name is "starting material + how it's made solid". Read it literally:

  • Vat (a tank of liquid) + photopolymerisation (hardening by light).
  • Powder bed + fusion (melting). Binder + jetting (glue sprayed onto powder).
  • Material extrusion (squeezed out like toothpaste). Sheet lamination (stacked sheets). Directed energy deposition (melted as it lands).

Know cold: FDM = extrusion, SLA = vat, SLS/SLM = powder bed.

6cWhen does AM create value?

The key fact: AM's cost per part hardly changes. It costs about the same to print one part as each of a thousand, and a complicated shape costs about the same as a simple one.

Traditional methods are the opposite. A mould or a machine setup costs a lot up front, so the cost per part falls as you make more. And complicated shapes need more operations, so their cost climbs.

When does AM create value?

The blue dashed line is AM: flat in both graphs, i.e. its unit cost is roughly constant. The red curve is traditional manufacture.

Left graph: it falls with volume (tooling spread over many parts) and crosses below AM at the dashed vertical line. Right graph: it rises with complexity and crosses above AM. The arrows under each graph show which side to choose.

So: low volume or high complexity → additive; high volume, simple shape → traditional.

6dJoining

Joining combines two or more components into one assembly, permanently or temporarily:

  • Threaded fasteners (bolt and nut, screw): removable.
  • Rivets: a pin whose end is squashed over to lock it. Permanent.
  • Brazing: a lower-melting filler metal glues the parts; the parts themselves don't melt.
  • Welding: the parts melt and fuse. You get a fusion zone (metal that melted) plus a heat-affected zone around it (didn't melt, but got hot enough to change its properties).

7Which process for this part?

To pick a process, go through four questions in order:

  1. Shape: round, flat, hollow or sheet?
  2. Material: metal or plastic? Does it start liquid or solid?
  3. Quantity: a few, or thousands?
  4. Tolerance and Ra: if tight or fine, add a finishing step.

Here are four typical parts worked through:

8Quick checks

1. Classify: (a) welding a bike frame, (b) printing a bracket by FDM, (c) bending a hinge plate, (d) drilling a hole in a flange.
Answer

(a) joining, (b) additive, (c) formative, (d) subtractive.

2. A flat face on a steel block is marked Ra 0.2. A student writes "face mill it". Good enough?
Hint

Check milling's typical Ra range in the comparison table.

Answer

No. Milling typically gives 0.8–6.3 µm. Mill it first, then surface grind (0.05–0.8 µm) to reach 0.2.

3. When a 2 mm sheet is bent, which side stretches, and why is the flat pattern measured along the neutral plane?
Answer

The outside stretches and the inside is compressed. The neutral plane neither stretches nor shrinks, so its length is the same flat and bent: that is the blank length to cut.

4. You need 20 titanium brackets with an internal lattice, and 200,000 simple plastic clips. Which process for each?
Answer

Brackets: additive (powder bed fusion, SLM): low volume and high complexity, where AM's flat unit cost beats traditional. Clips: injection moulding: high volume, simple shape, mould cost spread over many parts.
