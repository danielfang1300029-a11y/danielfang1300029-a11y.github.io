Technical drawing: title block, scale, lines and symbols

The rules every sheet must follow: what goes in the title block, how to state a scale without changing the numbers, which line type means what, and how to read the standard symbols and abbreviations.

0What the test wants

High This chapter feeds the title block ("corner labels") marks and supplies many errors for the 20-mark find-the-mistakes question (wrong line types, scaled dimension values, missing title-block entries). You need to be able to:

  • Fill in all 9 title-block items from memory, correctly, for any part (§5). Spot missing or wrong entries (§13).
  • State a scale from the recommended list and always write the true dimension value (§6).
  • Name and draw line types A–K and use each for the right job; know thick:thin ≥ 2:1 and the hatching-spacing rule (§7).
  • Recognise paper sizes, the drawing frame, drawing types, the main abbreviations and the conventional symbols (breaks, crossed diagonals, surface finish, welds, knurls, bearings) (§2–§4, §8–§11).

1Why technical drawing sl. 2–6

Quick pass

Why learn it well (sl. 3): later years build on it. Year 2 Drawing & Design 6 credits, Year 3 optional research project 6 credits, Year 4 design project 12 credits: 24 credits in all.

Two skills for mechanical design (sl. 4–5): (1) feasible design, engineering sense based on simple mechanics; (2) communication, i.e. spatial understanding (2D ↔ 3D) and representing the design as technical drawings.

A universal graphics language (sl. 6): idea → standardised drawing → technical staff at the workshop. Because the rules are standard, a machinist anywhere reads the drawing the same way you meant it.

2Types of drawing sl. 7–25

Must know
  • Four families (sl. 7): part drawings, assembly drawings, specific drawings (e.g. interface control drawing) and the drawing tree.
  • A part drawing describes one part. A tabulated drawing covers a family of similar parts with one drawing plus a table of the dimensions that change.
  • A general assembly (GA) shows all parts together, with: the views (assembled, sectioned or exploded), a parts list / bill of materials (BOM), numbered balloons linking each part to the list, and machining/assembly instructions.

Part drawings sl. 8–12

Part drawings come in five kinds (sl. 8): standard part drawings (starred on the slide as the main one), detail part drawings, tabulated drawings, tool drawings and composite material drawings. A simple part drawing (sl. 9) gives the general dimensions of a single part: the slide's example is a music player drawn in one view with its sizes (2.4", 4.1", R0.2", …), a frame with grid references and a title block. Sl. 10 asks what a simple part drawing contains: the views (shape), the dimensions, and the title block data (part name, material, scale, units, projection, who drew it), plus notes.

A tabulated drawing sl. 12
1 2
  1. 1The table: each row is one part number (…-1, …-3) with its own values of the lettered sizes (DIM A = 3.500 or 3.625, DIM B = 1.250 or 1.375).
  2. 2The drawing shows the shape once, with the changing sizes labelled A and B instead of numbers.

One sheet, several parts: you draw the shape once and the table does the rest.

  • Start from A4 (210 × 297). Double the shorter side to get the next size up (210 × 2 = 420, so A3 = 297 × 420).
  • The frame has a border, title block at bottom right, grid references (numbers along the top and bottom, letters down the sides), centring marks and an orientation mark.
How the A sizes fit together (written for these notes)

An A0 sheet (841 × 1189, about 1 m²) cut in half across its long side gives two A1 sheets; halve again for A2, A3, A4. Each step halves the area, and every size keeps the same shape (long side ÷ short side ≈ 1.414 = √2). The doubling rule is the same fact run upwards: 594 × 2 = 1188, which the standard rounds to 1189.

The drawing frame sl. 29–30
1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9
  1. 1Trimming mark at each corner of the sheet (an alternative style is a small filled corner): where the printed sheet is cut to size.
  2. 2Frame: the thick rectangle you draw inside. Nothing goes outside it.
  3. 3Grid reference: numbers 1, 2, 3 … along the top and bottom, letters A, B, C … down the sides. "Zone C4" lets anyone find a detail, like a map grid.
  4. 4Centring mark: a short line at the middle of each side, for lining the sheet up when it is copied or microfilmed.
  5. 5Orientation mark: a small triangle at the bottom centre, showing which way up the sheet is read.
  6. 6Title block at the bottom right, inside the frame.
  7. 7Border: the margin between the sheet edge and the frame (10 or 20 mm, table above).
  8. 8Scale bar: a printed bar of known length, so a reduced copy can still be measured.
  9. 9Grid reference line: the short tick dividing two grid zones.

Sl. 29 is landscape format, sl. 30 portrait. Both keep the title block at the bottom right.

The title block (also "title box") is the labelled table in the bottom-right corner of every sheet. It is the drawing's ID card: without it, the workshop doesn't know what the part is called, what to make it from, how many to make, which units and scale you used, or which way round to read the views. The handout shows HKU's own layout and puts a red star in every box you must fill.

Where marks go

Most lost marks are blank boxes, not wrong ones: the second half of "Sheet _ of _", the date next to your name, the unit, or the projection symbol (writing "3rd angle" in words is weaker than the symbol). Before you hand in, count to nine.

6★ Scale sl. 35–37

Must know
  • A scale must be used and stated on the drawing (in the title block).
  • Scale is written drawing size : true size. 1:2 = half size (reduction). 2:1 = twice size (enlargement).
  • Scale the drawing size but not the dimension value. Always show the true dimension!!! (sl. 37)
  • Use a recommended scale (table below), not 1:3 or 1:4.
Enlargement50:1 · 20:1 · 10:1 · 5:1 · 2:1
Full size1:1
Reduction1:2 · 1:5 · 1:10 · 1:20 · 1:50 · 1:100 · 1:200 · 1:500 · 1:1000 · 1:2000 · 1:5000 · 1:10000

The slide's table prints 1:20 twice; the second one, in the row 1:20 · 1:500 · 1:1000, is almost certainly 1:200 (the pattern is 1, 2, 5 per decade), as written here.

Scale changes only how big you draw. The numbers you write are what the workshop makes, so they never change. The class discussion (sl. 36) asks whether a square drawn 1, 2 and 4 long at scales 1:2, 1:1 and 2:1 is valid: the drawn sizes are right, but the values written on them must all be 2, because the part is 2 long.

The handout's answer sl. 37

The 1:2 square labelled "1" is crossed out: 1 is the drawn length, not the part's length. All three correct versions say 2.

Same part, three scales, one number (written for these notes)

A 40 × 20 mm block. On paper it is 20, 40 or 80 mm long, but every version is dimensioned 40.

The classic error (written for these notes)

A ruler on the 1:2 drawing reads 20 mm. Writing "20" tells the machinist to make a part half as big.

Worked example · choosing a scale

A bracket is 360 mm long. Its front view (360 wide) and top view (120 deep) will be stacked, with about 30 mm between views, on A3 landscape. Inside the 10 mm border, A3 leaves 400 × 277 mm.

At 1:1: width 360 fits in 400, but height 120 + 30 + front height (say 100) = 250, leaving only 27 mm for dimensions and nothing for the title block. Too tight. At 1:2: 180 wide and 60 + 30 + 50 = 140 high. Fits with room for dimensions and the title block. Write SCALE 1:2, and still dimension the length 360.

7★ Line types A–K sl. 38–48

Must know
  • Thick continuous (A) = visible edges. Thin continuous (B) = dimension, projection (extension), leader and hatching lines.
  • Dashed (E thick / F thin) = hidden edges. Thin chain (G) = centre lines, symmetry, pitch circles. Chain thin, thick at ends and corners (H) = cutting planes. Thin double-dashed chain (K) = alternative positions, adjacent parts.
  • Thick : thin ≥ 2 : 1 (thin 0.25 mm → thick 0.5 mm).
  • Gap between hatching lines ≥ 2 × the heaviest line (heaviest 0.7 mm → gap ≥ 1.4 mm).
  • All lines dense, clean and uniform, except construction lines: very thin and faint.

"The basis of any drawing is a line" (sl. 40). Each line type is a word in the drawing language: swap one for another and you've said something else. A dashed edge means "hidden behind material"; a chain line means "this is an axis, not an edge". That is why wrong line types are a favourite planted error.

Thickness, spacing, construction lines, lettering sl. 45–48

Rules with numbers

Thick : thin ≥ 2 : 1 (sl. 46). Thin 0.25 mm → thick at least 0.5 mm. In words: a visible edge must be at least twice as heavy as a dimension line, so the shape stands out.

Hatching gap ≥ 2 × heaviest line (sl. 47). Heaviest line 0.7 mm → hatching lines at least 1.4 mm apart. In words: closer than that and the hatching prints as a grey smudge. Line thickness depends on the size and type of drawing.

Construction lines (sl. 45) are the faint guide lines you draw first by hand (projectors, boxes, arcs found by construction). Draw them very thin and faint, so they are hardly visible in the finished drawing. All other lines must be dense, clean and uniform: every thick line equally thick, every dash the same length. Construction lines are common in hand drawing and seldom used in CAD.

Lettering (sl. 48): use one style throughout and keep it uniform (same height, same slope, same spacing). The slide shows a page of decorative fonts as what not to do.

9Conventional representations: breaks, flats, bearings, knurls sl. 53–57, 71–73

Must know (recognise)
  • Conventional break: shorten a long uniform part (bar, shaft, pipe) by cutting out the middle, so it fits without reducing the scale. The length dimension still gives the full true length.
  • Two crossed diagonals (thin) on a surface: either (1) the position of a bearing finish on a shaft, or (2) the surface is flat (e.g. a flat on a round shaft), usually parallel to the picture plane.
  • Bearing in section: any ball or roller bearing can be drawn as a rectangle with crossed diagonals.
  • Knurling: a few thin lines, widely spaced, over part of the surface only: parallel lines = straight knurl, crossed lines = diamond knurl.
Conventional breaks sl. 53–54
1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8
  1. 1Solid square or rectangular section: a freehand (type C) wavy break, with the exposed end lightly hatched.
  2. 2Solid round section: the "S" or figure-of-eight break, one loop hatched on each end.
  3. 3Small round section: a short, filled-in S-break.
  4. 4Pipe or tubing: the S-break drawn hollow, showing the wall as a ring.
  5. 5Pipe broken with a cut-away showing the hollow inside; below it, wood broken with splintered ends.
  6. 6Rolled shapes (9–15): angles, channels and beams broken square across, showing the section profile.
  7. 7Chain and rope or cable (16–18): drawn as a few links or a hatched band, the rest as a chain line.
  8. 8Shaft with bearings (19) and a bolt with a square section under its head (20), both shown with crossed diagonals.
Crossed diagonals: bearing seat and bearing convention sl. 55, 73

Left: a crankshaft-style shaft with two bearing seats marked by crossed diagonals (sl. 55; sl. 57 circles the same seats on a real engine crankshaft, beside its keyseats). Right: the bearing convention (sl. 73): one box with crossed diagonals on each side of the shaft stands for the whole bearing, ball or roller. Sl. 56 shows it used in a wheel-hub assembly drawing.

Knurling sl. 71–72

Knurling is a pattern of small ridges rolled into a round surface for grip (thumb screws, tool handles). Draw a few thin lines on part of the surface: parallel for straight, crossed for diamond. The example on sl. 72 labels the band "knurl" with a leader.

10Surface finish symbol sl. 58–62

Must know (recognise)
  • No surface is perfectly smooth; the drawing controls how rough it may be. If the usual process already gives an acceptable finish, don't specify it.
  • The symbol is a tick (✓-shaped V) touching the surface, with: roughness value or grade above the V; machining allowance to the left; direction of lay to the right; machining method / treatment on the extended line; sampling length under it.
  • Smaller roughness number = smoother surface = more expensive.
The symbol, the roughness table and an example sl. 59, 61
1 2 3 4
  1. 1The parts of the symbol: roughness value or grade above the V; machining allowance left; direction of lay right; machining method or treatment on the extended line, sampling length (other roughness values) below it.
  2. 2Filled-in example: roughness 12.5, machining allowance 5, lay ⊥ (perpendicular), "Milled", "Chrome plated", 0.25 (2.5).
  3. 3Roughness table: super finished (max roughness 0.05–0.16 µm, grades ∇14–∇10), fine finished (0.32–1.25, ∇9–∇7), semi-finished (2.5–40, ∇6–∇4), rough (80–320, ∇3–∇1).
  4. 4Example (sl. 61): each face gets its own tick, standing on the surface or on a leader; the curved notch is reached by a leader line.

The slide table's columns run 0.05, 0.1, 0.04, 0.08, 0.16 … 2.5, 20, 40 (out of order, and a gap between 2.5 and 20), so copy the bands, not the exact numbers. The sl. 61 example (from a US textbook) uses values 32, 63, 125, 250 that are probably in microinches; the slide doesn't say. Sl. 62 is a full shaft drawing using finish symbols throughout.

11Welding symbols sl. 63–70

Must know (recognise)
  • Arrow points at the joint; it leaves a horizontal reference line; an optional tail carries a specification (e.g. TYP), omitted if not needed.
  • Weld symbol below the reference line = weld on the arrow side (this side). Above = other side. Both = both sides.
  • Circle at the arrow/reference junction = weld all round. Flag = site weld (done on site, not in the shop).
  • Size to the left of the symbol; lengths to the right (welded length, then the gap in brackets).
  • Common symbols: fillet ◺, square butt ||, single-V butt V, single-bevel butt |/, single-U, single-J, backing run, plug ⊓, spot ○.
Anatomy of a weld symbol sl. 65

Read from the arrow: flag = site weld; circle = all round; 6 = weld size across the throat; triangle under the line = fillet weld on the arrow side, triangle over it = fillet on the other side; 100-(200) = welded length 100 ("hit") then 200 unwelded ("miss"); tail TYP = specification.

Weld symbols sl. 66

Each symbol is a little picture of the weld's cross-section: the V is the V-shaped groove, the triangle is the fillet's triangle. Sl. 63–64 give a longer chart in the same spirit (scarf, flare, seam, stud, surfacing, flush and convex/concave contours) for recognition only.

Reading examples sl. 67, 69

Sl. 67: 8 above the line → 8 mm fillet on the other side; 12 above and 6 below → 12 mm other side, 6 mm arrow side; 8 above and below → 8 mm both sides. Sl. 68 adds a 6 mm fillet all round (circle) and butt joints welded from the arrow side. Sl. 69: "5 × 150 (250)" = 5 welds, each 150 long, with 250 unwelded between them. Sl. 70 is a full steel-column drawing with weld symbols and a bill of materials.

Sl. 69 doesn't define "a5 z7"; in ISO 2553, a = throat thickness and z = leg length (general knowledge, not from the handout). The trailing "150" is labelled "Weld" without explanation.

13Spot the mistake (written for these notes)

Drills 1–2 are title blocks; drills 3–5 are drawings with line-type and scale errors of the kind used in the 20-mark question. Circle each error and name the rule.

Drill 1 · Written for these notes
A student hands in this title block for a two-sheet drawing of a shaft support. Which required information is missing? (Find 5. Some blank boxes are fine.)
Hint

Go through the nine items in order. Which empty boxes had no star on slide 31?

Answer
  1. Material missing (MATL box empty).
  2. Total sheets missing: "SHEET 1 OF __". It must say 1 OF 2.
  3. Date missing beside the drawer's name: drawn by and date are both required.
  4. Scale missing. A scale must always be stated, even 1:1.
  5. Projection symbol missing. Without it the reader can't tell first from third angle.

Not errors: WEIGHT, DESIGNED BY, CHECKED BY and APPROVED BY have no star, so leaving them blank costs nothing.

Drill 2 · Written for these notes
Job card: pulley hub, MECH2423-05, 4 required, aluminium alloy 6061-T6, sheet 2 of a 3-sheet set, drawn at half size in first angle, millimetres. The title block below has every box filled, but 5 entries are wrong. Find them.
Hint

Compare each entry with the job card. Check the symbol: which side is the cone on?

Answer
  1. Material too vague: "ALUMINIUM" doesn't say which alloy. Write AL 6061-T6.
  2. QTY 1: four are required. QTY is the number of parts to make.
  3. SHEET 2 OF 1: impossible; this is sheet 2 of 3.
  4. SCALE 2:1 means twice size. Half size is 1:2 (drawing : true).
  5. Third-angle symbol (circles on the left) on a first-angle drawing. First angle: cone on the left, circles on the right.
Drill 3 · Written for these notes
Third angle. A stepped block with a vertical through hole Ø20. Find 4 line-type errors.
Hint

Check each line against the A–K table: what should a hidden hole look like, an axis, a visible edge, a dimension line?

Answer
  1. Hole edges drawn continuous thin in the front view. They are behind the front face, so they must be dashed (F, or E if the set uses E).
  2. Hole axis drawn dashed. An axis is a centre line: chain thin (G), extending a little past the part.
  3. Step edge drawn thin in the top view. It's a visible edge (where the tall part drops to the low part): continuous thick (A).
  4. Dimension line drawn thick. Dimension lines are continuous thin (B); compare the 70 dimension, drawn correctly.
Drill 4 · Written for these notes
A bronze bush cut along its axis, drawn at SCALE 2:1 on a grid whose squares are 5 mm on the paper. Find 3 errors.
Hint

Count squares: how long is the bush on paper, and so how long is it really? Then check the hatching and the axis against the line table.

Answer
  1. Scaled dimension value. The bush is 12 squares = 60 mm long on paper; at 2:1 the real length is 60 ÷ 2 = 30. Write 30. (The Ø20 is correct: 8 squares = 40 mm on paper ÷ 2 = 20.)
  2. Hatching drawn thick. Hatching is continuous thin (B), spaced at least twice the heaviest line apart.
  3. Axis drawn as a double-dashed chain (K, phantom). An axis is a single chain thin line (G): long dash, short dash.
Drill 5 · Written for these notes
A flange plate with a central bore and four holes on a bolt circle, a cutting plane A–A, and a clamping lever shown in its working position and swung up. Find 3 line-type errors.
Hint

Which line types are for pitch circles, cutting planes and alternative positions?

Answer
  1. Pitch (bolt) circle drawn continuous. A pitch circle is chain thin (G). A solid circle reads as an edge.
  2. Cutting plane without thick ends. Type H is chain thin but thick at the ends (and at any change of direction), with the arrows and letters A.
  3. Alternative position drawn dashed. Dashed means hidden. An alternative or extreme position of a moving part is chain thin double-dashed (K).

14Common mistakes

✗Leaving a starred title-block box empty, especially the date, "of Y sheets", the unit or the projection symbol. ✗QTY = number of views or sheets. It is the number of parts to make. ✗Writing scale backwards: half size is 1:2, not 2:1. ✗Writing the measured (drawn) length instead of the true one on a scaled drawing. ✗Using a non-recommended scale such as 1:3 or 1:4. ✗Dimension, projection or hatching lines as heavy as the outline: they're all thin (B). ✗Hidden lines solid, centre lines dashed, pitch circles solid, alternative positions dashed. ✗A cutting plane drawn as a plain thin chain without its thick ends. ✗Mixing E and F hidden lines in one set, or several lettering styles on one sheet. ✗Hatching packed too tightly (gap less than twice the heaviest line). ✓Final check: count the nine title-block items; read the scale aloud as "drawing to true"; run your eye over every line type once.

15Practice (written for these notes)

1. Without looking: list the nine items that must be filled in the title block of a part drawing.
Answer

Part name; DWG No; QTY; MATL; Sheet X of Y; drawn by & date; dimension unit; scale; type of projection (symbol).

2. A hole of true diameter 12 mm appears on a drawing at scale 5:1. How big is the circle you draw, and what value do you write?
Hint

Drawing : true = 5 : 1.

Answer

Drawn diameter = 12 × 5 = 60 mm. Written value: Ø12, the true size.

3. A shaft 800 mm long must fit across an A3 landscape sheet (400 mm usable). Which recommended scale do you use, and how long is the shaft on paper? What else could you do to keep a larger scale?
Hint

Try the recommended reductions in order: 1:2, 1:5 …

Answer

1:2 gives 400 mm, exactly the usable width, with no room for dimensions or margins, so use 1:5: 800 ÷ 5 = 160 mm on paper. Alternatively, if the shaft is uniform along most of its length, draw it at 1:2 with a conventional break and dimension the full 800.

4. Give the line type letter for: (a) hatching, (b) a cutting plane, (c) the pitch circle of a gear, (d) the swung-open position of a lid, (e) a surface to be hardened, marked beside it, (f) the limit of a partial view, drawn by CAD.
Answer

(a) B · (b) H · (c) G · (d) K · (e) J · (f) D (C if freehand).

5. Your thick lines are 0.7 mm. What is the thickest your thin lines may be, and the closest your hatching lines may be?
Answer

Thick : thin ≥ 2 : 1, so thin ≤ 0.7 ÷ 2 = 0.35 mm. Hatching gap ≥ 2 × 0.7 = 1.4 mm.

6. Read this weld callout: an arrow to a T-joint, a circle where the arrow meets the reference line, and a fillet triangle below the reference line with the number 6 to its left.
Answer

A 6 mm fillet weld on the arrow side (symbol below the line), welded all round the joint (circle).
