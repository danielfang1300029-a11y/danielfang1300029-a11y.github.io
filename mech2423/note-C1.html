2Product development in one table

A product goes concept → prototype and feasibility → manufacturing → market → consumer → technical support. The course splits it into five phases:

PhaseStepsKey words to recognise
I Conceptual designNeed finding (observe, interview users, research existing products) → brainstorming with a mind map → product specification (features, performance, market, cost and price; measurable, with a schedule) → idea presentation (sketches, mock-up)Ends at the Concept Approval Meeting (CAM)
II Prototype and feasibilityProof-of-concept prototype (only the risky major features) → alpha prototype (CAD parts + assembly for motion and stress analysis; rapid prototyping: SLA, SLS, FDM, 3D printing) → review meetingAlpha = tested by the design team
III ProductionBeta prototype (small batch, tested by end users) → pre-production (documents to production standard, sales samples) → standardisation (standard parts such as aluminium profiles, to cut cost) → full production lineBeta = real-world problems
IV Introduction to marketLaunch—
V Succession planningWithdraw the product or develop new versionsCompletes the life cycle

Worked example: an apple-shaped pressure cooker goes freehand sketch → SolidWorks assembly → photorealistic rendering → part and assembly drawings. The drawing comes last: it is the only one a factory can make the part from.

3Anatomy of a production drawing

Here is a complete drawing of a part called a breech cylinder coupler. Each numbered element is a later chapter; the title block (7) is the part the test asks about.

A real production drawing, with its main parts numbered
1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8
  1. 1Notes: written instructions that don't fit as dimensions, such as material ("steel alloy…"), surface finish, fillet sizes and which standard applies.
  2. 2Orthographic views: flat 2D views looking straight at the part from different directions, lined up with each other. How they're arranged is the whole of Chapter 3 (Projection).
  3. 3Dimensions with tolerances: each size has a value and how far it may vary, e.g. 3.40 with +.02/−.00. Dimensioning rules are Chapter 5, and tolerances Chapter 7.
  4. 4Section view ("SECTION C-C"): the part drawn as if sliced along the line marked C–C, with hatching (diagonal lines) on the cut surfaces. Covered in Other Views.
  5. 5Detail view ("DETAIL A, SCALE 1.5 : 1"): a small region drawn larger so its tiny features can be dimensioned.
  6. 6Pictorial (3D) view: a shaded 3D picture to help the reader picture the part. It's for understanding only; nothing is measured from it.
  7. 7Title block: the boxed label in the bottom-right corner, with company, part title, drawing number, sheet size, scale, weight, revision, "sheet 1 of 2", who drew and checked it. This is the "corner labels" part of the test. Chapter 2 lists exactly what must go in it.
  8. 8Border and zone grid: the frame around the sheet, with numbers along the top and letters down the sides, so people can say "look at zone C5" like a map reference.

Tap the drawing to open it full size. This drawing follows the American (ANSI) style, with sizes in inches (e.g. ".40", "Ø1.50"). The course mainly uses the British/ISO style in millimetres. Chapter 5 lists the differences.

4Decision making

Must know
  • Observation uses the five senses; an inference is a logical interpretation of observations using prior knowledge.
  • Four strategies: optimising, satisficing, maximax, maximin (below).
  • Decision matrix total = Σ weight × score; score so that good is always high.

Decision strategies

Your decision can aim for one of four strategies:

OptimisingFind as many alternatives as possible and choose the very best. Thorough but slow.
SatisficingChoose the first satisfactory alternative instead of the best. Use it when there isn't enough time.
MaximaxChoose the alternative with the maximum possible payoff. Best when risk-taking is acceptable.
MaximinChoose the alternative whose worst possible outcome is the least bad. Best when a failed decision would be particularly harmful.
How to remember max-max and max-min

Read the names from the inside out. Maxi-max: look at each option's best case, then take the maximum of those. Maxi-min: look at each option's worst (minimum) case, then take the maximum of those. A gambler uses maximax. A bridge designer uses maximin.

The decision matrix

A product selection prioritisation matrix (decision matrix) is an easy but effective way to rank a list of options by group scoring:

  1. List all the options, the criteria (what matters), each criterion's weight (how much it matters) and the scoring scale.
  2. As a group, score each option on each criterion.
  3. Calculate each option's overall score.
  4. Rank the options from highest to lowest score, then check whether the ranking makes sense.

The overall score is a weighted sum:

\[ \text{Total}_j = \sum_{i} w_i \, s_{ij} \]

In words: for each option, multiply its score on every criterion by that criterion's weight, then add up the results.

Show the calculation: the three totals

Choice 1: \(1\times1 + 2\times4 + 1\times3 = 1+8+3 = 12\).

Choice 2: \(1\times3 + 2\times4 + 1\times2 = 3+8+2 = 13\).

Choice 3: \(1\times5 + 2\times1 + 1\times5 = 5+2+5 = 12\).

Choice 2 has the highest total, so it ranks 1st. Choices 1 and 3 tie at 12, so both rank 2nd.

The scale must say what each score means:

CriterionScore 1Score 5
Easy to useVery difficult (5 hrs learning)Very easy (5 mins learning)
CostHigh cost ($1000)Low cost ($100)
StrengthLow tensile strength (1 N/m²)High tensile strength (10 N/m²)
Score consistently

Beware the consistency of the scoring. A negative effect must always get the lower score. For cost, cheap is good, so a low price gets a high score. If you scored cost by "how big is the price", the expensive option would win on cost, which is backwards.

Last step: evaluate the decision

After acting on the decision, check how well it worked, for example by measuring customer satisfaction, and feed what you learn into the next decision.

5Quick checks

1. "Our beta testing was done by the design team in the lab, so we're ready for pre-production."
What's wrong?

Beta testing is done with or by end users, to find real-world problems the designers can't foresee. Testing by the design team in the lab is alpha testing.

2. A student's matrix: weights 1, 2, 1; Choice A scores 2, 3, 4; total = 2 + 3 + 4 = 9.
What's wrong?

The weights were ignored. Total = \(1\times2 + 2\times3 + 1\times4 = 12\).

3. "A spec line: the product should be fairly light and reasonably bright."
What's wrong?

It can't be measured, so you can't test against it. Write numbers with units, as the projector spec does: "weight ≤ 1.6 kg; luminance ≥ 3,000 lm".

4. "We chose the design with the best worst-case outcome. That's the maximax strategy."
What's wrong?

Choosing the best worst case is maximin. Maximax picks the best best case.
