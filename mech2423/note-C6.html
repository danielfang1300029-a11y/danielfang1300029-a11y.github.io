Other views: auxiliary, section and detail views

The four projection families, auxiliary views for sloping faces, and above all section views: the cutting-plane line, the hatching rules, what is never hatched, and the seven kinds of section. Ends with detail, assembly and exploded drawings.

0What the test wants

High Sections and hatching are a main source of errors in the 20-mark find-the-mistakes question. Auxiliary views and the projection families can turn up as short answers or in the 10-mark projection question. You need to be able to:

  • Check a section view: cutting-plane line, arrows, letters, visible edges behind the plane, no hidden lines (§5, §9).
  • Apply every hatching rule and spot breaks of it: 45°, thin, touches the outline, never crosses it, one part = one pattern, adjacent parts reversed (§6).
  • Know what is never hatched: ribs and webs cut lengthwise, bolts, nuts, shafts, keys, pins, balls (§8).
  • Name and recognise the section types (full, half, offset, broken-out, revolved, removed, scrap, aligned) and draw a simple full or offset section (§9, §13).
  • Construct an auxiliary view showing the true shape of a sloping face (§3), and tell the projection families apart (§1).

1Types of projection sl. 5–17

Must know
  • Projection plane: the imaginary sheet the image is drawn on. Projector: the line from the viewer's eye through a point on the object to the plane.
  • Three questions sort every projection: are the projectors converging or parallel? Do they meet the plane at 90° (orthogonal) or not (oblique)? Is the object's main face parallel or turned relative to the plane?
  • Only multiview (orthographic) is non-pictorial and uses more than one plane. The other three each give one pictorial (3D-looking) picture.

The four families seen from above (written for these notes)

Perspective: rays converge on the eye, so nearer things look bigger and you get a sense of depth (sl. 10). Multiview: rays parallel and square to the plane, eye "very far" away; a face parallel to the plane comes out true size and shape (sl. 11). Isometric: the same parallel square-on rays, but the object is turned so three faces show at once (sl. 12). Oblique: parallel rays hitting the plane at a slant, so the front face is true and the depth runs off at an angle (sl. 13).

The handout's summary sl. 14

The family tree: perspective vs parallel; parallel splits into orthographic (multiview and isometric) and oblique.

1st vs 3rd angle: a recap sl. 16–17

In first angle the object is imagined in the first quadrant (between viewer and plane), so each view lands on the far side: top view below the front view, right view on the left. In third angle the object is in the third quadrant behind the plane, so each view goes on the side you looked from: top above, right on the right. The views themselves are identical; only their positions and the symbol change. Full treatment, with the symbol: Chapter 3 §3.

2Types of views sl. 19

Quick pass

An object with complicated geometry gives so many hidden edges in a plain multiview drawing that it gets confusing. Six other kinds of view help: auxiliary (§3–4), pictorial (§4), section (§5–9), detail (§10), overlay and exploded (§10). The slides list "overlay view" but never show one. It normally means drawing a second position or part over a view in phantom (double-dashed chain) lines, as in Chapter 3's alternative positions.

3Auxiliary views: purpose and construction sl. 20–31

Must know
  • An auxiliary view looks at the object from a direction that is not one of the six principal views.
  • Purpose: show the true length / true shape of an inclined face or edge. In the principal views that face is either an edge (a line) or foreshortened (squashed).
  • How: choose a projection plane parallel to the inclined face, i.e. look perpendicular to it.
  • 4 steps: (1) find the face shown as a sloping edge; (2) draw a reference line parallel to that edge; (3) project every corner perpendicular to the edge and transfer depths from another view; (4) join the corners.
  • Kinds: single or multiple; full or partial (sl. 20).
Why it's needed sl. 21–22

The blue sloping face: a sloping line in the front view, and a squashed rectangle (labelled FORESHORTENED) in the top and right views. No principal view shows its real shape. Slide 22 adds a fourth glass-box wall parallel to the slope: on that wall the face shows true size.

The construction, step by step

The block below is 60 wide, 40 deep and 40 high, with its top-left corner cut off by a plane running from 15 up the left end to 30 along the top. Front and right views are given (third angle). In the right view the sloping face is out of sight, so its lower edge is a hidden line.

  1. Step 1: find the view where the sloping face is an edge (a line). Here the front view. You'll look at the face square-on, i.e. along the perpendicular to that line.
  2. Step 2: decide which true size will show (here: the face's length along the slope and the depth) and pick a reference. Draw a reference line parallel to the sloping edge, some distance away. It stands for the front face (depth 0), which is the left edge of the right view (the "edge view of the reference plane").
  3. Step 3: from every corner of the front view draw a projector perpendicular to the sloping edge (a "90° rotation" from the front view). Along each projector measure the depth from the reference line, taking it from the right view: here every corner has depth 0 (front) and 40 (back).
  4. Step 4: join the corners that share an edge. Visible edges thick; edges behind the sloping face hidden.

Check, written for these notes: the sloping edge runs 30 across and 25 up, so its true length is √(30² + 25²) = 39.05. The strip it makes in the auxiliary view is 39.05 wide (computed from the projected corners), and 40 deep: the true shape of the face. Looking down the slope you also see the left end face and the top face (thin strips either side) and, through the material, the bottom-right edge as a hidden line. That makes this a full auxiliary view.

The handout's example sl. 24, 28, 30–31

Every corner is numbered (1–9) in each view; where two corners line up the view shows both numbers ("4,7"). The blue projectors leave the front view at 90° to the sloping edge 1,2–5. Depths come from the top view. Slide 31 adds: the auxiliary view shows the true size and shape of the angled surface, so a hole in it could now be properly dimensioned.

5★ Section views: cutting plane and what you see sl. 37–41

Must know
  • Why: inside features drawn as hidden lines become a mess of dashes. A section pretends to cut the part and remove the piece nearer the viewer, so the inside shows as visible lines.
  • Cutting-plane line (in the view where the plane is seen edge-on): thin chain line, thick short lines at each end (and at each change of direction), arrows at the ends showing the viewing direction, and letters (A–A) identifying it. The section view is labelled with the same letters: "A–A".
  • Cut material is hatched. Everything else visible behind the cutting plane must still be drawn (thick).
  • No hidden lines in a section view (the section exists to get rid of them).

6★ Hatching rules sl. 42–45

Must know
  1. Hatching (section lines) goes only on cut surfaces, to show inside from outside.
  2. Thin lines at 45°, spaced more than 4 mm apart.
  3. Hatching never crosses an outline, and it touches the outline of the section (no gaps).
  4. If 45° would run parallel or perpendicular to a main outline of the area, use another angle.
  5. One part: same slope and same spacing everywhere in the view, even on separated areas.
  6. Adjacent parts: reverse the slope. A third part touching both: change the angle or vary the spacing.
  7. Thin material (sheet metal, gaskets) too thin to hatch: fill in solid. Adjacent thin parts: leave a small gap between them.

8★ What is never hatched sl. 50–55

Must know
  • In a part: webs, ribs, spokes and gear teeth are not hatched when the cutting plane runs along them (lengthwise). They are outlined only. Cut across (transversely), they are hatched as normal.
  • In an assembly, these are not hatched when cut lengthwise: nuts, bolts, screws, studs, rivets, solid shafts, small solid cylindrical parts, keys, cotters, split pins, taper pins, balls and rollers in bearings (and washers, as slide 54 labels them).
  • Reason: hatching them makes the part look like a solid block, which misleads (a web would look like a solid wall; a hatched bolt hides that it's a standard part).

Offset section sl. 58

The cutting plane jogs to pass through features that aren't in line. In the section the jog is not drawn: the cut faces are shown as if they were one flat plane. On the cutting-plane line, each change of direction gets a thick dash ("thick dashes to show change of cutting plane's direction").

Revolved and removed sections sl. 60

Both show the cross-section shape of a long part (arm, spoke, rib). A revolved section is turned 90° about its own axis and drawn right on the view (the view's lines may be broken around it). A removed section is the same shape drawn elsewhere on the sheet, and must be labelled (with a cutting-plane line) so the reader knows where it comes from.

Part / scrap section sl. 61

Like a broken-out section, but drawn in any convenient place away from the main view, with a thin irregular boundary.

Aligned section sl. 62

The cutting plane runs along the main centre line and then along a radial line to an arm or hole off to the side. The radial part is rotated into line with the main plane before projecting, so the section shows each feature at its true distance from the centre. (A straight projection would show it too close.)

10Detail view, assembly and exploded drawings sl. 63–65

Must know
  • Detail view: a small, crowded area of a view, circled and redrawn enlarged, usually at twice the scale. Circle and detail carry the same letter; the detail is labelled with its letter and scale, e.g. "DETAIL A, SCALE 2:1".
  • Assembly drawing: all parts in their working positions, often sectioned, with numbered balloons pointing at each part and a parts list (item no., name, quantity, material) above the title block.
  • Exploded view: a pictorial assembly with the parts pulled apart along their assembly lines, so each can be seen and identified; balloons and parts list as above.

11Spot the mistake (written for these notes)

Each drawing has 3 planted errors of the kind used in the 20-mark question (one error may appear in two places). Circle them and name the rule each breaks. Run the checklist: cutting-plane line → arrows and letters → hatch angle, spacing, outline → one part, one pattern → adjacent parts → never-hatched parts → lines behind the plane → hidden lines.

Drill 1 · Written for these notes
The base plate with the counterbored boss from §5, third angle. Section A–A. Find 3 errors.
Hint

Compare the section with the one in §5. Are there lines across the holes? Look closely at the top of the boss and at the small block on the right.

Answer
  1. Visible edges behind the plane omitted (marked twice). The counterbore floor and rim and the bottom of both holes need lines across them. Without them the section falls apart into separate blocks (slide 41, "incorrect").
  2. Hatching crosses the outline at the top-left of the boss. Hatching must stop exactly on the outline.
  3. Inconsistent hatching on one part: the right-hand area is hatched the other way. All three areas are the same part, so they need the same slope and spacing; reversed hatching says "a different part".
Drill 2 · Written for these notes
Same part, same projection. This time the section view is right. Find 3 errors in the cutting-plane line.
Hint

Line type, arrow direction, letters.

Answer
  1. Wrong line type: a thick chain line all the way. It should be a thin chain line with thick short lines only at the ends.
  2. Arrows reversed: they point down, towards the front view, meaning "look from the back". For a section drawn in the front-view position they must point away from it (up), the direction you look in.
  3. Letters don't match: A at one end, B at the other. Both ends carry the same letter, matching the label "A–A" on the section.
Drill 3 · Written for these notes
The hub with a web from §8, third angle. Section A–A runs along the web. Find 3 errors.
Hint

Which feature does the cutting plane run along? Then compare the spacing on the two hub walls, and look at where the hatch lines end in the base.

Answer
  1. Web hatched. A thin web cut lengthwise is not hatched; outline it only. Hatched, it looks like a solid block.
  2. Different spacing on the same part: the right hub wall is hatched wider than the rest. One part: same slope and spacing everywhere.
  3. Hatching stops short of the outline in the base. Section lines must touch the outline.
Drill 4 · Written for these notes
The flanged bush from §9, half section A–A. Find 3 errors.
Hint

What's allowed in the outside half? What line divides the halves? Is every hatched area really solid material?

Answer
  1. Hidden lines in the outside half (bore, counterbore and flange hole). A half section has no hidden lines: the cut half already shows the inside.
  2. Thick solid line between the halves. They are divided by a centre line (thin chain): the part isn't really split there.
  3. Hatching across the bore: the bore is empty space. Hatch only the material the plane cuts.
Drill 5 · Written for these notes
The bolted joint from §8 (two plates, gasket, bolt, washer, nut), sectioned through the bolt axis. Find 3 errors.
Hint

Count the parts. Which of them should be hatched at all, and how?

Answer
  1. Bolt and nut hatched. Bolts, nuts, washers (and studs, screws, shafts, keys, pins) cut lengthwise are left unhatched in assemblies.
  2. Both plates hatched the same way. Adjacent parts: reverse the slope, otherwise they read as one part.
  3. Gasket hatched: it is too thin. Thin material is filled in solid.
Drill 6 · Written for these notes
The stepped shaft from §9 with a broken-out section at the small end. Find 3 errors.
Hint

Line type of the boundary, where hatching may go, and how features exposed by a section are drawn.

Answer
  1. Boundary drawn thick. A broken-out boundary is a thin irregular line.
  2. Hatching runs past the boundary into the unsectioned part. It must stop on the break line.
  3. Hole drawn dashed inside the section. The section exposes it, so its edges are visible (thick), and a section view carries no hidden lines.

1st angle: object in 1st quadrant, views on the far side. 3rd: views on the near side.

Auxiliary view

  • Plane ∥ inclined face → true shape. Reference line ∥ edge; projectors ⟂ edge; depths from another view; join corners.
  • Full = with hidden lines. Partial = no hidden lines, linked by arrow / centre line / projection lines; break line not on a visible or hidden line. Half. Multiple = primary + secondary.

Cutting-plane line

  • Thin chain, thick ends (and thick at bends), arrows = viewing direction, same letters both ends, view labelled A–A.

Hatching

  • Thin, 45°, spacing > 4 mm; avoid parallel/⟂ to an outline.
  • Never crosses an outline; touches the outline.
  • One part: same slope + spacing (even separate areas).
  • Adjacent parts: reversed. Third part: new angle or spacing.
  • Thin parts: solid black, gaps between.
  • Draw everything visible behind the plane. No hidden lines.
  • Plain 45° = cast iron / general. Know steel, bronze, aluminium, rubber/plastic on sight.

Never hatched (lengthwise)

  • Part: webs, ribs, spokes, gear teeth (hatched if cut across).
  • Assembly: nuts, bolts, screws, studs, washers, rivets, solid shafts, small solid cylinders, keys, cotters, split / taper pins, balls and rollers.

Section types

  • Full · half (centre line divides, no hidden) · offset (step not shown) · broken-out (thin freehand boundary) · revolved (on view) · removed (off view, labelled) · scrap (away, thin boundary) · aligned (radial features rotated, true distance).
  • Detail: circled, letter, usually 2:1. Assembly: balloons + parts list. Exploded: pulled apart pictorial.

12Common mistakes

✗Drawing only the cut faces and forgetting the edges behind the plane (hole bottoms, counterbore floors, far outlines). ✗Leaving hidden lines in a section view, or in the outside half of a half section. ✗Hatching empty space: bores, slots, the gap between bolt and hole. ✗Hatching a rib, web, shaft, bolt, nut, key or pin that the plane cuts lengthwise. ✗Two areas of one part hatched differently, or two adjacent parts hatched the same way. ✗Hatch lines that poke past the outline, stop short of it, are thick, or are crowded closer than 4 mm. ✗Cutting-plane line drawn as a plain thick or thin line; arrows pointing the wrong way; missing letters or section label. ✗Drawing the jog of an offset section as a line in the section view. ✗A thick or ruled boundary on a broken-out section (it's thin and freehand). ✗Partial auxiliary view with hidden lines, or a break line sitting on top of a visible line. ✓Final check: every closed hatched area is solid material that the plane cut; every edge you could see from the arrow's direction is drawn.

13Practice (written for these notes)

1. Draw the section. The hub with a web (third angle): top view with cutting plane A–A, and the plain front view. Draw section A–A in place of the front view.
Hint

The plane runs along the centre of the web and through the hub's axis. Which cut faces are solid material? Which of them is a web?

Answer

Hatched (one part, one pattern): the base plate and the two hub walls. The bore between the walls is empty: the only lines there are its far edges at top and bottom. The web is not hatched (cut lengthwise): outline it, with a line along the base top and the hub wall where it meets them. The hidden bore lines are gone.

2. Draw the section. A plate with a hole and a boss that don't line up (third angle). The top view carries an offset cutting plane B–B; the exterior front view is given. Draw section B–B.
Hint

The plane passes through the centre of the small hole, steps, then passes through the centre of the boss. Do you draw the step?

Answer

Three hatched areas, all one part, so the same slope and spacing. Lines across the top and bottom of both holes (edges behind the plane). No line where the plane steps: an offset section is drawn as though the cut were flat. No hidden lines.

3. Classify: projectors parallel, meeting the projection plane at 90°, object turned so three faces show. Pictorial or not?
Answer

Isometric (axonometric) projection: an orthographic projection, but pictorial, on one plane. Same rays as multiview; the difference is the object's orientation.

4. In an assembly section through a pulley on a shaft, which of these are hatched: pulley, shaft, key, set screw, housing, ball bearing outer race, the balls?
Answer

Hatched: pulley, housing, bearing outer race (with adjacent ones reversed). Not hatched: shaft, key, set screw, balls.

5. A sloping face in the front view runs 40 mm across and 30 mm down. The part is 25 mm deep. What are the face's dimensions in its auxiliary view, and is the auxiliary view projected from the front or the top view?
Hint

Project from the view where the face is an edge.

Answer

True length √(40² + 30²) = 50 mm by the depth 25 mm. Project from the front view, where the face is seen as an edge; take the depth from the top (or side) view.

6. Which section type? (a) A handwheel's spoke cross-section drawn on the spoke itself. (b) A keyway at one point of a long shaft, shown by cutting away a small patch. (c) A flange with holes at 120°, drawn so every hole appears at its true radius.
Answer
