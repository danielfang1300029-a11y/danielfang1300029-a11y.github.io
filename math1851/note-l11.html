Antiderivatives and Area

0Where this lecture sits

Lecture 11 starts integration with two questions. The speedometer question: if you know how fast something changes at every moment, can you rebuild the thing itself? The area question: how big is a region with a curved top? The answer to the second is to fill the region with thin strips and add them up. Lecture 12 shows that the two questions are really the same.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 An antiderivative rebuilds the odometer from the speedometer. It is fixed only up to the starting reading, the \(+C\).
  2. §2 Read every derivative rule backwards. Power rule: raise the power by one, divide by the new power (not for \(\frac1x\)).
  3. §3 \(\int f(x)\,dx\) is a name for the whole family \(F(x)+C\).
  4. §4 Straight-edged shapes are rectangles and triangles. A curved top needs a new method.
  5. §5 Fill the region with thin rectangles and add them up. On a rising curve, left sums fall short and right sums stick out.
  6. §6 Sigma notation writes a long sum in one line, and it splits like antiderivatives do.
  7. §7 Squeeze the true area between the short strips and the tall strips. For \(x^2\) on \([0,1]\) both close in on \(\frac13\).
  8. §8 Area is the limit of the strip sums, for a continuous \(f\ge0\). The four-step recipe.

You need: the derivative rules of Lectures 6 and 7 (read backwards here), limits of rational functions as \(n\to\infty\) (Lecture 3) and the Sandwich Theorem idea (Lecture 2). In the exam: no past-paper questions have this lecture as their home; Riemann-sum questions on the papers need Lecture 12 (see §14 and the L12 exam guide).

Recall. First, a reminder from Lecture 10. An indeterminate form is a limit that the Limit Laws alone cannot decide, written symbolically as \(\frac00,\ \frac\infty\infty,\ \infty-\infty,\ 0\cdot\infty,\ \infty^0,\ 1^\infty,\ 0^0\). L'Hôpital's Rule: if \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable and \(\lim\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) is of the form \(\frac00\) or \(\frac\infty\infty\), then

\[ \lim\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\lim\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}, \]

provided the right-hand limit exists (or is \(\pm\infty\)). "\(\lim\)" may mean \(x\to a\), \(x\to a^\pm\) or \(x\to\pm\infty\), and differences, products and powers are first rewritten as a quotient. (Lecture 10 gives the precise conditions, including \(g'(a)\ne0\).) You will use L'Hôpital again in this lecture only implicitly; the limits here are simple rational limits.

Goals of this lecture: compute basic antiderivatives; introduce indefinite integrals and sigma notation; define area as a limit of finite sums. Review questions are on Ed.

1Antiderivatives ~4 min

What is this?

Picture a car trip. The speedometer shows how fast you are going at each moment; the odometer shows the total distance on the clock. Speed is the rate at which the odometer reading changes, so the speedometer record is the derivative of the odometer record. An antiderivative goes the other way: from the whole speedometer record, rebuild the odometer record. You can rebuild how far you went, but not the reading the odometer showed when you set off. That starting reading is the \(+C\). (If the car can reverse, use its position along the road instead of the odometer; the idea is the same.)

Definition

A function \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) on an interval \(I\) if

\[ F'(x)=f(x)\quad\text{for all } x \text{ in } I. \]

In words: an antiderivative of \(f\) is a function whose derivative is \(f\). Differentiation goes from \(F\) down to \(f\); antidifferentiation goes back up from \(f\) to \(F\).

For example, \(F(x)=\frac{x^3}{3}\) is an antiderivative of \(f(x)=x^2\), because \(\frac{d}{dx}\frac{x^3}{3}=\frac{3x^2}{3}=x^2\). But so is \(\frac{x^3}3+1\), and \(\frac{x^3}{3}-7\): adding a constant does not change the derivative, because the derivative of a constant is \(0\). So:

If \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\), then so is \(F(x)+C\) for any constant \(C\).

Analogy. Your car's speedometer records your speed at every moment (that is \(f\), the derivative). From the speed record alone you can reconstruct how far you travelled, but not where you started. Every starting point gives a different position record \(F\), and all of them have the same speed record. The unknown starting point is the constant \(C\).

What is this? (the most general antiderivative)

Two cars drive side by side with identical speedometer records for the whole trip. Their odometers may show different numbers, but the difference between them never changes, because both readings go up at the same rate. So once you know one possible odometer record, every other one is the same record plus a fixed amount. This needs one unbroken trip: if the record has a gap in the middle (the domain is not an interval), the odometer could be reset during the gap, and each part can get its own constant.

Theorem: the most general antiderivative

If \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) on an interval \(I\), then the most general antiderivative of \(f\) on \(I\) is

\[ F(x)+C, \]

where \(C\) is an arbitrary constant.

In words: once you have found one antiderivative, you have found them all: every other one is the same function shifted up or down by a constant.

Reading the formula
\(F(x)\)
one odometer record that matches the speedometer: the one you found.
\(C\)
the starting reading. It can be any real number.
\(F(x)+C\)
every odometer record that matches the speedometer, one for each starting reading. On an interval nothing else matches.

The theorem says two things. Every \(F+C\) works (shown above). And nothing else works: if \(G\) is any antiderivative, then \(G=F+C\) for some constant. The second part comes from the Mean Value Theorem (Lecture 8): \((G-F)'=f-f=0\) on the interval, and a function with zero derivative on an interval is constant. This is why the word interval matters: on a domain with a gap, the constant can be different on each piece (Concept check C2).

How to check an answer: differentiate it. If \(F'(x)=f(x)\), your antiderivative is right. This check is quick and catches almost every error, so do it every time.

You can now: check whether a function is an antiderivative by differentiating it; explain why adding any constant gives another antiderivative; and say why the theorem needs an interval.

Why \(n=-1\) is special, and why \(|x|\). \(x^{-1}=\frac1x\) is the one power that the power rule cannot reach; its antiderivative is a logarithm instead. The absolute value is there because \(\frac1x\) is defined for negative \(x\) too, while \(\ln x\) is not. For \(x\gt0\), \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln x=\frac1x\). For \(x\lt0\), by the chain rule \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln(-x)=\frac{-1}{-x}=\frac1x\). The single formula \(\ln|x|\) covers both sides.

Notation reminders: \(\sec x=\frac1{\cos x}\), \(\csc x=\frac1{\sin x}\), \(\cot x=\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\); \(\sin^{-1}x\) and \(\tan^{-1}x\) are the inverse trig functions (also written \(\arcsin x\), \(\arctan x\)), not reciprocals (Lecture 7).

Rules for combining

What is this? (linearity)

Walk forwards along a moving train. Your speed over the ground is the train's speed plus your walking speed, and the ground you cover is the train's distance plus your walking distance: add the speeds and you add the distances. In the same way, going twice as fast at every moment covers twice the distance. Those two facts are all that linearity says.

Suppose \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\), \(G\) is an antiderivative of \(g\), and \(k,\ell\) are constants.

RuleFunctionGeneral antiderivative
Constant multiple\(kf(x)\)\(kF(x)+C\)
Negative\(-f(x)\)\(-F(x)+C\)
Sum / difference\(f(x)\pm g(x)\)\(F(x)\pm G(x)+C\)
Linearity\(kf(x)\pm\ell g(x)\)\(kF(x)\pm\ell G(x)+C\)

In words: constants come out in front, and you may antidifferentiate a sum term by term. "Linearity" is the name for both facts together. They hold because the same is true for derivatives: \(\big(kF\pm\ell G\big)'=kF'\pm\ell G'=kf\pm\ell g\).

Trap: there is no product or quotient rule here

The antiderivative of a product is not the product of the antiderivatives: \(x\cdot x=x^2\) has antiderivative \(\frac{x^3}3\), not \(\frac{x^2}2\cdot\frac{x^2}2=\frac{x^4}4\). To antidifferentiate a product or quotient, first expand or divide it into a sum of powers (as in Group work 1(b)), or wait for the techniques of Lectures 13 and 14.

Going further · a linear expression inside (needed for Example 1)

The table has \(\sin x\) and \(e^x\), but Example 1 below has \(\sin 3x\) and Group work 1 has \(e^{2x}\). Guess, then fix the constant by differentiating:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\big(-\cos 3x\big)=3\sin3x \]

\(\Longrightarrow\) an antiderivative of \(\sin3x\) is \(-\tfrac13\cos3x\).

In general, if \(F'=f\) and \(a\ne0\), then by the chain rule \(\frac{d}{dx}\Big[\frac1aF(ax+b)\Big]=f(ax+b)\), so an antiderivative of \(f(ax+b)\) is

\[ \frac1a\,F(ax+b). \]

In words: when the inside is \(ax+b\), use the table as usual, then divide by \(a\), the number multiplying \(x\). This only works when the inside is linear; Lecture 13 (substitution) handles general insides.

Example 1

Compute the most general antiderivative of \(f(x)=x^3+\sin3x-1\).

Solution. Use linearity and treat each term separately.

  • \(x^3\): power rule with \(n=3\) gives \(\frac{x^4}{4}\).
  • \(\sin 3x\): \(-\frac13\cos3x\) (box above). differentiating \(\cos3x\) brings out a factor \(3\) (chain rule), so the \(\frac13\) is there to cancel it.
  • \(-1=-x^0\): power rule with \(n=0\) gives \(-x\).
\[ F(x)=\frac{x^4}{4}-\frac13\cos3x-x+C. \]

Check:

\[ \begin{aligned} F'(x)&=x^3-\tfrac13(-\sin3x)(3)-1\\ &=x^3+\sin3x-1=f(x).\ ✓ \end{aligned} \]

Only one \(+C\) is needed: the three separate constants add up to a single arbitrary constant.

Beyond Lecture 11: fixing \(C\) with one known value

If you are also told one value of \(F\), the constant is no longer arbitrary. Example: \(F'(x)=x^2\) and \(F(0)=1\). Then \(F(x)=\frac{x^3}3+C\) and \(F(0)=C=1\), so \(F(x)=\frac{x^3}{3}+1\). In the figure in section 1, the extra condition picks out exactly one curve of the family (the one through \((0,1)\)). Such "initial value problems" return in the ODE part of the course.

You can now: write down antiderivatives of powers, \(e^x\), \(\frac1x\), and the trig and inverse-trig functions in the table; split a sum term by term and take constants out; handle a linear inside such as \(\sin3x\) by dividing by the \(3\); and check every answer by differentiating.

3The indefinite integral ~3 min

What is this?

\(\int f(x)\,dx\) is a short way of asking the speedometer question: which odometer records give this speed record? The answer is not one function but the whole family \(F(x)+C\), one member for each starting reading. The word indefinite is a reminder of that: no starting reading has been fixed, so the answer is a family of functions, not a number.

Definition

The collection of all antiderivatives of \(f\) is called the indefinite integral of \(f\) with respect to \(x\), and is denoted

\[ \int f(x)\,dx. \]

The symbol \(\int\) is an integral sign, the function \(f\) is the integrand, and \(x\) is the variable of integration. If \(F\) is any antiderivative of \(f\),

\[ \int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C, \]

the most general antiderivative.

In words: "\(\int f(x)\,dx\)" is a name for the question "which functions have derivative \(f(x)\)?", and "\(F(x)+C\)" is its answer.

Reading the formula
\(\int\)
"find every function whose derivative is ...". It is a stretched S, for sum; sections 5 to 8 show where a sum comes in.
\(f(x)\)
the integrand: the speed record you are given.
\(dx\)
\(x\) is the variable that moves; any other letter is a constant.
\(F(x)+C\)
the answer: one antiderivative plus the unknown starting reading.

About the pieces of the notation:

  • The \(\int\) sign is a stretched letter S, for "sum". Sections 5 to 8 explain why a sum is involved; Lecture 12 makes the link precise.
  • \(dx\) tells you which letter is the variable. In \(\int t^2\,dt\) the variable is \(t\), and the answer is \(\frac{t^3}3+C\). In \(\int a^2\,dx\) the letter \(a\) is a constant and the answer is \(a^2x+C\). The integrand and the \(dx\) must use the same letter (see the note in Group work 2).
  • The word indefinite means there are no limits on the integral sign and the answer is a family of functions (with \(+C\)), not a number. Lecture 12 introduces the definite integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), which is a number.

The techniques for harder integrals (substitution, integration by parts, partial fractions and others) are covered in Lectures 13 and 14. In Lecture 11 you only need the table and linearity.

Example 2

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int\big(x^3+\sin3x-1\big)\,dx\).

Solution. This is Example 1 written in integral notation, so

\[ \begin{aligned} &\int\big(x^3+\sin3x-1\big)\,dx\\ &\quad=\frac{x^4}{4}-\frac13\cos3x-x+C. \end{aligned} \]

Lesson: "find the most general antiderivative of \(f\)" and "evaluate \(\int f(x)\,dx\)" are the same task. The answer must include \(+C\).

You can now: read and write the \(\int f(x)\,dx\) notation; name the integrand and the variable of integration; and evaluate simple indefinite integrals with the table and linearity, always ending with \(+C\).

Example 3

Find the area bounded by \(y=2-x\), \(x=0\) and \(y=0\).

Solution. "Bounded by" means the region enclosed by the three lines. \(x=0\) is the \(y\)-axis and \(y=0\) is the \(x\)-axis. The line \(y=2-x\) meets the \(y\)-axis at \((0,2)\) and the \(x\)-axis at \((2,0)\). The region is a right-angled triangle with legs 2 and 2:

\[ A=\tfrac12\times2\times2=2. \]

In words: area = ½ × base × height = ½ × 2 × 2 = 2 square units.

You can check this with the explorer in section 5 (choose "\(2-x\) on [0, 2]"): the rectangle sums approach 2.

What about curved edges?

Now take the region under a wavy curve \(y=f(x)\) between \(x=a\) and \(x=b\): bounded by the curve on top, the \(x\)-axis below, and the vertical lines \(x=a\), \(x=b\) at the sides. No formula from the table fits. The rest of the lecture builds a method that works for any such region.

You can now: find the area of a region bounded by straight lines (as in Example 3) from its base and height; spot a quarter circle or a pair of triangles hidden in a formula; and say why a curved top needs a new method.

5Approximating area with rectangles ~6 min

What is this?

Imagine filling the region under a curve with thin upright strips of card, side by side, each one a rectangle. Each strip is cut to the height of the curve at one point of its base, so its top is flat while the curve is not: a little bit sticks out above the curve, or falls short below it. Add up the strip areas to estimate the area. For a continuous curve, thinner strips leave smaller errors.

The idea. Slice the region into thin vertical strips. Each strip is almost a rectangle, and rectangles are easy. Add up the rectangle areas to get an approximation; use more, thinner strips to get a better one. Such sums are named after Bernhard Riemann (Georg Friedrich Bernhard Riemann, 1826–1866), the German mathematician.

Analogy. A city skyline drawn in pixels. With large pixels the outline is blocky; with smaller pixels it looks smooth. The area of the pixel picture approaches the true area as the pixels shrink.

The test case: area under \(y=x^2\) on \([0,1]\)

Find the area \(A\) between the parabola \(y=x^2\) and the \(x\)-axis, from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\). Choose \(n\) rectangles; start with \(n=4\).

  • Split \([0,1]\) into 4 equal subintervals \([0,\frac14],[\frac14,\frac12],[\frac12,\frac34],[\frac34,1]\). Each has width \(\frac14\), so each rectangle has width \(\frac14\).
  • The height of each rectangle is the value of \(f\) at some point of its subinterval: the left endpoint, the right endpoint, or anything in between.

Left and right sums with \(n=4\): \(L_4\le A\le R_4\)

What is this? (left and right sums)

A left sum cuts each strip to the curve's height at the strip's left edge; a right sum uses the right edge. On a rising curve the left edge is the lowest point of each strip, so every left strip stays under the curve, and every right strip pokes above it. The true area is caught between the two totals.

\(L_n\) means "the sum of \(n\) rectangles with heights taken at left endpoints", and \(R_n\) the same with right endpoints.

6Sigma notation ~5 min

What is this?

Adding fifty strip areas one by one is like writing out a till receipt line by line. Sigma notation is the one-line total: it says which strips to count (from the bottom number to the top number) and gives the area formula for strip number \(i\). Nothing new is calculated; it is shorthand for a long addition.

Writing out \(R_{50}\) term by term would take 50 terms. Sigma notation writes such sums in one line.

Definition: sigma notation \[ \sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i=a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots+a_n. \]

The Greek capital letter \(\Sigma\) (sigma, an S for "sum") means "add up". The index of summation \(i\) tells us where the sum begins (below the \(\Sigma\): \(i=1\)) and where it ends (above: \(n\)). \(a_i\) is the formula for the \(i\)th term.

In words: "\(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\)" = "put \(i=1,2,3,\ldots,n\) into the formula \(a_i\) in turn, and add all the results".

Reading the formula
\(\Sigma\)
"add up".
\(i=1\) (below)
the first strip counted.
\(n\) (above)
the last strip counted.
\(a_i\)
the formula for strip number \(i\): put in \(i=1,2,\ldots,n\) in turn.

Examples: \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{4}i^2=1+4+9+16=30\). \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{3}2^i=1+2+4+8=15\). \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac1k=1+\frac12+\frac13\).

  • The index letter does not matter (\(\sum_{i=1}^n i^2=\sum_{k=1}^n k^2\)); it is a placeholder, like the \(x\) in \(f(x)=x^2\).
  • The number of terms is (top − bottom + 1): \(\sum_{i=1}^n\) has \(n\) terms but \(\sum_{i=0}^n\) has \(n+1\).
  • Anything that does not involve the index is a constant inside the sum: in \(\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{i}{n}\), \(n\) is fixed while \(i\) changes.
Definition: Riemann sum

A Riemann sum of \(f\) over \([a,b]\) is a method to approximate the area of the region between the graph of \(y=f(x)\) and the \(x\)-axis by partitioning (cutting up) the region into shapes, here rectangles, and adding their areas. \(L_n\), \(R_n\) and the midpoint sum \(M_n\) are all Riemann sums.

What is this? (rules for sums)

Stack two strips on top of each other in every slot, and the total area is the sum of the two separate totals (rule 1). Make every strip three times taller, and the total triples (rule 3). These are the same splitting rules as for antiderivatives. Rules 5 to 7 are ready-made totals for \(1+2+\cdots+n\) and for the squares and cubes, which you need whenever the strip heights are powers of \(i\).

Theorem: properties of finite sums
RuleFormula
1. Sum\(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n(a_i+b_i)=\sum_{i=1}^na_i+\sum_{i=1}^nb_i\)
2. Difference\(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n(a_i-b_i)=\sum_{i=1}^na_i-\sum_{i=1}^nb_i\)
3. Constant multiple\(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^nca_i=c\sum_{i=1}^na_i\)
4. Constant value\(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^nc=n\cdot c\)
5. Sum of integers\(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^ni=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\)
6. Sum of squares\(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^ni^2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}\)
7. Sum of cubes\(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^ni^3=\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2\)

In words: (1–3) sums split over \(+\) and \(-\), and constants come out, exactly like antiderivatives (both are "linear"). (4) adding the same number \(c\) to itself \(n\) times gives \(nc\); note \(\sum_{i=1}^n1=n\), not \(1\). (5–7) the sums \(1+2+\cdots+n\), \(1^2+2^2+\cdots+n^2\) and \(1^3+\cdots+n^3\) have closed formulas in \(n\). Curiously, the sum of the first \(n\) cubes is the square of the sum of the first \(n\) integers.

Quick check with \(n=4\): \(1+2+3+4=10=\frac{4\cdot5}{2}\); \(1+4+9+16=30=\frac{4\cdot5\cdot9}6\); \(1+8+27+64=100=10^2\).

Rules 5 to 7 are proved by induction on \(n\): check the formula for \(n=1\), then show that if it holds for some \(n\) it also holds for \(n+1\). Induction is not part of HKDSE Core.

Going further · a two-line proof of the sum of integers

Write the sum forwards and backwards and add the two lines column by column:

\[ \begin{aligned} S&=1+2+\cdots+(n-1)+n\\ S&=n+(n-1)+\cdots+2+1\\ 2S&=(n+1)+(n+1)+\cdots+(n+1)\\ &=n(n+1). \end{aligned} \]

So \(S=\frac{n(n+1)}2\). (This is the familiar arithmetic-sequence sum formula from HKDSE.) There is no equally short trick for squares; the induction proof checks that \(\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6+(n+1)^2=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)(2n+3)}{6}\).

Trap: no product rule for sums

\(\sum a_ib_i\ne\big(\sum a_i\big)\big(\sum b_i\big)\) and \(\sum a_i^2\ne\big(\sum a_i\big)^2\). Rules 1 to 4 are the only ways to split a sum; everything else must be expanded first.

You can now: expand and evaluate a short sigma sum; count its terms as top − bottom + 1; split a sum with rules 1 to 4; and use rules 5 to 7 for \(\sum i\), \(\sum i^2\) and \(\sum i^3\).

7The exact area under \(y=x^2\) on \([0,1]\) ~4 min

What is this?

Here the strip picture gives an exact answer. For \(y=x^2\) on \([0,1]\) the left strips always stay below the curve and the right strips always reach above it, so the true area is squeezed between the two totals. Sigma notation turns each total into a formula in \(n\). As the strips get thinner, both formulas close in on the same number, and the area, trapped between them, must equal it.

A formula for \(R_n\)

Let \(\Delta x\) ("delta \(x\)", a single symbol meaning a small change in \(x\)) be the width of each rectangle. With \(n\) equal strips on \([0,1]\), \(\Delta x=\frac1n\), and the right endpoints are \(x_i=\frac in\) for \(i=1,\ldots,n\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} R_n&=\sum_{i=1}^nf(x_i)\,\Delta x\\ &=f(x_1)\Delta x+f(x_2)\Delta x+\cdots+f(x_n)\Delta x\\ &=\left(\frac1n\right)^2\frac1n+\left(\frac2n\right)^2\frac1n\\ &\quad+\left(\frac3n\right)^2\frac1n+\cdots+\left(\frac nn\right)^2\frac1n\\ &=\frac1{n^3}\big(1^2+2^2+3^2+\cdots+n^2\big)\\ &=\frac1{n^3}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6\\ &=\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2}, \end{aligned} \]

by the sum-of-squares formula (rule 6).

In words: right sum = (height at each right endpoint × width), added over all strips. Each term has a common factor \(\frac1{n^3}\); what remains is \(1^2+\cdots+n^2\), which has a closed formula.

Reading the formula

Check: \(n=4\) gives \(\frac{5\cdot9}{96}=0.46875=R_4\) and \(n=8\) gives \(\frac{9\cdot17}{384}=0.3984375=R_8\), matching the tables.

Example 4

Find a formula for \(L_n\) for \(y=x^2\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\).

Solution. Again \(\Delta x=\frac1n\), but now the heights come from the left endpoints \(x_{i-1}=\frac{i-1}n\), \(i=1,\ldots,n\), i.e. \(0,\frac1n,\ldots,\frac{n-1}n\). Write the sum with the index running from \(0\) to \(n-1\): the left edges are \(0,\frac1n,\ldots,\frac{n-1}n\), so counting from \(i=0\) makes the \(i\)th left edge simply \(\frac in\).

\[ \begin{aligned} L_n&=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac in\right)^2\frac1n\\ &=\frac1{n^3}\big(0^2+1^2+2^2+\cdots+(n-1)^2\big)\\ &=\frac1{n^3}\cdot\frac{(n-1)n(2n-1)}{6}\\ &=\frac{(n-1)(2n-1)}{6n^2}. \end{aligned} \]

Here rule 6 is used with \(n-1\) in place of \(n\), and the \(i=0\) term is \(0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}i^2\\ &=\frac{(n-1)\big((n-1)+1\big)\big(2(n-1)+1\big)}6\\ &=\frac{(n-1)n(2n-1)}{6}. \end{aligned} \]

Check: \(n=4\): \(\frac{3\cdot7}{96}=0.21875=L_4\). ✓ \(n=8\): \(\frac{7\cdot15}{384}=0.2734375=L_8\). ✓

Shortcut: \(L_n\) and \(R_n\) share every rectangle except the first left one (height \(0\)) and the last right one (height \(1\)), so \(L_n=R_n-\frac1n\cdot1^2\). Indeed \(\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2}-\frac1n=\frac{2n^2+3n+1-6n}{6n^2}\) \(=\frac{(n-1)(2n-1)}{6n^2}\).

Let \(n\to\infty\)

Since \(f(x)=x^2\) is increasing on \(I=[0,1]\), \(L_n\le A\le R_n\) for every \(n\). Now take limits (dividing top and bottom by \(n^2\), as in Lecture 3):

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{n\to\infty}R_n&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2}\\ &=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{2n^2+3n+1}{6n^2}\\ &=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{2+\frac3n+\frac1{n^2}}{6}=\frac13, \end{aligned} \] \[ \lim_{n\to\infty}L_n=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{2n^2-3n+1}{6n^2}=\frac13. \]

Then \(\lim_{n\to\infty}L_n\le A\le\lim_{n\to\infty}R_n\) gives \(\frac13\le A\le\frac13\), so \(A\) does not depend on \(n\), so the inequalities survive the limit, and a number squeezed between two limits that are equal must equal them.

\[ A=\frac13. \]

In words: the underestimates and overestimates both close in on \(\frac13\), and the true area is trapped between them, so it must be \(\frac13\). This is the Sandwich Theorem idea from Lecture 2.

Notice \(\frac13=\frac{1^3}{3}-\frac{0^3}{3}\), and \(\frac{x^3}3\) is an antiderivative of \(x^2\). That is no coincidence: it is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Lecture 12).

You can now: derive the formulas for \(R_n\) and \(L_n\) for \(x^2\) on \([0,1]\) with the sum-of-squares formula; check them against the \(n=4\) and \(n=8\) tables; and let \(n\to\infty\) to show that the area is \(\frac13\).

8Area is the limit of a finite sum ~6 min

What is this?

This is the strip picture in full generality. The strips may have different widths, and in each strip you may read the curve's height at any point of its base (the sample point). For a continuous curve, every such way of filling the region gives totals that settle on the same number as the strips get thinner. That number is what we mean by the area. The curve must also stay on or above the axis (\(f\ge0\)), so that each strip's height really is a height.

Now the general version, for any continuous \(f\ge0\) on any interval \([a,b]\).

Partition. Cut \([a,b]\) at points \(a=x_0\lt x_1\lt x_2\lt\cdots\lt x_{n-1}\lt x_n=b\). This gives \(n\) subintervals; the \(i\)th one is \([x_{i-1},x_i]\), with width \(\Delta x_i=x_i-x_{i-1}\).

A partition of \([a,b]\) with one sample point in each subinterval

The ticks cut \([a,b]\) into subintervals of widths \(\Delta x_1,\Delta x_2,\ldots\) (not necessarily equal). The red dots are the sample points \(x_i^*\), one anywhere inside each subinterval. The zigzag means "and so on".

Definition: sample points

Let \(x_i^*\) be any number in the \(i\)th subinterval \([x_{i-1},x_i]\). Then \(f(x_i^*)\) is the height of the \(i\)th approximating rectangle. The numbers \(x_1^*,x_2^*,\ldots,x_n^*\) are called the sample points.

In words: in each strip, pick one point; the curve's height there is the rectangle's height. (The star in \(x_i^*\) just means "the chosen point", not a power or an operation.)

Definition: area

The area of the region \(A\) that lies under the graph of a continuous function \(f\) is the limit of the sum of the areas of approximating rectangles with height \(f(x_i^*)\) and width \(\Delta x\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\big[f(x_1^*)\Delta x+f(x_2^*)\Delta x\\ &\qquad+\cdots+f(x_n^*)\Delta x\big]\\ &=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^nf(x_i^*)\,\Delta x. \end{aligned} \]

In words: area = the number that (height × width), added over all \(n\) strips, approaches as the number of strips grows without bound.

Up to now "area" of a curved region was an intuitive idea. This definition makes it precise: area is this limit. For a continuous function, the limit is the same whichever sample points you choose, which is why the definition can say "any number in the \(i\)th subinterval".

The standard choice

For simplicity, use equal widths \(\Delta x_i=\Delta x\) and the same rule (right, left or midpoint) in every strip. Then on \([a,b]\):

\[ \begin{gathered} \Delta x=\frac{b-a}{n},\\ x_i=a+i\,\Delta x\quad(i=0,1,\ldots,n). \end{gathered} \]

In words: width of each strip = length of the interval ÷ number of strips; the \(i\)th cut point is \(i\) widths to the right of \(a\).

Recipe: exact area as a limit of a sum (used in Group work 4 and 5)
  1. Do: Find \(\Delta x=\frac{b-a}n\) and the sample points, e.g. \(x_i=a+\frac{(b-a)i}{n}\). this fixes how wide each strip is and where its top is read, counting from \(a\), not from \(0\).
  2. Do: Substitute into \(f\) and expand \(f(x_i)\) into powers of \(i\): a constant, a multiple of \(i\), a multiple of \(i^2\), ... the strip heights become a few simple patterns in \(i\), and each pattern has a ready-made total.
  3. Do: Multiply by \(\Delta x\), sum, and split with rules 1 to 4; replace \(\sum1,\sum i,\sum i^2,\sum i^3\) by rules 4 to 7. this adds up all \(n\) strip areas at once, using the formulas of section 6 instead of adding term by term.
  4. Do: Simplify to a formula in \(n\) only (no \(i\) left), then let \(n\to\infty\). the formula is the total area of \(n\) strips; letting the strips become infinitely thin removes the bits that stick out or fall short.
Trap: \(\Delta x\) and the starting point

On \([1,4]\), \(\Delta x=\frac{4-1}{n}=\frac3n\), not \(\frac4n\), and \(x_i=1+\frac{3i}{n}\), not \(\frac{3i}n\). Every sample point starts from \(a\), not from \(0\).

This definition is for \(f\ge0\), so that "height" makes sense. Lecture 12 drops that condition: the same limit is then called the definite integral \(\int_a^bf(x)\,dx\), and strips below the \(x\)-axis count as negative.

You can now: find \(\Delta x=\frac{b-a}{n}\) and the left, right or midpoint sample points on any \([a,b]\); write the area as \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x\); and follow the four-step recipe to find an exact area, as in Group work 4 and 5.

9Summary

  • Indefinite integrals are the most general antiderivatives: \(\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C\).
  • Antiderivatives are not unique; on an interval they differ by a constant.
  • Antidifferentiation and finite summations are linear: they split over sums and differences, and constants come out.
  • Partition an interval into \(n\) subintervals and use rectangles to approximate the area between a curve and the \(x\)-axis.
  • For continuous functions, the limit of the finite sums of rectangle areas does not depend on the choice of sample point \(x_i^*\) in each subinterval.
  • Limits of finite sums are definite integrals (defined in Lecture 12).
  • The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus connects antiderivatives to area (Lecture 12).

✗Omitting the \(+C\): \(\int3x^2\,dx=x^3\). Why wrong: an indefinite integral is the whole family; \(x^3+5\) also has derivative \(3x^2\). ✓ \(\int3x^2\,dx=x^3+C\). ✗Misapplying the power rule: \(\int\frac1x\,dx=\frac{x^0}0+C\). Why wrong: the power rule excludes \(n=-1\) (division by zero). ✓ \(\int\frac1x\,dx=\ln|x|+C\). ✗Logarithm where there is none: \(\int\frac1{x^2}\,dx=\ln(x^2)+C\). Why wrong: differentiate: \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln(x^2+1)=\frac{2x}{x^2+1}\), which has an extra \(2x\) on top. ✓ \(\int\frac{1}{x^2+1}\,dx=\tan^{-1}x+C\) (table). ✗Ignoring the chain rule: \(\int\cos(3x)\,dx=\sin(3x)+C\). Why wrong: \(i=0\) to \(n\) is \(n+1\) terms. For \(n\) strips use \(i=1\) to \(n\) (right endpoints) or \(i=0\) to \(n-1\) (left endpoints). ✓ \(\sum_{i=1}^n\). (For this particular sum the extra \(i=0\) term happens to be \(0\), but with, say, \(\big(\frac in\big)^2+1\) it would add an extra \(1\).) ✗Misidentifying \(\Delta x\): on \([1,4]\) using \(\Delta x=\frac4n\). Why wrong: the width is (length of interval) ÷ \(n\), and the length is \(4-1=3\). ✓ \(\Delta x=\frac3n\). ✗ Antidifferentiating a product factor by factor: \(\int x\cdot x\,dx=\frac{x^2}{2}\cdot\frac{x^2}{2}\). ✓ Expand first: \(\int x^2\,dx=\frac{x^3}{3}+C\). ✗ Assuming \(L_n\) is always the underestimate. (c) Left endpoints \(i=0,\ldots,n-1\); with \(k=n-i\) running over \(1,\ldots,n\):

\[ \begin{aligned} L_n&=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{8(n-i)^3}{n^3}\cdot\frac2n=\frac{16}{n^4}\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^3\\ &=\frac{16}{n^4}\cdot\frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4}=\frac{4(n+1)^2}{n^2}. \end{aligned} \]

(d) Right endpoints \(i=1,\ldots,n\); now \(k=n-i\) runs over \(0,\ldots,n-1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} R_n&=\frac{16}{n^4}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k^3=\frac{16}{n^4}\cdot\frac{(n-1)^2n^2}{4}\\ &=\frac{4(n-1)^2}{n^2}. \end{aligned} \]

Checks: \(n=6\) gives \(\frac{4\cdot49}{36}=\frac{49}9\) and \(\frac{4\cdot25}{36}=\frac{25}9\). ✓

(e), (f) \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}L_n=\lim_{n\to\infty}4\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)^2=4\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}R_n=\lim_{n\to\infty}4\Big(1-\frac1n\Big)^2=4\). Both limits agree, so the area is 4.

5. Compute the exact area of the region bounded by \(f(x)=x^2+2x-1\), \(y=0\), \(x=1\) and \(x=3\) by taking the limit of a finite sum.

Hint

First check \(f\ge0\) on \([1,3]\) (where are the roots of \(x^2+2x-1\)?). Then follow the recipe in section 8 with \(\Delta x=\frac2n\) and \(x_i=1+\frac{2i}n\); expand \(f(x_i)\) as (constant) + (multiple of \(i\)) + (multiple of \(i^2\)).

Answer

The roots of \(x^2+2x-1\) are \(-1\pm\sqrt2\approx0.414,\,-2.414\), both left of 1, and \(f(1)=2\gt0\), so \(f\gt0\) on \([1,3]\): the region lies above the axis.

\(\Delta x=\frac2n\), \(x_i=1+\frac{2i}n\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x_i)&=\Big(1+\frac{2i}n\Big)^2+2\Big(1+\frac{2i}n\Big)-1\\ &=2+\frac{8i}{n}+\frac{4i^2}{n^2}. \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} R_n&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big(2+\frac{8i}n+\frac{4i^2}{n^2}\Big)\frac2n\\ &=\frac4n\cdot n+\frac{16}{n^2}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\\ &\quad+\frac{8}{n^3}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}. \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} R_n&=4+\frac{8(n+1)}{n}\\ &\quad+\frac{4(n+1)(2n+1)}{3n^2}\\ &=\frac{44}{3}+\frac{12}{n}+\frac{4}{3n^2}. \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} A=\lim_{n\to\infty}R_n&=4+8+\frac83\\ &=\frac{44}{3}\approx14.667. \end{aligned} \]

(The left sum \(L_n=\frac{44}3-\frac{12}n+\frac4{3n^2}\) has the same limit.)

13Concept checks

Short questions to test understanding, not computation.

C1. One student says \(\int2\sin x\cos x\,dx=\sin^2x+C\); another says \(-\cos^2x+C\). Who is right?

Answer

Both. Each differentiates to \(2\sin x\cos x\), and the two differ by a constant: \(\sin^2x-(-\cos^2x)\) \(=\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1\). Antiderivatives are not unique, so different-looking answers can both be correct; the constant \(C\) absorbs the difference.

C2. The theorem in section 1 says "on an interval". The domain of \(\frac1x\) is not an interval. Is \(\ln|x|+C\) really every antiderivative of \(\frac1x\) on its whole domain?

Answer

No. The domain \(x\ne0\) is two separate intervals, and the constant may differ on each. For example \(F(x)=\ln|x|+1\) for \(x\gt0\) and \(F(x)=\ln|x|-5\) for \(x\lt0\) also has \(F'(x)=\frac1x\) everywhere it is defined. On each single interval, though, \(\ln|x|+C\) is everything. (In practice the answer \(\ln|x|+C\) is accepted.)

C3. If \(\int f(x)\,dx=xe^x+C\), what is \(f(x)\)?

Answer

Differentiate: \(f(x)=\frac{d}{dx}(xe^x)\) \(=e^x+xe^x=(x+1)e^x\) (product rule). Reading an integral backwards is just differentiation.

C4. For a function that is decreasing on \([a,b]\), which of \(L_n\) and \(R_n\) is an overestimate of the area? What if the function goes up and then down?

Answer

Decreasing: the left endpoint is the highest point of each strip, so \(L_n\) overestimates and \(R_n\) underestimates (Group work 4). If the function rises then falls, neither is guaranteed to be above or below; some rectangles stick out and others fall short. Try the wavy curve in the explorer.

C5. For \(f(x)=2-x\) on \([0,2]\), the midpoint sum \(M_n\) equals the exact area 2 for every \(n\). Why?

Answer

For a straight line, the bit of each midpoint rectangle that sticks out above the line on one side of the midpoint is a small triangle exactly matching the bit missing on the other side, so each rectangle has exactly the area of its strip. This is true for every straight line \(f(x)=mx+c\), not just \(2-x\).

C6. How many terms does \(\sum_{i=3}^{10}a_i\) have? Rewrite \(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(i+1)^2\) as a sum that starts at 1, and evaluate it.

Answer

\(10-3+1=8\) terms. With \(j=i+1\), as \(i\) runs from \(0\) to \(n-1\), \(j\) runs from \(1\) to \(n\): \(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(i+1)^2=\sum_{j=1}^nj^2\) \(=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6\). Shifting the index like this is how Group work 4 was done.

C7. Is \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n i^2=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n i\Big)^2\)?

Answer

No. With \(n=2\): \(1+4=5\) but \((1+2)^2=9\). There is no product (or power) rule for sums; only rules 1 to 4 split a sum. (The tempting identity is true one power up: \(\sum i^3=\big(\sum i\big)^2\), rule 7.)

C8. Beyond Lecture 11 What happens if you compute \(\lim R_n\) for \(f(x)=x^3\) on \([-2,0]\), where the graph lies below the \(x\)-axis?

Answer

Every height \(f(x_i)\) is negative, so every "rectangle area" \(f(x_i)\Delta x\) is negative, and the limit is \(-4\): minus the area found in Group work 4. The limit of the sum measures signed area (above the axis positive, below negative). Lecture 12 calls this limit the definite integral; to get a geometric area of a region below the axis you take the absolute value.
