MATH1851 Exam Guide · The Derivative

The short version

What to expect

Questions built on the definition of the derivative appear in 5 of the 13 recent papers, usually as one part of a 10% question. By far the most common form is a piecewise function (a function given by different formulas on each side of a point, called the join) with "is it differentiable?" or "find the constants so that it is differentiable" (L5.2). The functions change every time, but the method does not: continuity first, then the two one-sided difference quotients, then compare. The two rarer types are a functional equation solved from the definition (L5.1) and a multiple-choice "which derivative does this limit represent?" (L5.3).

How to practise: use Start here to name the question, then work it with the type's procedure open. Check with the Hint if stuck and with the Solution at the end. Newest papers come first; 2013–2018 papers are tagged older format. For the ideas behind each step, use the Chapter note links. All answers checked with sympy.

Exam format

  • Where: one part of a Part I question: Q2(c) (Dec 2019, Dec 2021), Q4(c) (Dec 2020), Q1(b) (May 2025), multiple-choice Q2 (Dec 2025). Older papers ask it as a whole Q1 or Q2 (10–11%).
  • Speed: Dec 2025 moved part of Part I to multiple choice (2 points each, only the letter scores), and May 2026 made Part I Q1–3 answers only. On written papers the stems say "Determine with explanation", "Give precise reasons" or "Show your calculation": the one-sided limits carry the marks, not the final yes/no.
  • Formula sheet: recent papers print trigonometric identities, and about half also print the derivatives of \(\sin^{-1}x\) and \(\cos^{-1}x\) (most of those also \(\tan^{-1}x\)); nothing else. The definition of the derivative, "differentiable implies continuous", the standard limits \(\frac{\sin h}{h}\to1\), \(\frac{\cos h-1}{h}\to0\), \(\frac{e^h-1}{h}\to1\), and the differentiation rules are never printed.
  • Wording: "differentiable on \((-\infty,\infty)\)" or "for all values of \(x\)" means at every point, so the pieces away from the join must be checked too. "Evaluate \(f'(0)\) when exists" allows the answer "it does not exist".

Start here: name the question

Look at what the question gives you and what it asks for. The first move is always to write down the definition: \(f'(a)=\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\).

L5.1Derivative straight from the definition (functional equations)

Chapter note · definition of the derivative

Frequency: 1 of 13 recent papers (May 2025); 0 of 10 older papers.

Recognise it

There is no formula for \(f\). You get a rule linking \(f(x+y)\) to \(f(x)\) and \(f(y)\) (a functional equation), one derivative value such as \(f'(0)\), and the question asks for \(f'(x)\) at every \(x\).

Standard procedure
  1. Find \(f(0)\). Put \(x=y=0\) in the equation and solve.
  2. Write the difference quotient at a general \(x\). Put \(y=h\): the equation turns \(f(x+h)-f(x)\) into \(f(h)\) plus terms with no \(f\).
  3. Divide by \(h\) and spot the definition at 0. Because \(f(0)\) is known, \(\dfrac{f(h)}{h}=\dfrac{f(0+h)-f(0)}{h}\to f'(0)\).
  4. Finish with the limit laws. The limit exists for every \(x\), so \(f\) is differentiable everywhere; write \(f'(x)\). Asked: May 2025.
  5. Check (scratch work): find a simple \(f\) that satisfies the equation and has the given \(f'(0)\); its derivative must match.
Traps
  • Assuming a formula for \(f\). In May 2025 Q1(b), "guess \(f(x)=x+\frac{x^3}{3}\) and differentiate" proves nothing: the question says "show that \(f\) is differentiable", so the marks are for the limit. Use the guess only as the step 5 check.
  • Forgetting step 1. Writing \(\frac{f(h)}{h}\to f'(0)\) needs \(f(0)=0\); say where it comes from.

Past-paper questions

May 2025 Q1(b)Q1: 10%; part marks not shown

Suppose \(f\) is a function that satisfies

\[f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)+x^2y+xy^2\]

for any real numbers \(x\) and \(y\). Assume \(f'(0)=1\). Show that \(f\) is differentiable at every real number \(x\), and evaluate \(f'(x)\). [Hint: Calculate \(f(0)\) and recall the definition of differentiability.]

Part (a) of this question is in the L2 guide.

Hint

Type L5.1, functional equation. First step: put \(x=y=0\) to get \(f(0)\); then put \(y=h\) and write \(\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\) using the equation.

Solution

Step 1 \(f(0)\). With \(x=y=0\): \(f(0)=2f(0)\), so \(f(0)=0\).

Step 2 Quotient. With \(y=h\ne0\):

\[\begin{aligned}&f(x+h)-f(x)\\&\quad=f(h)+x^2h+xh^2,\\ &\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\\&\quad=\frac{f(h)}{h}+x^2+xh.\end{aligned}\]

Step 3 Definition at 0. Since \(f(0)=0\),

\[\begin{aligned}\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h)}{h}&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(0+h)-f(0)}{h}\\&=f'(0)=1.\end{aligned}\]

Step 4 Sum Law (each piece has a limit): for every real \(x\),

\[\begin{aligned}\lim_{h\to0}&\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\\&=1+x^2+0,\end{aligned}\]

so the limit exists, \(f\) is differentiable at every \(x\), and

\[\boxed{f'(x)=1+x^2}\]

Marker: the marks are for writing the limit of the difference quotient and naming \(\frac{f(h)}{h}\to f'(0)\) as the definition; a guessed formula for \(f\) scores at most the final answer.

Step 5 Check. \(f(x)=x+\frac{x^3}{3}\) satisfies the equation (sympy) and \(f'(0)=1\); its derivative is \(1+x^2\). ✓

Pointer: May 2018 Q2(iv)(v) (finding \(U'(4)\)) is in the L4 guide.

L5.2Is a piecewise function differentiable at the join?

Chapter note · one-sided derivatives · Chapter note · differentiable implies continuous

Frequency: 3 of 13 recent papers (Dec 2019, Dec 2020, Dec 2021); 2 of 10 older papers. The main L5 type.

Recognise it

\(f\) is given by one formula for \(x\lt a\) and another for \(x\gt a\) (the point \(a\), where the formulas meet, is the join). The question asks "is \(f\) differentiable at \(a\)?", "differentiable for all \(x\)?", or "find the constants that make it differentiable".

Standard procedure

The left difference quotient at \(a\) is \(\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\) with \(h\lt0\); its limit as \(h\to0^-\) is the left derivative. The right one uses \(h\gt0\).

  1. Away from the join. Each formula is differentiable on its own open interval wherever it is defined (sums, products, quotients and compositions of differentiable functions). If the question says "for all \(x\)" and a formula is undefined somewhere on its interval, \(f\) fails there: stop. Asked: Dec 2020.
  2. Continuity at the join (differentiable implies continuous). Find \(f(a)\) from the piece that contains \(a\) (the one with \(\le\) or \(\ge\)), the left limit and the right limit. Set all three equal.
    • 2a. No constants make them equal: \(f\) is not continuous, so not differentiable. Stop. Asked: Dec 2020.
    • 2b. Otherwise keep the condition (e.g. \(a=0\), or "\(a=0\) or \(b=0\)") and go on. Oscillating factors like \(x\sin\frac bx\) need the Sandwich Theorem here. Asked: Dec 2021, Dec 2019, Dec 2017, Dec 2015.
  3. One-sided difference quotients from the definition, using the left formula for \(h\lt0\), the right formula for \(h\gt0\), and the value \(f(a)\) from step 2.
    • 3a. Ordinary pieces (polynomials, \(e^x\), \(\sin x\), \(\cos x\)): cancel \(h\), or finish with \(\frac{\sin h}{h}\to1\), \(\frac{\cos h-1}{h}\to0\), \(\frac{e^h-1}{h}\to1\). Asked: Dec 2017, Dec 2015.
    • 3b. Pieces with \(\sin\frac bx\) or \(\frac ax\) in them: split into cases using the condition from step 2. \(h\sin(\dots)\to0\) by the Sandwich Theorem; \(\frac{\sin(h^2)}{h^2}\to1\); a quotient like \(\sin\frac4h\) swings between \(-1\) and \(1\) and has no limit. Asked: Dec 2021, Dec 2019.
  4. Compare. Both one-sided limits exist and are equal: \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) and \(f'(a)\) is that common value. Otherwise (different values, or one has no limit) it is not. Answer exactly what was asked: the constants, or "yes/no" with the reasons from steps 2–3.
Worked example · the procedure in action

Dec 2021 Q2(c), Q2: 10%. Let \(a,b\) be two constants. Consider the function

\[f(x)=\begin{cases}\left(x^2+\dfrac ax\right)\sin\left(x^2+\dfrac bx\right) & x\gt0,\\[2mm] -xe^{2x} & x\le0.\end{cases}\]

(c) Determine with explanation all the possible values of \(a,b\) so that \(f\) is differentiable on \((-\infty,\infty)\).

Step 1 For \(x\gt0\), \(x^2+\frac ax\) and \(\sin\left(x^2+\frac bx\right)\) are differentiable (no division by 0), so their product is. For \(x\lt0\), \(-xe^{2x}\) is differentiable. Only \(x=0\) is left.

"On \((-\infty,\infty)\)" means every point; one sentence for the other points earns its mark.

Step 2b \(f(0)=-0\cdot e^0=0\) and the left limit is \(0\). Right limit: if \(a=0\), \(\left|x^2\sin(\dots)\right|\le x^2\to0\) (Sandwich). If \(b=0\),

\[\begin{aligned}&\left(x^2+\tfrac ax\right)\sin x^2\\&\quad=x^2\sin x^2+ax\cdot\frac{\sin x^2}{x^2}\to0.\end{aligned}\]

If \(a\ne0\) and \(b\ne0\), the factor \(\frac ax\) is unbounded while \(\sin\left(x^2+\frac bx\right)\) keeps returning to \(\pm1\): no limit. So \(f\) is continuous at 0 only when \(a=0\) or \(b=0\).

This is part (b) of the question (in the L4 guide). Quote it; differentiable needs continuous, so only these two cases survive.

Step 3a Left quotient (\(h\lt0\)):

\[\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=\frac{-he^{2h}}{h}=-e^{2h}\to-1.\]

Step 3b Right quotient (\(h\gt0\)): \(\dfrac{f(h)}{h}=\left(h+\dfrac a{h^2}\right)\sin\left(h^2+\dfrac bh\right)\).

Case \(a=0\): \(\left|h\sin\left(h^2+\frac bh\right)\right|\le h\to0\), so the right limit is \(0\).

Case \(b=0\):

\[\begin{aligned}&\left(h+\tfrac a{h^2}\right)\sin h^2\\&\quad=h\sin h^2+a\cdot\frac{\sin h^2}{h^2}\to a.\end{aligned}\]

Split by the step 2 cases; each one becomes a standard limit. Write "Sandwich Theorem" and \(\frac{\sin u}{u}\to1\) by name.

Step 4 Case \(a=0\): right \(0\ne-1\) = left, not differentiable. Case \(b=0\): need \(a=-1\). Then both sides give \(-1\).

\[\boxed{a=-1,\ b=0}\]

Then \(f'(0)=-1\). The answer is one pair; "a = −1" alone loses the mark for \(b\).

Traps
  • Skipping continuity. In Dec 2015 Q1(b) the constant \(a\) appears only in the continuity condition, and in Dec 2021 Q2(c) continuity removes the case \(a\ne0,b\ne0\) before any derivative is taken. Matching slopes alone cannot find these.
  • The wrong \(f(a)\). Take \(f(a)\) from the piece with \(\le\) or \(\ge\). In Dec 2019 Q2(c), \(f(0)=\sqrt b\) (right formula), not \(2\).
  • Calling an oscillating quotient 0. In Dec 2019 Q2(c) the left quotient is \(\sin\frac4h\), which has no limit; in Dec 2021 it is \(h\sin(\dots)\), which does tend to 0. The factor \(h\) in front is the difference.
Shortcut · the derivatives of the pieces Beyond the handouts

If \(f\) is continuous at \(a\), the left formula is differentiable for \(x\) just below \(a\), and its derivative tends to \(L\) as \(x\to a^-\), then the left derivative of \(f\) at \(a\) is \(L\). The same holds on the right.

Why: the Mean Value Theorem (L8) on \([a+h,a]\) turns the quotient into a derivative value at a point between. It gives Dec 2017 (\(-\sin0=0\), \(\cos0=1\)) and Dec 2015 (\(-1\), \(-4\)) in one line. It says nothing when the derivative of a piece has no limit (pieces with \(\sin\frac bx\)), and it fails without continuity. On written papers show the difference quotients; use this to check.

Dec 2020 Q4(c)Q4: 10%; part marks not shown

For what values of \(a\) and \(b\) will

\[g(x)=\begin{cases}ax^2\tan\dfrac1x, & \text{for } x\gt0\\[2mm] ae^x-bx-3, & \text{for } x\le0\end{cases}\]

be differentiable for all values of \(x\)?

Reading: the paper image shows \(\tan\frac1x\) (plain tangent, no \(-1\)), and that is solved here. The question was probably meant with \(\tan^{-1}\frac1x\); that version is answered at the end of the solution. Part (a) is in the L7 guide; part (b) is in the L10 guide.

Hint

Type L5.2, but start at step 1: where is \(\tan\frac1x\) undefined for \(x\gt0\)? Then try the one case that step 1 leaves, \(a=0\), at step 2.

Solution

Step 1 Away from 0. \(\tan\frac1x\) is undefined when \(\frac1x=\frac\pi2+k\pi\), i.e. at \(x_k=\dfrac{1}{\frac\pi2+k\pi}\gt0\) (\(k=0,1,2,\dots\), e.g. \(x=\frac2\pi\)). If \(a\ne0\), \(g\) is not defined at these points, so it cannot be differentiable there.

Step 2a · stop The case \(a=0\). Then \(g(x)=0\) for \(x\gt0\) (where defined), so the right limit at 0 is \(0\), while \(g(0)=0-0-3=-3\). \(g\) is not continuous at 0, so not differentiable there.

\[\boxed{\text{No values of }a,b\text{ (as printed)}}\]

Marker: with the printed \(\tan\), the reasons are what score: name the points where \(\tan\frac1x\) is undefined, and the failed continuity when \(a=0\).

If the function is \(ax^2\tan^{-1}\frac1x\) (likely intended):

Step 2b \(\left|\tan^{-1}\frac1x\right|\lt\frac\pi2\), so \(ax^2\tan^{-1}\frac1x\to0\) (Sandwich); \(g(0)=a-3\). Continuity: \(a=3\).

Step 3a–3b Right: \(\dfrac{3h^2\tan^{-1}\frac1h}{h}=3h\tan^{-1}\frac1h\to0\). Left: \(\dfrac{3e^h-bh-3}{h}=3\cdot\dfrac{e^h-1}{h}-b\to3-b\).

Step 4 \(3-b=0\): then \(a=3\), \(b=3\), and \(g'(0)=0\).

Dec 2019 Q2(c)Q2: 10%; part marks not shown

Let

\[f(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{(x+b)^{3/2}-x^{3/2}}{b} & \text{for } x\ge0,\\[2mm] 2+x\sin\dfrac{b}{x} & \text{for } x\lt0,\end{cases}\]

where \(b\gt0\) is a constant. (c) Determine the value of \(b\) such that \(f\) is differentiable at \(x=0\), and evaluate \(f'(0)\) when exists. [Show your calculation.]

Part (a) is in the L6 guide and part (b) in the L2 guide. The left piece \(2+x\sin\frac bx\) was checked against the paper image.

Hint

Type L5.2, piecewise at \(x=0\). First step: \(f(0)\) comes from the top formula; find it and the left limit (Sandwich) to get \(b\). Then write the left quotient with that \(b\).

Solution

Step 2b Continuity. \(f(0)=\dfrac{b^{3/2}}{b}=\sqrt b\). Left: \(\left|x\sin\frac bx\right|\le|x|\to0\) (Sandwich), so \(f(x)\to2\). Right: the top formula is continuous at 0, so \(f(x)\to\sqrt b\). Continuity needs \(\sqrt b=2\), so \(b=4\); for any other \(b\), \(f\) is not differentiable at 0.

Step 3b Left quotient with \(b=4\), \(h\lt0\):

\[\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=\frac{2+h\sin\frac4h-2}{h}=\sin\frac4h.\]

At \(h_n=-\frac{4}{2n\pi}\) it equals \(0\); at \(h_n=-\frac{4}{2n\pi+\pi/2}\) it equals \(-1\). Both lists tend to \(0^-\), so the left limit does not exist.

Step 4 So \(f\) is not differentiable at 0 even for \(b=4\) (although the right derivative exists there and equals \(\frac34\)).

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{No value of }b;\\&f'(0)\text{ does not exist}\end{aligned}}\]

Marker: show the continuity condition \(b=4\) first, then the quotient \(\sin\frac4h\) and why it has no limit; "evaluate \(f'(0)\) when exists" allows this answer.

The wording "determine the value of \(b\)" suggests a value was expected; with the function as printed (checked on the paper image) none exists.

Dec 2017 Q2Q2: 10%older format

Define

\[g(x)=\begin{cases}\cos x, & x\lt0\\ 1, & x=0\\ 1+\sin x, & x\gt0.\end{cases}\]

Is \(g\) continuous at \(x=0\)? Is \(g\) differentiable at \(x=0\)? Give precise reasons to justify your conclusions.

Step 4 \(0\ne1\): the two one-sided derivatives differ.

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{Continuous at }0;\\&\text{not differentiable at }0\end{aligned}}\]

For what value of \(a\) will the following function

\[g(x)=\begin{cases}x^2-x-a, & \text{if } -1\lt x\le0\\ x^3-4x, & \text{if } 0\lt x\lt1\end{cases}\]

L5.3Spot which derivative a limit represents

Chapter note · definition of the derivative

Frequency: 1 of 13 recent papers (Dec 2025, multiple choice); 0 of 10 older papers.

Recognise it

A limit \(\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(\text{expression in }h)-(\text{number})}{h}\), and the question asks which function \(f\) and which point \(a\) it comes from.

Standard procedure
  1. Write the template \(\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\) next to the given limit.
  2. Read off \(f\) and \(a\) from the part with \(h\): it must be exactly \(f(a+h)\), e.g. \(\cos\left(\frac\pi3+h\right)\) gives \(f=\cos\), \(a=\frac\pi3\).
  3. Check the number: it must equal \(f(a)\). This rules out choices where \(a\) is "shifted into" \(f\). Asked: Dec 2025.
Traps
  • Two places for the same constant. In Dec 2025 MC2, \(f(x)=\cos\left(x+\frac\pi3\right)\) also fits, but with \(a=0\), not \(a=\frac\pi3\). Check \(f(a+h)\) by substituting, not by eye.
  • Taking the number as \(a\). \(\frac12\) is the value \(f(a)\), not the point \(a\).

Past-paper questions

Dec 2025 Q2Part I multiple choice: 2 pts

The following limit represents the derivative of some function \(f\) at some point \(a\):

\[\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos\left(\frac\pi3+h\right)-\frac12}{h}\]

What is the function \(f(x)\) and the point \(a\)?
(A) \(f(x)=\cos(x)\), \(a=\frac\pi3\)
(B) \(f(x)=\cos(x)\), \(a=\frac12\)
(C) \(f(x)=\cos\left(x+\frac\pi3\right)\), \(a=\frac\pi3\)
(D) \(f(x)=\cos\left(\frac\pi3\right)\), \(a=h\)
(E) \(f(x)=\sin(x)\), \(a=\frac\pi3\)

Hint

Type L5.3. First step: for each choice, write \(f(a+h)\) and \(f(a)\) and see which pair gives exactly \(\cos\left(\frac\pi3+h\right)\) and \(\frac12\).

Solution

Step 1–2 The part with \(h\) is \(\cos\left(\frac\pi3+h\right)=f(a+h)\) with \(f=\cos\), \(a=\frac\pi3\).

Step 3 \(f(a)=\cos\frac\pi3=\frac12\) ✓. So the limit is \(f'\left(\frac\pi3\right)\) for \(f=\cos\).

The others: (B) gives \(\cos\left(\frac12+h\right)\); (C) gives \(f\left(\frac\pi3+h\right)=\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}3+h\right)\), wrong (it would need \(a=0\)); (D) is a constant, and \(a\) cannot be \(h\); (E) gives \(\sin\left(\frac\pi3+h\right)\).

\[\boxed{\text{(A)}}\]

Marker: only the letter scores.

Equivalent forms. The limit's value is \(-\sin\frac\pi3=-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\) (sympy), not asked here.
