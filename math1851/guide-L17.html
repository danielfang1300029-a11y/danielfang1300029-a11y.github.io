MATH1851 Exam Guide · Arc Length and Surface Area

The short version

What to expect

Arc length or surface area appears in 7 of the 13 recent papers, nearly always as one part of the "region" question (Q4 or Q5, or Dec 2025 Q13) that also asks for a volume. The curve changes every time, but the work does not: pick the formula from how the curve is given, simplify the square root, then integrate. The middle step is the whole question. In almost every past paper the expression under the root turns into a perfect square, a half-angle identity, or the one standard integral \(\int\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du\). The procedures below tell you which to try first.

How to practise: use Start here to choose the formula, then work the question with the type's procedure open; check with the Hint if stuck and with the Solution at the end. Learn the Ready answer box by heart: no formula sheet gives these formulas. Newest papers come first; 2013–2018 papers are tagged older format. For where the formulas come from, use the Chapter note links. All answers checked with sympy.

Exam format

  • Where: one part of a Part I long question (Dec 2024 Q5(a) is 5%; most papers do not print part marks) about a curve or region, next to a volume part (in the L16 guide). Recent: May 2026 Q5(e), Dec 2025 Q13(b), May 2025 Q4(a), Dec 2024 Q5(a), May 2024 Q5(b), Dec 2021 Q5(b), Dec 2020 Q5(a).
  • Speed: Dec 2025 moved part of Part I to multiple choice and May 2026 made Part I Q1–3 answers only, but arc length and surface area have so far stayed in the written long questions (Dec 2025 Q13, May 2026 Q5). Show the formula, the simplified root and the integral: those lines carry the marks.
  • Formula sheet: recent formula sheets print trig identities and a few standard integrals and derivatives (e.g. \(\int\sec x\,dx\)), but no arc-length or surface-area formula and no \(\int\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du\). Two questions printed formulas inside the question itself: May 2024 Q5(b) listed \(\int\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx\) and \(\int2\pi y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx\); May 2025 Q4(a) gave only the Cartesian arc-length formula plus \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\), leaving you to build the polar one. Do not count on such hints.
  • Wording: "perimeter of the region" means add the lengths of every boundary piece; "surface area, i.e. the total area of the outer and inner faces" means add one surface per boundary curve. Older papers (May 2017, May 2015) let you leave the answer in terms of a given integral such as \(I(x)=\int_0^x\sqrt{u^2+1}\,du\), or \(a\) and \(b\): then do not evaluate it, just reach that form.

L17.1Arc length and perimeter (Cartesian, parametric, polar)

Chapter note · arc length · Chapter note · parametric and polar length · Chapter note · trig substitution

Frequency: 4 of 13 recent papers (May 2026, Dec 2025, May 2025, Dec 2021); 5 of 10 older papers. 9 parts in all.

Recognise it

The question asks for a length: "length of the curve", "arc length", "perimeter of the region". The answer is a distance along a curve, so it is always positive.

Standard procedure
  1. Pick the formula from how the curve is given (all three are in the Ready answer box).
    • \(y=f(x)\): \(\displaystyle\int_a^b\sqrt{1+\left(\tfrac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}\,dx\).
    • \(x=x(t)\), \(y=y(t)\): \(\displaystyle\int\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{dx}{dt}\right)^2+\left(\tfrac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt\).
    • \(r=f(\theta)\): \(\displaystyle\int\sqrt{r^2+\left(\tfrac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2}\,d\theta\). If asked to derive it, use \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\) as a parametric curve.
    • An implicit curve such as \(x^{2/3}+y^{2/3}=1\): parametrise it first (\(x=\cos^3t\), \(y=\sin^3t\)).
  2. Fix the limits and the pieces. For a perimeter, find where the boundary curves meet, list every piece (a straight piece is just the distance formula) and add them at the end. If the curve is symmetric, find one piece and multiply. For polar curves, translate "first quadrant" into a \(\theta\)-range.
  3. Build the expression under the root and simplify it. Expand it fully, then:
    • 3a. A square comes out: it is a perfect square, \((\dots)^2\), or has a square factor like \(9t^2\). Take the root, using \(\sqrt{A^2}=|A|\), and check the sign of \(A\) on your interval. Asked: May 2026, May 2025, Dec 2015, May 2013.
    • 3b. It is \(2\pm2\cos t\) or \(2\pm2\sin\theta\): use a half-angle identity, \(1+\cos t=2\cos^2\frac t2\) or \(1+\sin\theta=\left(\sin\frac\theta2+\cos\frac\theta2\right)^2\). Asked: Dec 2021, Dec 2017.
    • 3c. It stays \(\sqrt{1+k^2x^2}\): substitute \(u=kx\) to reach \(\int\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du\), then use the Ready answer (or stop at the \(I(x)\), \(a\), \(b\) the question gives you). Asked: Dec 2025, May 2016, May 2015.
  4. Integrate, add the pieces, give the exact value. A decimal is a quick check: the length must be more than the straight-line distance between the ends.
Worked example · the procedure in action

Dec 2025 Q13(b), Part I free response (Q13: 10 pts). Let \(\mathcal R\) be the region bounded by \(y_1=1-x^2\) and \(y_2=x^2-1\). Find the perimeter of the region \(\mathcal R\).

Step 1 Both boundary curves are \(y=f(x)\), so use \(\int\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx\).

"Perimeter" is a length, so this is L17.1, not area.

Step 2 The curves meet where \(1-x^2=x^2-1\), i.e. \(x=\pm1\), at \((\pm1,0)\). The boundary is two arcs: \(y_1\) on top, \(y_2\) below, no straight pieces. Since \(y_2=-y_1\), the arcs are mirror images and have equal length; each is also symmetric about the \(y\)-axis. So

\[P=2\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{1+y_1'^2}\,dx=4\int_0^1\sqrt{1+y_1'^2}\,dx.\]

Say why the factor 4 is allowed (two mirror arcs, each even in \(x\)). Forgetting the lower arc gives half the answer.

Step 3c \(y_1'=-2x\), so \(1+y_1'^2=1+4x^2\): no square comes out. Put \(u=2x\), \(dx=\frac12du\), \(x:0\to1\) becomes \(u:0\to2\):

\[\int_0^1\sqrt{1+4x^2}\,dx=\frac12\int_0^2\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du.\]

This is the step-3c shape. The substitution turns it into the one standard integral of this chapter.

Step 4 By the Ready answer, \(\displaystyle\int_0^2\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du=\tfrac12\left[u\sqrt{1+u^2}+\ln\!\big(u+\sqrt{1+u^2}\big)\right]_0^2\):

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^2\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du&=\tfrac12\left[2\sqrt5+\ln(2+\sqrt5)\right]\\&=\sqrt5+\tfrac12\ln(2+\sqrt5).\end{aligned}\]

So

\[\begin{aligned}P&=4\cdot\tfrac12\left(\sqrt5+\tfrac12\ln(2+\sqrt5)\right)\\&=2\sqrt5+\ln(2+\sqrt5).\end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{P=2\sqrt5+\ln\!\big(2+\sqrt5\big)\approx5.916}\]

No formula sheet gives \(\int\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du\). On a written paper, either quote it and differentiate to check, or derive it with \(u=\tan\theta\) (it becomes \(\int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta\), done by parts). Sanity check: the four straight chords from \((\pm1,0)\) to \((0,\pm1)\) total \(4\sqrt2\approx5.66\), a little less than \(P\). ✓

Traps
  • Dropping the absolute value. For the astroid (May 2026 Q5(e)) the root is \(3|\sin t\cos t|\). Integrating \(3\sin t\cos t\) over \([0,2\pi]\) gives \(0\). Work on one quarter, where the sign is known, and multiply by 4.
  • Missing a piece of a perimeter. Dec 2025 Q13(b) and May 2015 Q4(b) have two curved arcs; May 2016 Q4(b) has one arc plus a straight segment of length \(\sqrt2\).
  • Wrong polar formula. \(\int\sqrt{1+(dr/d\theta)^2}\,d\theta\) is wrong; the polar formula has \(r^2\), not 1 (May 2025 Q4(a), Dec 2021 Q5(b)). A negative \(r\) (as in May 2025 for \(\theta\lt1\)) does not matter, because only \(r^2\) appears.
Ready answer · the formulas no sheet gives you

Arc length (length of curve = sum of tiny straight pieces \(ds\)):

\[\begin{aligned}y=f(x):&\ L=\int_a^b\sqrt{1+\left(\tfrac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}dx\\x(t),y(t):&\ L=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\sqrt{\dot x^2+\dot y^2}\,dt\\r=f(\theta):&\ L=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\sqrt{r^2+r'^2}\,d\theta\end{aligned}\]

Here \(\dot x=\frac{dx}{dt}\), \(\dot y=\frac{dy}{dt}\), \(r'=\frac{dr}{d\theta}\). In words: length = integral of the square root of (horizontal rate)² + (vertical rate)².

Surface area (revolve about the \(x\)-axis; radius \(=y\)):

\[\begin{aligned}S&=\int2\pi y\,ds,\\ds&=\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx\\&\phantom{=}\text{ or }\sqrt{\dot x^2+\dot y^2}\,dt.\end{aligned}\]

In words: surface area = integral of (circumference of the circle swept by the point) × (tiny length of curve).

The standard integral (needed in Dec 2025, May 2017, May 2016, May 2015):

\[\begin{aligned}&\int\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du=\tfrac12u\sqrt{1+u^2}\\&\quad+\tfrac12\ln\!\big(u+\sqrt{1+u^2}\big)+C\end{aligned}\]

Check it by differentiating, or derive it with \(u=\tan\theta\) (L14 guide). Useful value: \(\int_0^2\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du=\sqrt5+\frac12\ln(2+\sqrt5)\approx2.958\).

Beyond the handouts Hyperbolic functions (May 2013 Q5, Dec 2020 Q5(a)): \(\cosh x=\frac{e^x+e^{-x}}2\), \(\sinh x=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}2\). Then \((\cosh x)'=\sinh x\), \((\sinh x)'=\cosh x\), \(\cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=1\), \(\cosh^2x=\frac{1+\cosh2x}2\). For \(y=\cosh x\) the root is just \(\sqrt{1+\sinh^2x}=\cosh x\).

Past-paper questions

May 2026 Q5(e)Q5: 10 pts (long question); part marks not shown

Consider the graph of the astroid \(x^{\frac23}+y^{\frac23}=1\), as shown in the figure below:

Figure recreated: cusps at \((\pm1,0)\) and \((0,\pm1)\); \(R\) is the shaded inside; \(A\left(\frac1{\sqrt8},\frac1{\sqrt8}\right)\) and \(B\left(\frac3{10},y^*\right)\) lie on the first-quadrant arc.

(e) What is the arc-length of the astroid?

Parts (a)–(c) (implicit differentiation, the tangent at \(A\), the point \(B\)) are in the L6 guide; part (d) (volume of revolution of \(R\)) is in the L16 guide.

Hint

Type L17.1, implicit curve. First step: parametrise with \(x=\cos^3t\), \(y=\sin^3t\), and work on the first-quadrant quarter only (\(0\le t\le\frac\pi2\)).

Solution

Step 1 Parametrise. \(x=\cos^3t\), \(y=\sin^3t\) gives \(x^{2/3}+y^{2/3}=\cos^2t+\sin^2t=1\). Use the parametric formula.

Step 2 Symmetry. The astroid is symmetric about both axes, so \(L=4\times\) the arc from \((1,0)\) to \((0,1)\), i.e. \(t\) from \(0\) to \(\frac\pi2\).

Step 3a \(\dot x=-3\cos^2t\sin t\), \(\dot y=3\sin^2t\cos t\):

\[\begin{aligned}\dot x^2+\dot y^2&=9\sin^2t\cos^2t\,(\cos^2t+\sin^2t)\\&=(3\sin t\cos t)^2.\end{aligned}\]

On \([0,\frac\pi2]\), \(\sin t\cos t\ge0\), so the root is \(3\sin t\cos t\).

Step 4

\[\begin{aligned}L&=4\int_0^{\pi/2}3\sin t\cos t\,dt\\&=4\left[\tfrac32\sin^2t\right]_0^{\pi/2}=6.\end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{L=6}\]

Marker: say why you use one quarter; over \([0,2\pi]\) the root is \(3|\sin t\cos t|\), and leaving out \(|\cdot|\) gives \(0\).

Check (Cartesian): \(y'=-(y/x)^{1/3}\), so \(1+y'^2=x^{-2/3}\) and \(4\int_0^1x^{-1/3}dx=4\cdot\frac32=6\). ✓

May 2025 Q4(a)Q4: 12%; part marks not shown

Find the length of the curve \(r=\theta^2-1\) from \(\theta=0\) to \(\theta=\frac\pi2\). [Hint: The arc length formula for smooth \(y=f(x)\) over \([a,b]\) in Cartesian coordinates is \(\displaystyle\int_a^b\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}dx\), and note the relations: \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\).]

Parts (b) (a Riemann-sum limit) and (c) (limit of an integral) are in the L12 guide.

Hint

Type L17.1, polar curve. First step: treat \(\theta\) as the parameter in \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\) to get \(ds=\sqrt{r^2+r'^2}\,d\theta\); then expand \(r^2+r'^2\) and look for a perfect square.

Solution

Step 1 Polar formula from the hint. With \(\theta\) as parameter,

\[\begin{aligned}\tfrac{dx}{d\theta}&=r'\cos\theta-r\sin\theta\\\tfrac{dy}{d\theta}&=r'\sin\theta+r\cos\theta\end{aligned}\]

Squaring and adding (the cross terms cancel): \(\left(\frac{dx}{d\theta}\right)^2+\left(\frac{dy}{d\theta}\right)^2=r^2+r'^2\).

Step 2 \(\theta\) runs from \(0\) to \(\frac\pi2\); \(r'=2\theta\).

Step 3a

\[\begin{aligned}r^2+r'^2&=(\theta^2-1)^2+4\theta^2\\&=\theta^4+2\theta^2+1=(\theta^2+1)^2.\end{aligned}\]

So the root is \(\theta^2+1\) (always positive).

Step 4

\[L=\int_0^{\pi/2}(\theta^2+1)\,d\theta=\frac{\pi^3}{24}+\frac\pi2.\]
\[\boxed{L=\dfrac{\pi^3}{24}+\dfrac{\pi}{2}\approx2.863}\]

Marker: the hint only gives the Cartesian formula, so show the line that turns \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\) into \(\sqrt{r^2+r'^2}\).

Equivalent forms. \(\dfrac{\pi(\pi^2+12)}{24}\).

Dec 2021 Q5(b)Q5: 10%; part marks not shown

Consider the curve \(C\) whose polar coordinate is \(r=p\cos(q\theta)+\sin\theta\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\), where \(0\le p,q\le2\) are constants. (b) Consider \(C\) for \(p=1,q=0\), i.e. \(r=1+\sin\theta\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\). Evaluate the length of the curve \(C\) in the first quadrant. [Show your calculation.]

Part (a) (matching \((p,q)\) to two sketched graphs) is in the L15 guide.

Hint

Type L17.1, polar curve. First step: since \(r\ge0\), "first quadrant" means \(0\le\theta\le\frac\pi2\); simplify \(r^2+r'^2\) to \(2+2\sin\theta\) and use a half-angle identity.

Solution

Step 1–2 Polar formula. \(r=1+\sin\theta\ge0\), so the point lies in the first quadrant exactly when \(0\le\theta\le\frac\pi2\).

Step 3b \(r'=\cos\theta\):

\[\begin{aligned}r^2+r'^2&=(1+\sin\theta)^2+\cos^2\theta\\&=2(1+\sin\theta).\end{aligned}\]

Since \(\sin^2\frac\theta2+\cos^2\frac\theta2=1\) and \(2\sin\frac\theta2\cos\frac\theta2=\sin\theta\),

\[1+\sin\theta=\left(\sin\tfrac\theta2+\cos\tfrac\theta2\right)^2,\]

and \(\sin\frac\theta2+\cos\frac\theta2\gt0\) here, so the root is \(\sqrt2\left(\sin\frac\theta2+\cos\frac\theta2\right)\).

Step 4

\[\begin{aligned}L&=\sqrt2\left[-2\cos\tfrac\theta2+2\sin\tfrac\theta2\right]_0^{\pi/2}\\&=\sqrt2\,\big[(-\sqrt2+\sqrt2)-(-2)\big]=2\sqrt2.\end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{L=2\sqrt2\approx2.828}\]

Marker: justify the \(\theta\)-range for "first quadrant"; it is where most answers go wrong.

Equivalent forms. \(\sqrt8\). The identity can also be written \(1+\sin\theta=2\cos^2\!\left(\frac\pi4-\frac\theta2\right)\).

Dec 2017 Q4(b)Q4: 10%; part marks not shownolder format

Consider the function defined by the parametric equations: \(x=\cos t\), \(y=t+\sin t\), for \(0\le t\le\pi\). (b) Find the length of the curve between the two points \((1,0)\) and \((-1,\pi)\).

Part (a) (slope at \(t=\frac\pi2\)) is in the L15 guide.

Hint

Type L17.1, parametric curve. First step: the two points are \(t=0\) and \(t=\pi\); simplify \(\dot x^2+\dot y^2\) to \(2+2\cos t\) and use \(1+\cos t=2\cos^2\frac t2\).

Solution

Step 1–2 Parametric formula; \((1,0)\) is \(t=0\) and \((-1,\pi)\) is \(t=\pi\).

Step 3b \(\dot x=-\sin t\), \(\dot y=1+\cos t\):

\[\begin{aligned}\dot x^2+\dot y^2&=\sin^2t+1+2\cos t+\cos^2t\\&=2+2\cos t=4\cos^2\tfrac t2.\end{aligned}\]

On \([0,\pi]\), \(\cos\frac t2\ge0\), so the root is \(2\cos\frac t2\).

Step 4 \(L=\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi}2\cos\tfrac t2\,dt=\left[4\sin\tfrac t2\right]_0^{\pi}=4.\)

\[\boxed{L=4}\]

Marker: state the half-angle identity you use and why \(\cos\frac t2\ge0\).

May 2013 Q5(a)Q5: 10%; part marks not shownolder formatBeyond the handouts

Suppose \(a\gt0\) is a constant. Let \(C\) be the curve \(y=\cosh x\), \(-a\le x\le a\). Let \(D\) be the region bounded by \(C\), \(|x|=a\) and the \(x\)-axis. (a) Find the length of \(C\).

Part (b) is under L17.2; part (c) (volume about the \(y\)-axis) is in the L16 guide.

Hint

Type L17.1, Cartesian. First step: \(\cosh x=\frac{e^x+e^{-x}}2\), so \(y'=\sinh x=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}2\); use \(1+\sinh^2x=\cosh^2x\).

Solution

Definitions (not in the calculus handouts): \(\cosh x=\frac{e^x+e^{-x}}2\), \(\sinh x=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}2\). Differentiating, \((\cosh x)'=\sinh x\) and \((\sinh x)'=\cosh x\); expanding the squares gives \(\cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=1\).

Step 3a \(1+y'^2=1+\sinh^2x=\cosh^2x\), and \(\cosh x\gt0\), so the root is \(\cosh x\).

Step 4 \(L=\displaystyle\int_{-a}^{a}\cosh x\,dx=\big[\sinh x\big]_{-a}^{a}=2\sinh a.\)

\[\boxed{L=2\sinh a=e^{a}-e^{-a}}\]

Equivalent forms. \(e^a-\frac1{e^a}\).

L17.2Surface area of revolution

Chapter note · surface area · Chapter note · solids of revolution

Frequency: 3 of 13 recent papers (Dec 2024, May 2024, Dec 2020); 2 of 10 older papers. 5 parts in all, every one about the \(x\)-axis.

Recognise it

"Surface area", "area of the surface generated by revolving", "surface area of the ellipsoid / of the solid". Only the skin is wanted, not the space inside (that is volume, L16).

Standard procedure
  1. Decide which curves sweep the surface.
    • One curve revolved: one surface.
    • A region revolved: one surface per boundary curve, the outer face and the inner face. If the curves do not meet at an end, that end also has a flat disc or ring (area \(\pi R^2\) or \(\pi(R^2-r^2)\)).
    • A closed curve (ellipse): revolve the top half \(y\ge0\) over its full \(x\)-range. Do not double it.
  2. Write \(S=\int2\pi y\,ds\) for each surface. \(y\) is the radius (distance to the axis); \(ds\) is the L17.1 root times \(dx\) or \(dt\). Fix the limits; use symmetry as in L17.1.
  3. Simplify \(y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\). Bring \(y\) inside first: \(y\sqrt{1+y'^2}=\sqrt{y^2+(yy')^2}\) or multiply out.
    • 3a. It collapses: to \(\sqrt{x+c}\), a perfect square (\(\cosh^2x\)), or a chain-rule form \(x^3\sqrt{1+9x^4}\). Integrate directly. Asked: May 2024, Dec 2020, May 2013.
    • 3b. It becomes \(\sqrt{c^2-k^2x^2}\) or \(\sqrt{1+u^2}\): trig substitution (\(kx=c\sin\theta\)), the Ready answer, or the \(I(x)\) the question gives. Asked: Dec 2024, May 2017.
  4. Integrate each surface and add (plus any flat ends). Give the exact value and a decimal.
Worked example · the procedure in action

May 2024 Q5(b) (Q5: 10%). Let \(E\) be the region enclosed between the curves \(y=x^3\) and \(y=\sqrt x\). Evaluate the surface area, i.e. the total area of the outer and inner faces, of the solid \(B\) generated by revolving the region \(E\) about the \(x\)-axis. [Hint: Below are some relevant formulae taught in the course: \(\int_a^by\,dx\), \(\int_a^b\pi y^2\,dx\), \(\int_a^b\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx\), \(\int_a^b2\pi y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx\).]

Step 1 \(x^3=\sqrt x\) at \(x=0\) and \(x=1\). On \([0,1]\), \(\sqrt x\ge x^3\), so \(y=\sqrt x\) sweeps the outer face and \(y=x^3\) the inner face. The curves meet at both ends, so there are no flat ends.

Of the four printed formulas, only the last one is surface area. The hint lists it once, but this solid has two faces.

Step 2

\[\begin{aligned}S&=2\pi\int_0^1\sqrt x\,\sqrt{1+\tfrac1{4x}}\,dx\\&\quad+2\pi\int_0^1x^3\sqrt{1+9x^4}\,dx.\end{aligned}\]

\(y'=\frac1{2\sqrt x}\) for the outer curve and \(y'=3x^2\) for the inner one.

Step 3a Outer: bring \(\sqrt x\) inside the root, \(\sqrt x\sqrt{1+\frac1{4x}}=\sqrt{x+\frac14}\). Inner: already a chain-rule form; put \(w=1+9x^4\), \(dw=36x^3dx\).

The outer integrand blows up at \(x=0\) if you keep \(\frac1{4x}\) separate; combining removes the problem.

Step 4 Outer face:

\[\begin{aligned}2\pi\cdot\tfrac23\Big[(x+\tfrac14)^{3/2}\Big]_0^1&=\tfrac{4\pi}3\left(\tfrac{5\sqrt5}8-\tfrac18\right)\\&=\tfrac{\pi(5\sqrt5-1)}6.\end{aligned}\]

Inner face:

\[\tfrac{2\pi}{36}\cdot\tfrac23\Big[w^{3/2}\Big]_1^{10}=\tfrac{\pi(10\sqrt{10}-1)}{27}.\]
\[\boxed{S=\tfrac{\pi(5\sqrt5-1)}6+\tfrac{\pi(10\sqrt{10}-1)}{27}\approx8.894}\]

Faces separately: about \(5.330\) (outer) and \(3.563\) (inner). Giving only one of them loses about half the marks.

Traps
  • One face only. May 2024 Q5(b) and May 2017 Q5(ii) revolve a region: both boundary curves make a surface.
  • Splitting the root too early. For the ellipsoid (Dec 2024 Q5(a)) and for \(\sqrt x\), \(y'\) is infinite at an end point; \(y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\) is not. Combine \(y\) into the root before integrating.
  • Volume instead of area. \(\pi y^2\) is the volume formula; surface area uses \(2\pi y\) times \(ds\), never \(dx\) alone.

Dec 2024 Q5(a)5% (Q5: 10%)

An ellipsoid is formed by rotating the ellipse \(\dfrac{x^2}{4}+y^2=1\) about the \(x\)-axis. (a) Compute the surface area of the ellipsoid.

Part (b) (volume of the ellipsoid) is in the L16 guide.

Hint

Type L17.2, closed curve. First step: revolve the top half \(y=\sqrt{1-x^2/4}\), \(-2\le x\le2\), and simplify \(y^2+(yy')^2\) before taking the root.

Solution

Step 1–2 Top half \(y=\sqrt{1-\frac{x^2}4}\), \(-2\le x\le2\): \(S=2\pi\int_{-2}^{2}y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx\).

Step 3b \(yy'=-\frac x4\), so

\[y\sqrt{1+y'^2}=\sqrt{y^2+(yy')^2}=\sqrt{1-\tfrac{3x^2}{16}}.\]

Put \(x=\frac4{\sqrt3}\sin\theta\); \(x=\pm2\) gives \(\theta=\pm\frac\pi3\), and \(\sqrt{1-\frac{3x^2}{16}}\,dx=\frac4{\sqrt3}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta\).

Step 4

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-2}^{2}\sqrt{1-\tfrac{3x^2}{16}}\,dx&=\tfrac4{\sqrt3}\Big[\tfrac\theta2+\tfrac{\sin2\theta}4\Big]_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3}\\&=\tfrac4{\sqrt3}\left(\tfrac\pi3+\tfrac{\sqrt3}4\right)\\&=1+\tfrac{4\pi}{3\sqrt3}.\end{aligned}\]

Multiply by \(2\pi\):

\[\boxed{S=2\pi+\dfrac{8\pi^2}{3\sqrt3}\approx21.48}\]

Marker: show the substitution with its new limits \(\pm\frac\pi3\); the trig substitution is the main step.

Equivalent forms. \(2\pi+\dfrac{8\sqrt3\,\pi^2}{9}\), \(\dfrac{2\pi(9+4\sqrt3\,\pi)}{9}\).

Dec 2020 Q5(a)Q5: 10%; part marks not shownBeyond the handouts

Evaluate the area of the surface generated by revolving the curve \(y=\cosh x\), \(0\le x\le1\), about the \(x\)-axis. [Show your calculation.]

Part (b) (a Riemann-sum limit) is in the L12 guide.

Hint

Type L17.2, one curve. First step: with \(\cosh x=\frac{e^x+e^{-x}}2\) and \(\sinh x=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}2\), \(1+\sinh^2x=\cosh^2x\), so the integrand is \(2\pi\cosh^2x\).

Solution

Definitions (not in the calculus handouts): \(\cosh x=\frac{e^x+e^{-x}}2\), \(\sinh x=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}2\); \((\cosh x)'=\sinh x\), \(\cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=1\), \(\cosh^2x=\frac{1+\cosh2x}2\).

Step 1–2 One curve: \(S=2\pi\int_0^1\cosh x\sqrt{1+\sinh^2x}\,dx\).

Step 3a \(\sqrt{1+\sinh^2x}=\cosh x\), so the integrand is \(2\pi\cosh^2x=\pi(1+\cosh2x)\).

Step 4

\[S=\pi\Big[x+\tfrac12\sinh2x\Big]_0^1=\pi\left(1+\tfrac12\sinh2\right).\]
\[\boxed{S=\pi+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\sinh2\approx8.839}\]

Marker: if you prefer exponentials, \(\cosh^2x=\frac{e^{2x}+2+e^{-2x}}4\) gives the same result; show that line.

Equivalent forms. \(\pi+\frac\pi4\left(e^2-e^{-2}\right)\), \(\pi(1+\sinh1\cosh1)\).

May 2017 Q5(ii)Q5: 9%; part marks not shownolder format

Let \(R\) be the below region that is enclosed by \(y=\sqrt x+1\) and \(y=2^x\). (Recall \(2^x=e^{x\ln2}\) and evaluate the two intersection points by inspection.)

Write \(S\) for the solid generated by revolving \(R\) about the \(x\)-axis. Calculate (i) the volume and (ii) the surface area of \(S\). (For Part (ii), you may express your answer in terms of \(I(x)=\displaystyle\int_0^x\sqrt{u^2+1}\,du\) with suitable values of \(x\).)

Figure recreated from the paper's sketch. Part (i) (the volume, by washers) is in the L16 guide.

Hint

Type L17.2, region with two faces, ending in \(I(x)\). First step: the curves meet at \((0,1)\) and \((1,2)\). For the outer face substitute \(u=2\sqrt x\); for the inner face substitute \(u=2^x\ln2\).

Solution

Step 1 At \(x=0\) both are 1; at \(x=1\) both are 2. On \((0,1)\), \(\sqrt x+1\gt2^x\) (e.g. \(x=\frac14\): \(1.5\) vs \(1.19\)). Outer face from \(y=\sqrt x+1\), inner from \(y=2^x\); no flat ends.

Step 2–3b Outer. \(y'=\frac1{2\sqrt x}\), so \(\sqrt{1+y'^2}=\frac{\sqrt{4x+1}}{2\sqrt x}\) and

\[\begin{aligned}&(\sqrt x+1)\tfrac{\sqrt{4x+1}}{2\sqrt x}\\&=\tfrac12\sqrt{4x+1}+\tfrac{\sqrt{4x+1}}{2\sqrt x}.\end{aligned}\]

First piece: \(2\pi\int_0^1\frac12\sqrt{4x+1}\,dx=\frac{\pi(5\sqrt5-1)}6\). Second piece: \(u=2\sqrt x\), \(\frac{dx}{2\sqrt x}=\frac{du}2\), \(u:0\to2\):

\[\begin{aligned}&2\pi\int_0^1\tfrac{\sqrt{4x+1}}{2\sqrt x}\,dx\\&=\pi\int_0^2\sqrt{u^2+1}\,du=\pi I(2).\end{aligned}\]

Step 2–3b Inner. \(y'=2^x\ln2\). Put \(u=2^x\ln2\), \(du=2^x(\ln2)^2dx\), \(u:\ln2\to2\ln2\):

\[\begin{aligned}&2\pi\int_0^12^x\sqrt{1+u^2}\,dx\\&=\tfrac{2\pi}{(\ln2)^2}\int_{\ln2}^{2\ln2}\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du\\&=\tfrac{2\pi}{(\ln2)^2}\big[I(2\ln2)-I(\ln2)\big].\end{aligned}\]

Step 4 Add the faces:

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}S=&\ \tfrac{\pi(5\sqrt5-1)}6+\pi I(2)\\&+\tfrac{2\pi}{(\ln2)^2}\big[I(2\ln2)-I(\ln2)\big]\\\approx&\ 27.76\end{aligned}}\]

Marker: the answer is wanted in terms of \(I\); show each substitution and its new limits rather than evaluating.

Equivalent forms. \(I(2)=\sqrt5+\frac12\ln(2+\sqrt5)\) (Ready answer); outer face \(\approx14.62\), inner face \(\approx13.14\).
