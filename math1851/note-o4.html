Non-Homogeneous Linear ODEs

0Where this part sits

1Non-homogeneous equations: complementary function + particular solution ~8 min

What is this?

Picture a mass hanging on a spring. Left alone, it bobs at its own rhythm and, if there is friction, slowly settles. Now an outside hand pushes it, following a fixed schedule over time. The equation says: the spring's own forces (inertia, friction, stiffness) on the left balance the push on the right. A non-homogeneous equation is simply a spring with a hand on it.

Definition

An inhomogeneous (also called non-homogeneous) second-order linear equation with constant coefficients is

\[ y''+a_1y'+a_2y=f(x),\qquad f(x)\ne0. \]

In words: second derivative + (constant) × first derivative + (constant) × function = a given function of \(x\) that is not zero.

Here \(a_1,a_2\) are constants, and \(f(x)\) is called the forcing term or inhomogeneous term: it is the part of the equation that does not contain \(y\). "Homogeneous" in Parts O3–O4 simply means \(f(x)=0\).

Reading the formula
\(y''\)
the acceleration term: the mass resisting a change of speed (inertia).
\(a_1y'\)
friction: a force proportional to the speed.
\(a_2y\)
the spring pulling back, in proportion to how far it is stretched.
\(f(x)\)
the outside hand: the push at time \(x\). It does not depend on \(y\); the hand follows its own schedule whatever the spring does.
Two meanings of "homogeneous"

In Part O2, a "homogeneous equation" was a first-order equation \(y'=g(x,y)\) where \(g\) depends only on \(y/x\) (solved with \(y=vx\)). Here "homogeneous" means "right-hand side zero". The words are the same; the ideas are unrelated. Part O2 warned about this too.

What is this? (CF and PI)

The complementary function is everything the spring can do with no hand on it: all its free motions, one for each way of starting it. The particular solution is one single motion that copes with the push. Neither is the full answer on its own; section 1's key result says how they combine.

Definitions
  • The complementary function (CF), written \(y_c\), is the general solution of the equation with \(f(x)\) replaced by 0, i.e. of \(y''+a_1y'+a_2y=0\). You already know how to find it (Part O3). It contains the arbitrary constants \(C_1,C_2\).
  • A particular solution or particular integral (PI), written \(y_p\), is any one function that satisfies the full equation with \(f(x)\). It contains no arbitrary constants.
  • The particular solution can be found by undetermined coefficients (section 2) or variation of parameters (section 6).

Why the general solution is CF + PI

What is this? (CF + PI)

Take two motions of the same pushed spring, started in different ways. Both feel exactly the same push, so the push cancels out of their difference. What is left is a motion the spring could make with no hand at all. So every pushed motion is one chosen pushed motion plus some free motion.

Here is the proof for \(y''+k^2y=f(x)\) (\(k\) a constant). Suppose \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) are any two solutions:

\[ y_1''+k^2y_1=f(x),\qquad y_2''+k^2y_2=f(x). \]

Subtract the first from the second. Since \((y_2-y_1)''=y_2''-y_1''\), we get, with \(Y=y_2-y_1\),

\[ Y''+k^2Y=0. \]

In words: the difference of two solutions of the forced equation solves the unforced equation, because the two copies of \(f(x)\) cancel.

So \(Y\) is a solution of the homogeneous equation, and any solution can be written \(y_2=y_1+Y\). Read this as: pick one fixed solution \(y_1\) (the particular solution); every other solution is that one plus something from the complementary function. Exactly the same subtraction works for \(y''+a_1y'+a_2y=f(x)\), and for any linear equation, even with variable coefficients.

Key result \[ y=y_c+y_p \]

In words: general solution = complementary function (general solution of the homogeneous equation, with the arbitrary constants) + particular solution (any one solution of the full equation).

Reading the formula
\(y\)
the whole motion of the pushed spring.
\(y_c\)
its free motion. The constants \(C_1,C_2\) record how it was started, and with friction this part dies away (the transient).
\(y_p\)
one response to the push, with no constants: what the hand forces on the spring.
\(+\)
for a linear equation the two parts just add, without disturbing each other.

Analogy. Think of all solutions as all the points on a straight line in the plane. To describe the line you need one point on it (the particular solution) plus "all multiples of the direction" (the complementary function). Which point you pick does not matter: a different particular solution just moves the starting point along the same line. That is why two correct methods can give particular solutions that look different but differ only by CF terms (see sections 4 and 6).

Example 1

Find the general solution of \(y''+3y'+2y=10\cos x\).

CF. Characteristic equation \(m^2+3m+2=(m+1)(m+2)=0\), so \(m=-1,-2\) and

\[ y_c=C_1e^{-x}+C_2e^{-2x}. \]

PI. (The method is explained in section 2.) Try \(y_p=A\cos x+B\sin x\). Then \(y_p'=-A\sin x+B\cos x\), \(y_p''=-A\cos x-B\sin x\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} &y_p''+3y_p'+2y_p\\ &=(A+3B)\cos x+(B-3A)\sin x. \end{aligned} \]

Matching with \(10\cos x+0\sin x\): \(A+3B=10\) and \(B-3A=0\).The two sides must agree at every \(x\), and a cosine can never be made out of a sine, so the cosine parts and the sine parts must match separately. So \(B=3A\), \(10A=10\), \(A=1\), \(B=3\).

\[ y=C_1e^{-x}+C_2e^{-2x}+\cos x+3\sin x. \]

Play with \(C_1,C_2\) in the first graph below: whatever you choose, the solution settles onto the particular solution, because the CF terms die away.

What is this? (splitting the push)

Two hands pushing the spring at once: the motion they cause together is the motion caused by the first hand plus the motion caused by the second. This is true because the spring's equation is linear.

Splitting the forcing term Going further

If \(y_{p1}\) solves the equation with right-hand side \(f_1\), and \(y_{p2}\) solves it with \(f_2\), then \(y_{p1}+y_{p2}\) solves it with \(f_1+f_2\).

In words: for a linear equation, the response to a sum of forces is the sum of the responses. You may find a particular solution for each piece of \(f(x)\) separately and add them.

Reason: the left-hand side of a linear equation is a sum of derivatives times constants, and the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives (Lecture 6). This is the superposition principle of Part O1 applied to the forcing.

You can now: say what the forcing term, the complementary function and the particular solution are; explain why every solution is \(y_c+y_p\); and split a forcing term into pieces and add their particular solutions.

2Undetermined coefficients ~8 min

What is this?

Undetermined coefficients means guessing a response shaped like the push. If the hand pushes back and forth like a cosine, the spring ends up moving back and forth at the same rhythm, perhaps with a different size and a delay. A delayed cosine is a mix of a cosine and a sine, which is why the guess always needs both. You write the shape with unknown sizes in front, and the equation tells you the sizes.

Idea. For some simple, standard right-hand sides, the particular solution has the same shape as \(f(x)\). You write down that shape with unknown constants (the "undetermined coefficients"), substitute it into the equation, and choose the constants so that both sides match. This works because differentiating an exponential, a sine/cosine or a polynomial gives back a function of the same family.

Working rules
  • \(f(x)=\) constant: try \(y_p=A\) (a constant).
  • \(f(x)=e^{ax}\): try \(y_p=Ce^{ax}\).
  • \(f(x)=\cos x\) or \(\sin x\): try \(y_p=C\cos x+D\sin x\) (both, even if \(f\) has only one of them). Likewise \(\cos bx\) or \(\sin bx\): try \(C\cos bx+D\sin bx\).
  • \(f(x)=x^n\): try a polynomial of degree \(n\).

In words: guess a function of the same type as the right-hand side, with unknown numbers in front, then find the numbers.

For \(f(x)=x^n\), the trial is a polynomial of degree \(n\) with all its lower powers: \(y_p=A_nx^n+A_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\dots+A_1x+A_0\). Trying only a single power usually fails (Example 2(d) below shows why).

What is this? (the clash rule)

Sometimes the guessed shape is one of the spring's own free motions. Then the spring's own forces cancel it completely: put it into the left-hand side and you get zero, so it can never balance the push. This is resonance (section 3): the push matches a rhythm the spring already has. The cure is to multiply by \(x\), a response that keeps growing with time.

The clash rule (multiply by \(x\) or \(x^2\))

Before substituting, compare each trial group with the complementary function. If the number \(r\) in the last column is a root of the characteristic equation, the trial "clashes" with the CF: it would make the left-hand side zero, so it can never produce \(f(x)\). Then multiply that group by \(x^s\), where \(s\) is how many times \(r\) is a root:

  • \(r\) is not a root: \(s=0\), use the table trial as it is;
  • \(r\) is a simple (single) root: multiply by \(x\);
  • \(r\) is a double root: multiply by \(x^2\).

In words: if your guess is already a solution of the unforced equation, it is useless; multiply it by \(x\), and again by \(x\) if it is still useless.

Only the clashing group is multiplied. For \(f=12(e^{2x}-x)\) with roots \(2,-1\), the trial is \(Axe^{2x}+Bx+C\), not \(x(Ae^{2x}+Bx+C)\). Section 3 explains the physical meaning (resonance) and section 5 explains why multiplying by \(x\) is exactly right.

Reading the formula
\(r\)
the push's own "rhythm number": \(0\) for a polynomial, \(a\) for \(e^{ax}\), \(\pm bi\) for \(\cos bx\) and \(\sin bx\).
\(r\) a root
the spring can already move like the push with no hand on it.
\(s\)
how many of the spring's free motions share that rhythm (0, 1 or 2).
\(x^s\times\)(trial)
a response that grows with time instead of staying the same size, which the spring's own forces cannot cancel.

The method in steps:

  1. Do: Find the CF \(y_c\) first (Part O3). You need the spring's free motions before you can spot a clash.
  2. Do: Write the trial from the table, one group per piece of \(f(x)\), with unknown constants. The response has the shape of the push, and each hand gets its own response (splitting the forcing term, section 1).
  3. Do: Compare each group's number \(r\) with the characteristic roots, and multiply a clashing group by \(x^s\). A guess that is already a free motion gives zero on the left, so it could never balance the push.
  4. Do: Substitute, then match the coefficients of each kind of term. The equation must hold at every \(x\), and different kinds of term (\(\cos\), \(\sin\), \(x^2\), \(x\), constants) cannot stand in for each other.
  5. Do: Write \(y=y_c+y_p\). The full motion is the free motion plus the one response.
Example 2: one equation, four right-hand sides

Equation: \(y''-y'-2y=f(x)\). Characteristic equation \(m^2-m-2=(m-2)(m+1)=0\), roots \(2,-1\), so \(y_c=C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{-x}\). None of the trials below clashes.

(a) \(f=4\). Try \(y_p=A\): \(y_p'=y_p''=0\), so \(-2A=4\), \(A=-2\). \(\;y_p=-2\).

(b) \(f=5e^{3x}\). Try \(y_p=Ae^{3x}\): \(y_p'=3Ae^{3x}\), \(y_p''=9Ae^{3x}\), so \((9-3-2)Ae^{3x}=5e^{3x}\), \(4A=5\). \(\;y_p=\tfrac54e^{3x}\).

(c) \(f=10\sin x\). Try \(y_p=A\cos x+B\sin x\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} y_p''-y_p'-2y_p&=(-3A-B)\cos x\\ &\quad+(A-3B)\sin x. \end{aligned} \]

Match: \(-3A-B=0\) (no cosine on the right) and \(A-3B=10\). So \(B=-3A\), \(10A=10\): \(A=1\), \(B=-3\). \(\;y_p=\cos x-3\sin x\). Note the cosine is needed even though \(f\) has only a sine.

(d) \(f=4x^2\). Try \(y_p=Ax^2+Bx+C\): \(y_p'=2Ax+B\), \(y_p''=2A\).

\[ \begin{aligned} &y_p''-y_p'-2y_p\\ &=-2Ax^2+(-2A-2B)x+(2A-B-2C). \end{aligned} \]

Match with \(4x^2+0x+0\): \(A=-2\); \(-2A-2B=0\Rightarrow B=2\); \(2A-B-2C=0\Rightarrow C=-3\). \(\;y_p=-2x^2+2x-3\). If you had tried only \(Ax^2\), the \(x\) and constant terms could not be matched.

In each case the general solution is \(y=C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{-x}+y_p\).

Class example

Solve \(y''''-y''=3x^2-\sin 2x\). (\(y''''\) is the fourth derivative.)

CF. Try \(e^{mx}\): \(m^4-m^2=m^2(m-1)(m+1)=0\), roots \(0,0,1,-1\). A double root \(0\) gives \(e^{0x}=1\) and \(x\cdot1=x\) (Part O3), so

\[ y_c=C_1+C_2x+C_3e^{x}+C_4e^{-x}. \]

Trial for \(3x^2\). The table says \(Ax^2+Bx+C\), with \(r=0\). But \(0\) is a double root, and indeed the constant and \(x\) terms are already in the CF. Why the naive trial fails: for any \(Ax^2+Bx+C\), \(y''''-y''=-2A\), a constant, which can never equal \(3x^2\). So multiply by \(x^2\):

\[ x^2(Ax^2+Bx+C)=Ax^4+Bx^3+Cx^2. \]

Trial for \(-\sin2x\). \(D\cos2x+E\sin2x\), with \(r=\pm2i\), not roots: no change.

Substitute. With \(y_p=Ax^4+Bx^3+Cx^2+D\cos2x+E\sin2x\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y_p''&=12Ax^2+6Bx+2C\\ &\quad-4(D\cos2x+E\sin2x),\\ y_p''''&=24A+16(D\cos2x+E\sin2x). \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} y_p''''-y_p''&=-12Ax^2-6Bx+(24A-2C)\\ &\quad+20(D\cos2x+E\sin2x). \end{aligned} \]

Match with \(3x^2-\sin2x\): \(x^2\): \(-12A=3\), \(A=-\tfrac14\). \(x\): \(B=0\). Constant: \(24A-2C=0\), \(C=12A=-3\). \(\cos2x\): \(D=0\). \(\sin2x\): \(20E=-1\), \(E=-\tfrac1{20}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} y&=C_1+C_2x+C_3e^{x}+C_4e^{-x}\\ &\quad-\frac{x^4}{4}-3x^2-\frac{1}{20}\sin2x. \end{aligned} \]

In words: general solution = four-constant CF + polynomial response to \(3x^2\) (raised by \(x^2\) because of the double root 0) + sine response to \(-\sin2x\).

You can now: write the trial for a constant, polynomial, exponential or trigonometric right-hand side, or a sum of them; spot a clash and multiply only that group by \(x\) or \(x^2\); and find the constants by matching coefficients.

3Resonance and clashes ~6 min

What is this?

Push the spring at exactly its own rhythm, each push arriving just when it helps the motion, and every push adds energy. If there is no friction to take energy away, the swings get bigger and bigger without limit. In the equation this is the clash of section 2: the push has the shape of a free motion, and the response needs an extra factor of time, \(x\).

Resonance

When a system is forced at one of its natural frequencies, a large amplitude results from the synchronised ("in-phase") energy input to the oscillator.

Mathematically: when the inhomogeneous term satisfies the homogeneous equation, the trial function must be chosen carefully.

Terms. An oscillator is anything that swings back and forth (a mass on a spring, a pendulum, a circuit). Its natural frequency is how fast it oscillates when left alone: for \(y''+\omega_0^2y=0\) the CF is \(\cos\omega_0t,\ \sin\omega_0t\), so the natural (angular) frequency is \(\omega_0\). The amplitude is the size of the swing. In phase means each push arrives at the moment it adds to the motion.

Analogy. Pushing a child on a swing: if you push once per swing, at the right moment, every push adds energy and the swing goes higher and higher. If you push at a random rhythm, some pushes help and some fight the motion, and the swing never gets big.

Simple example of resonance

Solve \(y''+y=\cos x\).

CF. \(Y''+Y=0\) has \(m^2+1=0\), \(m=\pm i\), so \(Y=\cos x,\ \sin x\): the natural frequency is 1. One of them, \(\cos x\), is exactly the forcing. Hence this is a problem of resonance.

Why the table trial fails. \(A\cos x+B\sin x\) gives \(y_p''+y_p=0\), never \(\cos x\).

Trial. The safe trial (clash rule, \(r=\pm i\) simple roots) is

\[ y_p=x(A\cos x+B\sin x). \]

Differentiate (product rule, Lecture 6):

\[ \begin{aligned} y_p'&=A\cos x+B\sin x\\ &\quad+x(-A\sin x+B\cos x),\\ y_p''&=2(-A\sin x+B\cos x)\\ &\quad-x(A\cos x+B\sin x). \end{aligned} \]

The \(x(\dots)\) terms cancel when we add \(y_p\):

\[ y_p''+y_p=-2A\sin x+2B\cos x=\cos x. \]

So \(A=0\), \(B=\tfrac12\) (so a guess of (constant) \(\times\,x\sin x\) alone happens to be enough here, but you cannot know that in advance):

\[ y_p=\frac{x\sin x}{2}, \] \[ y=C_1\cos x+C_2\sin x+\frac{x\sin x}{2}. \]

In words: the response to forcing at the natural frequency is a sine wave whose amplitude grows in proportion to \(x\) (time).

Reading the formula
\(\sin x\)
the spring's own rhythm (natural frequency 1). The push is a cosine and the response a sine: the spring's velocity, a cosine, keeps in step with the push, so every push adds energy.
\(x\)
time. The size of the swing grows steadily, with no ceiling, because nothing takes energy out.
\(\tfrac12\)
how fast the swing grows, set by the push's strength (1 here) and the natural frequency: in general the growth rate is \(\frac{1}{2\omega_0}\) per unit push.

Going further

For \(\omega\ne\omega_0\), the trial \(A\cos\omega t+B\sin\omega t\) gives \(y_p=\dfrac{\cos\omega t}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2}\) (check: \(y_p''=-\omega^2y_p\)). Fitting \(y(0)=y'(0)=0\):

\[ y=\frac{\cos\omega t-\cos\omega_0t}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2}. \]

As \(\omega\to\omega_0\) the denominator goes to 0, which is why the amplitude blows up near resonance. Treating \(\omega\) as the variable and using L'Hôpital's Rule (Lecture 10) on this \(\frac00\) form gives the limit \(\dfrac{t\sin\omega_0t}{2\omega_0}\): the resonant solution with the extra factor \(t\).

Reading the formula
\(\cos\omega t\)
the forced part, moving at the push's rhythm.
\(-\cos\omega_0t\)
a free motion at the spring's own rhythm, needed so the spring starts at rest. The two rhythms drift in and out of step: the beats in the graph.
\(\omega_0^2-\omega^2\)
the gap between the two rhythms. A small gap means a big response; no gap is resonance.

You can now: say what resonance is; recognise it from the CF (the forcing is one of the free motions); find the growing particular solution such as \(\tfrac12x\sin x\); and explain why the amplitude grows near and at the natural frequency.

4Initial conditions ~3 min

What is this?

The initial conditions say how the spring was started: where it was and how fast it was moving when the hand began. They pick one motion out of the whole family. The push moves the spring from the very first moment too, so the starting values belong to the whole motion, the free part plus the response.

Exams often ask for a solution with initial conditions. Initial conditions are given values such as \(y(0)\) and \(y'(0)\) that pick out one solution from the general solution. A second-order equation needs two of them (one per arbitrary constant).

Order of work
  1. Do: Find the CF \(y_c\) (with \(C_1,C_2\)). The free motion carries the two dials \(C_1,C_2\) that the starting values will set.
  2. Do: Find a particular solution \(y_p\). This is the response to the push. It has no dials to set.
  3. Do: Write \(y=y_c+y_p\) and differentiate it. The conditions give a starting position and a starting speed, so you need both \(y\) and \(y'\).
  4. Do: Only now substitute the initial conditions and solve for \(C_1,C_2\). \(y_p\) has its own starting position and speed (in Example 3, \(\cos x+3\sin x\) starts at 1 with slope 3), and the dials must make up for them.

In words: constants last. The initial conditions belong to the whole solution, not to the CF.

Example 3

Solve \(y''+3y'+2y=10\cos x\) with \(y(0)=0\), \(y'(0)=0\).

From Example 1, \(y=C_1e^{-x}+C_2e^{-2x}+\cos x+3\sin x\), so

\[ y'=-C_1e^{-x}-2C_2e^{-2x}-\sin x+3\cos x. \]

\(y(0)=C_1+C_2+1=0\) and \(y'(0)=-C_1-2C_2+3=0\). So \(C_1+C_2=-1\), \(C_1+2C_2=3\), giving \(C_2=4\), \(C_1=-5\):

\[ y=-5e^{-x}+4e^{-2x}+\cos x+3\sin x. \]

Set the sliders in the first graph to \(C_1=-5\), \(C_2=4\): the readout shows \(y(0)=0\), \(y'(0)=0\).

Example 4: the resonance graph is an initial value problem

Solve \(y''+y=\cos x\), \(y(0)=0\), \(y'(0)=0\). From section 3, \(y=C_1\cos x+C_2\sin x+\tfrac12x\sin x\). Then \(y(0)=C_1=0\). Also

\[ \begin{aligned} y'&=-C_1\sin x+C_2\cos x\\ &\quad+\tfrac12(\sin x+x\cos x), \end{aligned} \]

so \(y'(0)=C_2=0\). The answer is \(y=\tfrac12x\sin x\): the system starts at rest, and the resonant forcing alone builds up the growing swing shown in the resonance graph above.

Two methods, one answer

Different methods can give particular solutions that differ by CF terms (for example \(\frac x2e^{x}\) and \(\frac x2e^{x}-\frac14e^{x}\) both solve \(y''-y=e^{x}\); see Group work 3). After you fit the initial conditions, the constants adjust and the final answers are identical. If an exam asks you to "solve two ways and compare", this is the comparison to make.

You can now: fit \(y(0)\) and \(y'(0)\) to the full \(y_c+y_p\); and explain why two methods that give different particular solutions still give the same final answer.

5The D-operator method ~4 min

What is this?

\(D\) is short for "take the rate of change". An annihilator is a filter that turns the push into silence: for \(\cos x\), "differentiate twice and add the original" gives zero. Apply that filter to both sides and the pushed spring becomes a bigger spring with no hand on it, which Part O3 can solve. The free motions of the bigger problem that the original spring did not have are exactly the shape of the response.

Definition

Write \(D=\dfrac{d}{dx}\), so \(Dy=y'\), \(D^2y=D(Dy)=y''\), and so on. An operator is a rule that takes a function and gives back a function, the way a function takes a number and gives a number. Polynomials in \(D\) act term by term, e.g. \((D^2+1)y=y''+y\). So

\[ \begin{aligned} &y''+y=\cos x\quad\text{becomes}\\ &(D^2+1)y=\cos x. \end{aligned} \]

In words: "apply (differentiate twice, then add the original) to \(y\), and you get \(\cos x\)".

With constant coefficients, operators can be multiplied and factorised exactly like ordinary polynomials, and the order does not matter: \((D-2)(D+2)y=(D^2-4)y=y''-4y\).

The question. What operator will destroy \(\cos x\), i.e. turn it into zero? Such an operator is called an annihilator of \(\cos x\).

The D-operator method on \(y''+y=\cos x\)

Annihilate. \((\cos x)''+\cos x=-\cos x+\cos x=0\), i.e. \((D^2+1)\cos x=0\). Apply \((D^2+1)\) to both sides of \((D^2+1)y=\cos x\):

\[ (D^2+1)^2y=(D^2+1)\cos x=0. \]

This is now a homogeneous fourth-order equation, which we can solve with Part O3.

Characteristic equation. With \(y=e^{mx}\): \((m^2+1)^2=0\), so \(m=i,i,-i,-i\). Repeated complex roots (Part O3) give

\[ \cos x,\quad\sin x,\quad x\cos x,\quad x\sin x. \]

Pick the new ones. \(\cos x,\ \sin x\) are already the CF of the original equation. The NEW solutions \(x\cos x,\ x\sin x\) are the correct choice for the particular solution: try \(y_p=x(A\cos x+B\sin x)\). Substituting (as in section 3) gives \(A=0\), \(B=\tfrac12\), \(y_p=\tfrac12x\sin x\).

In words: kill the right-hand side with an extra operator, solve the bigger homogeneous equation, and the solutions that are not already in the CF are the shape of the particular solution.

This is why the clash rule works: the annihilator adds roots \(r\). If \(r\) is new, the trial is the ordinary table trial; if \(r\) was already a root, it becomes a repeated root, and repeated roots bring an extra factor \(x\).

The method in steps:

  1. Do: Write the equation as \(L(D)y=f(x)\), with \(L\) a polynomial in \(D\). The spring's own forces become a polynomial in \(D\), which factorises like an ordinary polynomial.
  2. Do: Find an annihilator \(A(D)\) of \(f\) and apply it to both sides: \(A(D)L(D)y=0\). The push is silenced, leaving a homogeneous equation that Part O3 can solve.
  3. Do: Solve its characteristic equation and list the solutions. Every solution of the original equation also solves the bigger one, so the particular solution is among these.
  4. Do: Cross out the ones already in the CF; the rest, with unknown constants, is the trial. The crossed-out ones are free motions and give zero on the left; only the new ones can answer the push.
  5. Do: Substitute the trial to find the constants. The bigger equation gives the shape of the response, not its size.
Example 5

Solve \(y''-4y=8e^{2x}\) by the D-operator method.

\((D-2)(D+2)y=8e^{2x}\). Since \((D-2)e^{2x}=2e^{2x}-2e^{2x}=0\), the annihilator is \(D-2\). Apply it:

\[ (D-2)^2(D+2)y=0,\qquad m=2,2,-2. \]

Solutions: \(e^{2x},\ xe^{2x},\ e^{-2x}\). The CF is \(C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{-2x}\), so the new one is \(xe^{2x}\): try \(y_p=Axe^{2x}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} y_p'&=A(1+2x)e^{2x},\\ y_p''&=A(4+4x)e^{2x}. \end{aligned} \]

\(y_p''-4y_p=4Ae^{2x}=8e^{2x}\), so \(A=2\) and

\[ y=C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{-2x}+2xe^{2x}. \]

6Variation of parameters ~7 min

What is this?

In the free motion, the constants \(C_1,C_2\) are two dials set once at the start. Variation of parameters lets the dials keep turning as time goes on: at each moment the spring moves like a free motion, but with dial settings that drift. The push decides how fast each dial must turn. There is no guessing, so it works for any push, but it does need the spring's two independent free motions.

When. For more complicated inhomogeneous terms (e.g. \(\sec x\), \(\ln x\), \(\frac1x\), which are not in the trial table), or when you simply cannot see the right form of the particular solution, use variation of parameters (VoP). It also works when the coefficients are functions of \(x\):

\[ y''+p(x)y'+q(x)y=f(x). \]

Setup. Suppose the homogeneous equation has two independent solutions \(y_1(x)\), \(y_2(x)\), so its general solution is \(C_1y_1+C_2y_2\). Vary the parameters: replace the constants by unknown functions,

\[ y_p(x)=u_1(x)y_1(x)+u_2(x)y_2(x). \]

In words: particular solution = (unknown function) × first CF solution + (unknown function) × second CF solution.

What is this? (the extra condition)

Two drifting dials describe one motion, so there is one spare choice. We use it to demand that the turning of the dials does not change the velocity: the velocity is just the free-motion velocity with the dials at their current settings. Then only the acceleration feels the turning dials, and that is where the push acts.

One free choice. We are using two unknown functions \(u_1,u_2\) to describe one unknown function \(y_p\): two degrees of freedom for a one-degree-of-freedom problem. A degree of freedom is a quantity you are free to choose. Analogy: if all you need is \(a+b=10\), you may also demand anything reasonable you like (say \(a=3\)) and still succeed. So we may impose one extra condition of our choosing, picked to make the algebra easy.

Derivation

Differentiate \(y_p\) with the product rule:

\[ \begin{aligned} y_p'&=u_1y_1'+u_2y_2'\\ &\quad+\big(u_1'y_1+u_2'y_2\big). \end{aligned} \]

To stop second derivatives of \(u\) from appearing later, choose the extra conditionThis is the spare choice: the velocity of \(y_p\) is then the free-motion velocity with the dials at their current settings.

\[ u_1'y_1+u_2'y_2=0. \tag{1} \]

Then \(y_p'=u_1y_1'+u_2y_2'\), and differentiating again,

\[ y_p''=u_1y_1''+u_2y_2''+u_1'y_1'+u_2'y_2'. \]

Now substitute into \(y''+py'+qy=f\) and group by \(u_1\) and \(u_2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &u_1\big(y_1''+py_1'+qy_1\big)\\ &+u_2\big(y_2''+py_2'+qy_2\big)\\ &+u_1'y_1'+u_2'y_2'=f. \end{aligned} \]

Both brackets are 0, because \(y_1,y_2\) solve the homogeneous equation. What is left:

\[ u_1'y_1'+u_2'y_2'=f(x). \tag{2} \]
The VoP system \[ \begin{aligned} u_1'y_1+u_2'y_2&=0\\ u_1'y_1'+u_2'y_2'&=f(x) \end{aligned} \]

In words: (unknown slope 1)×(solution 1) + (unknown slope 2)×(solution 2) = 0, and the same with the solutions differentiated = right-hand side.

Solve for \(u_1',u_2'\) algebraically (two simultaneous linear equations), then integrate to find \(u_1,u_2\). Eliminating gives

\[ u_1'=-\frac{y_2f}{W},\qquad u_2'=\frac{y_1f}{W}, \] \[ W=y_1y_2'-y_2y_1'. \]

In words: \(W\) is the Wronskian of the two CF solutions (Part O3); it is never zero because they are independent. \(u_1'\) = minus (other solution × right side) ÷ \(W\); \(u_2'\) = (first solution × right side) ÷ \(W\).

(To get \(u_1'\): multiply (1) by \(y_2'\) and (2) by \(y_2\), subtract: \(u_1'(y_1y_2'-y_2y_1')=-y_2f\). Similarly for \(u_2'\).)

Reading the formula

Be careful

The coefficient of \(y''\) must be ONE (unity). The derivation substituted into \(y''+py'+qy=f\). If the equation is \(a(x)y''+\dots=g(x)\), first divide everything by \(a(x)\); the \(f\) in the formulas is \(g(x)/a(x)\). Forgetting this is the most common VoP error, especially for Cauchy–Euler equations (section 7).

Constants of integration in \(u_1,u_2\) may be dropped: adding a constant to \(u_1\) just adds a multiple of \(y_1\) (a CF term) to \(y_p\).

The method in steps:

  1. Do: Make the coefficient of \(y''\) equal to 1 and read off \(f(x)\). Equation (2) was derived from \(y''+py'+qy=f\); otherwise the push is measured wrongly.
  2. Do: Find \(y_1,y_2\) from the CF and compute \(W=y_1y_2'-y_2y_1'\). These are the two free motions whose dials will drift; \(W\ne0\) lets you solve for the dial speeds.
  3. Do: \(u_1'=-\dfrac{y_2f}{W}\), \(u_2'=\dfrac{y_1f}{W}\). These are the dial speeds the push demands.
  4. Do: Integrate to get \(u_1,u_2\); drop the constants of integration. A constant in \(u_1\) or \(u_2\) is a fixed dial setting, a free motion that the CF already contains.
  5. Do: \(y_p=u_1y_1+u_2y_2\), and \(y=C_1y_1+C_2y_2+y_p\). The response is the free motions with the dials at their drifting settings.
Example 6: a right-hand side the table cannot handle

Find the general solution of \(y''+y=\sec x\) on \(-\frac\pi2\lt x\lt\frac\pi2\).

Undetermined coefficients fails: the derivatives of \(\sec x\) (\(\sec x\tan x\), \(\sec x\tan^2x+\sec^3x\), …) keep producing new kinds of terms, so no finite trial works.

CF: \(y_1=\cos x\), \(y_2=\sin x\). Coefficient of \(y''\) is 1, so \(f=\sec x\). \(W=\cos x\cdot\cos x-\sin x\cdot(-\sin x)=1\).

\[ u_1'=-\sin x\sec x=-\tan x, \] \[ u_2'=\cos x\sec x=1. \]

Integrate (Lecture 13): \(u_1=\ln(\cos x)\) (as \(\cos x\gt0\) here), \(u_2=x\). So

\[ y_p=\cos x\,\ln(\cos x)+x\sin x, \] \[ \begin{aligned} y&=C_1\cos x+C_2\sin x\\ &\quad+\cos x\ln(\cos x)+x\sin x. \end{aligned} \]
Example 7: VoP on a clash

Redo Example 5, \(y''-4y=8e^{2x}\), by VoP. \(y_1=e^{2x}\), \(y_2=e^{-2x}\), \(f=8e^{2x}\).

\[ W=e^{2x}(-2e^{-2x})-e^{-2x}(2e^{2x})=-4. \] \[ u_1'=-\frac{e^{-2x}\cdot8e^{2x}}{-4}=2, \] \[ u_2'=\frac{e^{2x}\cdot8e^{2x}}{-4}=-2e^{4x}. \]

So \(u_1=2x\), \(u_2=-\tfrac12e^{4x}\), andThe dial on \(e^{2x}\) turns at a steady rate 2, so it grows like \(2x\): that is the extra factor \(x\) of the clash, found without any rule.

\[ y_p=2xe^{2x}-\tfrac12e^{2x}. \]

The term \(-\frac12e^{2x}\) is a CF term, so it can be absorbed into \(C_1e^{2x}\): the general solution is the same as in Example 5. VoP needs no clash rule: it finds the factor \(x\) by itself.

You can now: set up \(y_p=u_1y_1+u_2y_2\) and the two VoP equations; compute \(W\), \(u_1'\), \(u_2'\) and integrate; and handle right-hand sides such as \(\sec x\) that the trial table cannot.

7Variation of parameters for Cauchy–Euler equations ~3 min

What is this?

A Cauchy–Euler equation is a spring whose friction and stiffness change with \(x\). Its free motions are powers of \(x\) instead of exponentials, but the drifting-dial method of section 6 works the same. The one trap is the push: \(y''\) carries a factor \(x^2\), so the push felt per unit of \(y''\) is the right-hand side divided by \(x^2\).

This combines Part O3 (Cauchy–Euler CF) with the warning that the coefficient of \(y''\) must be 1. Exams set it often, and usually remind you that "the coefficient of the second derivative term is not unity".

Recap (Part O3). A second-order Cauchy–Euler equation has the form \(x^2y''+bxy'+cy=g(x)\) (\(b,c\) constants; the power of \(x\) matches the order of the derivative). For the CF, try \(y=x^m\): \(m(m-1)+bm+c=0\). Distinct real roots give \(x^{m_1},x^{m_2}\); a double root \(m\) gives \(x^m,\ x^m\ln x\); complex roots \(a\pm bi\) give \(x^a\cos(b\ln x),\ x^a\sin(b\ln x)\). Take \(x\gt0\).

Method
  1. Do: Find \(y_1,y_2\) from \(m(m-1)+bm+c=0\). These are the free motions. \(x^m\) works because each derivative lowers the power by one and the matching power of \(x\) in front puts it back.
  2. Do: Divide the whole equation by \(x^2\), so that the coefficient of \(y''\) is 1. Then \(f(x)=\dfrac{g(x)}{x^2}\). The VoP formulas come from \(y''+py'+qy=f\); skipping this overstates the push by a factor \(x^2\).
  3. Do: \(W=y_1y_2'-y_2y_1'\); \(u_1'=-\dfrac{y_2f}{W}\), \(u_2'=\dfrac{y_1f}{W}\); integrate. These are the dial speeds of section 6, unchanged.
  4. Do: \(y=C_1y_1+C_2y_2+u_1y_1+u_2y_2\). Free motion plus the response, as always.

In words: the right-hand side used in VoP is the original right-hand side divided by \(x^2\).

Example 8

Solve \(x^2y''-2xy'+2y=x^3e^{x}\), \(x\gt0\).

CF. \(m(m-1)-2m+2=m^2-3m+2=(m-1)(m-2)=0\): \(y_1=x\), \(y_2=x^2\).

Divide by \(x^2\). \(y''-\frac2xy'+\frac2{x^2}y=xe^{x}\), so \(f=xe^{x}\) (not \(x^3e^x\)).

\(W=x\cdot2x-x^2\cdot1=x^2\).

\[ u_1'=-\frac{x^2\cdot xe^{x}}{x^2}=-xe^{x}, \] \[ u_2'=\frac{x\cdot xe^{x}}{x^2}=e^{x}. \]

By parts (Lecture 13), \(u_1=-(x-1)e^{x}=(1-x)e^{x}\); and \(u_2=e^{x}\). So

\[ y_p=x(1-x)e^{x}+x^2e^{x}=xe^{x}, \] \[ y=C_1x+C_2x^2+xe^{x}. \]

Check: \(y_p'=(1+x)e^x\), \(y_p''=(2+x)e^x\); \(x^2(2+x)e^x-2x(1+x)e^x+2xe^x=x^3e^x\). ✓

Shortcut when \(g(x)=kx^n\)

For a pure power on the right, you may also try \(y_p=Ax^n\): then \(x^2y_p''+bxy_p'+cy_p=A\big[n(n-1)+bn+c\big]x^n\), so \(A=\dfrac{k}{n(n-1)+bn+c}\), provided \(n\) is not a root of the characteristic equation. If the question says "use variation of parameters", you must use VoP, but this is a quick check of your answer.

You can now: find the CF of a Cauchy–Euler equation; divide by \(x^2\) and apply variation of parameters; and check a power right-hand side with the \(Ax^n\) shortcut.

8Which method? Insights on solving ODEs ~5 min

What is this?

This section is a sorting chart for every ODE in the course. Two questions do most of the work. Is the equation linear, so that two pushes together give the sum of the two responses? And are the coefficients constant, like a spring whose stiffness and friction never change? Linear with constant coefficients can always be solved; nonlinear and higher order usually cannot.

The whole ODE part can be summarised as five situations. Here they are, with the method for each and where it was taught.

(1) First order, linear\(y'+p(x)y=q(x)\). Can always be solved exactly by an integrating factor \(e^{\int p\,dx}\) (Part O1). Make the coefficient of \(y'\) equal to 1 first.
(2) First order, nonlinearCan usually make progress: separable (Part O1), exact (Part O1), homogeneous in \(y/x\) (Part O2), Bernoulli (Part O2); also Riccati with a given solution (Part O2).
(3) Second or higher order, linear, constant coefficientsCan always be solved exactly: characteristic equation for the CF (Part O3), then undetermined coefficients, D-operator or variation of parameters for the PI (this part).
(4) Second or higher order, linear, variable coefficients(a) Special case: Cauchy–Euler, try \(x^m\) (Part O3), PI by VoP after dividing by \(x^2\) (section 7). (b) Generally: solutions as an infinite series ("Frobenius series"). Also: reduction of order if one solution is known (Part O2).
(5) Second or higher order, nonlinearUsually hopeless. You are really lucky if you can solve the ODE exactly.
Going further: Frobenius series

A Frobenius series writes the solution as an infinite sum \(y=x^r(a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+\dots)\) and finds the numbers \(a_0,a_1,\dots\) one after another by substituting into the equation. It is how equations like Bessel's equation are solved in later courses. It is mentioned here only to say that it exists; it is not examined in this course. Similarly, "hopeless" in (5) means no general method exists: such equations are usually solved numerically by computer.

A decision path for exam questions

  1. Do: Order? First order: go to step 2. Second or higher: go to step 3. The order sets how many constants you need and which toolbox applies (Parts O1–O2 or O3–O4).
  2. Do: First order. Can you put all \(y\) on one side and all \(x\) on the other? Separable (O1.1). Is it \(y'+p(x)y=q(x)\)? Integrating factor (O1.2). Is it \(M\,dx+N\,dy=0\) with \(\partial M/\partial y=\partial N/\partial x\)? Exact (O1.3). Does it depend only on \(y/x\)? \(y=vx\) (O2.1). Is there a \(y^n\) term on the right of a linear equation? Bernoulli, \(u=y^{1-n}\) (O2.2). A \(y^2\) term and a given solution? Riccati (O2.3). Each first-order method works for one form only, so recognising the form is the whole job.
  3. Do: Linear, second or higher order. Constant coefficients? Characteristic equation (O3.1–O3.2). Coefficients \(x^2,\ x,\ 1\) in front of \(y'',y',y\)? Cauchy–Euler (O3.3). One solution given? Reduction of order (O2.5). The shape of the coefficients decides the free motions: constants give \(e^{mx}\), Cauchy–Euler gives \(x^m\).
  4. Do: Right-hand side not zero. Find the CF first. Then:
    • constant coefficients and \(f\) in the trial table: undetermined coefficients; check for a clash (O4.1, O4.2);
    • the question says "D operator": annihilate, solve, take the new solutions (O4.6);
    • \(f\) not in the table (\(\sec x\), \(\ln x\), \(1/x\), …), variable coefficients, or the question says "variation of parameters": divide by the \(y''\) coefficient, then VoP (O4.4, O4.5).
    The CF is needed either way: for the clash check, or as \(y_1,y_2\) in VoP.
  5. Do: Initial conditions? Fit them last, to the full \(y_c+y_p\) (O4.3). The starting values belong to the whole motion, response included (section 4).

9Summary

  • Non-homogeneous linear equation: \(y''+a_1y'+a_2y=f(x)\) with \(f\ne0\). General solution = complementary function (solution with \(f=0\), contains the constants) + particular solution (any one solution).
  • Undetermined coefficients: trial of the same shape as \(f\): constant → constant; polynomial → full polynomial of the same degree; \(e^{ax}\) → \(Ae^{ax}\); \(\cos bx\) or \(\sin bx\) → \(A\cos bx+B\sin bx\); sums → sums.
  • Clash: if a trial group solves the homogeneous equation, multiply that group by \(x\) (simple root) or \(x^2\) (double root).
  • Resonance: forcing at a natural frequency; the response grows like \(x\sin x\); e.g. \(y''+y=\cos x\) has \(y_p=\tfrac12x\sin x\).
  • Initial conditions: fit to \(y_c+y_p\), never to \(y_c\) alone.
  • D-operator: write \(L(D)y=f\), apply the annihilator of \(f\), solve the homogeneous equation; the new solutions give the trial.
  • Variation of parameters: \(y_p=u_1y_1+u_2y_2\) with \(u_1'y_1+u_2'y_2=0\), \(u_1'y_1'+u_2'y_2'=f\); \(u_1'=-y_2f/W\), \(u_2'=y_1f/W\). Coefficient of \(y''\) must be 1: divide Cauchy–Euler equations by \(x^2\).
  • Linear equations are always solvable when the coefficients are constant (or Cauchy–Euler); nonlinear higher-order equations usually are not.

1. A student solves \(x^2y''-2xy'+2y=x^3e^{x}\) (\(x\gt0\)) by variation of parameters:

\(y_1=x,\ y_2=x^2,\ W=x^2,\ f=x^3e^x\)

\(u_1'=-\dfrac{x^2\cdot x^3e^x}{x^2}=-x^3e^x,\quad u_2'=\dfrac{x\cdot x^3e^x}{x^2}=x^2e^x\)

Hint

The integrations are correct. Look at the very first line, and at the form of the equation the VoP formulas come from.

Answer

The student used \(f=x^3e^x\), but the formulas need the coefficient of \(y''\) to be 1. Dividing by \(x^2\) gives \(f=xe^x\). Then \(u_1'=-xe^x\), \(u_2'=e^x\), and \(y_p=xe^x\) (Example 8). Substituting the student's answer back leaves an error of \(x^3(x^2-1)e^x\), not 0.

2. A student solves \(y''+3y'+2y=10\cos x\), \(y(0)=0\), \(y'(0)=0\):

\(y_c=C_1e^{-x}+C_2e^{-2x}\). \(y_c(0)=0\): \(C_1+C_2=0\). \(y_c'(0)=0\): \(-C_1-2C_2=0\). So \(C_1=C_2=0\).

\(y_p=\cos x+3\sin x\), so \(y=\cos x+3\sin x\).

Hint

Check the final answer: what is \(y(0)\)?

Answer

\(y(0)=1\ne0\) and \(y'(0)=3\ne0\). The initial conditions were applied to the CF alone. Apply them to \(y=C_1e^{-x}+C_2e^{-2x}+\cos x+3\sin x\): \(C_1+C_2+1=0\), \(-C_1-2C_2+3=0\), so \(C_1=-5\), \(C_2=4\), and \(y=-5e^{-x}+4e^{-2x}+\cos x+3\sin x\).

12Group work

1. Solve \(y''+4y=8\sin2x\), \(y(0)=0\), \(y'(0)=0\). Describe the motion for large \(x\).

Hint 1

CF: \(m=\pm2i\), so \(\cos2x,\ \sin2x\). The forcing \(\sin2x\) is in the CF: resonance.

Hint 2

Try \(y_p=x(A\cos2x+B\sin2x)\). A useful fact: for this trial, \(y_p''+4y_p=4(-A\sin2x+B\cos2x)\).

Answer

\(-4A=8\), \(B=0\), so \(y_p=-2x\cos2x\). With \(y=C_1\cos2x+C_2\sin2x-2x\cos2x\): \(y(0)=C_1=0\); \(y'(0)=2C_2-2=0\), \(C_2=1\).

\[ y=\sin2x-2x\cos2x. \]

For large \(x\) the term \(-2x\cos2x\) dominates: oscillations whose amplitude grows like \(2x\) (resonance).

2. Find the general solution of \(y''-2y'+y=\dfrac{e^{x}}{x}\), \(x\gt0\).

Hint 1

\(\frac{e^x}{x}\) is not in the trial table: use variation of parameters. CF: \(m=1\) (double), \(y_1=e^x\), \(y_2=xe^x\).

Hint 2

\(W=e^x(e^x+xe^x)-xe^x\cdot e^x=e^{2x}\).

Answer

\(u_1'=-\dfrac{xe^x\cdot e^x/x}{e^{2x}}=-1\), \(u_2'=\dfrac{e^x\cdot e^x/x}{e^{2x}}=\dfrac1x\); \(u_1=-x\), \(u_2=\ln x\). \(y_p=-xe^x+xe^x\ln x\); the \(-xe^x\) is a CF term.

\[ y=(C_1+C_2x)e^{x}+xe^{x}\ln x. \]

3. Solve \(y''-y=e^{x}\) with \(y(0)=0\), \(y'(0)=0\) twice: (a) by undetermined coefficients, (b) by variation of parameters. Compare.

Hint

(a) Roots \(\pm1\): \(e^x\) clashes once, so try \(Axe^x\). (b) \(y_1=e^x\), \(y_2=e^{-x}\), \(W=-2\).

Answer

(a) \(y_p=\frac12xe^x\). \(y=C_1e^x+C_2e^{-x}+\frac12xe^x\); conditions give \(C_1=-\frac14\), \(C_2=\frac14\).

(b) \(u_1'=\frac12\), \(u_2'=-\frac12e^{2x}\); \(u_1=\frac x2\), \(u_2=-\frac14e^{2x}\); \(y_p=\frac12xe^x-\frac14e^x\). Conditions give \(C_1=0\), \(C_2=\frac14\).

The particular solutions differ by \(-\frac14e^x\), a CF term, and the constants absorb it. Both give

\[ y=\tfrac12xe^{x}-\tfrac14e^{x}+\tfrac14e^{-x}. \]

13Concept checks

1. What trial would you use for \(y''-4y'+4y=e^{2x}\)? Find \(y_p\).

Answer

\((m-2)^2=0\): 2 is a double root, so \(Ax^2e^{2x}\). Substituting gives \(2A=1\): \(y_p=\frac12x^2e^{2x}\).

2. \(y_a\) and \(y_b\) both solve \(y''+y=x\). Which equations do \(y_a-y_b\), \(y_a+y_b\) and \(2y_a-y_b\) solve?

Answer

\(y_a-y_b\): \(y''+y=0\) (the CF equation). \(y_a+y_b\): \(y''+y=2x\). \(2y_a-y_b\): \(y''+y=2x-x=x\), the original equation.

3. For \(y''+9y=\cos\omega x\) (\(\omega\gt0\)), for which \(\omega\) does the trial need an extra factor \(x\)? What happens physically?

Answer

\(\omega=3\), the natural frequency (roots \(\pm3i\)). The forcing is in phase with the natural motion: resonance, amplitude grows like \(x\).

4. Why must the coefficient of \(y''\) be 1 before using \(u_1'y_1+u_2'y_2=0,\ u_1'y_1'+u_2'y_2'=f\)?

Answer

The second equation comes from substituting into \(y''+py'+qy=f\). For \(a(x)y''+\dots=g\), the same steps give \(a(x)(u_1'y_1'+u_2'y_2')=g\), i.e. the right side is \(g/a\), not \(g\).

5. How many arbitrary constants does the general solution of a third-order linear non-homogeneous ODE have, and which part contains them?

Answer

Three, all in the complementary function. The particular solution contains none.

6. Which method would you use for (a) \(y''+y=\tan x\); (b) \(y''+y=x^2+e^{-x}\); (c) \(x^2y''+xy'-y=\ln x\)?

Answer

(a) Variation of parameters (\(\tan x\) is not in the table). (b) Undetermined coefficients: \(Ax^2+Bx+C+Ee^{-x}\), no clash (roots \(\pm i\)). (c) Cauchy–Euler CF (\(x,\ x^{-1}\)), then variation of parameters after dividing by \(x^2\).
