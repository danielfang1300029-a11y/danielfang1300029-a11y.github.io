Homogeneous Linear ODEs

0Where this part sits

This part solves linear ODEs with 0 on the right-hand side: the equations of a mass on a spring left to move on its own. Guess a solution that keeps its shape when differentiated, and the calculus problem becomes a quadratic. The kind of roots then decides whether the spring creeps back, settles just in time, or wobbles while dying down.

1Hyperbolic functions ~4 min

What is this?

Take a quantity that grows steadily in proportion to itself, \(e^x\), and its mirror image \(e^{-x}\), which dies away at the same rate. Their average is cosh: a U shape, exactly the shape of a chain or cable hanging between two posts. Half their difference is sinh: an S shape through the origin. They are named after cos and sin because they obey almost the same rules, with a few signs flipped. In this part they are a tidy way to write the two building blocks \(e^x\) and \(e^{-x}\).

We begin with six functions built from \(e^x\) and \(e^{-x}\). They turn up naturally in this part: section 4 shows that \(\cosh x\) and \(\sinh x\) are exactly the solutions of \(y''-y=0\). They are called hyperbolic because the point \((\cosh t,\sinh t)\) moves along the hyperbola \(X^2-Y^2=1\), in the same way that \((\cos t,\sin t)\) moves along the circle \(X^2+Y^2=1\). Names are usually read "cosh", "shine" or "sinch", "tanh" ("than"), "sech", "cosech" (also written csch) and "coth".

Definition \[ \cosh x=\frac{e^{x}+e^{-x}}{2},\qquad \sinh x=\frac{e^{x}-e^{-x}}{2} \]

In words: hyperbolic cosine = average of \(e^x\) and \(e^{-x}\); hyperbolic sine = half the difference \(e^x\) minus \(e^{-x}\).

\[ \tanh x=\frac{\sinh x}{\cosh x},\qquad \operatorname{sech}x=\frac{1}{\cosh x} \] \[ \operatorname{cosech}x=\frac{1}{\sinh x},\qquad \coth x=\frac{1}{\tanh x} \]

In words: exactly the same pattern as trigonometry: tanh = sinh ÷ cosh, and sech, cosech, coth are the reciprocals of cosh, sinh, tanh.

Some facts you can read off the definitions (and the graph below): \(\cosh 0=1\), \(\sinh 0=0\); \(\cosh x\ge1\) for every \(x\) and \(\cosh\) is even (\(\cosh(-x)=\cosh x\)), like \(\cos\); \(\sinh\) is odd (\(\sinh(-x)=-\sinh x\)), like \(\sin\); \(\tanh x\) lies strictly between \(-1\) and \(1\). Unlike \(\cos\) and \(\sin\), they are not periodic: cosh and sinh grow like \(\tfrac12e^{|x|}\). \(\operatorname{cosech}x\) and \(\coth x\) are undefined at \(x=0\) because \(\sinh 0=0\).

The hyperbolic functions

"cosh and sinh": the dashed curves are \(\tfrac12e^{x}\) and \(\tfrac12e^{-x}\). cosh is their sum (a hanging-chain shape, the catenary), sinh their difference. For large \(x\) all three almost coincide because \(\tfrac12e^{-x}\to0\).

Identity and derivatives \[ \cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=1\quad\text{for all }x \]

In words: (hyperbolic cosine)² − (hyperbolic sine)² = 1 always; compare \(\cos^2x+\sin^2x=1\), with a minus sign instead of a plus.

Why these are true

Identity. Square and subtract:

\[ \cosh^2x=\frac{e^{2x}+2+e^{-2x}}{4} \] \[ \sinh^2x=\frac{e^{2x}-2+e^{-2x}}{4} \] \[ \cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=\frac{2-(-2)}{4}=1. \]

cosh and sinh. Using \(\frac{d}{dx}e^{-x}=-e^{-x}\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\cosh x=\frac{e^{x}-e^{-x}}{2}=\sinh x \] \[ \frac{d}{dx}\sinh x=\frac{e^{x}+e^{-x}}{2}=\cosh x \]

tanh by the quotient rule (Lecture 6), then the identity:

\[ \begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dx}\tanh x&=\frac{\cosh x\cosh x-\sinh x\sinh x}{\cosh^2x}\\&=\frac{1}{\cosh^2x}=\operatorname{sech}^2x.\end{aligned} \]

sech by the chain rule on \((\cosh x)^{-1}\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dx}\operatorname{sech}x&=-\frac{\sinh x}{\cosh^2x}=-\frac1{\cosh x}\cdot\frac{\sinh x}{\cosh x}\\&=-\operatorname{sech}x\tanh x.\end{aligned} \]

cosech the same way: \(\frac{d}{dx}(\sinh x)^{-1}=-\frac{\cosh x}{\sinh^2x}=-\operatorname{cosech}x\coth x\).

coth by the quotient rule:

\[ \begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\cosh x}{\sinh x}&=\frac{\sinh^2x-\cosh^2x}{\sinh^2x}\\&=\frac{-1}{\sinh^2x}=-\operatorname{cosech}^2x.\end{aligned} \]

You can now: write cosh, sinh, tanh, sech, cosech and coth in terms of \(e^x\) and \(e^{-x}\); prove \(\cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=1\) and the derivative table from the definitions; and remember that cosh differentiates to plus sinh.

2Linear independence and the Wronskian ~9 min

What is this?

Picture the spring again. To describe every way it can move, you want a small kit of basic motions that you can scale and add together. A motion and the same motion twice as big are not two different items in the kit: the second adds nothing new. Linearly independent means genuinely different: no mix of the functions, with amounts that are not all zero, cancels out to nothing at every point.

A second-order equation such as \(y''-y=0\) has infinitely many solutions: \(e^x\), \(2e^x\), \(-7e^x\), \(e^{-x}\), \(3e^x-e^{-x}\), … To write down all of them we need a small set of "building blocks" that are genuinely different from each other. "Genuinely different" is made precise by linear independence.

Definition

A finite set of functions \(\{u_1,\dots,u_n\}\) is linearly dependent on an interval \(I\) if and only if there exist constants \(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n\) (numbers, called scalars), not all zero, such that

\[ \alpha_1u_1(x)+\alpha_2u_2(x)+\dots+\alpha_nu_n(x)=0 \]

is satisfied identically on \(I\), i.e. for every \(x\) in \(I\), not just for some \(x\). If this can only happen when all the \(\alpha\)'s are zero, the set is linearly independent on \(I\).

In words: dependent = some mixture of the functions (with at least one non-zero weight) cancels to zero everywhere, so at least one function is a combination of the others and adds nothing new; independent = the only way to mix them into the zero function is to use zero of each.

A linear combination of \(u_1,\dots,u_n\) means an expression \(\alpha_1u_1+\dots+\alpha_nu_n\) with constant weights. For just two functions the definition says something very simple: \(u\) and \(v\) are dependent exactly when one is a constant multiple of the other (if \(\alpha u+\beta v=0\) with \(\beta\ne0\), then \(v=-\tfrac{\alpha}{\beta}u\)).

Example

We do not want to count \(y=e^x\) and \(y=2e^x\) as two different solutions of \(y''-y=0\): the second is just twice the first. The "truly distinct" (linearly independent) solutions are \(e^x\) and \(e^{-x}\).

\(e^x\) and \(2e^x\) are linearly DEPENDENT: take \(\alpha=2\), \(\beta=-1\); then \(2\cdot e^x+(-1)\cdot2e^x=0\) for all \(x\), and the weights are not both zero.

\(e^x\) and \(e^{-x}\) are linearly INDEPENDENT: we cannot find constants \(\alpha,\beta\), not both zero, with

\[ \alpha e^{x}+\beta e^{-x}=0\quad\text{for ALL }x. \]

Proof: put \(x=0\): \(\alpha+\beta=0\). Differentiate the identity (allowed, since it holds for all \(x\)) and put \(x=0\): \(\alpha-\beta=0\). Together \(\alpha=\beta=0\). So the only possibility for the equation to hold for all \(x\) is \(\alpha=\beta=0\).One value of \(x\) gives only one equation for two unknowns; an identity that holds for every \(x\) stays true when differentiated, which gives the second equation.

Both are solutions of \(y''-y=0\): \((e^x)''=e^x\) and \((e^{-x})''=e^{-x}\), so \(y''-y=0\) in each case.

What is this? (the Wronskian)

The Wronskian is a quick test of whether one graph is just a stretched copy of the other. If \(v\) is always the same multiple of \(u\), their ratio \(v/u\) never changes. Apart from a factor \(u^2\), the Wronskian is exactly the rate at which that ratio changes. So "zero everywhere" points to a stretched copy, and "not zero everywhere" means genuinely different. The Going further box below says when the "zero everywhere" direction is safe: always, for two solutions of the same equation.

The Wronskian

For two functions \(u(x)\), \(v(x)\), the Wronskian is

\[ W(u,v)(x)=u(x)\,v'(x)-v(x)\,u'(x). \]

In words: Wronskian = first function × derivative of the second − second function × derivative of the first.

  • If \(W\) is identically zero (zero for ALL \(x\)), the two functions are linearly dependent.
  • If \(W\) is not zero everywhere, the two functions are linearly independent.

Why it works, informally: if \(v=ku\) then \(uv'-vu'=u(ku')-(ku)u'=0\). Conversely, where \(u\ne0\), the quotient rule gives \(\left(\dfrac vu\right)'=\dfrac{uv'-vu'}{u^2}=\dfrac{W}{u^2}\); so \(W=0\) everywhere means \(v/u\) has zero derivative, i.e. \(v/u\) is a constant (Lecture 8, consequence of the MVT).

Example: Wronskians of these pairs \[ W(e^x,2e^x)=e^x\cdot2e^x-2e^x\cdot e^x=0 \]

so \(e^x,2e^x\) are dependent;

\[ W(e^x,e^{-x})=e^x(-e^{-x})-e^{-x}e^{x}=-2\ne0 \]

so \(e^x,e^{-x}\) are independent.

Only the first pair has \(W\equiv0\) (the green line lies on the \(x\)-axis): \(2e^x\) is just a stretched copy of \(e^x\). The other pairs are independent.

Three or more functions. For \(n\) functions the Wronskian is a "determinant" of order \(n\), built from the functions and their first \(n-1\) derivatives. The rule is the same: identically zero means dependent (for solutions of the same linear ODE), not identically zero means independent.

Going further

Determinants. A determinant is a number calculated from a square table (matrix) of entries. For a 2 × 2 table, \(\begin{vmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{vmatrix}=ad-bc\), so the two-function Wronskian is \(\begin{vmatrix}u&v\\u'&v'\end{vmatrix}=uv'-vu'\). For three functions,

\[ W(u,v,w)=\begin{vmatrix}u&v&w\\u'&v'&w'\\u''&v''&w''\end{vmatrix}, \]

where a 3 × 3 determinant is expanded along the top row:

\[ \begin{aligned}\begin{vmatrix}a&b&c\\d&e&f\\g&h&i\end{vmatrix}&=a(ei-fh)-b(di-fg)\\&\quad+c(dh-eg).\end{aligned} \]

Example: \(W(1,e^x,e^{-x})=1\cdot(e^x e^{-x}-(-e^{-x})e^x)-0+0=2\ne0\), so \(1,e^x,e^{-x}\) are independent.

A caution about the "identically zero ⇒ dependent" direction. For arbitrary functions it can fail: \(u=x^2\) and \(v=x|x|\) have \(W=0\) for every \(x\), yet on \(-1\lt x\lt 1\) neither is a constant multiple of the other. The rule is completely safe when \(u,v\) are both solutions of the same linear second-order ODE (with continuous coefficients, leading coefficient 1): then \(W\) is either zero at every point or zero at no point (Abel's theorem), and \(W\equiv0\) exactly when they are dependent. That is the only situation this course uses it in.

What is this? (the general solution)

A spring's motion is fixed by two pieces of starting information: where you let go, and how fast it is moving then. With two genuinely different basic motions, choosing their two amounts lets you match any such start. With two copies of the same motion you would really have only one amount to choose, and most starts could not be matched.

Why we care: the general solution

For a linear homogeneous ODE of order \(n\), if \(y_1,\dots,y_n\) are \(n\) linearly independent solutions, the general solution (the formula containing every solution) is

\[ y=C_1y_1+C_2y_2+\dots+C_ny_n . \]

In words: every solution = a mixture of \(n\) genuinely different building-block solutions, one arbitrary constant per block; the order of the equation tells you how many blocks you need.

Reading the formula
\(y_1,\dots,y_n\)
the building blocks: \(n\) genuinely different solutions.
\(C_1,\dots,C_n\)
how much of each block you use: one free amount per block.
\(n\)
the order. An \(n\)-th order equation needs \(n\) starting values (\(y\), \(y'\), …, \(y^{(n-1)}\) at the start), so it needs \(n\) amounts to choose.
the sum
mixing solutions gives a solution (superposition, section 3), and every solution is such a mix.

This part uses this fact throughout without stating it as a theorem; its proof is in a Going further box. The easy half, that a mixture of solutions is again a solution (the principle of superposition), is shown in section 3.

You can now: test two functions for linear dependence from the definition or with the Wronskian \(W=uv'-vu'\); explain why a single zero of \(W\) proves nothing; and say how many independent solutions an \(n\)-th order linear homogeneous equation needs.

3Constant coefficients: the characteristic equation ~5 min

What is this?

Look for a function whose derivatives are just scaled copies of itself. An exponential is exactly that: differentiate \(e^{\lambda x}\) and you get the same curve stretched by \(\lambda\); differentiate again and it is stretched by \(\lambda\) once more. Put such a guess into the equation and every term is the same curve times a number, so the curve cancels and only the numbers remain: an ordinary quadratic. For the spring, it is like asking for a motion whose speed and acceleration are always in fixed proportion to the displacement itself.

The equation

A homogeneous second-order linear equation with constant coefficients has the form

\[ y''+a_1y'+a_2y=0,\qquad a_1,a_2\ \text{constants}. \]

In words: (second derivative) + (constant) × (first derivative) + (constant) × (the function) = 0.

  • Second order: the highest derivative is \(y''\).
  • Linear: \(y\), \(y'\), \(y''\) appear only to the first power, are never multiplied together, and are not inside other functions (no \(y^2\), \(yy'\), \(\sin y\), …). In fact, a linear second-order equation can always be written as \(y''+p(x)y'+q(x)y=r(x)\); here \(p,q\) are constants.
  • Homogeneous: the right-hand side, i.e. every term other than those containing \(y''\), \(y'\), \(y\), must be zero. (An equation like \(y''+y=\cos x\) is non-homogeneous; that is Part O4.)
  • Constant coefficients: the numbers multiplying \(y'\) and \(y\) do not depend on \(x\). (If the given equation is \(ay''+by'+cy=0\) with \(a\ne0\), divide by \(a\) first, or simply use \(a\lambda^2+b\lambda+c=0\) below: the roots are the same.)
What is this? (superposition)

Two motions of the spring that each obey the law can be added, and the sum obeys the law too. This works because every term of the equation is a plain multiple of \(y\) or one of its derivatives: doubling the motion doubles every term, adding two motions adds the terms, and zero plus zero is still zero. It fails as soon as there is a \(y^2\) term or a non-zero right-hand side.

Superposition. If \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) are solutions, so is \(y=C_1y_1+C_2y_2\), because derivatives respect sums and constant multiples:

\[ \begin{aligned} y''+a_1y'+a_2y&=C_1(y_1''+a_1y_1'+a_2y_1)\\&\quad+C_2(y_2''+a_1y_2'+a_2y_2)\\&=C_1\cdot0+C_2\cdot0=0. \end{aligned} \]

In words: mixing solutions of a linear homogeneous equation gives another solution. This only works because the equation is linear and the right side is 0.

The idea: differentiating \(e^{x}\) gives \(e^{x}\) back, and differentiating \(e^{\lambda x}\) gives the same function times the number \(\lambda\). So if we try an exponential, every term of the equation becomes "a number × the same exponential", and the exponential can be cancelled.

Characteristic equation

Try \(y=e^{\lambda x}\) (\(\lambda\) is the Greek letter lambda, a constant to be found). Then \(y'=\lambda e^{\lambda x}\) and \(y''=\lambda^2e^{\lambda x}\), so

\[ y''+a_1y'+a_2y=e^{\lambda x}\left(\lambda^2+a_1\lambda+a_2\right)=0. \]

Since \(e^{\lambda x}\) is never zero, this holds exactly when

\[ \lambda^2+a_1\lambda+a_2=0. \]

In words: replace \(y''\) by \(\lambda^2\), \(y'\) by \(\lambda\), \(y\) by 1; the ODE turns into an algebraic equation (an ordinary quadratic) for \(\lambda\). Each root \(\lambda\) gives a solution \(e^{\lambda x}\).

Reading the formula
\(e^{\lambda x}\)
the shape-keeping guess. It is never zero, so it can be cancelled.
\(\lambda^2\)
what two differentiations do to the guess: stretch it by \(\lambda\) twice.
\(a_1\lambda\)
one differentiation, times the coefficient of \(y'\).
\(a_2\)
the \(y\) term itself, not differentiated at all.
bracket \(=0\)
the guess works for exactly those \(\lambda\) that make the bracket vanish: the roots.

Because the equation is linear, there is no need for a constant in front of the exponential: trying \(y=Ce^{\lambda x}\) gives \(Ce^{\lambda x}(\lambda^2+a_1\lambda+a_2)=0\) and the \(C\) cancels too; constants are put back at the end by superposition. The ODE has turned into an algebraic equation, and that is the whole point: a calculus problem becomes a quadratic you solved in HKDSE.

The method for \(y''+a_1y'+a_2y=0\):

  1. Do: Write the characteristic equation: replace \(y''\) by \(\lambda^2\), \(y'\) by \(\lambda\), \(y\) by 1 (for \(ay''+by'+cy=0\), use \(a\lambda^2+b\lambda+c=0\)). This is what the shape-keeping guess turns each term into, once the common factor \(e^{\lambda x}\) is cancelled.
  2. Do: Solve the quadratic, looking at its discriminant. Each root is a stretch factor for which the guess really is a solution; the discriminant says what kind of roots there are.
  3. Do: Write the general solution from the matching case: distinct real or repeated roots (section 4), complex roots (section 6). A second-order equation needs two genuinely different building blocks (section 2), and how to get them depends on the case.
  4. Do: If initial conditions are given, use them last to find the constants. By superposition the constants can be chosen freely, so they can be fitted to the starting value and slope.

A quadratic \(\lambda^2+a_1\lambda+a_2=0\) has discriminant \(\Delta=a_1^2-4a_2\), giving three possibilities: \(\Delta\gt0\) two distinct real roots; \(\Delta=0\) a repeated real root; \(\Delta\lt0\) two complex roots (section 6).

You can now: recognise a second-order linear homogeneous equation with constant coefficients; explain why the trial \(e^{\lambda x}\) turns it into a quadratic; write down its characteristic equation; and say which of the three cases the discriminant gives.

4Distinct and repeated real roots ~4 min

What is this?

Real roots mean the guess works with a real stretch factor, so the building blocks are plain exponentials: each one steadily grows (positive root) or dies away (negative root), with no wobble. For a spring with heavy damping both roots are negative, and the mass creeps back to rest without swinging: one part fades quickly, the other slowly. A repeated root is the borderline case, where the two decay rates have merged into one.

Case (1): distinct real roots \(m\ne n\) \[ y=C_1e^{mx}+C_2e^{nx} \]

In words: two different roots give two different exponentials; the general solution is any mixture of them.

They are independent: \(W(e^{mx},e^{nx})=e^{mx}\,ne^{nx}-e^{nx}\,me^{mx}=(n-m)e^{(m+n)x}\ne0\) because \(m\ne n\).

Example

Solve \(y''-y'-6y=0\).

Characteristic equation: \(\lambda^2-\lambda-6=0\), i.e. \((\lambda+2)(\lambda-3)=0\), roots \(\lambda=-2,\,3\). General solution

\[ y(x)=C_1e^{-2x}+C_2e^{3x}. \]

Check one piece: \(y=e^{3x}\) gives \(9e^{3x}-3e^{3x}-6e^{3x}=0\). ✓

Example (from section 2): \(y''-y=0\) and hyperbolic functions

\(\lambda^2-1=0\), \(\lambda=\pm1\), so \(y=C_1e^{x}+C_2e^{-x}\). Since \(\cosh x\) and \(\sinh x\) are mixtures of \(e^x,e^{-x}\) (and \(W(\cosh,\sinh)=\cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=1\ne0\)), the same general solution can be written

\[ y=A\cosh x+B\sinh x, \] \[ C_1=\tfrac{A+B}{2},\quad C_2=\tfrac{A-B}{2}. \]

This form is convenient with conditions at \(x=0\), because \(\cosh0=1\), \(\sinh0=0\). For example \(y''-4y=0\), \(y(0)=3\), \(y'(0)=4\): \(y=A\cosh2x+B\sinh2x\), \(y(0)=A=3\), \(y'(0)=2B=4\Rightarrow B=2\), so

\[ y=3\cosh2x+2\sinh2x=\tfrac52e^{2x}+\tfrac12e^{-2x}. \]
What is this? (repeated root)

When the two roots merge there is only one exponential, but a second-order equation still needs two genuinely different blocks. The missing one is the same exponential multiplied by \(x\): the same fading curve with a factor that grows slowly in a straight line. For the spring this is critical damping, where the mass gets back to rest as quickly as it can without overshooting.

Case (2): repeated root \(m,m\) \[ y=C_1e^{mx}+C_2\,x\,e^{mx}=(C_1+C_2x)e^{mx} \]

In words: a double root gives only one exponential, so the second building block is the same exponential multiplied by \(x\).

Reading the formula
\(e^{mx}\)
the one exponential that the double root gives.
\(C_1\)
how much of it you use; it is the starting value, \(y(0)=C_1\).
\(C_2x\)
the second block: the same exponential with a slowly growing factor \(x\). It lets you match the starting slope as well, \(y'(0)=mC_1+C_2\).

Why the extra \(x\)? Proof by reduction of order (Part O2): put \(y=u\,e^{mx}\). By the product rule

\[ y'=(u'+mu)e^{mx}, \] \[ y''=(u''+2mu'+m^2u)e^{mx}. \]

Substituting into \(y''+a_1y'+a_2y=0\) and dividing by \(e^{mx}\):

\[ u''+(2m+a_1)u'+(m^2+a_1m+a_2)u=0. \]

Because \(m\) is a root, \(m^2+a_1m+a_2=0\). Because it is a double root, the sum of the two roots, \(m+m\), equals \(-a_1\) (sum of roots of \(\lambda^2+a_1\lambda+a_2\) is \(-a_1\), HKDSE Core), so \(2m+a_1=0\). What is left is \(u''=0\), so \(u=C_1+C_2x\) and \(y=(C_1+C_2x)e^{mx}\).A double root is a root that sits exactly at the vertex of the parabola \(\lambda^2+a_1\lambda+a_2\), which is at \(\lambda=-a_1/2\). Being a root removes the \(u\) term; being at the vertex removes the \(u'\) term.

Example

Solve \(y''-2y'+y=0\).

\(\lambda^2-2\lambda+1=(\lambda-1)^2=0\), roots \(\lambda=1,1\). General solution

\[ y(x)=C_1e^{x}+C_2\,x\,e^{x}. \]

Check \(y=xe^x\): \(y'=(1+x)e^x\), \(y''=(2+x)e^x\); \((2+x)-2(1+x)+x=0\). ✓

Trap

Writing \(y=C_1e^{x}+C_2e^{x}\) for a repeated root is wrong: that is just \((C_1+C_2)e^x\), really one constant, so it cannot match two initial conditions. You need the factor \(x\).

You can now: solve \(y''+a_1y'+a_2y=0\) when the roots are real and distinct or repeated; rewrite \(C_1e^{kx}+C_2e^{-kx}\) as \(A\cosh kx+B\sinh kx\) and use it with conditions at 0; and explain where the extra factor \(x\) comes from.

5Complex numbers and Euler's formula ~5 min

What is this?

A complex number is a point on a flat map: the real part says how far east, the imaginary part how far north. Ordinary real numbers live on the east–west line. Multiplying by \(i\) turns any point a quarter turn anticlockwise about the origin, so multiplying by \(i\) twice turns it half way round, from 1 to \(-1\): that is why \(i^2=-1\).

When the discriminant is negative, as in \(\lambda^2-4\lambda+5=0\) (\(\Delta=16-20=-4\)), HKDSE Core says "no real roots". To keep going we need complex numbers. This background is not in HKDSE Core, so here it is from scratch.

Definition: complex numbers

Introduce a new number \(i\) with \(i^2=-1\) (the imaginary unit). A complex number is \(z=a+bi\) with \(a,b\) real; \(a\) is its real part and \(b\) its imaginary part. Complex numbers are added and multiplied like ordinary algebra, replacing \(i^2\) by \(-1\) whenever it appears:

\[ (a+bi)(c+di)=(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i. \]

The complex conjugate of \(a+bi\) is \(a-bi\) (flip the sign of the imaginary part). A complex number can be drawn as the point \((a,b)\) in a plane, the complex plane: horizontal axis = real part, vertical axis = imaginary part.

In words: a complex number is a pair (real part, imaginary part); \(i\) is a number whose square is \(-1\).

Example: a quadratic with negative discriminant

Since \(\sqrt{-4}=\sqrt{4}\sqrt{-1}=2i\), the quadratic formula still works:

\[ \lambda=\frac{4\pm\sqrt{-4}}{2}=\frac{4\pm2i}{2}=2\pm i. \]

Check \(\lambda=2+i\): \((2+i)^2=4+4i+i^2=3+4i\), and \(3+4i-4(2+i)+5=0\). ✓

In general, if \(\Delta=a_1^2-4a_2\lt0\) the roots are \(a\pm bi\) with \(a=-\dfrac{a_1}{2}\) and \(b=\dfrac{\sqrt{4a_2-a_1^2}}{2}\).

Notice the two roots are conjugates of each other. That always happens when the quadratic has real coefficients: the \(\pm\) in front of \(\sqrt{\Delta}=\sqrt{|\Delta|}\,i\) produces \(a+bi\) and \(a-bi\).

What is this? (Euler's formula)

Imagine a point that moves so that its velocity is always its own position arrow turned a quarter turn. It never moves towards or away from the centre, so it goes round a circle at a steady speed. The rule that the derivative of \(e^{i\theta}\) is \(i\) times itself says exactly this, so \(e^{i\theta}\) runs round the circle of radius 1, with cosine as its east coordinate and sine as its north coordinate. This is where the oscillations in the spring's motion come from.

Euler's formula \[ e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta \qquad(\theta\ \text{in radians}) \]

In words: the exponential of "\(i\) times an angle" is the point on the unit circle at that angle: its real part is the cosine and its imaginary part is the sine.

What does \(e\) to an imaginary power even mean? We define it so that the usual rules keep working: \(e^{u+v}=e^ue^v\), and \(\frac{d}{d\theta}e^{k\theta}=ke^{k\theta}\) also for \(k=i\). Here is a short reason why the definition must be \(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\):

Going further: why Euler's formula holds

Let \(f(\theta)=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\). Then \(f'(\theta)=-\sin\theta+i\cos\theta=i(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=i\,f(\theta)\) (use \(i\cdot i=-1\)), and \(f(0)=1\). The function \(e^{i\theta}\) satisfies exactly the same two facts: derivative = \(i\) × itself, value 1 at 0. A first-order ODE with an initial condition has only one solution, so \(e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\).

(Another route uses the power series \(e^z=1+z+\frac{z^2}{2!}+\dots\): putting \(z=i\theta\) and grouping real and imaginary terms gives the series for \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\).) A famous special case: \(\theta=\pi\) gives \(e^{i\pi}=-1\).

As θ increases, \(e^{i\theta}\) runs round the circle of radius 1. That is why complex exponentials produce oscillations (cos and sin) in the solutions below.

Combining with the ordinary rule \(e^{u+v}=e^ue^v\):

\[ e^{(a+bi)x}=e^{ax}e^{ibx}=e^{ax}\big(\cos bx+i\sin bx\big). \]

In words: the real part \(a\) of the exponent controls growth or decay (\(e^{ax}\)); the imaginary part \(b\) controls how fast it oscillates (\(\cos bx\), \(\sin bx\)).

6Case (3): complex roots ~3 min

What is this?

This is the spring with light damping: the mass swings through the rest position, and each swing is smaller than the one before. The two numbers in the roots share the job. The real part \(a\) sets how fast the swings die down (for a spring it is negative); the imaginary part \(b\) sets how fast it wobbles. The cosine and sine are the same wobble a quarter cycle apart, so between them they can match any starting position and starting speed.

In practice the characteristic equation almost always has real coefficients (\(a_1,a_2\) are real numbers), so complex roots come in a conjugate pair \(a+bi\) and \(a-bi\) (\(b\ne0\)).

Case (3): complex roots \(a\pm bi\) \[ y=C_1e^{(a+bi)x}+C_2e^{(a-bi)x} \]

or, equivalently and much more usefully,

\[ y=e^{ax}\big[A\cos(bx)+B\sin(bx)\big]. \]

In words: real part \(a\) of the roots → the factor \(e^{ax}\) (growth or decay); imaginary part \(b\) → the angular frequency of the \(\cos\) and \(\sin\). Use \(b\), not \(\pm b\): both signs are already covered.

Reading the formula
\(e^{ax}\)
the envelope: the size of the swings, shrinking if \(a\lt0\), growing if \(a\gt0\), steady if \(a=0\).
\(\cos bx,\ \sin bx\)
the wobble: \(b\) radians per unit of \(x\), so one full swing every \(2\pi/b\).
\(A\)
the starting value, \(y(0)=A\).
\(B\)
together with \(A\), the starting slope: \(y'(0)=aA+bB\).

From one form to the other. To simplify the algebra we use Euler's formula on each term, with \(\cos(-bx)=\cos bx\) and \(\sin(-bx)=-\sin bx\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y&=C_1e^{(a+bi)x}+C_2e^{(a-bi)x}\\ &=e^{ax}\big[C_1(\cos bx+i\sin bx)\\&\qquad+C_2(\cos bx-i\sin bx)\big]\\ &=e^{ax}\big[(C_1+C_2)\cos bx\\&\qquad+i(C_1-C_2)\sin bx\big]. \end{aligned} \]

To relate \(A\), \(B\) to the complex constants \(C_1\), \(C_2\), set

\[ A=C_1+C_2,\qquad B=i(C_1-C_2) \]

gives \(y=e^{ax}[A\cos bx+B\sin bx]\).

In words: the two forms are the same family of functions, just with the constants renamed.

The constants \(C_1,C_2\) are allowed to be complex. For a real solution (the only kind that describes a real spring or circuit) we choose \(C_2\) to be the conjugate of \(C_1\); then \(A\) and \(B\) come out as real numbers.If \(C_1=p+qi\) and \(C_2=p-qi\), then \(A=C_1+C_2=2p\) and \(B=i(C_1-C_2)=i\cdot2qi=-2q\), both real. In practice you skip \(C_1,C_2\) altogether and write the real form directly, with \(A,B\) real.

Solve \(y''-4y'+5y=0\).

\[ y=C_1e^{(2+i)x}+C_2e^{(2-i)x} \]

or

\[ y=e^{2x}\big[A\cos x+B\sin x\big]. \]

\(a\lt0\): oscillations die away; \(a=0\): steady oscillation; \(a\gt0\): oscillations grow. \(b\) sets how many wiggles fit in a given length (period \(2\pi/b\)).

7Higher-order equations ~4 min

What is this?

A higher-order equation needs more starting information (starting value, starting slope, and more), so it needs more building blocks: one for each root of the characteristic polynomial. Nothing else is new. Each real root still gives an exponential, each complex pair still gives a growing or dying wobble, and each extra copy of a repeated root still earns one more factor \(x\).

Extending to higher-order equations is conceptually straightforward. For

\[ y^{(n)}+a_1y^{(n-1)}+\dots+a_{n-1}y'+a_ny=0 \]

(\(y^{(n)}\) means the \(n\)-th derivative), trying \(y=e^{\lambda x}\) gives the characteristic equation

\[ \lambda^n+a_1\lambda^{n-1}+\dots+a_{n-1}\lambda+a_n=0. \]

In words: each \(k\)-th derivative becomes \(\lambda^k\); an \(n\)-th order ODE gives a degree-\(n\) polynomial, whose \(n\) roots (counted with repeats, complex allowed) give the \(n\) building blocks.

The method:

  1. Do: Write the characteristic polynomial: each \(y^{(k)}\) becomes \(\lambda^k\). The shape-keeping guess turns every derivative into a power of its stretch factor.
  2. Do: Factorise the polynomial using HKDSE tools (taking out common factors, the factor theorem: if \(p(r)=0\) then \(\lambda-r\) is a factor, then long division) and translate each root with the table below. Each factor gives a root, and each root is a stretch factor for which the guess works.
  3. Do: List every root with its repeats before writing the answer. A repeated root gives fewer different exponentials, so the extra factors \(x\) supply the missing blocks.
  4. Do: Check that the number of constants equals the order. An \(n\)-th order equation needs \(n\) genuinely different blocks (section 2); a missing constant usually means a forgotten root 0 or a forgotten repeat.

If a root (real or a complex pair) is repeated \(k\) times, multiply its building blocks by \(1,x,x^2,\dots,x^{k-1}\). E.g. roots \(3,3,3\) give \((C_1+C_2x+C_3x^2)e^{3x}\); a root \(0\) repeated twice gives \(e^{0x}=1\) and \(x\), i.e. \(C_1+C_2x\). Also beyond: every degree-\(n\) polynomial has exactly \(n\) complex roots counted with repetition (the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra), which is why an \(n\)-th order equation always has \(n\) building blocks.

Example

Solve \(y^{(5)}-7y^{(3)}+12y'=0\).

Characteristic equation \(\lambda^5-7\lambda^3+12\lambda=0\). Factorise:

\[ \begin{aligned}\lambda^5-7\lambda^3+12\lambda&=\lambda(\lambda^4-7\lambda^2+12)\\&=\lambda(\lambda^2-3)(\lambda^2-4),\end{aligned} \]

(treat \(\lambda^4-7\lambda^2+12\) as a quadratic in \(\lambda^2\)), with roots \(\lambda=0,\ 2,\ -2,\ \sqrt3,\ -\sqrt3\). All five are real and different, so

\[ \begin{aligned}y(x)=C_1&+C_2e^{2x}+C_3e^{-2x}\\&+C_4e^{\sqrt3x}+C_5e^{-\sqrt3x}.\end{aligned} \]

The root \(\lambda=0\) gives \(e^{0x}=1\), i.e. the constant \(C_1\). Five constants for a fifth-order equation.

Example: repeated complex roots

A special case for higher-order equations: solve

\[ y''''-8y'''+26y''-40y'+25y=0. \]

The characteristic equation is

\[ \lambda^4-8\lambda^3+26\lambda^2-40\lambda+25=0. \]

This is a perfect square: \((\lambda^2-4\lambda+5)^2=\lambda^4-8\lambda^3+26\lambda^2-40\lambda+25\) (expand to check: \(\lambda^2\) coefficient \(16+5+5=26\)). The quadratic \(\lambda^2-4\lambda+5\) has roots \(2\pm i\) (section 5), so the quartic has roots \(2+i,\,2+i,\,2-i,\,2-i\). The pair \(2\pm i\) is repeated, so

\[ \begin{aligned}y=e^{2x}\big\{&A\cos x+B\sin x\\&+Cx\cos x+Dx\sin x\big\}.\end{aligned} \]

In words: exactly like a repeated real root: the second copy of the pair gets an extra factor \(x\).

You can now: factorise a cubic or quartic characteristic polynomial; translate each root, including 0 and repeated or complex roots, into building blocks; and check that there is one constant per order.

9The mass–spring oscillator ~12 min

What is this?

Hang a mass on a spring, pull it down and let go. The spring pulls it back, its momentum carries it past the rest position, and friction (air, oil, a shock absorber) takes away some energy on every swing. How much friction there is decides the story. A little, and it wobbles while dying down. A lot, and it creeps back slowly without ever passing the rest position. Exactly the critical amount, and it gets back as fast as it can without overshooting. These are the three root cases of sections 4 and 6.

The set-up

A particle attached to a spring is stretched beyond the equilibrium position and released. The picture below shows three stages.

Mechanical mass–spring system

(a) The spring alone, at its natural length. (b) A mass is hung on it; the spring stretches by \(s_0\) and the system rests at the static equilibrium position, labelled \(y=0\). (c) The system in motion: \(y\) is the displacement of the mass from equilibrium (positive downwards).

Physics needed (HKDSE Physics, not Maths, so here in brief): Hooke's law says a spring stretched by a distance \(s\) pulls back with force \(ks\), where \(k\) (the spring constant, stiffness) is a positive constant. Newton's second law says mass × acceleration = total force, and acceleration is the second derivative of position, \(y''\).

  • In (b) the mass is at rest, so the spring's upward pull balances gravity: \(ks_0=mg\).
  • In (c) the spring is stretched by \(s_0+y\), so the total downward force is \(mg-k(s_0+y)=-ky\) (the \(mg\) and \(ks_0\) cancel). Newton's law gives \(my''=-ky\), i.e. \(my''+ky=0\), the "vibrating mass" equation of the gallery. Measuring from equilibrium makes gravity disappear from the equation.

Free oscillation with damping

What is this? (damping)

Damping is a brake that pushes against the motion, harder the faster the mass moves, like a car's shock absorber: a piston forced through oil. It always takes energy out and never puts any in, so the motion can only die down.

A particle of mass \(m\) is attached to a spring with constant \(k\). Damping (a force that resists motion, e.g. friction or air or oil resistance) is proportional to the speed, and there is no external forcing (no outside push), so the oscillation is called free. Writing the displacement as \(x(t)\), Newton's law of motion becomes

Damped free oscillator \[ mx''+cx'+kx=0 \]

In words: mass × acceleration + damping constant × velocity + spring constant × displacement = 0, i.e. the spring force \(-kx\) and the damping force \(-cx'\) together produce the acceleration.

Here \(m\gt0\), \(k\gt0\), and the damping constant \(c\ge0\).

Reading the formula
\(mx''\)
mass × acceleration: the mass's reluctance to change speed.
\(cx'\)
the brake: proportional to the speed, and pushing against it.
\(kx\)

\(=0\)
no outside push: the motion is free, driven only by the starting stretch and speed.

This is a constant-coefficient equation in the variable \(t\). Characteristic equation \(m\lambda^2+c\lambda+k=0\), with roots

\[ \lambda=\frac{-c\pm\sqrt{c^2-4mk}}{2m}, \]

and the character of the motion depends on the nature of the roots. Rewrite this with the critical damping constant \(c_{cr}\):

Three kinds of damping \[ \lambda=\frac{-c\pm\sqrt{c^2-c_{cr}^2}}{2m},\qquad c_{cr}^2=4mk \]

In words: critical damping \(c_{cr}=2\sqrt{mk}\) is the amount of damping that makes the discriminant exactly zero.

Reading the formula
\(-\dfrac{c}{2m}\)
the part both roots share: how fast the motion dies down, as long as the square root is imaginary or zero.
\(c^2-c_{cr}^2\)

\(c_{cr}=2\sqrt{mk}\)

  1. Underdamped vibration: \(c\lt c_{cr}\). Negative discriminant: complex roots \(-\dfrac{c}{2m}\pm i\,\dfrac{\sqrt{c_{cr}^2-c^2}}{2m}\).
  2. Critically damped vibration: \(c=c_{cr}\). Repeated real root \(\lambda=-\dfrac{c}{2m}\).
  3. Overdamped vibration: \(c\gt c_{cr}\). Two distinct real roots, both negative (since \(\sqrt{c^2-c_{cr}^2}\lt c\)).

What each looks like. Physically: overdamped and critically damped oscillators give exponentially decaying solutions, the motion dies down without swinging back and forth; the underdamped oscillator gives a product of exponential and trigonometric functions, slowly decaying oscillations. Mathematically: overdamped = two distinct real roots, dying exponentials; critically damped = two equal real roots, \(e^{-\gamma t}\) and \(te^{-\gamma t}\) behaviours; underdamped = complex conjugate roots, \(e^{-\gamma t}\cos\omega_d t\) and \(e^{-\gamma t}\sin\omega_d t\).

Here \(\gamma=\dfrac{c}{2m}\) is the decay rate and \(\omega_d=\dfrac{\sqrt{c_{cr}^2-c^2}}{2m}\) the frequency of the damped oscillation.

Dashpots and RLC circuits

What is this? (RLC circuits)

An electric circuit with a coil, a resistor and a capacitor behaves like the spring. The coil resists changes in current, as a mass resists changes in speed; the resistor turns energy into heat, like the brake; the capacitor stores charge and pushes back, like the spring. So the same kind of equation, and the same three cases, describe the circuit.

Other examples come from engineering. In mechanical engineering, damping is provided by a dashpot: a piston moving in a cylinder of oil, which resists with a force proportional to speed (the \(cx'\) term). In electrical engineering, RLC circuits contain an \(R\) = resistor, \(L\) = inductor (a coil) and \(C\) = capacitor. Both systems are drawn here.

Mass–spring–dashpot system and RLC circuit

Left: spring (constant \(k\)) holding a mass \(m\), with a dashpot (damping constant \(c\)) underneath. Right: a loop containing a capacitor \(C\), resistor \(R\), inductor \(L\) and a voltage source \(E(t)\).

\[ LI''+RI'+\tfrac1C I=0 \]

In words: inductance plays the role of mass, resistance the role of damping, and 1/capacitance the role of spring stiffness; current behaves like displacement. So the same three cases (over-, critically, under-damped) occur, with critical resistance \(R^2=4L/C\).

For small angles (in radians) \(\sin\theta\approx\theta\) (Lecture 2: \(\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\to1\)), giving the linear equation \(\theta''+\frac gL\theta=0\). Its roots are \(\pm i\sqrt{g/L}\), so \(\theta=A\cos\big(\sqrt{g/L}\,t\big)+B\sin\big(\sqrt{g/L}\,t\big)\): simple harmonic motion with period \(2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\), independent of the size of the swing.

You can now: derive \(my''+ky=0\) from Hooke's law and Newton's second law; find \(c_{cr}=2\sqrt{mk}\) and classify a damped oscillator; write its motion in each case; and carry the same results over to an RLC circuit.

10Cauchy–Euler equations ~7 min

What is this?

Here each derivative is multiplied by the matching power of \(x\). Such an equation looks exactly the same if you change the unit you measure \(x\) in, say centimetres instead of metres: it has no built-in length scale. Powers of \(x\) share that property, since zooming \(x^m\) by any factor only multiplies it by a constant. So the shape-keeping guess is now \(x^m\) instead of \(e^{\lambda x}\): each derivative knocks one power off, and the \(x\) in front puts it back.

So far the coefficients were constants. With variable coefficients (coefficients that depend on \(x\)) there is usually no exact formula, but one special class can be solved exactly.

Definition \[ \begin{aligned}x^ny^{(n)}&+c_1x^{n-1}y^{(n-1)}+\dots\\&+c_{n-1}xy'+c_ny=0\end{aligned} \]

with constants \(c_1,\dots,c_n\) is a Cauchy–Euler equation (also called an Euler or equidimensional equation).

In words: each derivative \(y^{(k)}\) is multiplied by a constant times \(x^k\), the power of \(x\) matching the order of the derivative.

The second-order case, used in the exam, is

\[ x^2y''+c_1xy'+c_2y=0. \]

Reasoning: similar to the constant coefficient case, but we try

\[ y=x^{\lambda}. \]

Each differentiation lowers the power by one (\((x^\lambda)'=\lambda x^{\lambda-1}\)), and the coefficient \(x^r\) in front of \(y^{(r)}\) compensates for this loss by raising it back by \(r\). So every term becomes a number times the same \(x^\lambda\), just as every term became a number times \(e^{\lambda x}\) before.

Characteristic equation for second order

With \(y=x^m\): \(y'=mx^{m-1}\), \(y''=m(m-1)x^{m-2}\), so

\[ \begin{aligned}&x^2y''+c_1xy'+c_2y\\&=x^m\big[m(m-1)+c_1m+c_2\big]=0.\end{aligned} \]

For \(x\gt0\), \(x^m\ne0\), so

\[ m(m-1)+c_1m+c_2=0, \]

i.e.

\[ m^2+(c_1-1)m+c_2=0. \]

In words: replace \(x^2y''\) by \(m(m-1)\), \(xy'\) by \(m\), \(y\) by 1. It is a quadratic again, often called the indicial equation.

Reading the formula
\(m(m-1)\)
from \(x^2y''\): two differentiations bring down \(m\), then \(m-1\), and the \(x^2\) restores the power.
\(c_1m\)
from \(c_1xy'\): one differentiation brings down \(m\), and the \(x\) restores the power.
\(c_2\)
from \(c_2y\), not differentiated at all.
\(x\gt0\)
needed so that \(x^m\ne0\) can be cancelled, and so that \(\ln x\) exists.
Trap

\(x^2y''\) becomes \(m(m-1)\), not \(m^2\). Using \(m^2+c_1m+c_2=0\) (the constant-coefficient habit) gives wrong roots.

The method for \(x^2y''+c_1xy'+c_2y=0\):

  1. Do: Check the form: the power of \(x\) in front of each derivative equals its order. If \(x^2y''\) has a number in front, divide through by it. Only then does every term become a number times the same \(x^m\).
  2. Do: Write the indicial equation \(m(m-1)+c_1m+c_2=0\), not \(m^2+c_1m+c_2=0\). Two differentiations of \(x^m\) bring down \(m\) and then \(m-1\), not \(m\) twice.
  3. Do: Solve it and read the answer from the three cases below, for \(x\gt0\). The root cases are the same as before, with \(\ln x\) playing the part that \(x\) played for exponentials.

As before there are three cases:

Solutions of \(x^2y''+c_1xy'+c_2y=0\), \(x\gt0\)
  1. Two distinct real roots \(m,n\): \(x^m\) and \(x^n\) are two linearly independent solutions, so \(y=C_1x^m+C_2x^n\).
  2. Two coincident (equal) roots \(m,m\): the solutions are \(x^m\) and \(x^m\ln x\), so \(y=(C_1+C_2\ln x)x^m\). The second solution, involving \(\ln x\), is found by reduction of order (Part O2).
  3. Two complex roots \(a\pm ib\): the solutions are \(x^a\cos(b\ln x)\) and \(x^a\sin(b\ln x)\), so \(y=x^a\big[A\cos(b\ln x)+B\sin(b\ln x)\big]\).

In words: same pattern as constant coefficients with \(e^{x}\) replaced by \(x\): an extra factor \(x\) became an extra factor \(\ln x\), and \(\cos bx\) became \(\cos(b\ln x)\).

Reasoning for the complex case. Use \(x=e^{\ln x}\) (Lecture 7) and Euler's formula:

\[ \begin{aligned} x^{ib}&=\exp\big[\ln\big(x^{ib}\big)\big]=\exp(ib\ln x)\\&=\cos(b\ln x)+i\sin(b\ln x). \end{aligned} \]

So \(x^{a+ib}=x^a\,x^{ib}=x^a[\cos(b\ln x)+i\sin(b\ln x)]\), and, exactly as in section 6, suitable combinations of the two complex solutions give the real solutions \(x^a\cos(b\ln x)\) and \(x^a\sin(b\ln x)\).

Reduction of order for the repeated case (sketch)

For a repeated root \(m\), the sum of the two roots of \(m^2+(c_1-1)m+c_2=0\) is \(m+m=-(c_1-1)\), so \(2m+c_1=1\). Put \(y=u\,x^m\) into the equation and simplify:

\[ \begin{aligned}&x^{m+2}u''+(2m+c_1)x^{m+1}u'\\&\quad+\big[m(m-1)+c_1m+c_2\big]x^mu=0.\end{aligned} \]

The bracket is 0 because \(m\) is a root, and \(2m+c_1=1\); dividing by \(x^{m+1}\) leaves \(xu''+u'=0\). Then \((xu')'=0\), so \(xu'=C_2\), \(u=C_2\ln x+C_1\), and \(y=(C_1+C_2\ln x)x^m\).

Example: one of each case

(1) \(x^2y''-2y=0\): \(m(m-1)-2=m^2-m-2=(m-2)(m+1)=0\), \(m=2,-1\):

\[ y=C_1x^2+\frac{C_2}{x}. \]

(2) \(x^2y''-xy'+y=0\): \(m(m-1)-m+1=(m-1)^2=0\), \(m=1,1\):

\[ y=C_1x+C_2x\ln x. \]

(3) \(x^2y''+xy'+y=0\): \(m(m-1)+m+1=m^2+1=0\), \(m=\pm i\) (\(a=0\), \(b=1\)):

\[ y=A\cos(\ln x)+B\sin(\ln x). \]

Cauchy–Euler solutions (\(x\gt0\))

Cauchy–Euler solutions behave like powers of \(x\), not exponentials. In the complex case \(\cos(\ln x)\) oscillates infinitely often as \(x\to0^+\) (where \(\ln x\to-\infty\)) but ever more slowly as \(x\) grows: it repeats each time \(x\) is multiplied by \(e^{2\pi}\approx535\).

Going further

Why \(x\gt0\)? \(\ln x\) and powers like \(x^{\sqrt2}\) are only defined for \(x\gt0\); also the equation's leading coefficient \(x^2\) is zero at \(x=0\), where solutions can blow up (e.g. \(1/x\)). For \(x\lt0\), replace \(x\) by \(|x|\) in the formulas.

Link to constant coefficients. The substitution \(x=e^t\) turns \(x^2y''+c_1xy'+c_2y=0\) into the constant-coefficient equation \(\ddot y+(c_1-1)\dot y+c_2y=0\) in \(t\) (dots = \(d/dt\)), with the same characteristic equation. Since \(t=\ln x\), \(e^{mt}=x^m\), \(te^{mt}=x^m\ln x\) and \(\cos bt=\cos(b\ln x)\): this explains every formula above.

You can now: recognise a Cauchy–Euler equation; write its indicial equation with \(m(m-1)\); write the general solution for distinct, repeated and complex roots using \(x^m\), \(\ln x\) and \(\cos(b\ln x)\); and explain the \(\ln x\) with reduction of order.

12Common mistakes

✗Repeated root \(\lambda=3,3\) written as \(C_1e^{3x}+C_2e^{3x}\). ✓ \((C_1+C_2x)e^{3x}\). The first is really only one constant. ✗Roots \(-1\pm2i\) written as \(e^{2x}(A\cos x+B\sin x)\) (real and imaginary parts swapped). ✓ \(e^{-x}(A\cos2x+B\sin2x)\): the real part goes in the exponential, the imaginary part inside cos and sin. ✗Leaving the answer as \(C_1e^{(-1+2i)x}+C_2e^{(-1-2i)x}\) when a real solution is wanted. ✓ Correct but unhelpful; convert with Euler's formula. ✗Forgetting the root \(\lambda=0\), or treating a double root 0 as single: \(y''''-y''=0\) answered as \(C_1+C_3e^x+C_4e^{-x}\). ✓ \(\lambda^2(\lambda^2-1)\): 0 is a double root, so \(C_1+C_2x\) plus \(C_3e^x+C_4e^{-x}\). Count: four constants for a fourth-order equation. ✗Cauchy–Euler: replacing \(x^2y''\) by \(m^2\). ✓ \(x^2y''\to m(m-1)\), \(xy'\to m\), \(y\to1\). ✗Cauchy–Euler repeated root written as \((C_1+C_2x)x^m\). ✓ \((C_1+C_2\ln x)x^m\); the "extra \(x\)" of the constant-coefficient case becomes "extra \(\ln x\)". ✗Cauchy–Euler complex roots \(a\pm ib\) written with \(\cos bx\). ✓ \(\cos(b\ln x)\) and \(\sin(b\ln x)\), times \(x^a\). ✗"\(W(0)=0\), so the functions are dependent." ✓ Dependence needs \(W=0\) for all \(x\) in the interval; one zero proves nothing (e.g. \(W(x,x^2)=x^2\) is zero only at 0, and \(x,x^2\) are independent). ✗Mixing up the damping names: "\(c\lt c_{cr}\) is overdamped". ✓ Small damping (\(c\lt c_{cr}\)) = underdamped = it still oscillates. ✗Initial-value problems: forgetting the product rule when differentiating \(e^{ax}(A\cos bx+B\sin bx)\) before using \(y'(0)\). ✓ \(y'(0)=aA+bB\).

1. Solve the initial-value problem \(y''+2y'+10y=0\), \(y(0)=1\), \(y'(0)=2\).

Hint 1

The characteristic equation \(\lambda^2+2\lambda+10=0\) has discriminant \(4-40=-36\).

Hint 2

Roots \(-1\pm3i\), so \(y=e^{-x}(A\cos3x+B\sin3x)\). Then \(y(0)=A\) and \(y'(0)=-A+3B\).

Answer

\(A=1\), \(-1+3B=2\Rightarrow B=1\):

\[ y=e^{-x}(\cos3x+\sin3x). \]

2. Find a homogeneous linear ODE with constant coefficients whose general solution is \(y=C_1+C_2e^{x}+C_3xe^{x}\).

Hint

Work backwards: which roots produce \(1\), \(e^x\) and \(xe^x\)? Multiply out the corresponding factors.

Answer

Roots \(0,1,1\): characteristic polynomial \(\lambda(\lambda-1)^2=\lambda^3-2\lambda^2+\lambda\). So

\[ y'''-2y''+y'=0. \]

3. A mass \(m=1\) hangs on a spring with \(k=4\), with damping constant \(c\): \(x''+cx'+4x=0\).
(a) Find \(c_{cr}\) and classify the motion for \(c=2\), \(c=4\), \(c=5\).
(b) For \(c=5\), find \(x(t)\) if \(x(0)=1\), \(x'(0)=0\).

Hint

\(c_{cr}=2\sqrt{mk}\). For (b) the roots of \(\lambda^2+5\lambda+4=0\) factorise nicely.

Answer

(a) \(c_{cr}=2\sqrt{4}=4\). \(c=2\): underdamped (roots \(-1\pm\sqrt3\,i\)); \(c=4\): critically damped (root \(-2\) twice); \(c=5\): overdamped.

(b) \((\lambda+1)(\lambda+4)=0\), \(x=C_1e^{-t}+C_2e^{-4t}\); \(C_1+C_2=1\), \(-C_1-4C_2=0\) give \(C_1=\frac43\), \(C_2=-\frac13\):

\[ x(t)=\frac{4e^{-t}-e^{-4t}}{3}. \]

4. Solve \(x^2y''-3xy'+13y=0\), \(x\gt0\).

Hint

Cauchy–Euler: \(m(m-1)-3m+13=0\).

Answer

\(m^2-4m+13=0\), \(m=2\pm3i\):

\[ y=x^2\big[A\cos(3\ln x)+B\sin(3\ln x)\big]. \]

15Concept checks

1. Are \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) linearly independent? Use the Wronskian.

Answer

\(W(\sin x,\cos x)=\sin x(-\sin x)-\cos x\cos x=-1\ne0\). Independent (they are the two building blocks for \(y''+y=0\)).

2. If one root of a characteristic equation with real coefficients is \(1+2i\), what must another root be? What two solutions do they give?

Answer

Its conjugate \(1-2i\). Together: \(e^{x}\cos2x\) and \(e^{x}\sin2x\).

3. A characteristic equation has roots \(3,3,3\). What is the order of the ODE and its general solution?

Answer

Third order (three roots). \(y=(C_1+C_2x+C_3x^2)e^{3x}\) (multiplicity three: multiply by \(1,x,x^2\)).

4. A spring with no damping (\(c=0\)) is released. Does it ever stop? Which case of the characteristic equation is this?

Answer

Roots \(\pm i\sqrt{k/m}\): complex with real part 0, so \(x=A\cos\omega t+B\sin\omega t\) with \(\omega=\sqrt{k/m}\). The factor \(e^{0t}=1\) does not decay: it oscillates forever (simple harmonic motion).

5. A car's suspension should settle quickly after a bump without bouncing. Should it be under-, critically or overdamped? Why not very heavily overdamped?

Answer

Critically damped (or just under). Heavy overdamping makes one root \(-\zeta\omega+\omega\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\) very close to 0, so the term \(e^{r_1t}\) decays very slowly: the car creeps back to level. See the explorer in section 9.

6. Which constant-coefficient ODE has general solution \(y=A\cosh2x+B\sinh2x\)?

Answer

Equivalent to \(C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{-2x}\): roots \(\pm2\), \(\lambda^2-4=0\), so \(y''-4y=0\).

7. Why does the Cauchy–Euler method use \(y=x^m\) rather than \(y=e^{\lambda x}\)?

Answer

With \(y=e^{\lambda x}\), \(x^2y''=\lambda^2x^2e^{\lambda x}\) and \(y\) would carry different powers of \(x\) in front of \(e^{\lambda x}\), so \(e^{\lambda x}\) could not be cancelled to leave a number. With \(y=x^m\), each derivative loses one power of \(x\) and the coefficient \(x^k\) gives it back, so every term is a constant times \(x^m\).
