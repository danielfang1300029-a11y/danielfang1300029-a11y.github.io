Parameterization: Parametric and Polar Curves

0Where this lecture sits

Until now every curve was a graph \(y=f(x)\), with one \(y\) for each \(x\). Loops, circles and spirals are not like that. This lecture describes them in two new ways: as the trail of a bug walking on paper while a clock ticks (parametric curves), and as a marker on a lighthouse beam, given by the beam's angle and the distance along it (polar curves). Then it finds slopes and areas for both.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 A parametric curve is the trail of a bug: the clock \(t\) says where it is. Lines and circles have standard forms.
  2. §2 Eliminating the parameter throws away the clock and keeps the trail. Say which part of the Cartesian graph the bug really visits.
  3. §3 Slope = vertical speed ÷ horizontal speed. Zero horizontal speed means a vertical tangent.
  4. §4 Area = add up height × sideways step as the bug walks. Walking right to left gives a minus sign.
  5. §5 A polar point is a beam angle and a distance along the beam. Negative distance means behind the lighthouse.
  6. §6 Fixed angle gives a line through the pole; fixed distance gives a circle about it.
  7. §7 Sweep the beam and follow the marker to sketch \(r=f(\theta)\). Most common L15 exam type
  8. §8 A polar curve is a parametric curve with the beam angle as the clock. The slope is not \(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\).
  9. §9 Polar area adds up thin pizza slices: \(\frac12r^2\,d\theta\) each.

You need: integration so far (Lectures 11 to 14), the Chain and Product Rules (Lecture 6), and radians and right-angled-triangle trigonometry. In the exam: 15 appearances in 23 past papers: slope, speed or tangent of a parametric curve; sketching a polar curve and its symmetry (the most common); and slopes or horizontal and vertical tangents of polar curves. Lecture 16 (area and volume) and Lecture 17 (arc length and surface area) use these ideas again. See the L15 exam guide.

Recall: integration so far

The techniques of integration met so far are: the Substitution Rule (Lecture 13), Integration by Parts (Lecture 13), trigonometric identities, trigonometric substitution and partial fraction decomposition (Lecture 14). Some advice:

  • The best strategy for integration is practice: recognising which technique fits comes from doing many examples.
  • To check an answer to an indefinite integral, differentiate your answer: you should get back the integrand (the function you were integrating).
  • An elementary function is one built from polynomials, roots, exponentials, logarithms, trigonometric functions and inverse trigonometric functions using \(+,-,\times,\div\) and composition (putting one function inside another). If \(f(x)\) is elementary, then \(f'(x)\) is always elementary, but \(\int f(x)\,dx\) need not be. For example \(f(x)=e^{x^2}\) has no elementary antiderivative: an antiderivative exists (by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Lecture 12, \(\int_0^x e^{s^2}\,ds\) is one), but it cannot be written as a formula made of elementary functions.

Learning objectives: define and trace parametric curves; define and sketch polar curves; apply calculus to parameterized curves to compute tangents and areas.

Going further · radians

HKDSE Core measures angles in degrees. In calculus, angles are always in radians: \(\pi\) radians \(=180^\circ\), so \(\frac{\pi}{2}=90^\circ\), \(\frac{\pi}{3}=60^\circ\), \(\frac{\pi}{4}=45^\circ\), \(\frac{\pi}{6}=30^\circ\), and \(1\) radian \(\approx 57.3^\circ\). An angle of \(\theta\) radians cuts an arc of length \(r\theta\) from a circle of radius \(r\), and a sector (pizza slice) of area \(\frac12 r^2\theta\). Every \(\theta\) and \(t\) in this lecture is in radians.

1Parametric curves ~8 min

What is this?

A bug walks across a sheet of paper while a clock ticks. At each clock reading \(t\) the bug is at one spot \((x,y)\), and its footprints form the curve. The bug may cross the same vertical line many times, or walk over its own trail, which a graph \(y=F(x)\) can never do. The clock reading is the parameter.

Why parameters?

A function \(y=F(x)\) gives exactly one \(y\) for each \(x\). On a graph, this means every vertical line meets the graph at most once (the vertical line test). Picture a wiggly path of a moving particle that loops back on itself: some vertical lines cut it three times, so it is not the graph of any function \(y=F(x)\). (The first figure below shows a curve of this kind.)

The fix: instead of making \(y\) depend on \(x\), let both \(x\) and \(y\) depend on a third variable \(t\), called the parameter:

\[x=f(t),\qquad y=g(t).\]

In words: horizontal position = a function of time, vertical position = another function of time.

Then every point of the curve \(C\) is \((x,y)=(f(t),g(t))\) for some value of \(t\). Think of a bug walking on a sheet of paper: at each time \(t\) it is somewhere, and its footprints form the curve. The bug may walk over the same \(x\) many times; that is no problem.

Definition · parametric curve

If \(x\) and \(y\) are given as functions \(x=f(t)\), \(y=g(t)\) over a closed interval \([a,b]\) of \(t\)-values, then the set of points \((x,y)=(f(t),g(t))\) is a parametric curve. The equations are parametric equations for the curve.

In words: curve = all the places the moving point visits while the time runs from \(a\) to \(b\).

  • Parametric curves describe the motion and direction of a particle. The initial point is \((f(a),g(a))\) (where the particle starts) and the terminal point is \((f(b),g(b))\) (where it ends). The direction in which the curve is traced as \(t\) increases is called its orientation; on sketches it is shown with arrows.
  • In applications the parameter \(t\) usually represents time.
  • A parameterization (a choice of parametric equations) of a curve is not unique: the same set of points can be traced at different speeds, in a different direction, or starting at a different place. Example: \(x=\cos t,\ y=\sin t,\ 0\le t\le 2\pi\) and \(x=\cos 2t,\ y=\sin 2t,\ 0\le t\le\pi\) both trace the unit circle once anticlockwise; the second goes twice as fast. Try both in the figure.

What is this? (parametric line)

The bug walks in a straight line at a steady pace. Every tick of the clock it takes the same step: 1 to the right and \(m\) up. It is at the point \((a,b)\) when the clock reads 0.

Definition · parametric line

The line with slope \(m\) through the point \((a,b)\) has parametric equations

\[x=a+t,\qquad y=b+mt.\]

In words: each time \(t\) goes up by 1, the point moves 1 to the right and \(m\) up (that is what "slope \(m\)" means), starting from \((a,b)\) at \(t=0\).

Example 1 is the same kind of line written differently: put \(s=2t\) and it becomes \(x=1+s,\ y=2-\frac12 s\), i.e. \(a=1,\ b=2,\ m=-\frac12\). Another illustration that parameterizations are not unique.

Example 2 · circles

Plot the curve \(x=\cos t,\ y=\sin t,\ 0\le t\le 2\pi\).

Solution. For every \(t\), \(x^2+y^2=\cos^2t+\sin^2t=1\), so every point lies on the circle of radius 1 centred at the origin (the unit circle). At \(t=0\) the point is \((1,0)\); at \(t=\frac{\pi}{2}\) it is \((0,1)\); at \(t=\pi\), \((-1,0)\); at \(t=\frac{3\pi}{2}\), \((0,-1)\); at \(t=2\pi\), back to \((1,0)\). So the curve is the whole unit circle, traced once, anticlockwise, starting and ending at \((1,0)\). Here \(t\) is exactly the angle from the positive \(x\)-axis to the point.

What is this? (parametric circle)

Now the bug runs round a circular track of radius \(r\) with centre \((h,k)\), anticlockwise. The clock reading \(t\) is the angle it has turned through so far, measured from the rightmost point of the track. One lap takes \(t\) from 0 to \(2\pi\).

Definition · parametric circle

A circle with radius \(r\) and centre \((h,k)\) has parametric equations

\[x=h+r\cos t,\qquad y=k+r\sin t.\]

In words: start at the centre, then go \(r\cos t\) across and \(r\sin t\) up: a point at distance \(r\) from the centre in the direction of angle \(t\).

2Eliminating the parameter; Lissajous curve and "helix" ~8 min

What is this?

Eliminating the parameter means throwing away the clock and keeping only the trail. You get the shape of the path as one equation in \(x\) and \(y\), but you lose when, and in which direction, the bug walked. The Cartesian graph can also be longer than the trail: the bug may only ever visit part of it, so you must say which part.

A Cartesian equation is an ordinary equation in \(x\) and \(y\) only (like \(x^2+y^2=1\)). Turning parametric equations into one is called eliminating the parameter:

  1. Do: Start with the two parametric equations. They say where the bug is at each clock reading.
  2. Do: Solve one of them for the parameter \(t\). This reads the clock off the bug's position, so the time can be written in terms of where the bug is.
  3. Do: Substitute that expression for \(t\) into the other equation. Now the other coordinate is linked straight to the first one, with no clock left.
  4. Do: The result is one Cartesian equation. Restrict the domain as needed: the parametric curve may only be part of the Cartesian graph. The Cartesian graph contains every point that fits the equation, but the bug only reaches the values its \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\) can take (for example \(x=t^2\) is never negative).

Be warned: it is not always possible to solve for \(t\) neatly. Sometimes a trick works instead (e.g. \(\cos^2t+\sin^2t=1\) in Example 2); sometimes nothing works and the curve can only be studied in parametric form.

Example · why restrict the domain

\(x=t^2,\ y=t^4\). Solve the first: \(t^2=x\). Substitute: \(y=(t^2)^2=x^2\). But \(x=t^2\) is never negative, so the curve is only the right half of the parabola \(y=x^2\), i.e. \(y=x^2\) with \(x\ge 0\). Forgetting this is a common mistake (section 11).

What is this? (Lissajous curve)

Imagine the bug being pushed by two swings at once: a left-right swing, and an up-down swing that goes twice as fast. Each swing on its own just moves the bug back and forth along a segment. Together they draw a closed loop; here, a figure-eight.

3Tangents to parametric curves ~6 min

What is this?

Freeze the bug at one instant. It has a horizontal speed (how fast \(x\) changes) and a vertical speed (how fast \(y\) changes), each with a sign: negative when it moves left or down. The steepness of its trail is rise per run, which is vertical speed divided by horizontal speed: the clock cancels out. This needs the bug to be moving sideways at that instant. If its horizontal speed is zero but it is still moving up or down, the trail is vertical there.

A tangent line touches a curve at a point and has the same direction as the curve there (Lecture 6). Its slope is \(\frac{dy}{dx}\). For a parametric curve, \(y\) is not given as a function of \(x\), but we can still find \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) from the rates \(\frac{dx}{dt}\) and \(\frac{dy}{dt}\).

Slope of a parametric curve

Suppose \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable (Lecture 5: they have derivatives). At a point of the curve \(x=f(t),\ y=g(t)\) where \(y\) is also a differentiable function of \(x\), the slope of the tangent line is

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}\qquad\text{if }\frac{dx}{dt}\ne 0.\]

In words: slope = (how fast the height changes in time) ÷ (how fast the across-position changes in time) = rise per second ÷ run per second = rise per run.

Reading the formula
\(\frac{dy}{dt}\)
the bug's vertical speed: rise per tick of the clock.
\(\frac{dx}{dt}\)
its horizontal speed: run per tick.
the quotient
rise per run, the slope of the trail; the ticks cancel.
\(\frac{dx}{dt}\ne 0\)
the bug must be moving sideways. If it is not, the tangent is vertical, or, if both speeds are 0, the bug has stopped and the point needs a closer look.

Why. By the Chain Rule (Lecture 6), \(\frac{dy}{dt}=\frac{dy}{dx}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt}\). Divide both sides by \(\frac{dx}{dt}\), which is allowed because it is not zero. Analogy: if a car climbs 3 m per second while moving 6 m per second horizontally, the road rises \(\frac{3}{6}=\frac12\) m per metre.

Second derivative. If \(f\) and \(g\) are twice-differentiable (their derivatives are themselves differentiable) and \(\frac{dx}{dt}\ne0\), then apply the same rule to the function \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) (which is a function of \(t\)) in place of \(y\):

\[\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)=\frac{\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)}{dx/dt}.\]

In words: second derivative = (rate of change of the slope with respect to time) ÷ (rate of change of \(x\) with respect to time).

Trap

\(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\) is not \(\dfrac{d^2y/dt^2}{d^2x/dt^2}\). Differentiate the slope \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) with respect to \(t\) first, then divide by \(\frac{dx}{dt}\) (not by \(\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}\)). The sign of \(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\) tells concavity, as in Lecture 9.

Going further · horizontal and vertical tangents
  • Horizontal tangent (slope 0): \(\frac{dy}{dt}=0\) and \(\frac{dx}{dt}\ne0\).
  • Vertical tangent (slope undefined): \(\frac{dx}{dt}=0\) and \(\frac{dy}{dt}\ne0\).
  • If both are 0, the formula says nothing; the point needs a closer look (it may be a sharp point called a cusp).

The exams use these often (see Group Work 1 and the exam taster).

Example 5 · tangents

Find the slope of the tangent line at \((1,0)\) for \(x=t+\cos t,\ y=t+\sin t\) (the curve of Example 4).

Step 1: find \(t\) at the point. We need \(t+\cos t=1\) and \(t+\sin t=0\). \(t=0\) works: \(0+\cos0=1\), \(0+\sin 0=0\). It is the only value, because \(t+\sin t\) is increasing (its derivative \(1+\cos t\ge 0\), zero only at isolated points), so it equals 0 only once. The slope formula works with clock readings, so a point given as \((x,y)\) must first be turned into its \(t\).

Step 2: differentiate. \(\frac{dx}{dt}=1-\sin t\), \(\frac{dy}{dt}=1+\cos t\).

Step 3: at \(t=0\): \(\frac{dx}{dt}=1\ne0\), \(\frac{dy}{dt}=2\), so The horizontal speed is checked first because the rule divides by it.

\[\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{t=0}=\frac{1+\cos 0}{1-\sin 0}=\frac{2}{1}=2.\]

Slope \(=2\). Tangent line: \(y-0=2(x-1)\), i.e. \(y=2x-2\).

Extra (second derivative, for practice). \(\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1+\cos t}{1-\sin t}\). By the Quotient Rule, \(\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)=\frac{-\sin t(1-\sin t)+(1+\cos t)\cos t}{(1-\sin t)^2}\), which is \(2\) at \(t=0\). Dividing by \(\frac{dx}{dt}=1\): \(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=2\gt 0\), so the curve is concave up at \((1,0)\).

4Area in parametric form ~4 min

What is this?

As the bug walks above the \(x\)-axis, colour in the strip below it. In one tiny tick it moves sideways a little and is at some height, so it adds a thin strip of area: height × sideways step. Adding the strips over the whole walk gives the area. If the bug walks right to left, its sideways steps count as negative and the total comes out negative; and if it walks over the same stretch twice, that area is counted twice.

For a curve \(y=F(x)\) from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\) (with \(F\ge0\)), the area under it is \(A=\int_a^b F(x)\,dx\) (Lecture 12). Now suppose the same curve is given parametrically by \(x=f(t),\ y=g(t)\) for \(\alpha\le t\le\beta\), so that \(g(t)=F(f(t))\), with \(f(\alpha)=a\) and \(f(\beta)=b\). Substitute \(x=f(t)\), \(dx=f'(t)\,dt\) (Substitution Rule, Lecture 13):

Area under a parametric curve \[A=\int_a^b F(x)\,dx=\int_\alpha^\beta g(t)\,f'(t)\,dt.\]

In words: area = sum over time of (height \(y\)) × (small sideways step \(dx\)), where the sideways step is \(dx=\frac{dx}{dt}\,dt\). In short, \(A=\int y\,dx\) with everything written in \(t\).

Trap · direction of tracing Going further

The limits must match: \(t=\alpha\) gives the left end \(x=a\) and \(t=\beta\) the right end \(x=b\). Example 6 · area under a cycloid arch

Find the area under one arch of the cycloid \(x=r(\theta-\sin\theta),\ y=r(1-\cos\theta)\).

A cycloid is the path of a point on the rim of a wheel of radius \(r\) rolling along a straight line (the \(x\)-axis). Here \(\theta\) is a parameter: the angle the wheel has turned through (not a polar angle). The point touches the ground when \(y=0\), i.e. \(\cos\theta=1\): \(\theta=0,2\pi,4\pi,\dots\). One arch is one full turn, \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\), going from \(x=0\) to \(x=2\pi r\). The arches meet the \(x\)-axis in sharp points at \(0\) and \(2\pi r\).

Solution. \(\frac{dx}{d\theta}=r(1-\cos\theta)\ge 0\), so the arch is traced left to right. With \(y=r(1-\cos\theta)\): Checking the direction first tells us the integral will come out positive, so no sign fix is needed.

\[\begin{aligned}A&=\int_0^{2\pi} r(1-\cos\theta)\cdot r(1-\cos\theta)\,d\theta\\&=r^2\int_0^{2\pi}\left(1-2\cos\theta+\cos^2\theta\right)d\theta.\end{aligned}\]

Use \(\cos^2\theta=\frac{1+\cos2\theta}{2}\) (Lecture 14):

\[\begin{aligned}A&=r^2\int_0^{2\pi}\left(\tfrac32-2\cos\theta+\tfrac12\cos2\theta\right)d\theta\\&=r^2\left[\tfrac32\theta-2\sin\theta+\tfrac14\sin2\theta\right]_0^{2\pi}\\&=r^2\cdot 3\pi=3\pi r^2.\end{aligned}\]

So the area under one arch is \(3\pi r^2\): exactly three times the area of the rolling wheel.

5Polar coordinates ~7 min

What is this?

Stand at a lighthouse at the pole. To point out a ship you give two numbers: the direction of the beam (the angle \(\theta\), measured anticlockwise from the beam's starting position along the positive \(x\)-axis) and how far along the beam the ship is (the distance \(r\)). Spirals and flowers are easy to describe this way, because they are built from "how far out" in "which direction".

Some curves would be very awkward to write as \(y=F(x)\) or even as a Cartesian equation: a spiral, a many-petalled flower and a looped curve. The figure below shows curves of this kind. In polar coordinates each of them has a short equation, because they are naturally described by "how far from the centre, in which direction".

Curves that are simple in polar form

spiral \(r=0.25\theta\)16-petal rose \(r=1.6\sin 8\theta\)looped limaçon \(r=1+2\cos\theta\)

Each of these has a one-line polar equation \(r=f(\theta)\). Their Cartesian equations are long or impossible to write.

The polar coordinate system

Fix a point \(O\), called the pole (it plays the role of the origin), and a ray (half-line) starting at \(O\), called the initial ray or polar axis. We always take the initial ray along the positive \(x\)-axis. A point \(P\) is described by two numbers \((r,\theta)\), its polar coordinates:

  • \(r\) = the (directed) distance from the pole \(O\) to \(P\);
  • \(\theta\) = the angle from the initial ray to the ray \(OP\).
  • \(\theta\gt 0\) when the angle is measured counterclockwise (anticlockwise); a negative \(\theta\) means measuring clockwise.

Polar coordinates are an alternative to Cartesian coordinates \((x,y)\): the same point, described differently. Analogy: "walk 3 m east and 4 m north" (Cartesian) versus "face 53° north of east and walk 5 m" (polar).

What is this? (negative r)

A negative distance means: aim the beam at angle \(\theta\), then walk the distance backwards, through the lighthouse and out the other side. You end up on the beam pointing the opposite way. Turning the beam a full turn brings it back to the same direction, so one ship has many names.

Negative \(r\) and repeated names Going further

Pictures usually show \(r\ge0\), but polar equations often produce negative \(r\). The rule: to plot \((r,\theta)\) with \(r\lt 0\), face direction \(\theta\), then walk \(|r|\) backwards (in the opposite direction, \(\theta+\pi\)). So

\[(-r,\theta)\ \text{is the same point as}\ (r,\theta+\pi).\]

In words: a negative distance means "go the same distance the opposite way".

Also, adding a full turn does not change the direction: \((r,\theta)=(r,\theta+2\pi)=(r,\theta-2\pi)=\dots\) And the pole is \((0,\theta)\) for every \(\theta\). So, unlike Cartesian coordinates, every point has infinitely many polar representations. Example: \((2,\frac{\pi}{4})\), \((2,\frac{9\pi}{4})\), \((-2,\frac{5\pi}{4})\) and \((-2,-\frac{3\pi}{4})\) are all the same point.

Polar and Cartesian correspondence

What is this? (converting)

The beam to the ship, the ground due east of the lighthouse, and a line dropped straight from the ship to that ground make a right-angled triangle. The beam is the hypotenuse, the across distance is the base, and the up distance is the height. Converting between the two kinds of coordinates is just this triangle.

Put the pole at the origin and the initial ray along the positive \(x\)-axis (picture a circle of radius \(r\) about the common origin, the ray \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\) along the \(y\)-axis, and a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse \(r\), base \(x\), height \(y\), angle \(\theta\) at the origin). From that triangle (HKDSE Core trigonometry):

\[\begin{aligned}\cos\theta=\frac{x}{r}\ &\Rightarrow\ x=r\cos\theta,\\ \sin\theta=\frac{y}{r}\ &\Rightarrow\ y=r\sin\theta,\end{aligned}\]

In words: across = distance × cos(angle); up = distance × sin(angle).

\[x^2+y^2=r^2,\qquad \tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}.\]

In words: distance² = across² + up² (Pythagoras); tan(angle) = up ÷ across.

Reading the formula
\(r\)
the distance along the beam from the lighthouse to the ship.
\(x=r\cos\theta\)
how far east of the lighthouse the ship is (negative means west): the base of the triangle.
\(y=r\sin\theta\)
how far north it is (negative means south): the height of the triangle.
\(x^2+y^2=r^2\)
Pythagoras on that triangle.
\(\tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}\)
the steepness of the beam. A beam and the opposite beam have the same steepness, which is why the quadrant must be checked.

The first pair converts polar to Cartesian (always works, also for negative \(r\)). The second pair converts Cartesian to polar, but \(\tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}\) has two solutions in each full turn (they differ by \(\pi\)): choose the one in the same quadrant as the point. If \(x=0\), use \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\) (for \(y\gt0\)) or \(-\frac{\pi}{2}\) (for \(y\lt 0\)).

Example 7 · point conversion

(a) Convert \((2,\frac{\pi}{3})\) from polar to Cartesian.

\[\begin{aligned}x&=2\cos\frac{\pi}{3}=2\cdot\frac12=1,\\ y&=2\sin\frac{\pi}{3}=2\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}=\sqrt3.\end{aligned}\]

So the point is \((1,\sqrt3)\).

(b) Convert \((1,-1)\) from Cartesian to polar.

\(r=\sqrt{1^2+(-1)^2}=\sqrt2\). \(\tan\theta=\frac{-1}{1}=-1\), so \(\theta=-\frac{\pi}{4}\) or \(\frac{3\pi}{4}\) (within one turn). The point \((1,-1)\) is in quadrant IV (right and down), so \(\theta=-\frac{\pi}{4}\). Answer: \((\sqrt2,-\frac{\pi}{4})\), or equivalently \((\sqrt2,\frac{7\pi}{4})\). (Choosing \(\frac{3\pi}{4}\) with \(r=\sqrt2\) would give \((-1,1)\), the wrong point; \((-\sqrt2,\frac{3\pi}{4})\) is also correct.) \(\tan\theta\) cannot tell a beam from the opposite beam; the quadrant picks the half that actually points at the ship.

You can now: plot a point from its polar coordinates, including negative \(r\); list other polar names for the same point; convert between polar and Cartesian coordinates, choosing \(\theta\) in the quadrant of the point.

6Lines and circles in polar form ~6 min

What is this?

The two simplest rules for the lighthouse: fix the direction of the beam and allow any distance, and you get a straight line through the lighthouse (negative distances give the back half). Fix the distance and sweep the beam all the way round, and you get a circle centred at the lighthouse. Other lines and circles have longer polar equations, found by putting the polar conversion into their Cartesian equations.

A polar curve (polar equation) is an equation linking \(r\) and \(\theta\), usually \(r=f(\theta)\). Its graph is the set of points whose polar coordinates satisfy it (made precise in section 7).

Example 8 · lines

Sketch the polar curve \(\theta=1\).

Solution. The equation fixes the angle at 1 radian (\(\approx57.3^\circ\)) and puts no condition on \(r\). With \(r\ge0\) we get the ray from the pole at angle 1; with \(r\lt0\) we get the opposite ray (angle \(1+\pi\)). Together: the whole straight line through the origin making angle 1 radian with the positive \(x\)-axis. In Cartesian form, \(\frac{y}{x}=\tan 1\), i.e. \(y=(\tan 1)\,x\approx 1.557x\).

Line in polar form

The line with slope \(m\) through the point \((a,b)\) has polar equation

\[r=\frac{b-ma}{\sin\theta-m\cos\theta}.\]

In words: distance from the pole to the line in direction \(\theta\) = (a fixed number) ÷ (sin of angle − slope × cos of angle).

Reading the formula
\(b-ma\)
the value of \(y-mx\) on the line (its \(y\)-intercept), the same at every point of the line.
\(\sin\theta-m\cos\theta\)
the value of \(y-mx\) at the tip of a beam of length 1 at angle \(\theta\); a beam of length \(r\) gives \(r\) times this.
the quotient
how long the beam must be for its tip to reach the line.
denominator 0
the beam is parallel to the line and never reaches it.

Where it comes from. The Cartesian line is \(y-b=m(x-a)\), i.e. \(y-mx=b-ma\). Substitute \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\): \(r(\sin\theta-m\cos\theta)=b-ma\); divide. Directions with \(\sin\theta=m\cos\theta\) (parallel to the line) never meet it, and the formula correctly gives no \(r\) there.

If the line passes through the pole, then \(b-ma=0\) and the formula gives only \(r=0\). Such lines are written as \(\theta=\) constant instead, as in Example 8. Vertical lines (no slope) are \(r\cos\theta=a\).

Example 9 · circles

Sketch the polar curve \(r=1\).

Solution. Every point at distance 1 from the pole, in every direction \(\theta\): the circle of radius 1 centred at the origin (Cartesian \(x^2+y^2=1\)). Compare with Example 8: "\(\theta\) = constant" is a line through the pole, "\(r\) = constant" is a circle about the pole.

What is this? (polar circle)

The line along a beam can cross a circle twice, touch it once, or miss it. The two signs in the formula give the two crossing points; one of them may be behind the lighthouse, with negative \(r\).

Circle in polar form

A circle with radius \(R\) and centre \((h,k)\) has polar equation

\[\begin{aligned}r={}&h\cos\theta+k\sin\theta\\&\pm\sqrt{R^2-(h\sin\theta-k\cos\theta)^2}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: in direction \(\theta\), the ray from the pole can meet the circle twice; the "+" and "−" give the far and near crossing points.

Where it comes from. Start from \((x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=R^2\) and substitute \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\):

\[r^2-2r(h\cos\theta+k\sin\theta)+h^2+k^2-R^2=0.\]

Solve this quadratic in \(r\) with the quadratic formula (HKDSE Core) and simplify using \((h\cos\theta+k\sin\theta)^2-h^2-k^2=-(h\sin\theta-k\cos\theta)^2\).

Special cases: centre at the pole (\(h=k=0\)) gives \(r=\pm R\), the circle \(r=R\) (Example 9). A circle through the pole has \(h^2+k^2=R^2\), and the formula collapses to \(r=2h\cos\theta+2k\sin\theta\) (one sign) or \(r=0\) (the other); e.g. \(r=2\cos\theta\) in Example 10.

7Sketching polar curves ~7 min

What is this?

Sweep the lighthouse beam slowly anticlockwise. At each angle a marker sits on the beam at the distance the equation gives (behind the lighthouse if that distance is negative). The trail the marker leaves is the polar curve. A point is on the curve if the marker passes over it at some moment of the sweep, even if other names for that point do not fit the equation.

Definition · graph of a polar equation

The graph of a polar equation \(r=f(\theta)\) consists of all the points \(P\) that have at least one polar representation \((r,\theta)\) whose coordinates satisfy the equation.

In words: a point is on the curve if some name of it fits the equation, even if other names of it do not.

Example: is the Cartesian point \((0,-1)\) on \(r=\cos2\theta\)? The name \((1,\frac{3\pi}{2})\) does not fit, since \(\cos3\pi=-1\ne1\). But the name \((-1,\frac{\pi}{2})\) (walk 1 backwards from direction \(\frac{\pi}{2}\)) fits: \(\cos\pi=-1\). So yes, it is on the curve.

Techniques:

  1. Do: Plot points and use symmetry. Make a table of \(\theta\) and \(r\) (use angles like \(0,\frac{\pi}{6},\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{3},\frac{\pi}{2},\dots\)), plot each point, join them in order of \(\theta\). Symmetry halves the work (tests below). Each row of the table is one snapshot of the beam and its marker; symmetry lets you mirror half of the snapshots instead of computing them.
  2. Do: Graph \(y=f(\theta)\) in the Cartesian \(r\theta\)-plane first: draw an ordinary graph with \(\theta\) on the horizontal axis and \(r\) on the vertical axis. Then, as \(\theta\) increases, read off \(r\) and translate into the polar picture: where \(r\) is large the curve is far from the pole, where \(r=0\) it passes through the pole, where \(r\lt0\) it is on the opposite side. The big figure below shows both pictures side by side. This graph is the marker's distance against the beam angle, so you see at a glance when the marker is far out, at the lighthouse, or behind it.
  3. Do: Follow the curve-sketching guidelines (Lecture 9): domain, where \(r=0\) (curve meets the pole), largest and smallest \(r\), symmetry, period. These are the special moments of the sweep (marker at the lighthouse, furthest out, starting to repeat), and they fix the shape before the fine detail.
Going further · symmetry tests

The polar curve is symmetric

  • about the \(x\)-axis if replacing \(\theta\) by \(-\theta\) gives the same equation (e.g. any \(r=f(\cos\theta)\));
  • about the \(y\)-axis if replacing \(\theta\) by \(\pi-\theta\) gives the same equation (e.g. any \(r=f(\sin\theta)\));
  • about the origin if replacing \(r\) by \(-r\), or \(\theta\) by \(\theta+\pi\), gives the same equation.

These are sufficient tests (because of the many names of each point, a curve can be symmetric without passing them). Past papers often ask "discuss for symmetry".

8Tangents to polar curves ~4 min

What is this?

A polar curve is a parametric curve where the beam angle plays the part of the clock: as the beam turns, the marker's across and up positions both change. So the slope is again rise ÷ run, each measured per bit of turning. How fast the marker slides along the beam is not the slope: on a circle around the lighthouse the marker does not slide at all, yet the trail points in every direction in turn.

A polar curve \(r=f(\theta)\) is a parametric curve in disguise, with \(\theta\) as the parameter:

\[x=r\cos\theta=f(\theta)\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta=f(\theta)\sin\theta.\]

Use the parametric slope rule (section 3) with \(\theta\) in place of \(t\), and the Product Rule (Lecture 6) on each product:

Slope of a polar curve \[\begin{aligned}\frac{dy}{dx}&=\frac{dy/d\theta}{dx/d\theta}=\frac{\dfrac{dr}{d\theta}\sin\theta+r\cos\theta}{\dfrac{dr}{d\theta}\cos\theta-r\sin\theta}\\&\text{if }\frac{dx}{d\theta}\ne0.\end{aligned}\]

In words: slope = (rate of change of height with angle) ÷ (rate of change of across-position with angle), where height = \(r\sin\theta\) and across = \(r\cos\theta\).

Reading the formula
\(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\sin\theta\)
height gained because the marker slides outwards along the beam.
\(r\cos\theta\)
height gained just because the beam turns while the marker sits at distance \(r\).
numerator
the two together: the rise per bit of turning, \(\frac{dy}{d\theta}\).
denominator
the same two effects for the across position: the run per bit of turning, \(\frac{dx}{d\theta}\).
\(\frac{dx}{d\theta}\ne0\)
the marker must be moving sideways; if not, the tangent is vertical.
Trap

The slope is not \(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\). \(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\) only measures how fast the distance from the pole changes; e.g. for the circle \(r=1\), \(\frac{dr}{d\theta}=0\) everywhere, but the tangent slopes take every value.

Going further · horizontal/vertical tangents, tangent at the pole

Horizontal tangent: numerator \(\frac{dy}{d\theta}=0\), denominator \(\ne0\). Vertical tangent: \(\frac{dx}{d\theta}=0\), numerator \(\ne0\). If \(r=0\) at \(\theta=\theta_0\) (the curve passes through the pole) and \(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\ne0\) there, the formula simplifies to \(\frac{dy}{dx}=\tan\theta_0\): the tangent at the pole is the line \(\theta=\theta_0\).

Example 12 · tangent to the cardioid

\[\begin{aligned}x&=r\cos\frac{\pi}{3}=\frac{2+\sqrt3}{4},\\ y&=r\sin\frac{\pi}{3}=\frac{(2+\sqrt3)\sqrt3}{4}=\frac{3+2\sqrt3}{4}.\end{aligned}\]

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dy}{d\theta}&=\frac12\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}+\frac{2+\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac12\\&=\frac{\sqrt3+2+\sqrt3}{4}=\frac{1+\sqrt3}{2},\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}\frac{dx}{d\theta}&=\frac12\cdot\frac12-\frac{2+\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\&=\frac{1-2\sqrt3-3}{4}=-\frac{1+\sqrt3}{2}\ne0.\end{aligned}\]

9Area in polar form ~6 min

What is this?

As the lighthouse beam turns through a tiny angle, it sweeps out a thin pizza slice from the lighthouse to the curve. Add up all the slices as the beam turns from the starting angle to the finishing angle, and you have the area. Each part of the region must be swept exactly once, so choose the range of angles that traces it once.

For \(y=f(x)\), the area under the curve was built from thin rectangles (Riemann sums, Lecture 11):

\[A=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.\]

In words: area = limit of the total of (height × width) over thinner and thinner strips.

For a polar curve, the natural pieces are thin sectors (pizza slices) fanning out from the pole. Three pictures help: (1) a single sector with radius \(r\) and angle \(\theta\); (2) the region \(\mathcal R\) bounded by the curve \(r=f(\theta)\) and the two rays \(\theta=a\) and \(\theta=b\); (3) that region cut into thin sectors between rays \(\theta=\theta_{i-1}\) and \(\theta=\theta_i\), each of angle \(\Delta\theta\), with the \(i\)-th sector given radius \(f(\theta_i^*)\).

A sector of radius \(r\) and angle \(\Delta\theta\) is the fraction \(\frac{\Delta\theta}{2\pi}\) of a full disc, so its area is \(\frac{\Delta\theta}{2\pi}\cdot\pi r^2=\frac12r^2\Delta\theta\). Adding the sectors:

Area in polar form \[\begin{aligned}A&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac12\left[f(\theta_i^*)\right]^2\Delta\theta\\&=\int_a^b\frac12\left[f(\theta)\right]^2d\theta=\int_a^b\frac12r^2\,d\theta.\end{aligned}\]

In words: area swept out by the radius = integral of half the radius squared, with respect to the angle, from the starting ray \(\theta=a\) to the finishing ray \(\theta=b\).

Reading the formula
\(\frac12r^2\,d\theta\)
the area of one thin slice of radius \(r\) and angle \(d\theta\).
\(r^2\)
a slice with twice the radius has four times the area, not twice.
\(a\), \(b\)
the beam's starting and finishing directions.
\(\int\)
adding up all the slices as the beam turns.
Trap

Each piece of the region must be swept once. Choose the \(\theta\)-interval that traces the region exactly once (e.g. one loop), not automatically \(0\) to \(2\pi\).

Example 13 · one loop of a four-leaf rose

Find the area enclosed by one loop of the four-leaf rose \(r=\cos2\theta\).

A rose is a curve \(r=a\cos n\theta\) or \(a\sin n\theta\): it has petals ("leaves", "loops"). This one has four equal loops, centred on the positive and negative \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes, each of length 1.

Step 1: find the \(\theta\)-range of one loop. The loop on the positive \(x\)-axis starts and ends at the pole, where \(r=0\): \(\cos2\theta=0\) gives \(\theta=\pm\frac{\pi}{4}\). Between them, \(\cos2\theta\gt0\), so the loop is traced once for \(-\frac{\pi}{4}\le\theta\le\frac{\pi}{4}\). A loop starts and ends at the lighthouse, so the marker is at \(r=0\) at both ends of the sweep.

Step 2: integrate, using \(\cos^2 2\theta=\frac{1+\cos4\theta}{2}\):

\[\begin{aligned}A&=\int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4}\frac12\cos^2 2\theta\,d\theta\\&=\frac14\int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4}(1+\cos4\theta)\,d\theta\\&=\frac14\left[\theta+\frac{\sin4\theta}{4}\right]_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4}\\&=\frac14\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}=\frac{\pi}{8}.\end{aligned}\]

So one loop has area \(\frac{\pi}{8}\) (all four loops together: \(\frac{\pi}{2}\)).

Note on the other loops: for \(\frac{\pi}{4}\lt\theta\lt\frac{3\pi}{4}\), \(r\lt0\), so the points are on the opposite side: this draws the loop on the negative \(y\)-axis, e.g. \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\) gives \(r=-1\), the point \((0,-1)\). Check this in the sketching figure of section 7.

Polar area from thin sectors

curvesectors

Each sector uses the smaller of the radii at its two edges, so the sectors sit (almost entirely) inside the region and the total \(\sum\frac12 r^2\Delta\theta\) is a little too small. As \(n\) grows it approaches the integral. (For \(r=2\) every sector fits exactly, so the sum equals \(4\pi\) for every \(n\).)

You can now: explain why polar area uses sectors \(\frac12r^2\Delta\theta\), not rectangles; find the \(\theta\)-range that traces a loop once; compute the area of a loop of a rose, or the inside of a cardioid, with \(A=\int\frac12r^2\,d\theta\).

10Summary

  • Parameterization frees us from the constraint of \(y=f(x)\) (one \(y\) per \(x\)) and unlocks more natural and powerful tools to model curves.
  • Polar coordinates are a circle-centric way of seeing things: position = distance from a centre + direction.

11Common mistakes

Parametric curves

✗Assuming \(\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{dt}\). For \(x=t^2,\ y=t^3\), writing \(\frac{dy}{dx}=3t^2\) is wrong. ✓ Divide by \(\frac{dx}{dt}=2t\): \(\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{3t^2}{2t}=\frac{3t}{2}\) (for \(t\ne0\)). \(\frac{dy}{dt}\) is the vertical speed, not the slope. ✗Computing the area without the differential. For \(x=t^2,\ y=t^3,\ 0\le t\le1\), writing \(A=\int y\,dx=\int t^3\,dt\) forgets that \(dx\ne dt\). ✓ \(dx=2t\,dt\), so \(A=\int_0^1 t^3\cdot2t\,dt=\int_0^1 2t^4\,dt=\frac25\). (Check: the curve is \(y=x^{3/2}\), and \(\int_0^1x^{3/2}dx=\frac25\). The wrong version gives \(\frac14\).) ✗Ignoring the parameter range. For \(x=t^2,\ y=t^4\), concluding "the curve is \(y=x^2\)". ✓ Since \(x=t^2\ge0\), it is only the right half: \(y=x^2,\ x\ge0\) (figure below).

x = t², y = t⁴ is only half of y = x²

x = t², y = t⁴ for −1.2 ≤ t ≤ 1.2rest of y = x² (never reached)

Both \(t\) and \(-t\) give the same point, so the right half is traced twice (down, then back up) and the left half is never reached.

Polar coordinates

✗Computing areas with rectangles instead of sectors. For \(r=2\): \(A=\int_0^{2\pi}2\,d\theta\) is the wrong formula (\(\int r\,d\theta\)). ✓ \(A=\int_0^{2\pi}\frac12\cdot2^2\,d\theta=4\pi\), the disc area \(\pi\cdot2^2\). ✗Plotting negative radii in the wrong direction. Saying "\((-2,\frac{\pi}{4})\) is in quadrant II or IV". ✓ Negative \(r\) means the opposite direction, \(\frac{\pi}{4}+\pi=\frac{5\pi}{4}\): the point is \((-\sqrt2,-\sqrt2)\), in quadrant III. (Try the "Negative r" preset in section 5.) ✗Using the wrong formula for slope, e.g. \(\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dr}{d\theta}\), or forgetting the Product Rule terms \(r\cos\theta\), \(-r\sin\theta\). ✓ Use \(\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{r'\sin\theta+r\cos\theta}{r'\cos\theta-r\sin\theta}\) where \(r'=\frac{dr}{d\theta}\).

About the \(r=2\) example above: the wrong integral \(\int_0^{2\pi}2\,d\theta\) also equals \(4\pi\), the correct area, only by coincidence (because \(\frac12\cdot2^2=2\)). With \(r=3\) the wrong method gives \(6\pi\) but the true area is \(\int_0^{2\pi}\frac92\,d\theta=9\pi\).

Treating θ like t

✗Using a too-long range for a polar curve. \(r=\cos\theta\) for \(\theta\in[0,4\pi]\) gives multiple coordinates for the same points: \(r=\cos\theta\) is a circle (centre \((\frac12,0)\), radius \(\frac12\)) traced once every \(\pi\), so this range traces it four times. ✓ Find the range that traces the curve once (here \([0,\pi]\)) before computing an area. ✗Same for parametric curves. \(x=\cos t,\ y=\sin t,\ t\in[0,4\pi]\) traces the unit circle twice; \(\int y\,dx\) over that range would double count. ✓ Use \([0,2\pi]\) (or the part you need).

Two more:

✗Dividing by \(\frac{dx}{dt}=0\). Where \(\frac{dx}{dt}=0\) and \(\frac{dy}{dt}\ne0\) the tangent is vertical, not "slope 0" or "undefined, so ignore". ✓ Check \(\frac{dx}{dt}\) first and say "vertical tangent". ✗Picking the wrong quadrant from \(\tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}\). For \((-1,1)\), \(\tan^{-1}(-1)=-\frac{\pi}{4}\) points into quadrant IV. ✓ The point is in quadrant II, so \(\theta=\frac{3\pi}{4}\).

12Group work

1. A curve is defined by the parametric equations \(x(t)=t^2-1,\ y(t)=t^3-3t\).
(a) Find the coordinates when \(t=0,1,2\). (b) Find \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) in terms of \(t\). (c) Find the points where the tangent line is horizontal.

Hint

(a) Substitute. (b) Differentiate \(x\) and \(y\) with respect to \(t\) and divide. (c) Horizontal means \(\frac{dy}{dt}=0\) while \(\frac{dx}{dt}\ne0\). Try the "Group work 1" curve in the section 3 figure.

Answer

(a) \(t=0\): \((-1,0)\). \(t=1\): \((0,-2)\). \(t=2\): \((3,2)\).

(b) \(\frac{dx}{dt}=2t,\ \frac{dy}{dt}=3t^2-3\), so \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{3t^2-3}{2t}=\dfrac{3(t^2-1)}{2t}\) for \(t\ne0\).

(c) \(\frac{dy}{dt}=0\iff t=\pm1\), and there \(\frac{dx}{dt}=\pm2\ne0\). \(t=1\): \((0,-2)\); \(t=-1\): \((0,2)\). Horizontal tangents at \((0,-2)\) and \((0,2)\). (Also: at \(t=0\), \(\frac{dx}{dt}=0\) and \(\frac{dy}{dt}=-3\ne0\), so the tangent at \((-1,0)\) is vertical.) The curve crosses itself at \((2,0)\) (\(t=\pm\sqrt3\)).

2. Find the area under the curve \(x=\cos t,\ y=\sin t,\ 0\le t\le\frac{\pi}{2}\).

Hint

Use \(A=\int y\,\frac{dx}{dt}\,dt\). Watch which way \(x\) moves as \(t\) increases.

Answer

\(\frac{dx}{dt}=-\sin t\). \(\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin t\cdot(-\sin t)\,dt=-\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2t\,dt=-\frac{\pi}{4}\).

The minus sign appears because \(x\) goes from \(1\) down to \(0\) (right to left). Either take the absolute value, or reverse the limits so that \(x\) runs left to right: \(A=\int_{\pi/2}^{0}\sin t(-\sin t)\,dt=\frac{\pi}{4}\). Area \(=\frac{\pi}{4}\), which is a quarter of the unit disc, as expected.

3. Consider the polar equation \(r=2-2\cos\theta\).
(a) Sketch the curve by plotting points for \(\theta=0,\frac{\pi}{3},\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{2\pi}{3},\pi,\frac{4\pi}{3},\frac{3\pi}{2},\frac{5\pi}{3}\). (b) Identify the type of curve and any symmetries. (c) Find the Cartesian equation by eliminating \(\theta\).

Hint

(a) Fill a table of \(r\). (b) Compare with the cardioid of Example 11; test \(\theta\to-\theta\). (c) Write \(\cos\theta=\frac{x}{r}\), multiply by \(r\), and use \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\).

Answer
\(\theta\)\(0\)\(\frac{\pi}{3}\)\(\frac{\pi}{2}\)\(\frac{2\pi}{3}\)\(\pi\)\(\frac{4\pi}{3}\)\(\frac{3\pi}{2}\)\(\frac{5\pi}{3}\)
\(r\)\(0\)\(1\)\(2\)\(3\)\(4\)\(3\)\(2\)\(1\)

(a), (b) A cardioid with its cusp at the pole (\(\theta=0\)), pointing left: furthest point \((-4,0)\) at \(\theta=\pi\), passing through \((0,\pm2)\). Symmetric about the \(x\)-axis (\(\cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta\)). See "Group work 3" in the section 7 figure.

(c) \(r=2-2\frac{x}{r}\Rightarrow r^2=2r-2x\Rightarrow x^2+y^2+2x=2\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). Squaring:

\[(x^2+y^2+2x)^2=4(x^2+y^2).\]

4. Find the area enclosed by one loop of the polar curve \(r=2\cos(2\theta)\).

Hint

Same loop range as Example 13: \(-\frac{\pi}{4}\le\theta\le\frac{\pi}{4}\). Use \(\cos^22\theta=\frac{1+\cos4\theta}{2}\).

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}A&=\int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4}\frac12\cdot4\cos^22\theta\,d\theta\\&=\int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4}(1+\cos4\theta)\,d\theta\\&=\left[\theta+\frac{\sin4\theta}{4}\right]_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4}=\frac{\pi}{2}.\end{aligned}\]

Area \(=\frac{\pi}{2}\): four times Example 13's \(\frac{\pi}{8}\), since doubling \(r\) multiplies areas by 4.

5. For the polar curve \(r=1+2\sin\theta\): (a) sketch the curve; (b) find the slope of the tangent line at \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{6}\).

Hint

(a) Find where \(r=0\) and where \(r\lt0\); negative \(r\) makes a small inner loop. (b) Use the polar slope formula with \(\frac{dr}{d\theta}=2\cos\theta\).

Answer

(a) A limaçon with an inner loop (a limaçon is \(r=a+b\sin\theta\) or \(a+b\cos\theta\)), symmetric about the \(y\)-axis. Outer loop: top point \((0,3)\) at \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\), crosses the \(x\)-axis at \((\pm1,0)\). \(r=0\) at \(\theta=\frac{7\pi}{6},\frac{11\pi}{6}\); for \(\frac{7\pi}{6}\lt\theta\lt\frac{11\pi}{6}\), \(r\lt0\), giving a small inner loop above the pole reaching \((0,1)\) (at \(\theta=\frac{3\pi}{2}\), \(r=-1\)). See "Group work 5" in the section 7 figure.

(b) At \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{6}\): \(r=2\), \(\frac{dr}{d\theta}=2\cos\frac{\pi}{6}=\sqrt3\).

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{\sqrt3\cdot\frac12+2\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}}{\sqrt3\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}-2\cdot\frac12}=\frac{\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}}{\frac12}=3\sqrt3.\]

Slope \(=3\sqrt3\approx5.196\).

13Concept checks

1. Do \(x=t,\ y=t^2\) and \(x=t^3,\ y=t^6\) (both for all real \(t\)) give the same curve? What about \(x=t^2,\ y=t^4\)?

Answer

The first two both give the whole parabola \(y=x^2\) (because \(t^3\) takes every real value): same curve, different parameterizations. \(x=t^2,\ y=t^4\) gives only the right half \(x\ge0\).

2. Convert the polar point \((-3,\frac{\pi}{2})\) to Cartesian, and give a polar name for it with \(r\gt0\).

Answer

\(x=-3\cos\frac{\pi}{2}=0,\ y=-3\sin\frac{\pi}{2}=-3\): the point \((0,-3)\). With positive \(r\): \((3,\frac{3\pi}{2})\) or \((3,-\frac{\pi}{2})\).

3. For \(x=\cos t,\ y=\sin t\), at which \(t\) in \([0,2\pi)\) is the tangent vertical? Does this match the picture?

Answer

\(\frac{dx}{dt}=-\sin t=0\) at \(t=0,\pi\), and there \(\frac{dy}{dt}=\cos t=\pm1\ne0\). Vertical tangents at \((1,0)\) and \((-1,0)\): the left and right ends of the circle, as expected.

4. Why does the polar area formula have \(\frac12r^2\) and not \(r\)? Check it on the disc \(r=2\).

Answer

The pieces are sectors, of area \(\frac12r^2\Delta\theta\), not rectangles of area \(r\Delta\theta\). For \(r=2\): \(\int_0^{2\pi}\frac12\cdot4\,d\theta=4\pi=\pi\cdot2^2\). Correct.

5. Show that \(r=2\sin\theta\) is a circle, and find its centre and radius.

Answer

Multiply by \(r\): \(x^2+y^2=2y\), i.e. \(x^2+(y-1)^2=1\). Centre \((0,1)\), radius 1 (it passes through the pole). It also matches the circle formula of section 6 with \(h=0,k=1,R=1\): \(r=\sin\theta\pm|\sin\theta|\), i.e. \(2\sin\theta\) or \(0\).

6. A particle moves along \(x=t^2,\ y=t^3\). Its speed is \(\sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2+\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\). Find the speed at \(t=1\), and the slope of its path there.

Answer

\(\frac{dx}{dt}=2,\ \frac{dy}{dt}=3\) at \(t=1\). Speed \(=\sqrt{4+9}=\sqrt{13}\). Slope \(=\frac32\).
