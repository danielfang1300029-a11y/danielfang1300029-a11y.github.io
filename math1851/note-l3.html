Limits Involving Infinity

0Where this lecture sits

Infinity enters this lecture in two ways. Either the graph shoots up (or down) a wall at some \(x\), or \(x\) runs off for ever and we ask what level the graph settles to in the long run. Both lead to asymptotes: straight lines that a graph gets closer and closer to far away from the origin.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 An infinite limit is the graph shooting up or down a wall. Check each side of the wall for the sign.
  2. §2 A limit at infinity is the long-run level the graph settles to.
  3. §3 The Limit Laws and the Sandwich Theorem still work far out, as long as every limit you split into is a real number.
  4. §4 Divide by the highest power to see which term dominates far away. Watch the sign of \(\sqrt{x^2}\), and rationalise a difference of roots.
  5. §5 Asymptotes are the lines the graph hugs: walls (vertical), long-run levels (horizontal) and long-run slopes (oblique).
  6. §6–§11 Summary, common mistakes, find the mistake, group work, concept checks and the exam taster.

Recall the Sandwich Theorem from Lecture 2:

Recall: Sandwich Theorem (Lecture 2)

Suppose that (i) \(g(x)\le f(x)\le h(x)\) for all \(x\) in some open interval containing \(c\), except possibly at \(x=c\), and (ii) \(\lim_{x\to c}g(x)=\lim_{x\to c}h(x)=L\). Then

\[\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L.\]

In words: if \(f\) is trapped between a lower function and an upper function, and both of those head to the same value, then \(f\) is squeezed to that value too.

Corollary:

\[\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}=1\]

In words: for a small angle in radians, sine of the angle ÷ the angle ≈ 1.

Picture a wiggly \(f\) sandwiched between a lower curve \(g\) and an upper curve \(h\), all three pinched together at the point \((c,L)\).

What this lecture adds. So far every limit was a finite number and \(x\) approached a finite number. Now infinity enters in two different ways:

  • Infinite limits: the output \(f(x)\) grows without bound (for example \(\frac1{x^2}\) as \(x\to0\)).
  • Limits at infinity: the input \(x\) grows without bound (for example \(\frac1x\) as \(x\to\infty\)).

Both lead to asymptotes: straight lines that a graph gets closer and closer to far away from the origin.

1Infinite limits ~10 min

What is this?

Picture a graph running towards a wall that stands at \(x=x_0\). As it gets nearer the wall it climbs higher and higher, past any ceiling you care to name. That climb is an infinite limit, and the wall is a vertical asymptote. Only the approach matters: what happens exactly at the wall (if anything) plays no part. The graph may climb on one side of the wall and plunge on the other, so always look at each side on its own.

The picture

Picture the graph of a function like \(f(x)=\frac1{x^2}\): a tall spike at \(x=0\), symmetric on both sides, with the graph rising higher and higher as \(x\) gets near \(0\) from either side. Two observations:

  • The graph shows that \(\lim_{x\to0}f(x)\) does not exist: there is no real number \(L\) that \(f(x)\) settles towards.
  • But we can say more: \(\lim_{x\to0^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to0^+}f(x)=\infty\notin\mathbb R\).

Here \(\mathbb R\) is the set of all real numbers and \(\notin\) means "is not a member of". So "\(\infty\notin\mathbb R\)" says: infinity is not a real number. Writing "\(=\infty\)" is not claiming the limit is a number; it is a short way to say how the limit fails to exist: the values grow beyond every bound, in the positive direction.

Definition: infinite limit (intuitive)

We write

\[\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=+\infty\quad\text{or}\quad\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=\infty\]

and say "the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(x_0\) is infinity", or "\(f(x)\) becomes infinite as \(x\) approaches \(x_0\)", or "\(f(x)\) increases without bound as \(x\) approaches \(x_0\)", if the values of \(f(x)\) can be made arbitrarily large for all \(x\) sufficiently close to \(x_0\) but not equal to \(x_0\).

In words: name any height you like (100, a million, a billion); as long as \(x\) is close enough to \(x_0\) (and \(x\ne x_0\)), \(f(x)\) is above that height.

\(\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=-\infty\) is defined similarly: the values of \(f(x)\) can be made arbitrarily large negative (below any depth you name) for all \(x\) sufficiently close to \(x_0\).

One-sided versions (\(x\to x_0^-\), \(x\to x_0^+\)) work the same way, using only \(x\) on one side of \(x_0\).

Terms. "Arbitrarily large" means "as large as anyone demands". "Sufficiently close" means "close enough; how close may depend on the demand". The pattern is a challenge and response: you demand a height, and a closeness can always be found that guarantees it.

Does not exist vs infinite limit (learning objective 1)

"The limit is \(\infty\)" is a special kind of "does not exist". Three different situations, all "DNE" (does not exist):

\(\lim_{x\to0}\frac1{x^2}=\infty\)Both sides go up. We may write \(=\infty\).
\(\lim_{x\to0}\frac1x\)Left side \(\to-\infty\), right side \(\to+\infty\). The sides disagree: DNE, and we may not write \(\infty\) for the two-sided limit.
\(\lim_{x\to0}\sin\frac1x\)Wobbles between \(-1\) and \(1\) for ever. DNE, and not infinite either.

When an exam says "if the limit involves infinity explain whether it is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\)", it wants you to find the sign from each side.

How to find the sign: "nonzero over something heading to 0"

What is this? (the sign rule)

Dividing by a tiny number asks "how many tiny pieces fit in?". Six divided into pieces of size 0.001 gives 6000 pieces; make the pieces smaller and the count grows without limit. So a fixed amount over a shrinking amount is huge: that is the climb up the wall. Whether it climbs or plunges is just the usual sign rule.

Most infinite limits in this course look like \(\frac{\text{top}}{\text{bottom}}\) where the top approaches a nonzero number and the bottom approaches \(0\). Then the fraction blows up, and its sign is decided by the signs of the top and of the bottom.

Working rule

If \(\text{top}\to a\ne0\) and \(\text{bottom}\to0\) while staying positive (written \(0^+\)), or staying negative (written \(0^-\)), then

\[\frac{a}{0^+}\to\begin{cases}+\infty,&a\gt0\\-\infty,&a\lt0\end{cases}\] \[\frac{a}{0^-}\to\begin{cases}-\infty,&a\gt0\\+\infty,&a\lt0\end{cases}\]

In words: a fixed number divided by a tiny number is huge; the sign follows the usual rule "same signs give positive, different signs give negative".

\(0^+\) and \(0^-\) are shorthand, not numbers: "a quantity heading to 0 through positive values" and "through negative values".

Reading the formula
\(a\)
the fixed amount on top. It does not shrink, which is why the fraction blows up.
\(0^+\), \(0^-\)
the bottom shrinking to nothing, staying on the positive or on the negative side: which side of the wall you approach from decides this.
\(\pm\infty\)
the graph climbs (\(+\)) or plunges (\(-\)) at the wall. Same signs on top and bottom: climb; different signs: plunge.

The method, as used in Example 1 below:

  1. Do: Try substituting. If the top tends to a nonzero number and the bottom to \(0\), expect an infinite limit (not the \(\frac00\) case, where a factor may cancel). Nonzero over tiny is huge, so you are standing next to a wall; only the direction of the climb is left to find.
  2. Do: Factor the bottom and find the sign of each factor near the point. Factors that keep one sign near the point cannot change the answer; only the factor that passes through \(0\) flips sign as you cross the wall.
  3. Do: Left side: decide whether the bottom \(\to0^-\) or \(\to0^+\), and use the working rule. Each side of the wall is a separate approach, and the graph may go a different way on each.
  4. Do: Right side: the same. Same reason: the other side of the wall.
  5. Do: Compare. Same sign on both sides: write \(=\infty\) or \(=-\infty\). Different signs: the two-sided limit does not exist; state both one-sided limits. Either way \(x=x_0\) is a vertical asymptote. A two-sided limit needs both approaches to agree; the wall is there as soon as one side climbs or plunges.

Example 1

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\frac{\sqrt{x+1}}{x^2-1}\).

Step 1: try substituting. At \(x=1\) the top is \(\sqrt2\) and the bottom is \(1-1=0\). "Nonzero over zero" is not a number, and it is not the \(\frac00\) situation of Lecture 1 either (no factor will cancel). It signals an infinite limit, so look at each side.

Step 2: factor the bottom.

\[\frac{\sqrt{x+1}}{x^2-1}=\frac{\sqrt{x+1}}{(x-1)(x+1)}\]

As \(x\to1\): \(\sqrt{x+1}\to\sqrt2\gt0\) and \(x+1\to2\gt0\). The sign of the bottom is therefore the sign of \(x-1\). \(x+1\) stays positive near \(x=1\), so only \(x-1\), which changes sign at the wall, decides climb or plunge.

Step 3: left side. For \(x\) slightly less than 1, \(x-1\lt0\), so the bottom \(\to0^-\):

\[\lim_{x\to1^-}\frac{\sqrt{x+1}}{x^2-1}=\frac{\sqrt2}{0^-}=-\infty\]

Step 4: right side. For \(x\) slightly more than 1, \(x-1\gt0\), so the bottom \(\to0^+\):

\[\lim_{x\to1^+}\frac{\sqrt{x+1}}{x^2-1}=\frac{\sqrt2}{0^+}=+\infty\]

Conclusion. The one-sided limits are \(-\infty\) and \(+\infty\). They disagree, so \(\lim_{x\to1}\frac{\sqrt{x+1}}{x^2-1}\) does not exist (and is neither \(\infty\) nor \(-\infty\)). The line \(x=1\) is a vertical asymptote. Choose "Example 1" in the explorer above to see both sides.

The domain is \(x\ge-1\), \(x\ne\pm1\). At the edge \(x=-1\) both top and bottom are \(0\), but cancelling \(\sqrt{x+1}\) gives \(\frac1{(x-1)\sqrt{x+1}}\), whose bottom \(\to(-2)(0^+)=0^-\). So \(\lim_{x\to-1^+}f(x)=-\infty\): the line \(x=-1\) is a vertical asymptote too (only the right side exists).

You can now: tell an infinite limit apart from other ways a limit fails to exist; find each one-sided limit of "nonzero over something heading to 0" with its sign; and say when a two-sided limit may be written as \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\).

2Limits at infinity ~5 min

What is this?

Leave a hot cup of tea on the desk. Its temperature keeps falling, more and more slowly, and in the long run it settles at room temperature: wait long enough and it is as close to room temperature as you like. That long-run level is the limit at infinity, and on the graph of temperature against time it is a horizontal line the curve settles onto. Not everything has a long-run level: a savings balance that keeps growing runs off to \(\infty\), and the tide, rising and falling for ever, never settles (does not exist).

The picture

Now the input \(x\) runs off to the right for ever. The graph below shows three functions that behave differently as \(x\to\infty\).

The curves drawn are \(f(x)=1+\frac{0.8}{x}\), \(g(x)=0.5x-2+\frac{2}{x+0.6}\) and \(h(x)=6+\sin 3x\), chosen to show the three behaviours. Move the slider: \(f\) gets closer and closer to the dashed line \(y=1\), so \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=1\); \(g\) gets bigger than any number, so \(\lim_{x\to\infty}g(x)=\infty\); \(h\) never settles, so \(\lim_{x\to\infty}h(x)\) does not exist.

These are the three possible outcomes listed later in the summary: as \(x\to\pm\infty\), \(f(x)\to L\) (a number), \(f(x)\to\pm\infty\), or the limit does not exist (DNE).

Definition: limit at infinity (intuitive)

Suppose \(f(x)\) is a function defined on an open interval \((c,\infty)\) (that is, for every \(x\gt c\)). We write

\[\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=L\]

and say "the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches infinity is \(L\)", or "the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) increases without bound is \(L\)", if we can make the values of \(f(x)\) arbitrarily close to \(L\) by taking \(x\) sufficiently large.

In words: name any tolerance you like (within 0.1 of \(L\), within 0.000 001 of \(L\)); from some point onwards along the \(x\)-axis, every value of \(f(x)\) is within that tolerance of \(L\).

Reading the formula
\(x\to\infty\)
time running on: you wait longer and longer, with no end.
\(f(x)\)
the tea's temperature at time \(x\).
\(L\)
room temperature: the long-run level.
\(=\)
"gets as close as you like if you wait long enough". The curve need not ever equal \(L\).

The limit at negative infinity, \(\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)=L\), is defined similarly, with \(f\) defined on some \((-\infty,c)\) and \(x\) taken sufficiently large negative.

The condition "defined on \((c,\infty)\)" just makes sure \(f(x)\) exists for all large \(x\), so that it makes sense to ask where it heads. For example \(\sqrt{x^2-1}\) is not defined for \(-1\lt x\lt1\), but it is defined on \((1,\infty)\), which is all we need for \(x\to\infty\).

What is this? (the precise definition)

The precise definition is a challenge game. Someone names a tolerance, say 0.01 °C. You must answer with a time after which the tea always stays within 0.01 °C of room temperature. If you can answer every challenge, however small the tolerance, the limit is room temperature.

Going further: the precise definitions

The definitions above use intuitive wording; the rules are proved using the "\(\epsilon,\delta\)" style of definition. For completeness, the precise versions are:

  • \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=L\) means: for every \(\epsilon\gt0\) there is a number \(M\) such that \(x\gt M\) implies \(|f(x)-L|\lt\epsilon\).
  • \(\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=\infty\) means: for every \(B\gt0\) there is a \(\delta\gt0\) such that \(0\lt|x-x_0|\lt\delta\) implies \(f(x)\gt B\).

In words: \(\epsilon\) is the demanded tolerance, \(M\) is "how far right is far enough"; \(B\) is the demanded height, \(\delta\) is "how close is close enough".

Example: for \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1x=0\), given \(\epsilon\), take \(M=\frac1\epsilon\): if \(x\gt\frac1\epsilon\) then \(0\lt\frac1x\lt\epsilon\).

You can now: read \(\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)=L\) as the long-run level of a graph; name the three possible outcomes (a number, \(\pm\infty\), or DNE); and say why \(f\) must be defined on some \((c,\infty)\).

3Limit laws and the Sandwich Theorem at ±∞ ~6 min

What is this?

The Limit Laws are a recipe: find the long-run level of each piece, then combine the levels with ordinary arithmetic. The recipe only takes real numbers as ingredients. A piece that runs off to \(\infty\) jams it, because \(\infty\) is not a number: two pieces that both run off can leave a gap of any size between them.

The good news: almost all the tools of Lectures 1 and 2 still work when \(x\to\infty\) or \(x\to-\infty\). We simply replace "\(x\to c\)" by "\(x\to\pm\infty\)". (The notation \(x\to\pm\infty\) is shorthand: each statement holds for \(x\to\infty\), and separately for \(x\to-\infty\).)

Theorem: Limit Laws at ±∞

If \(\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)\) and \(\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}g(x)\) exist (as real numbers), then:

1. Sum Rule

\[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}[f(x)+g(x)]=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)+\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}g(x)\]

In words: limit of a sum = sum of the limits.

2. Difference Rule

\[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}[f(x)-g(x)]=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)-\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}g(x)\]

In words: limit of a difference = difference of the limits.

3. Constant Multiple Rule

\[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}[k\,f(x)]=k\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)\]

In words: a constant factor \(k\) can be pulled outside the limit.

4. Product Rule

\[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}[f(x)\,g(x)]=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)\cdot\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}g(x)\]

In words: limit of a product = product of the limits.

5. Quotient Rule, provided \(\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}g(x)\ne0\):

\[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)}{\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}g(x)}\]

In words: limit of a quotient = limit of the top ÷ limit of the bottom, as long as the bottom's limit is not zero.

6. Power Rule, for \(n\in\mathbb Z^+\) (a positive whole number):

\[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}[f(x)]^n=\Big[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)\Big]^n\]

In words: limit of a power = the power of the limit.

7. Root Rule, for \(n\in\mathbb Z^+\):

\[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\sqrt[n]{f(x)}=\sqrt[n]{\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)}\]

In words: limit of an \(n\)th root = the \(n\)th root of the limit.

If \(n\) is even, we assume that \(\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)=L\gt0\) (an even root of a negative number is not real).

Two remarks: these are exactly the Limit Laws stated in Lecture 1 with \(c\) replaced by \(\pm\infty\); and, as in Lecture 1, use substitution, factorisation and rationalisation as needed to get an expression the laws can handle. ("Rationalisation" means multiplying top and bottom by a conjugate such as \(\sqrt A+B\) to remove a difference of square roots.)

On the even-root condition: it is stated above with \(L\gt0\); the rule also holds when \(L=0\) (provided \(f(x)\ge0\)). Strictness does not matter in this lecture.

Trap: the laws need limits that exist

"Exist" means "is a real number". \(\infty\) is not one. So you may not split \(\lim(\sqrt{x^2+5x}-x)\) into \(\lim\sqrt{x^2+5x}-\lim x\): both pieces are \(\infty\), and "\(\infty-\infty\)" tells you nothing. This is the first item in Common Mistakes (Section 7).

What is this? (the Sandwich Theorem at ±∞)

Picture a prisoner walked down a long corridor between two guards. If, from some point of the walk onwards, the prisoner is always between the guards, and both guards end up at the same door, the prisoner ends up at that door too. What happens early in the walk does not matter.

Theorem: Sandwich Theorem at ±∞

Suppose that (i) \(g(x)\le f(x)\le h(x)\) for all sufficiently large \(x\), and (ii) \(\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}g(x)=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}h(x)=L\). Then

\[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)=L.\]

In words: if, far enough out, \(f\) is trapped between two functions that both level off at \(L\), then \(f\) levels off at \(L\) too.

This is the Sandwich Theorem of Lecture 2 with \(c\) replaced by \(\pm\infty\). "For all sufficiently large \(x\)" replaces "in some open interval containing \(c\)": the trapping only has to hold from some point onwards (for \(x\to-\infty\): for all sufficiently large negative \(x\)).

What is this? (the basic limits)

Share one pizza among \(x\) people. As the crowd grows without bound, each slice shrinks towards nothing: that is \(\frac1x\to0\). Dividing by \(x\) again (\(\frac1{x^2}\)) shrinks it faster still. When \(x\to-\infty\), \(\frac1x\) is a small negative number, but it still heads to 0.

Theorem: basic limits at infinity

1. Constant Rule

\[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}k=k\]

In words: a constant stays the same however far out you go.

2. Reciprocal Rule

\[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac1x=0\]

In words: 1 divided by a huge number (positive or negative) is nearly 0.

3. Reciprocal Power Rule, for \(n\in\mathbb Z^+\):

\[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\Big[\frac1x\Big]^n=0\]

In words: \(\frac1{x^n}\) also goes to 0, for any positive whole number \(n\).

These are proved using the precise (\(\epsilon\)) definition of a limit at infinity (see the "Going further" box in Section 2). Rule 3 also follows from Rule 2 and the Power Rule: \(\big[\frac1x\big]^n\to0^n=0\).

Together, the Limit Laws plus these three rules handle any rational function (polynomial ÷ polynomial) after one trick: divide top and bottom by a suitable power of \(x\). Section 4 shows how.

Going further: also true for fractional powers

For any positive power \(p\) (not just whole numbers), \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1{x^p}=0\); for example \(\frac1{x^{1/3}}=\frac1{\sqrt[3]x}\to0\). Group work question 1 needs this.

You can now: use the Limit Laws at \(\pm\infty\) only when every limit involved is a real number (and the bottom's is nonzero); trap a bounded wobbly function and apply the Sandwich Theorem at \(\pm\infty\); and quote \(\frac1{x^n}\to0\).

4Worked examples at ±∞ ~7 min

What is this?

Picture a fortune made of a large company, a car and some coins in a pocket. Ask "roughly how rich?" and only the company matters. For huge \(x\), \(x^4\) is the company and \(3x^2\) and \(x\) are the car and the coins: they are still there, but next to \(x^4\) they hardly count. Dividing top and bottom by the highest power measures everything in units of the biggest term, so the small terms visibly shrink to 0 and only the leading terms are left to compare.

Example 2

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^4-3x^2+x}{2-x+2x^4}\).

Why we cannot substitute. Top and bottom both grow without bound: "\(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\)" is not an answer, and the Quotient Rule needs finite limits.

The method. For limits at infinity of rational functions, divide the numerator and the denominator by the highest power of \(x\) in the denominator. Here that is \(x^4\):

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{x^4-3x^2+x}{2-x+2x^4}&=\frac{\frac{x^4}{x^4}-\frac{3x^2}{x^4}+\frac{x}{x^4}}{\frac{2}{x^4}-\frac{x}{x^4}+\frac{2x^4}{x^4}}\\&=\frac{1-\frac{3}{x^2}+\frac{1}{x^3}}{\frac{2}{x^4}-\frac{1}{x^3}+2}\end{aligned}\]

Dividing top and bottom by the same nonzero number does not change the fraction. Now every piece has a limit: by the Reciprocal Power Rule, \(\frac3{x^2},\frac1{x^3},\frac2{x^4}\to0\). The bottom's limit is \(2\ne0\), so the Quotient Rule applies:

\[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^4-3x^2+x}{2-x+2x^4}=\frac{1-0+0}{0-0+2}=\frac12\]

In words: for huge \(x\), only the biggest powers matter: \(\frac{x^4}{2x^4}=\frac12\).

Reading the formula
\(1\) and \(2\)
the leading terms \(x^4\) and \(2x^4\), measured in units of \(x^4\): the company on top and on the bottom.
\(\frac3{x^2},\ \frac1{x^3},\ \frac2{x^4}\)
the car and the coins, measured against the company: they shrink to 0 far out.
\(\frac12\)
the long-run level: the ratio of the two dominant terms.

So \(y=\frac12\) is a horizontal asymptote (Section 5). The same working with \(x\to-\infty\) also gives \(\frac12\).

The method, as steps:

  1. Do: Find the highest power of \(x\) in the denominator. That is the dominant term of the bottom: the company in the picture.
  2. Do: Divide the numerator and the denominator, term by term, by that power. Dividing top and bottom by the same nonzero number does not change the fraction, but now every term is measured against the dominant one.
  3. Do: Send every \(\frac{k}{x^n}\) to 0 with the Reciprocal Power Rule. These are the small terms (the car and the coins), which are negligible far out.
  4. Do: If the bottom's limit is a nonzero number, finish with the Quotient Rule. Only now does every piece have a real limit, and a nonzero bottom is exactly what the Quotient Rule asks for.
Going further: the degree shortcut for rational functions

Write \(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\) with leading terms \(a x^m\) on top and \(b x^n\) on the bottom (\(m,n\) are the degrees, the highest powers). Dividing by \(x^n\) always gives:

  • \(m\lt n\): the limit as \(x\to\pm\infty\) is \(0\).
  • \(m=n\): the limit is \(\frac ab\), the ratio of the leading coefficients.
  • \(m\gt n\): the limit is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\) (no horizontal asymptote); when \(m=n+1\) there is an oblique asymptote (Section 5).

Use it to check answers; in written work show the division, as the markers expect the reasoning.

Example 3

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}+\sqrt{x^2-1}}\).

What is this? (square roots far to the left)

A square root forgets the sign: \(\sqrt{x^2}\) is the size of \(x\), its distance from 0, never negative. Far to the left \(x\) is a large negative number such as \(-1000\), and its size is \(1000=-x\). So when you pull the dominant size out of a root on the left, a minus sign appears.

Key fact. \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\) (the square root is never negative). When \(x\to-\infty\), \(x\) is negative, so

\[\sqrt{x^2}=|x|=-x\quad(x\lt0)\]

In words: for a negative number, the square root of its square is the number with its minus sign removed, which is \(-x\).

So the "size" of the bottom is about \(|x|+|x|=-2x\). Divide top and bottom by \(|x|=-x\). \(|x|\) is the dominant size of the bottom; it is positive, so dividing by it keeps every sign honest, and it can go inside a root as \(\sqrt{x^2}\). Inside a square root, dividing by \(|x|\) means dividing by \(\sqrt{x^2}\):

\[\frac{\sqrt{x^2+1}}{|x|}=\sqrt{\frac{x^2+1}{x^2}}=\sqrt{1+\frac1{x^2}}\]

and similarly for \(\sqrt{x^2-1}\). Also \(\frac{x}{-x}=-1\). Hence

\[\begin{aligned}&\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}+\sqrt{x^2-1}}\\&=\frac{-1}{\sqrt{1+\frac1{x^2}}+\sqrt{1-\frac1{x^2}}}\\&\to\frac{-1}{\sqrt{1+0}+\sqrt{1-0}}\\&=-\frac12\end{aligned}\]

(Root Rule with limit \(1\gt0\), then Sum and Quotient Rules.) Answer: \(-\frac12\).

As \(x\to+\infty\) the same steps with \(|x|=x\) give \(+\frac12\). The function has two different horizontal asymptotes, \(y=\frac12\) on the right and \(y=-\frac12\) on the left. Treating \(\sqrt{x^2}\) as \(x\) for negative \(x\) would give the wrong sign.

Example 4

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{x^2+5x}-x\right)\).

What is this? (a difference of two huge terms)

Picture two cars driving off along the same road: one is at distance \(\sqrt{x^2+5x}\), the other at \(x\). Both distances grow without bound, yet the gap between the cars can settle to a fixed length. "\(\infty-\infty\)" says nothing about that gap. Multiplying by the conjugate rewrites the gap as a fraction, and then the dominant-term method of Example 2 can measure it.

Why it is tricky. Both \(\sqrt{x^2+5x}\) and \(x\) grow without bound. "\(\infty-\infty\)" could be anything; we need to see exactly how much bigger the root is.

Rationalise: write \(A=\sqrt{x^2+5x}\) for short. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate \(A+x\), and use \((A-x)(A+x)=A^2-x^2\):

\[\begin{aligned}A-x&=\frac{(A-x)(A+x)}{A+x}=\frac{A^2-x^2}{A+x}\\&=\frac{(x^2+5x)-x^2}{\sqrt{x^2+5x}+x}\\&=\frac{5x}{\sqrt{x^2+5x}+x}\end{aligned}\]

Divide by \(x\) (here \(x\gt0\), so \(x=\sqrt{x^2}\)): after rationalising, top and bottom both grow like \(x\), so this is now Example 2's situation: measure everything in units of \(x\).

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{5x}{\sqrt{x^2+5x}+x}&=\frac{5}{\sqrt{1+\frac5x}+1}\\&\to\frac{5}{\sqrt1+1}=\frac52\end{aligned}\]

Answer: \(\frac52\).

In words: the square root is bigger than \(x\) by an amount that settles at \(2.5\).

Intuition: for large \(x\), \(\sqrt{x^2+5x}=x\sqrt{1+\frac5x}\approx x\left(1+\frac{5}{2x}\right)=x+\frac52\). (The approximation \(\sqrt{1+t}\approx1+\frac t2\) for small \(t\) is beyond this course; use it only to check.)

Example 5

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x}\).

Why the laws alone fail. \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\sin x\) does not exist (it oscillates), so the Quotient Rule cannot be used. But \(\sin x\) is bounded (trapped between \(-1\) and \(1\)), which is perfect for the Sandwich Theorem.

Trap it. For all \(x\), \(-1\le\sin x\le1\). For \(x\gt0\), dividing by \(x\) keeps the inequality signs: \(\sin x\) never settles, but it can never leave the band from \(-1\) to \(1\); dividing by a growing positive \(x\) squeezes that band shut. These are the two guards.

\[-\frac1x\le\frac{\sin x}{x}\le\frac1x\qquad(x\gt0)\]

In words: \(\frac{\sin x}x\) always lies between the curves \(-\frac1x\) and \(\frac1x\).

Squeeze. \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(-\frac1x\right)=0\) and \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1x=0\) (Reciprocal Rule). By the Sandwich Theorem at \(\infty\),

\[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x}=0.\]

sin x / x±1/x

The blue curve bounces between the two orange curves, which both close in on \(0\). Note that the graph crosses the line \(y=0\) (its horizontal asymptote) infinitely many times: a graph may cross a horizontal asymptote.

You can now: find limits at \(\pm\infty\) of rational functions by dividing by the highest power in the denominator; handle square roots with \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\), taking \(-x\) when \(x\to-\infty\); rationalise an \(\infty-\infty\) difference of roots; and squeeze a bounded oscillating top with the Sandwich Theorem.

5Asymptotes of graphs ~9 min

What is this?

Zoom out on a graph. Wiggles near the origin shrink away, and if a far end of the graph lines up with a straight line, that line is an asymptote. There are three kinds. A vertical asymptote is the wall of Section 1. A horizontal asymptote is the long-run level of Section 2, like room temperature for the cooling tea. An oblique asymptote is a long-run slope: far out, the graph runs alongside a slanted straight road.

An asymptote is a straight line that a graph gets arbitrarily close to as it heads off to infinity (either sideways or up/down). There are three kinds.

Definition: horizontal asymptote

A line \(y=L\) is a horizontal asymptote of the graph of a function \(y=f(x)\) if either

\[\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=L\quad\text{or}\quad\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)=L.\]

In words: far to the right, or far to the left, the graph levels off at height \(L\).

A function can have at most 2 horizontal asymptotes: one from \(x\to\infty\) and one from \(x\to-\infty\) (each limit has at most one value). Example 3 had two: \(y=\frac12\) and \(y=-\frac12\).

Definition: vertical asymptote

A line \(x=c\) is a vertical asymptote of the graph of \(y=f(x)\) if either

\[\lim_{x\to c^+}f(x)=\pm\infty\quad\text{or}\quad\lim_{x\to c^-}f(x)=\pm\infty.\]

In words: as \(x\) closes in on \(c\) from at least one side, the graph shoots up or down without bound.

Where to look: try all \(c\) such that \(f(x)\) is undefined (zeros of a denominator, edges of the domain of a root or log). Then check the one-sided limits there. Only one side needs to blow up.

Trap: undefined does not mean asymptote

Being undefined at \(c\) makes \(c\) a candidate, nothing more. For example \(\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=x+1\) (for \(x\ne1\)) is undefined at \(x=1\), but \(\lim_{x\to1}\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=2\): the graph is a straight line with a single missing point (a "hole"), not an asymptote. Always compute the limit. If the top also tends to \(0\), factor and cancel first.

What is this? (oblique asymptote)

Picture a car joining a straight motorway from a slip road. Near the junction it wanders, but far along it drives in the lane, closer and closer to the lane's centre line. That line is the oblique asymptote. To find it you need two things: the road's slope (how fast the height grows per step along) and where the road sits (its intercept).

Definition: oblique asymptote

A line \(y=mx+b\) is an oblique (slanted) asymptote of the graph of \(y=f(x)\) if and only if

\[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{f(x)}{x}=m\quad\text{and}\] \[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\big[f(x)-mx\big]=b.\]

In words: far out, (height ÷ distance along) settles at the slope \(m\), and (height − slope × \(x\)) settles at the intercept \(b\). Together: far out, the graph and the line \(y=mx+b\) get as close as you like.

Reading the formula
\(\lim\frac{f(x)}{x}=m\)
height ÷ distance along, far out: the slope of the road.
\(\lim\big[f(x)-mx\big]=b\)
take away the climb due to the slope; what is left settles at the road's height where it crosses the \(y\)-axis.
\(y=mx+b\)
the road itself: the straight line the graph drives alongside far out.

"If and only if" means the two conditions are exactly equivalent to the line being an oblique asymptote: both must hold (for the same direction, \(x\to\infty\) or \(x\to-\infty\)).

Why these two limits? If \(f(x)\approx mx+b\) for huge \(x\), then \(\frac{f(x)}x\approx m+\frac bx\to m\), which finds the slope; once \(m\) is known, \(f(x)-mx\approx b\), which finds the intercept. (If \(m\) turns out to be \(0\), the line is \(y=b\): a horizontal asymptote is the special case \(m=0\).)

The "Example 6" option in the explorer below shows a graph with a vertical asymptote and a slanted dashed line: the two branches hug the slanted line far out. Some facts:

  • Oblique asymptotes occur in rational functions whose numerator degree is 1 more than the denominator degree (for example quadratic ÷ linear).
  • A function can have at most 2 oblique asymptotes (one for \(x\to\infty\), one for \(x\to-\infty\)).
  • \(mx+b\) is the dominant term, also known as the quotient in polynomial division. "Dominant term" means the part of \(f(x)\) that matters for huge \(x\); the leftover remainder part tends to \(0\).

Polynomial division (HKDSE Core): dividing \(P(x)\) by \(D(x)\) gives a quotient \(Q(x)\) and a remainder \(R(x)\) of lower degree than \(D(x)\), with \(P(x)=D(x)Q(x)+R(x)\), so

\[\frac{P(x)}{D(x)}=Q(x)+\frac{R(x)}{D(x)}\]

In words: fraction = quotient + remainder ÷ divisor. Far out, remainder ÷ divisor \(\to0\), so the graph approaches \(y=Q(x)\).

Reading the formula
\(Q(x)\)
the road: when it is \(mx+b\), it is the oblique asymptote.
\(\frac{R(x)}{D(x)}\)
the detour from the road. The remainder has lower degree than the divisor, so far out the detour fades to 0.
\(D(x)=0\)
where the detour itself blows up: candidates for walls (vertical asymptotes).

Finding all asymptotes, as steps (Example 6 follows them):

  1. Do: Vertical: try all \(c\) such that \(f(x)\) is undefined, and compute the one-sided limits there. Walls can only stand where the formula breaks down, but a hole is not a wall, so the limit must be checked.
  2. Do: Horizontal: compute \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)\) and \(\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)\) separately. The long-run level on the right and on the left can differ (Example 3).
  3. Do: Oblique (in a direction with no horizontal asymptote): find \(m=\lim\frac{f(x)}x\), then \(b=\lim\big[f(x)-mx\big]\). First the slope of the road; once its climb is taken away, what is left settles at the road's height.
  4. Do: For a rational function, check by polynomial division. The quotient is the road and remainder ÷ divisor is the detour that fades away.
Example 6

Find all asymptotes of \(\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{x^2+3x-4}{x-2}\).

Vertical. \(f\) is undefined only at \(x=2\). There the top is \(4+6-4=6\ne0\) and the bottom \(\to0\): nonzero over zero, so an infinite limit. Signs:

\[\lim_{x\to2^-}f(x)=\frac{6}{0^-}=-\infty\] \[\lim_{x\to2^+}f(x)=\frac{6}{0^+}=+\infty\]

So \(x=2\) is a vertical asymptote (the graph goes down on the left of it and up on the right).

Horizontal. The top has degree 2 and the bottom degree 1. Dividing by \(x\): \(f(x)=\frac{x+3-\frac4x}{1-\frac2x}\), whose top \(\to\pm\infty\) while the bottom \(\to1\). So \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=\infty\) and \(\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)=-\infty\): no horizontal asymptote.

Oblique. Slope:

\[\begin{aligned}m&=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{f(x)}{x}\\&=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{x^2+3x-4}{x^2-2x}\\&=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{1+\frac3x-\frac4{x^2}}{1-\frac2x}=1\end{aligned}\]

Intercept:

\[\begin{aligned}b&=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\big[f(x)-x\big]\\&=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{x^2+3x-4-x(x-2)}{x-2}\\&=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{5x-4}{x-2}\\&=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{5-\frac4x}{1-\frac2x}=5\end{aligned}\]

So \(y=x+5\) is an oblique asymptote, in both directions.

Check by polynomial division: \(x^2+3x-4=(x-2)(x+5)+6\), so

\[f(x)=x+5+\frac{6}{x-2}\]

In words: \(f\) = the line \(x+5\) + a remainder part \(\frac6{x-2}\) that dies away far out (and blows up near \(x=2\)).

Answer: vertical asymptote \(x=2\); oblique asymptote \(y=x+5\); no horizontal asymptote. Choose "Example 6" in the zoom-out explorer to see it.

You can now: find the vertical asymptotes of a function from its one-sided limits (ruling out holes); find horizontal asymptotes from the limits at \(\infty\) and \(-\infty\) separately; and find an oblique asymptote from \(m\) and \(b\), checked by polynomial division.

6Summary

Limits involving infinity describe the behaviour of functions:

  • Infinite limits: the output grows without bound.
  • Limits at infinity: the input grows without bound.

For \(c,L\in\mathbb R\) (real numbers):

Infinite limitsLimits at infinity
Variable behaviour\(x\to c\)\(x\to\pm\infty\)
Function behaviour\(f(x)\to\pm\infty\)\(f(x)\to L\), \(\pm\infty\), or DNE
Asymptote\(x=c\) (vertical)\(y=L\) (horizontal)
Example\(\lim_{x\to0}\frac1{x^2}=+\infty\)\(\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1x=0\)

Use limits to find the asymptotes that describe the behaviour of functions as \(x\) or \(y\) grow very large:

  • Vertical: \(x=c\) for all \(c\) such that \(\lim_{x\to c^\pm}f(x)=\pm\infty\) (at least one side).
  • Horizontal: \(y=L\) where \(\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)=L\).
  • Oblique: \(y=mx+b\) where
\[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{f(x)}{x}=m\quad\text{and}\] \[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\big[f(x)-mx\big]=b\]

In words: slope = limit of \(f(x)\div x\); intercept = limit of \(f(x)-\text{slope}\times x\).

7Common mistakes

An indeterminate form is a pattern such as \(\infty-\infty\), \(\frac\infty\infty\), \(\frac00\) or \(1^\infty\) that can come out as any value, depending on the functions involved. Reaching one means "more work needed", never "the answer is…".

Using Limit Laws when the conditions are not met

✗\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{x^2+5x}-x\right)=\lim_{x\to\infty}\sqrt{x^2+5x}-\lim_{x\to\infty}x\)
The Difference Rule needs both limits to exist as real numbers; both are \(\infty\). ✓ Rationalise first (Example 4): the limit is \(\frac52\). ✗\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^2}{e^x}=\frac{\lim_{x\to\infty}x^2}{\lim_{x\to\infty}e^x}\)
The Quotient Rule needs both limits to exist (and the bottom's to be nonzero); both are \(\infty\). ✓ The true limit is \(0\) (see the box below).

Substitutions resulting in indeterminate forms

✗\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{x^4+3x}-\sqrt{x^4-x^2}\right)=\infty-\infty=0\)
\(\infty-\infty\) is indeterminate, not \(0\). ✓ Rationalise: \[\begin{aligned}&\frac{(x^4+3x)-(x^4-x^2)}{\sqrt{x^4+3x}+\sqrt{x^4-x^2}}\\&=\frac{x^2+3x}{\sqrt{x^4+3x}+\sqrt{x^4-x^2}}\end{aligned}\] then divide by \(x^2\): \(\dfrac{1+\frac3x}{\sqrt{1+\frac3{x^3}}+\sqrt{1-\frac1{x^2}}}\to\dfrac12\). The answer is \(\frac12\), not \(0\). ✗\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^2}{e^x}=\frac\infty\infty=1\)
\(\frac\infty\infty\) is indeterminate; two things that both grow can grow at very different speeds. ✓ The limit is \(0\). ✗\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(1+\frac1x\right)^x=1^\infty=1\)
\(1^\infty\) is indeterminate: the base is slightly more than 1 but it is raised to a huge power. ✓ The limit is \(e\approx2.718\).
Going further: the two \(e\) answers

\(e\approx2.71828\) is a special constant, and \(e^x\) is the exponential function with that base; both are studied in Lecture 7. \(e^x\) grows faster than any power of \(x\), which is why \(\frac{x^2}{e^x}\to0\) (e.g. at \(x=20\): \(\frac{400}{e^{20}}\approx8\times10^{-7}\)). The value \(\lim_{x\to\infty}(1+\frac1x)^x=e\) can be taken as a definition of \(e\). The general tool for \(\frac\infty\infty\) and \(1^\infty\) forms is L'Hôpital's Rule (Lecture 10). In this lecture, the point is only that the substitutions above are wrong.

Neglecting one-sided limits

✗\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac1x=\infty\)
✓ \(\lim_{x\to0^-}\frac1x=-\infty\) and \(\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac1x=+\infty\). The sides disagree, so the two-sided limit does not exist and is not \(\infty\). (Choose "1/x" in the explorer of Section 1.)

Two more

✗"\(\sqrt{x^2}=x\)" when \(x\to-\infty\). ✓ \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|=-x\) for negative \(x\) (Example 3). This flips the sign of the answer. ✗"\(f\) is undefined at \(c\), so \(x=c\) is a vertical asymptote." ✓ Compute the one-sided limits; a common factor may cancel and leave a hole instead (Section 5).

Here is a student's handwritten answer to this question:

4. Find the horizontal asymptotes of the function \(f(x)=\sqrt{x^2+3x}-\sqrt{x^2-2x}\). Justify each step in your computation.

The student's work, reproduced faithfully:

\[\begin{aligned}y&=\lim_{x\to\infty}\sqrt{x^2+3x}-\sqrt{x^2-2x}\\&=\lim_{x\to\infty}x-x=0\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}y&=\lim_{x\to-\infty}\sqrt{x^2+3x}-\sqrt{x^2-2x}\\&=\lim_{x\to-\infty}(-x)-(-x)=0\end{aligned}\]
Hint 1

The student replaced \(\sqrt{x^2+3x}\) by \(x\) and \(\sqrt{x^2-2x}\) by \(x\). Each replacement is "roughly right" for huge \(x\), but what is the size of the error in each, and do the errors cancel?

Hint 2

This is Example 4 in disguise: an \(\infty-\infty\) form with square roots. Rationalise with the conjugate \(\sqrt{x^2+3x}+\sqrt{x^2-2x}\). For \(x\to-\infty\) remember \(\sqrt{x^2}=-x\).

Answer

The mistake. Dropping the \(3x\) and \(-2x\) inside the roots throws away exactly the part that survives. The approximations are \(\sqrt{x^2+3x}\approx x+\frac32\) and \(\sqrt{x^2-2x}\approx x-1\) (for large positive \(x\)); their difference is about \(\frac52\), not \(0\). Replacing each piece by \(x\) and subtracting is really the forbidden step "\(\infty-\infty=0\)". (The student did correctly use \(\sqrt{x^2}=-x\) for negative \(x\); the error is the same in both parts.)

Correct working. Rationalise:

\[\begin{aligned}f(x)&=\frac{(x^2+3x)-(x^2-2x)}{\sqrt{x^2+3x}+\sqrt{x^2-2x}}\\&=\frac{5x}{\sqrt{x^2+3x}+\sqrt{x^2-2x}}\end{aligned}\]

As \(x\to\infty\), divide by \(x=\sqrt{x^2}\):

\[\begin{aligned}f(x)&=\frac{5}{\sqrt{1+\frac3x}+\sqrt{1-\frac2x}}\\&\to\frac{5}{1+1}=\frac52\end{aligned}\]

As \(x\to-\infty\), divide by \(|x|=-x\): the top becomes \(\frac{5x}{-x}=-5\), so

\[\begin{aligned}f(x)&=\frac{-5}{\sqrt{1+\frac3x}+\sqrt{1-\frac2x}}\\&\to-\frac52\end{aligned}\]

Answer: two horizontal asymptotes, \(y=\frac52\) (as \(x\to\infty\)) and \(y=-\frac52\) (as \(x\to-\infty\)). Choose "Find the mistake" in the zoom-out explorer of Section 5 to see both.

\(f\) is defined for \(x\le-3\) or \(x\ge2\) (both expressions under the roots must be \(\ge0\)), so both limits make sense.

9Group work

1. Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^{1/3}-5x+3}{2x+x^{2/3}-4}\).

Hint

The highest power in the denominator is \(x^1\) (since \(\frac23\lt1\)). Divide top and bottom by \(x\); the fractional powers become \(x^{-2/3}=\frac1{x^{2/3}}\) and \(x^{-1/3}=\frac1{x^{1/3}}\), which tend to 0.

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}\frac{x^{1/3}-5x+3}{2x+x^{2/3}-4}&=\frac{\frac1{x^{2/3}}-5+\frac3x}{2+\frac1{x^{1/3}}-\frac4x}\\&\to\frac{0-5+0}{2+0-0}=-\frac52\end{aligned}\]

Answer: \(-\frac52\).

2. Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{4x-3}{\sqrt{25x^2+4x}}\).

Hint

\(x\to-\infty\), so \(\sqrt{x^2}=-x\). Divide top and bottom by \(-x\) (as in Example 3).

Answer

For \(x\lt0\), \(\sqrt{25x^2+4x}=|x|\sqrt{25+\frac4x}=-x\sqrt{25+\frac4x}\). Dividing by \(-x\):

\[\frac{4x-3}{\sqrt{25x^2+4x}}=\frac{-4+\frac3x}{\sqrt{25+\frac4x}}\to\frac{-4}{5}\]

Answer: \(-\frac45\). (A common slip gives \(+\frac45\) by using \(\sqrt{x^2}=x\).)

3. Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x+\cos x}{x^2}\).

Hint

\(\cos x\) has no limit but is bounded: \(-1\le\cos x\le1\). Trap the whole fraction and use the Sandwich Theorem (as in Example 5).

Answer

Since \(-1\le\cos x\le1\), for \(x\gt0\):

\[\frac{x-1}{x^2}\le\frac{x+\cos x}{x^2}\le\frac{x+1}{x^2}\]

Both outer functions equal \(\frac1x\mp\frac1{x^2}\to0\). By the Sandwich Theorem, the limit is \(0\).

Answer: \(0\).

4. Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{4x^4+9x}-2x^2\right)\).

Hint

\(\infty-\infty\) with a square root: rationalise with \(\sqrt{4x^4+9x}+2x^2\), then divide by \(x^2\).

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}&\sqrt{4x^4+9x}-2x^2\\&=\frac{(4x^4+9x)-4x^4}{\sqrt{4x^4+9x}+2x^2}\\&=\frac{9x}{\sqrt{4x^4+9x}+2x^2}\\&=\frac{\frac9x}{\sqrt{4+\frac9{x^3}}+2}\to\frac{0}{4}=0\end{aligned}\]

Answer: \(0\). (Here the top is only degree 1 while the bottom behaves like \(4x^2\), so it dies away; compare Example 4, where top and bottom had the same degree and the answer was nonzero.)

Hint 1

Vertical: is \(4x^2+3x+2\) ever zero (or negative)? Look at its discriminant \(\Delta=b^2-4ac\).

Hint 2

Horizontal: compute the limit at \(\infty\) (divide by \(x\)) and at \(-\infty\) (divide by \(-x\)) separately.

Horizontal: as \(x\to\infty\), dividing by \(x=\sqrt{x^2}\):

\[f(x)=\frac{1-\frac9x}{\sqrt{4+\frac3x+\frac2{x^2}}}\to\frac12\]

As \(x\to-\infty\), dividing by \(-x=\sqrt{x^2}\):

\[f(x)=\frac{-1+\frac9x}{\sqrt{4+\frac3x+\frac2{x^2}}}\to-\frac12\]

Oblique: none (in each direction the graph levels off, so \(m=\lim\frac{f(x)}x=0\): the asymptotes are the horizontal ones).

6. Sketch the graph of a function \(f(x)\) that satisfies all of the listed properties:

  • (b) \(\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)=1\)

10Concept checks

1. True or false: "\(\lim_{x\to2}\frac1{(x-2)^2}=\infty\), so the limit exists."

Answer

False. Writing \(=\infty\) is allowed (both sides go up), but \(\infty\) is not a real number, so the limit does not exist. "\(=\infty\)" describes how it fails to exist. The line \(x=2\) is a vertical asymptote.

2. Find \(\lim_{x\to3^-}\frac{x+1}{x-3}\) and \(\lim_{x\to3^+}\frac{x+1}{x-3}\).

Answer

The top \(\to4\gt0\). From the left, \(x-3\to0^-\): \(\frac{4}{0^-}=-\infty\). From the right, \(x-3\to0^+\): \(\frac4{0^+}=+\infty\). So \(-\infty\) and \(+\infty\); the two-sided limit does not exist.

3. Can the graph of a function cross its horizontal asymptote?

Answer

Yes. An asymptote only describes behaviour far out. \(\frac{\sin x}{x}\) crosses its horizontal asymptote \(y=0\) at every \(x=k\pi\) (\(k\ne0\)), infinitely many times (Example 5).

4. Find all vertical and horizontal asymptotes of \(\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{x^2-3x+2}\).

Answer

Factor: \(f(x)=\frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{(x-1)(x-2)}=\frac{x+1}{x-2}\) for \(x\ne1\). Candidates: \(x=1\) and \(x=2\).

  • \(x=1\): \(\lim_{x\to1}f(x)=\frac{2}{-1}=-2\), finite, so a hole at \((1,-2)\), not an asymptote.
  • \(x=2\): \(\frac{3}{0^-}\to-\infty\) from the left and \(\frac3{0^+}\to+\infty\) from the right: vertical asymptote \(x=2\).
  • \(x\to\pm\infty\): equal degrees, ratio of leading coefficients \(1\): horizontal asymptote \(y=1\).

Answer: \(x=2\) and \(y=1\) only.

5. Without long working, what is \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{2x^3-x}{1-4x^3}\)?

Answer

Equal degrees (3 and 3); dividing by \(x^3\) gives \(\frac{2-\frac1{x^2}}{\frac1{x^3}-4}\to\frac{2}{-4}=-\frac12\). Odd powers do not cause a sign issue here because the same power is divided out of top and bottom (no square roots). Answer: \(-\frac12\).

6. Can one function have a horizontal asymptote on one side and an oblique asymptote on the other? Test \(f(x)=\sqrt{x^2+1}+x\).

Answer

Yes. As \(x\to\infty\): \(\frac{f(x)}x=\sqrt{1+\frac1{x^2}}+1\to2\) and

\[\begin{aligned}f(x)-2x&=\sqrt{x^2+1}-x\\&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+1}+x}\to0\end{aligned}\]

so \(y=2x\) is an oblique asymptote on the right. As \(x\to-\infty\):

\[\begin{aligned}f(x)&=\frac{(x^2+1)-x^2}{\sqrt{x^2+1}-x}\\&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+1}-x}\to0\end{aligned}\]

(the bottom \(\to+\infty\) because \(-x\gt0\)), so \(y=0\) is a horizontal asymptote on the left. Each direction contributes at most one asymptote, horizontal or oblique.
