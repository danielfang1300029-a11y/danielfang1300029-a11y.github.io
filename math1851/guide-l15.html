MATH1851 Exam Guide · Parametric and Polar Curves

The short version

What to expect

Parametric and polar curves are a small but steady topic: 4 of the 13 recent papers have a part of this chapter (Dec 2025, Dec 2021, Dec 2020, May 2019), and 6 of the 10 older papers. Three more recent questions (May 2026, May 2025, Dec 2021) ask for the arc length of a polar or parametric curve; those are in the L17 guide but start with the set-up practised here. The curves change every time, but the jobs do not: find a slope, a speed or a tangent; sketch a polar curve; or match a polar equation to a picture. Each of the three types below has a standard procedure; follow it step by step.

How to practise: use Start here to pick the type from what the question gives you. Work the question with the procedure open, check with the Hint if stuck and with the Solution at the end. Every sketch solution shows the correct curve, drawn from computed points. Newest papers come first; 2013–2018 papers are tagged older format. For the ideas behind the formulas, use the Chapter note links. All answers checked with sympy.

Exam format

  • Where: one part (3–5%) of a longer Part I question, usually next to an arc-length, area or curve-sketching part about the same curve (Dec 2021 Q5, Dec 2020 Q2–3, May 2019 Q3). Dec 2025: free-response Q12 (10 pts), part (a).
  • Speed: Dec 2025 moved part of Part I to multiple choice, and May 2026 made Part I Q1–3 answers only. A slope or speed is a natural answers-only item, so be quick with \(\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}\). Sketch and "justify" questions are still written: there the table of points and the symmetry argument carry the marks.
  • Formula sheet: recent papers print trigonometric identities and a few standard derivatives and integrals only. They do not print \(\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}\), \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\), \(r^2=x^2+y^2\) or the symmetry tests. (Dec 2025 printed the speed formula inside the question; May 2025 Q4(a) gave \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\) as a hint. Do not count on it.)
  • Notation: a point on a polar question may be polar \((r,\theta)\) (May 2014 Q2(b): "the point \((2,\frac\pi3)\)") or Cartesian (Dec 2020 Q3(ii): "\((-1,0)\) (on the Cartesian plane)"). The angle may be called \(t\) instead of \(\theta\) (Dec 2020). "Horizontal speed" means \(\frac{dx}{dt}\) (May 2017).

L15.1Parametric curves: slope, speed and sketch

Chapter note · parametric curves · Chapter note · slope of a parametric curve

Frequency: 2 of 13 recent papers (Dec 2025, May 2019); 2 of 10 older papers (Dec 2017, Dec 2015). Arc length of a parametric curve is the L17 guide.

Recognise it

Both coordinates are given in terms of a third variable \(t\) (the parameter, often time): \(x=x(t)\), \(y=y(t)\). Or the curve is \(y=f(x)\) and only \(x(t)\) is given ("a particle moves along the curve …").

Standard procedure
  1. Write both coordinates in \(t\). If the curve is \(y=f(x)\) with \(x=x(t)\), either put \(y(t)=f(x(t))\), or keep the chain rule \(\frac{dy}{dt}=f^{\prime}(x)\,\frac{dx}{dt}\) for step 3.
  2. Find the parameter of the point. If \(t\) is given, skip. If a point \((a,b)\) is given, solve \(x(t)=a\) and \(y(t)=b\); look first for a \(t\) that makes the trig parts 0 or 1, and check both coordinates.
  3. Differentiate. Find \(\frac{dx}{dt}\) and \(\frac{dy}{dt}\) and put in that \(t\).
  4. Answer what is asked.
    • 4a. Slope: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}\) when \(\frac{dx}{dt}\ne0\). If \(\frac{dx}{dt}=0\) but \(\frac{dy}{dt}\ne0\), the tangent is vertical and has no slope. A tangent line is then \(y-y_0=m(x-x_0)\). Asked: May 2019, Dec 2017.
    • 4b. Speed: \(\sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2+\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\) at that \(t\). Asked: Dec 2025.
    • 4c. Sketch: table of \(t\), \(x\), \(y\) (endpoints and a middle value); remove \(t\) if easy (\(y\) in terms of \(x\)); mark where \(\frac{dx}{dt}=0\) (vertical tangent) or \(\frac{dy}{dt}=0\) (horizontal); arrow for the direction of increasing \(t\). Asked: Dec 2015.
Worked example · the procedure in action

May 2019 Q3(c) (Q3: 10%). Let \(h(t)=\sqrt t\,e^{-t}\) be defined for \(t\ge0\). The function \(h(t)\) attains a local maximum at \(t=a\) and has an inflection point at \(t=b\). Evaluate the slope of the tangent to the curve whose equation in parametric form is

\[\begin{aligned}x&=h(t)+\cos(\pi t),\\ y&=h(t)+\sin(2\pi t),\quad t\gt0,\end{aligned}\]

at the point \(\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2e}},\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2e}}\right)\).

Step 1 Both coordinates are already in \(t\).

Nothing to convert; go straight to finding \(t\).

Step 2 At \(t=\frac12\): \(\cos\frac\pi2=0\) and \(\sin\pi=0\), and

\[h\!\left(\tfrac12\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\,e^{-1/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2e}}.\]

So both \(x\) and \(y\) equal \(\frac1{\sqrt{2e}}\): the point is at \(t=\frac12\).

The trig parts vanish together at \(t=\frac12\), and \(\frac12\) is the \(a\) of part (a), so the question is built around it. Check both coordinates. (A numerical scan finds no other \(t\) giving this point.)

Step 3 \(h^{\prime}(t)=e^{-t}\left(\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt t}-\sqrt t\right)\), so \(h^{\prime}\!\left(\frac12\right)=0\) (it is the local maximum). Then

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dx}{dt}&=h^{\prime}(t)-\pi\sin(\pi t)\\&=0-\pi=-\pi,\\ \frac{dy}{dt}&=h^{\prime}(t)+2\pi\cos(2\pi t)\\&=0-2\pi=-2\pi.\end{aligned}\]

Using \(h^{\prime}(a)=0\) from part (a) saves the whole product-rule computation; say so in one line.

Step 4a \(\dfrac{dx}{dt}=-\pi\ne0\), so

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}=\frac{-2\pi}{-\pi}.\]
\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=2}\]

Common slip: dividing the other way round (\(\frac12\)). "\(y\) on top" because slope is rise over run.

Traps
  • Point given in \(x,y\), not \(t\). May 2019 gives \(\left(\frac1{\sqrt{2e}},\frac1{\sqrt{2e}}\right)\); differentiating before finding \(t=\frac12\) gets nowhere.
  • Curve given as \(y=f(x)\). Dec 2025 gives \(y=\ln(\sec x)\) and \(x(t)\). You need \(\frac{dy}{dt}\), not \(\frac{dy}{dx}\): use the chain rule \(\frac{dy}{dt}=\tan x\cdot\frac{dx}{dt}\).
  • Sketch without the key features. Dec 2015: the curve leaves the origin vertically (\(\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac1t\to\infty\)); a straight start or missing endpoint labels lose marks.

Dec 2025 Q12(a)Q12: 10 pts (Part I free response); part marks not shown

A particle moves along the curve \(y=\ln(\sec x)\) for \(0\le x\lt\frac\pi2\), with its \(x\)-coordinate given by \(x(t)=\tan^{-1}(t)\), \(t\ge0\).

(a) The speed of a particle with coordinates \((x(t),y(t))\) is given by \[v(t)=\sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2+\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}.\] Find the speed at time \(t=1\).

Part (c) (average curvature) is in the L12 guide; part (b) belongs to an earlier chapter.

Hint

Type L15.1, speed (step 4b). First step: \(\frac{dx}{dt}=\frac1{1+t^2}\); get \(\frac{dy}{dt}\) from the chain rule, \(\frac{dy}{dt}=\frac{dy}{dx}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt}\).

Solution

Step 1–2 \(t=1\) is given, so \(x=\tan^{-1}1=\frac\pi4\).

Step 3 \(\dfrac{dx}{dt}=\dfrac1{1+t^2}=\dfrac12\). Since \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln(\sec x)=\frac{\sec x\tan x}{\sec x}=\tan x\),

\[\frac{dy}{dt}=\tan x\cdot\frac{dx}{dt}=\tan\frac\pi4\cdot\frac12=\frac12.\]

Step 4b \(v(1)=\sqrt{\left(\frac12\right)^2+\left(\frac12\right)^2}=\sqrt{\frac12}\).

\[\boxed{v(1)=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}\]

Marker: show both \(\frac{dx}{dt}\) and \(\frac{dy}{dt}\) at \(t=1\) before substituting.

Equivalent forms. \(\frac1{\sqrt2}\approx0.707\). In general \(y=\frac12\ln(1+t^2)\) (because \(\sec(\tan^{-1}t)=\sqrt{1+t^2}\)) and \(v(t)=\frac1{\sqrt{1+t^2}}\).

Consider the function defined by the parametric equations: \(x=\cos t\), \(y=t+\sin t\), for \(0\le t\le\pi\). (a) Find the slope of the curve at \(t=\frac\pi2\).

Hint

Type L15.1, slope (step 4a); \(t\) is given. First step: \(\frac{dx}{dt}=-\sin t\), \(\frac{dy}{dt}=1+\cos t\).

Solution

Step 3 \(\dfrac{dx}{dt}=-\sin t\), \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=1+\cos t\). At \(t=\frac\pi2\): \(\frac{dx}{dt}=-1\), \(\frac{dy}{dt}=1\).

\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=-1}\]

Equivalent forms. The point is \(\left(0,\frac\pi2+1\right)\); tangent line \(y=-x+\frac\pi2+1\).

L15.2Polar curves: sketch, symmetry, identify the curve

Chapter note · polar coordinates · Chapter note · sketching polar curves and symmetry

Frequency: 2 of 13 recent papers (Dec 2021, Dec 2020); 4 of 10 older papers (May 2018, May 2017, Dec 2016, May 2014). The most common L15 type: 8 parts.

Recognise it

The curve is \(r=f(\theta)\) (\(r\) = distance from the origin, \(\theta\) = angle from the positive \(x\)-axis), and the question says "sketch", "plot", "graph", "discuss for symmetry", "find the equation in polar coordinates" or "which values of the constants match the figure".

Standard procedure
  1. Where is \(r\) defined, and for which \(\theta\)? Use the range given. For \(r^2=g(\theta)\) you need \(g(\theta)\ge0\), and both \(r=\pm\sqrt{g(\theta)}\) count. For \(\sin\frac\theta2\) or \(\cos\frac\theta2\), the full curve needs \(0\le\theta\le4\pi\).
  2. Symmetry tests. Replace, simplify, and compare with the original equation:
    • \(x\)-axis: \(\theta\to-\theta\) (or \(r\to-r,\ \theta\to\pi-\theta\)).
    • \(y\)-axis: \(\theta\to\pi-\theta\) (or \(r\to-r,\ \theta\to-\theta\)).
    • Origin: \(r\to-r\) (or \(\theta\to\theta+\pi\)).
    • An unchanged equation proves the symmetry. A changed one proves nothing: to rule a symmetry out, name one point on the curve whose mirror image is not on it.
  3. Table of \(r\) at the key angles \(\theta=0,\frac\pi6,\frac\pi3,\frac\pi2,\frac{2\pi}3,\pi,\dots\) (half the range if step 2 found a symmetry). Mark where \(r=0\) (the curve passes through the origin, along the line at that angle), the largest \(|r|\), and where \(r\lt0\) (the point is plotted in the opposite direction, at angle \(\theta+\pi\)).
  4. Join the points in order of \(\theta\), then reflect using step 2. Label the axis crossings.
  5. Answer what is asked.
    • 5a. Sketch / plot / graph: steps 1–4 are the answer. Name the shape if it is standard: \(r=2a\cos\theta\) circle, \(r=a(1\pm\cos\theta)\) cardioid, \(r=a+b\cos\theta\) limaçon. Asked: Dec 2020, May 2018, May 2017, Dec 2016, May 2014.
    • 5b. Polar equation of a Cartesian curve: put \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\), \(x^2+y^2=r^2\), then divide by \(r\) (the origin stays on the curve). Asked: Dec 2016.
    • 5c. Match constants to a figure: the points at \(\theta=0,\frac\pi2,\pi,\frac{3\pi}2\) lie on the axes, so read them off the figure and turn them into equations for the constants; then confirm with a feature such as how many times \(r=0\). Asked: Dec 2021.
    • 5d. Largest disk / extreme distance inside the curve: along a ray from the origin at angle \(\theta\) the region is \(0\le r\le f(\theta)\); bound the answer with that, then show a candidate reaches the bound (or optimise a distance, L9.4). Asked: May 2017.

The graphs of \(C\) (in Cartesian coordinate) for two different pairs of \((p,q)\) are sketched below. Determine the values of \(p\) and \(q\) for each figure. Justify your answer.

Step 1 \(r\) is defined for every \(\theta\) in \([0,2\pi]\); the unknowns are \(p\) and \(q\).

Nothing to exclude; go to the key angles.

Step 3 The four axis points, in terms of \(p,q\):

\(\theta\)\(r\)Cartesian point
\(0\)\(p\)\((p,0)\)
\(\frac\pi2\)\(p\cos\frac{q\pi}2+1\)\(\left(0,\,1+p\cos\frac{q\pi}2\right)\)
\(\pi\)\(p\cos q\pi\)\((-p\cos q\pi,\,0)\)
\(\frac{3\pi}2\)\(p\cos\frac{3q\pi}2-1\)\(\left(0,\,1-p\cos\frac{3q\pi}2\right)\)

These four angles give points on the axes, which are exactly what you can read accurately from a figure.

Step 5c Read the figures. Both figures meet the \(x\)-axis only at \(x=-1,0,1\) and the \(y\)-axis only at \(y=0\) and \(y=2\).

Step 5c Find \(p\). \((p,0)\) is on the curve and \(p\ge0\), so \(p=0\) or \(p=1\). If \(p=0\), \(r=\sin\theta\) is the circle \(x^2+\left(y-\frac12\right)^2=\frac14\), which is neither figure. So \(p=1\) for both.

"Justify" means: show the figure forces the value. Ruling out \(p=0\) explicitly is part of that.

Step 5c Find \(q\). \(\left(0,1+\cos\frac{q\pi}2\right)\) must have \(y=0\) or \(2\), so \(\cos\frac{q\pi}2=\pm1\), i.e. \(\frac q2\) is a whole number. With \(0\le q\le2\): \(q=0\) or \(q=2\).

Step 5c Which is which? Count the passes through the origin (\(r=0\)).

\[\begin{aligned}q=0:\ r&=1+\sin\theta=0\\&\iff\theta=\tfrac{3\pi}2\ \text{(once)},\\ q=2:\ r&=\cos2\theta+\sin\theta\\&=(1-\sin\theta)(1+2\sin\theta)=0\\&\iff\theta=\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac{7\pi}6,\tfrac{11\pi}6.\end{aligned}\]

One pass with a cusp is the cardioid of Figure 1; three passes give the three petals of Figure 2. Check the top point: \(q=0\), \(\theta=\frac\pi2\) gives \(r=2\), the point \((0,2)\); \(q=2\), \(\theta=\frac{3\pi}2\) gives \(r=-2\), again \((0,2)\).

Use \(\cos2\theta=1-2\sin^2\theta\) (on the formula sheet) to factorise. The marker wants a reason that separates the two figures, not "it looks like a cardioid".

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{Figure 1: }(p,q)=(1,0)\\&\text{Figure 2: }(p,q)=(1,2)\end{aligned}}\]

Figure 1 is \(r=1+\sin\theta\); Figure 2 is \(r=\cos2\theta+\sin\theta\).

Traps
  • Ignoring negative \(r\). May 2018 (\(r^2=\cos\theta\)) and Dec 2020 Q3(i) (\(r=m(\cdot)\lt0\) for \(0\le\theta\lt\frac\pi2\)) both need the points with \(r\lt0\) plotted in the opposite direction. Missing them loses half the curve.
  • Wrong \(\theta\) range. May 2014's \(r=1+2\sin\frac\theta2\) repeats only after \(4\pi\); stopping at \(2\pi\) misses the inner loops.
  • "Not symmetric" from a failed test. A test that changes the equation proves nothing. Give a point whose mirror image is not on the curve, e.g. \((-2,0)\) is on \(r=1-\cos\theta\) but \((2,0)\) is not (May 2017).
Ready answer · standard polar shapes
  • \(r=2a\cos\theta\): circle, centre \((a,0)\), through the origin. \(r=2a\sin\theta\): circle, centre \((0,a)\).
  • \(r=a(1\pm\cos\theta)\), \(r=a(1\pm\sin\theta)\): cardioid, cusp at the origin.
  • \(r=a+b\cos\theta\) with \(a\gt b\gt0\): limaçon without a loop (dimpled if \(a\lt2b\)); with \(a\lt b\): inner loop.

Use them to check a sketch, not to replace the table: the marks are for the table and the symmetry argument.

Let \(C\) be the plane curve whose polar equation is \(r=1-\cos\theta\) with \(\theta\) in the range \([0,2\pi]\). (c) Suppose \(D\) is a disk inside the region enclosed by curve \(C\) and the boundary of \(D\) may touch \(C\). What is the largest possible area of \(D\)? Explain clearly your answer.

This part also uses L9.4 (optimisation); it is solved here.

Hint

Type L15.2, largest disk (step 5d). First step: the origin is on \(C\), so it cannot be inside \(D\); follow the ray from the origin through the centre of \(D\).

Solution

Step 5d Bound. In the direction \(\theta\), the region is \(0\le r\le1-\cos\theta\le2\). Let \(D\) have centre \(P\) and radius \(\rho\). The origin \(O\) is on \(C\), so it is not inside \(D\): \(OP\ge\rho\). On the ray from \(O\) through \(P\), the far edge of \(D\) is at distance \(OP+\rho\ge2\rho\) from \(O\) and is in the region, so

\[2\rho\le OP+\rho\le1-\cos\theta\le2,\quad\text{so }\rho\le1.\]

Step 5d Reached. Take centre \((-1,0)\), radius 1. For a point of \(C\), with \(c=\cos\theta\) and \(r=1-c\),

\[\begin{aligned}(x+1)^2+y^2&=r^2+2r\cos\theta+1\\&=(1-c)^2+2(1-c)c+1\\&=2-c^2\ \ge\ 1.\end{aligned}\]

So every point of \(C\) is at distance at least 1 from \((-1,0)\): the disk fits, touching \(C\) at \(O\) and \((-2,0)\).

\[\boxed{\text{Largest area}=\pi}\]

Marker: both halves are needed: why no disk is bigger, and a disk that achieves it.

Equivalent forms. Radius 1, centre \((-1,0)\). L9.4 route: for centre \((-c,0)\) on the axis, the squared distance to \(C\) is \((1-2c)u^2+(2c-2)u+1+c^2\) with \(u=\cos\theta\); its minimum over \([-1,1]\) is \(\min(c,2-c)^2\), largest at \(c=1\). L15.3Polar curves: slope and horizontal/vertical tangents

Chapter note · slope of a polar curve

Frequency: 1 of 13 recent papers (Dec 2020); 3 of 10 older papers (May 2017, Dec 2016, May 2014).

Recognise it

A polar curve \(r=f(\theta)\) and the words "slope", "tangent", "horizontal" or "vertical", or a speed in the \(x\)- or \(y\)-direction.

Standard procedure
  1. Make it parametric in \(\theta\): \(x=f(\theta)\cos\theta\), \(y=f(\theta)\sin\theta\). (If the angle is called \(t\), use \(t\).)
  2. Find every \(\theta\) of the point. Polar point \((r_0,\theta_0)\): check \(f(\theta_0)=r_0\). Cartesian point: on the \(x\)-axis use \(\sin\theta=0\) (or \(r=0\) for the origin). Also check the other names of the same point, \((r_0,\theta_0+2\pi)\) and \((-r_0,\theta_0+\pi)\), inside the range.
  3. Differentiate. \(r^{\prime}=f^{\prime}(\theta)\), then \[\begin{aligned}\frac{dx}{d\theta}&=r^{\prime}\cos\theta-r\sin\theta,\\ \frac{dy}{d\theta}&=r^{\prime}\sin\theta+r\cos\theta.\end{aligned}\]
  4. Answer what is asked.
    • 4a. Slope at a point: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy/d\theta}{dx/d\theta}\). If \(\frac{dx}{d\theta}=0\) but \(\frac{dy}{d\theta}\ne0\), the tangent is vertical and the slope does not exist. Asked: May 2014, Dec 2020, Dec 2016.
    • 4b. Where is the tangent horizontal / vertical? Solve \(\frac{dy}{d\theta}=0\) (horizontal) or \(\frac{dx}{d\theta}=0\) (vertical) over the whole range; points where both are 0 (often the origin) need a separate look. "Horizontal speed is zero" with constant \(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\) means \(\frac{dx}{dt}=\frac{dx}{d\theta}\cdot\frac{d\theta}{dt}=0\), i.e. \(\frac{dx}{d\theta}=0\). Asked: May 2017.
    • 4c. "Explain why a horizontal tangent exists": find two angles where \(\frac{dy}{d\theta}\) has opposite signs; the Intermediate Value Theorem gives a zero between them; then show \(\frac{dx}{d\theta}\ne0\) on that interval. Asked: May 2014.
Worked example · the procedure in action

May 2014 Q2(b) (Q2: 10%). The curve (C) is given in polar coordinates by \(r=1+2\sin\frac\theta2\). Find the slope of the curve (C) at the point \(\left(2,\frac\pi3\right)\).

Step 1 \(x=\left(1+2\sin\frac\theta2\right)\cos\theta\), \(y=\left(1+2\sin\frac\theta2\right)\sin\theta\).

The slope is \(\frac{dy}{dx}\), never \(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\); we need \(x\) and \(y\).

Step 2 \(\left(2,\frac\pi3\right)\) is a polar point: \(r\left(\frac\pi3\right)=1+2\sin\frac\pi6=2\). ✓ Its other names, \(\left(2,\frac{7\pi}3\right)\) and \(\left(-2,\frac{4\pi}3\right)\), \(\left(-2,\frac{10\pi}3\right)\), are not on \(C\) (\(r=0\), \(1+\sqrt3\), \(1-\sqrt3\) there), so \(\theta=\frac\pi3\) is the only parameter.

Write the check \(r\left(\frac\pi3\right)=2\); it shows the marker you read the point as polar (Cartesian \((1,\sqrt3)\)).

Step 3 \(r^{\prime}=\cos\frac\theta2=\frac{\sqrt3}2\) at \(\theta=\frac\pi3\). With \(r=2\), \(\cos\theta=\frac12\), \(\sin\theta=\frac{\sqrt3}2\):

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dx}{d\theta}&=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\cdot\tfrac12-2\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}2=-\tfrac{3\sqrt3}4,\\ \frac{dy}{d\theta}&=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}2+2\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac74.\end{aligned}\]

Chain rule: \(\frac{d}{d\theta}\,2\sin\frac\theta2=\cos\frac\theta2\). Getting the \(\frac12\) wrong is the usual slip.

Step 4a \(\frac{dx}{d\theta}\ne0\), so

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{7/4}{-3\sqrt3/4}=-\frac{7}{3\sqrt3}.\]
\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{7\sqrt3}{9}}\]

\(\approx-1.347\). The curve falls as it passes \((1,\sqrt3)\) on the outer loop, which matches the sketch in May 2014 Q2(a).

Traps
  • Polar or Cartesian point? May 2014's \(\left(2,\frac\pi3\right)\) is polar; Dec 2020's \((-1,0)\) is Cartesian and is reached with \(r=-1\) at angle 0.
  • "Slope 0" for a vertical tangent. In Dec 2020 Q3(ii), \(\frac{dx}{dt}=0\) and \(\frac{dy}{dt}=-1\): the tangent is vertical and the slope does not exist.
  • Lost or doubled solutions. May 2017: \(\sin\theta(2\cos\theta-1)=0\) has five roots in \([0,2\pi]\) but four points (\(\theta=0\) and \(2\pi\) are both the origin).
Ready answer · tangent at the origin Beyond the handouts

If \(r(\theta_0)=0\) and \(r^{\prime}(\theta_0)\ne0\), the tangent at the origin is the line \(\theta=\theta_0\) (slope \(\tan\theta_0\)).

Why: put \(r=0\) in step 3: \(\frac{dy}{d\theta}=r^{\prime}\sin\theta_0\), \(\frac{dx}{d\theta}=r^{\prime}\cos\theta_0\). Useful for sketches (May 2018, Dec 2020 Q3(i): the curve reaches the origin along the \(y\)-axis).
