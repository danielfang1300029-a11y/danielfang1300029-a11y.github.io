Sandwich Theorem

0Where this lecture sits

Some limits cannot be done with the limit laws, because one piece has no limit at all. Picture a suspect walked between two police officers: however much he wriggles, if both officers walk through the same door, so does he. This lecture turns that picture into the Sandwich Theorem.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 Trap a function between a floor and a ceiling that head to the same value, and it must go there too.
  2. §2 A bounded wiggle times something shrinking to 0 shrinks to 0. Use absolute values when the multiplier can be negative.
  3. §3 For a tiny angle in radians, sine of the angle is almost the angle itself: \(\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\to1\). You will meet it again whenever derivatives of trig functions appear.
  4. §4 One over a limit is the limit of one over, as long as the limit is not 0.
  5. §5 Give every sine its own box: rearrange so each \(\sin\Box\) sits over the same \(\Box\).

You need: the limit from Lecture 1: \(\lim_{x\to c} f(x)=L\) means \(f(x)\) can be made as close to \(L\) as we like by taking \(x\) close enough to \(c\), but not equal to \(c\) (the value at \(c\) does not matter); \(x\to c^{+}\) approaches from the right (values bigger than \(c\)) and \(x\to c^{-}\) from the left (values smaller than \(c\)). In the exam: this lecture's question types (squeeze a bounded wiggle; make the boxes match) appear 8 times in the 23 past papers, usually inside Q1. See the L2 exam guide.

Recall: the limit laws

Recall the limit laws from Lecture 1. They all start with the same condition: if \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\) and \(\lim_{x\to c}g(x)\) both exist (are finite numbers), then:

RuleFormulaIn words
1. Sum\(\lim\limits_{x\to c}[f(x)+g(x)]=\lim\limits_{x\to c}f(x)+\lim\limits_{x\to c}g(x)\)limit of a sum = sum of the limits
2. Difference\(\lim\limits_{x\to c}[f(x)-g(x)]=\lim\limits_{x\to c}f(x)-\lim\limits_{x\to c}g(x)\)limit of a difference = difference of the limits
3. Constant multiple\(\lim\limits_{x\to c}[kf(x)]=k\lim\limits_{x\to c}f(x)\)a constant factor can be taken outside the limit
4. Product\(\lim\limits_{x\to c}[f(x)g(x)]=\lim\limits_{x\to c}f(x)\cdot\lim\limits_{x\to c}g(x)\)limit of a product = product of the limits
5. Quotient\(\lim\limits_{x\to c}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\dfrac{\lim_{x\to c}f(x)}{\lim_{x\to c}g(x)}\), if \(\lim\limits_{x\to c}g(x)\ne0\)limit of a quotient = quotient of the limits, as long as the bottom limit is not 0
6. Power\(\lim\limits_{x\to c}[f(x)]^n=\Big[\lim\limits_{x\to c}f(x)\Big]^n\), \(n\in\mathbb Z^{+}\)limit of a power = power of the limit
7. Root\(\lim\limits_{x\to c}\sqrt[n]{f(x)}=\sqrt[n]{\lim\limits_{x\to c}f(x)}\), \(n\in\mathbb Z^{+}\)limit of a root = root of the limit

Here \(\mathbb Z^{+}\) means the positive integers \(1,2,3,\dots\). For the Root Rule with \(n\) even (square roots, fourth roots, …) we must also assume \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\gt0\), because an even root of a negative number is not a real number. Note also that all seven laws work for one-sided limits too (replace \(x\to c\) by \(x\to c^{+}\) or \(x\to c^{-}\) throughout).

Why we need something new

Every law needs both limits to exist. In \(x^2\sin\frac1x\) the factor \(\sin\frac1x\) has no limit as \(x\to0\), so the Product Rule cannot even be started. The Sandwich Theorem is the tool for exactly this situation.

1The Sandwich Theorem ~5 min

What is this?

A suspect walks between two police officers, one on each side. He can wriggle as much as he likes, but he can never get past either officer. If both officers walk through the same door, he goes through that door too. The suspect is the function we cannot handle directly; the officers are two simpler functions whose limits we can work out.

First, three terms used in the statement.

What is this? (only the stretch near the point)

Only the last stretch of the walk matters. The officers may be far from the suspect at the start, as long as they hold him between them for the final few steps before the door. What happens in the doorway itself is never checked.

Definitions

Open interval containing \(c\): a stretch of the number line \((a,b)\), meaning all \(x\) with \(a\lt x\lt b\), that has \(c\) inside it, for example \((c-0.1,\,c+0.1)\). It can be as small as you like. So the condition only has to hold near \(c\), not everywhere.

“Except possibly at \(x=c\)”: the inequality does not need to hold at \(c\) itself, and the function does not even need to be defined there. This matches the idea of a limit (Lecture 1): the value at \(c\) never matters.

Bound: a function used as a floor (lower bound) or a ceiling (upper bound) for another function.

Theorem: Sandwich Theorem

Suppose that

  1. \(g(x)\le f(x)\le h(x)\) for all \(x\) in some open interval containing \(c\), except possibly at \(x=c\), and
  2. \(\lim\limits_{x\to c}g(x)=\lim\limits_{x\to c}h(x)=L\).

Then

\[\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L.\]

In words: if a function is trapped between a floor and a ceiling near \(c\), and the floor and the ceiling both head to the same height \(L\), then the trapped function has no choice: it also heads to \(L\).

* The theorem also applies to one-sided limits: if the inequality holds just to the right of \(c\) and both bounds tend to \(L\) as \(x\to c^{+}\), then \(\lim_{x\to c^{+}}f(x)=L\) (and similarly for \(x\to c^{-}\)).

Reading the formula
\(g(x)\le f(x)\le h(x)\) near \(c\)
the suspect \(f\) walks between the officers \(g\) (below) and \(h\) (above) for the last stretch before the door.
except possibly at \(x=c\)
nobody checks the doorway itself; \(f\) need not even be defined there.
\(\lim g=\lim h=L\)
both officers walk through the same door, at height \(L\).
\(\lim f=L\)
so the suspect goes through that door too. If the officers aimed at different doors, he could end up anywhere between them.

It is also called the Squeeze Theorem or the Pinching Theorem. Picture a sandwich: \(g\) is the bottom slice of bread, \(h\) is the top slice, and \(f\) is the filling. Squash the two slices together at \(x=c\) and the filling is squashed to the same point.

In the picture below, \(h\) (top, red) and \(g\) (bottom, orange) both pass through the point \((c,L)\), and \(f\) (blue) wiggles between them, so it is forced through \((c,L)\) too.

The idea of the Sandwich Theorem

h (upper bound)f (squeezed)g (lower bound)

Away from \(c\) the three curves can be far apart and \(f\) can wiggle freely. Near \(c\) the gap between \(g\) and \(h\) closes, so \(f\) is pinned to \(L\).

Analogy

Two police officers walk a suspect between them. If both officers walk into the police station, the suspect ends up in the police station too, however much he wriggles on the way.

Both conditions matter

The theorem needs both: the inequality \(g\le f\le h\) near \(c\) and equal limits of \(g\) and \(h\). If the bounds tend to different numbers (say \(-1\) and \(1\)), the filling can be anywhere in between, and the theorem says nothing. If no such bounds exist (the function is unbounded, meaning it shoots off towards \(\pm\infty\)), the theorem cannot be used at all.

You can now: state the Sandwich Theorem with both of its conditions; explain why the value at \(c\) itself does not matter; and say why bounds with different limits prove nothing.

2Examples 1–3: squeezing a wiggle ~6 min

What is this?

On its own, \(\sin\frac1x\) is a suspect bouncing between two walls that never move, one at height 1 and one at height −1. He never settles, so there is no limit. Now multiply by \(x^2\): the walls become officers who close in on him, and at \(x=0\) they meet at height 0. Every example here is the same move: a wiggle that stays between fixed walls, multiplied by something that shrinks to 0.

All three examples contain \(\sin\frac1x\). As \(x\to0\), \(\frac1x\) becomes huge, so \(\sin\frac1x\) runs through complete waves faster and faster, swinging between \(-1\) and \(1\) forever. It has no limit at 0. But it is bounded: a function is bounded if its values stay between two fixed numbers. Here, for every \(x\ne0\),

\[-1\le\sin\frac1x\le1.\]

In words: sine of anything is between −1 and 1.

That fact is what we sandwich with. The interactive picture below shows each example with its bounds. Use the slider to zoom in towards \(x=0\).

Squeeze explorer: a wiggle trapped between two shrinking bounds

fupper boundlower bound

Zoom in: in Examples 1–3 the band between the bounds shrinks to nothing at \(x=0\), pinning \(f\) to 0. For \(\sin\frac1x\) on its own the bounds stay at \(\pm1\), so nothing is pinned and there is no limit.

Example 1

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}x^2\sin\left(\frac1x\right)\).

Why the limit laws fail. The Product Rule would need \(\lim_{x\to0}\sin\frac1x\), which does not exist.

Step 1: start from the bounded part. For \(x\ne0\): \(-1\le\sin\frac1x\le1\).

Step 2: multiply through by \(x^2\). Since \(x^2\gt0\) for \(x\ne0\), multiplying keeps the inequality signs the same way round:

\[-x^2\le x^2\sin\frac1x\le x^2\quad(x\ne0).\]

Step 3: limits of the bounds. \(\lim_{x\to0}(-x^2)=0\) and \(\lim_{x\to0}x^2=0\). They are equal.

Step 4: conclude. By the Sandwich Theorem (with \(g(x)=-x^2\), \(h(x)=x^2\), \(L=0\)),

\[\lim_{x\to0}x^2\sin\frac1x=0.\]

In words: (something shrinking to 0) × (something stuck between −1 and 1) shrinks to 0.

Example 2

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}x\sin\left(\frac1x\right)\).

The catch. If we multiply \(-1\le\sin\frac1x\le1\) by \(x\), the direction of the inequality depends on the sign of \(x\): for \(x\lt0\) the signs flip. So “\(-x\le x\sin\frac1x\le x\)” is false for negative \(x\) (this is the first common mistake in Section 7).

The fix: use absolute values. \(|x\sin\frac1x|=|x|\,|\sin\frac1x|\le|x|\cdot1=|x|\). A number whose size is at most \(|x|\) lies between \(-|x|\) and \(|x|\):For negative \(x\), the "ceiling" \(x\) would sit below the "floor" \(-x\): the officers would have swapped sides. With \(\pm|x|\) the upper officer stays on top on both sides of 0.

\[-|x|\le x\sin\frac1x\le|x|\quad(x\ne0).\]

Limits of the bounds. \(\lim_{x\to0}|x|=0\) and \(\lim_{x\to0}(-|x|)=0\).

Conclude. By the Sandwich Theorem,

\[\lim_{x\to0}x\sin\frac1x=0.\]

In words: the size of the function is at most the distance of \(x\) from 0, and that distance goes to 0.

Useful pattern

If \(|f(x)|\le B(x)\) near \(c\) and \(\lim_{x\to c}B(x)=0\), then \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=0\) (sandwich between \(-B(x)\) and \(B(x)\)).

In words: if the size of \(f\) is squeezed to 0, then \(f\) itself goes to 0.

Reading the formula
\(|f(x)|\)
how far the suspect is from height 0, above or below.
\(\le B(x)\)
he is never further than \(B(x)\) away, so the officers are \(-B(x)\) and \(B(x)\), one on each side.
\(\lim B(x)=0\)
the officers close in on height 0 from both sides, so \(f\) is pinned to 0.
Example 3

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^{+}}\sqrt{x}\,\sin\left(\frac1x\right)\).

Why one-sided? \(\sqrt x\) is only defined for \(x\ge0\), and \(\frac1x\) needs \(x\ne0\). So the function only exists for \(x\gt0\): we can only approach 0 from the right.

Bounds. For \(x\gt0\), \(\sqrt x\gt0\), so multiplying \(-1\le\sin\frac1x\le1\) by \(\sqrt x\) keeps the signs:

\[-\sqrt x\le\sqrt x\sin\frac1x\le\sqrt x\quad(x\gt0).\]

Limits of the bounds. \(\lim_{x\to0^{+}}\sqrt x=0\) and \(\lim_{x\to0^{+}}(-\sqrt x)=0\).

Conclude. By the (one-sided) Sandwich Theorem,

\[\lim_{x\to0^{+}}\sqrt x\sin\frac1x=0.\]

In words: from the right, the wiggle is squeezed between \(-\sqrt x\) and \(\sqrt x\), and both go to 0.

The method in four lines
  1. Do: Find the bounded wiggly part and write its bounds (\(-1\le\sin(\text{anything})\le1\), \(-1\le\cos(\text{anything})\le1\), \(0\le\sin^2(\text{anything})\le1\)). These are the fixed walls the wiggle can never pass, true for every input, however fast it oscillates.
  2. Do: Multiply or add to rebuild \(f(x)\). When multiplying by something that could be negative, use absolute values. This turns the fixed walls into officers that close in; a negative multiplier would swap floor and ceiling, which absolute values prevent.
  3. Do: Check that both bounds have the same limit. Both officers must head for the same door, or the suspect could end up anywhere between them.
  4. Do: Conclude, naming the Sandwich Theorem. The marks are for the reason: the bounds force the limit, not the limit laws.

You can now: bound \(\sin\), \(\cos\) and \(\sin^2\) of anything; multiply the bounds by a shrinking factor, using absolute values when its sign can change; handle one-sided cases such as \(\sqrt x\); and write a full squeeze argument.

3The key limit \(\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\to1\) ~6 min

What is this?

Start at the right-hand end of a circle of radius 1 and walk a short way anticlockwise along the edge. The distance you walk is the angle (in radians); how high you have climbed is its sine. At the start the edge points straight up, so for a very short walk almost all of it is climbing: height ≈ distance walked. The proof makes this exact with two officers: \(\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\) is held between \(\cos\theta\) and 1, and both head to 1.

A corollary is a result that follows quickly from a theorem just proved. The next limit is called the Sandwich Corollary, because it is proved with the Sandwich Theorem.

Corollary: Sandwich \[\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}=1\]

In words: for a very small angle measured in radians, sine of the angle ÷ the angle ≈ 1, i.e. sine of a tiny angle is almost the angle itself.

Reading the formula
\(\theta\)
the distance walked along the edge of the unit circle (that is what a radian measures).
\(\sin\theta\)
the height climbed during that walk.
\(\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\)
the share of the walk that went into climbing.
\(\to1\) as \(\theta\to0\)
for a very short walk the edge is almost straight up, so almost all of it is climbing. At \(\theta=0\) itself there is no walk at all, so the value there is never used.
What is this? (radian)

A radian measures an angle by distance walked: on a circle of radius 1, walk along the edge and the angle you have turned through, in radians, is the distance you have walked. Degrees are an arbitrary split of a full turn into 360 parts, so they do not match distances, and the key limit fails in degrees.

Definition: radian

The limit is only true when \(\theta\) is measured in radians. On a circle of radius 1, an angle of \(\theta\) radians cuts off an arc of length \(\theta\). A full turn is \(2\pi\) radians (\(=360^\circ\)), so \(\pi\) radians \(=180^\circ\) and \(1\) radian \(\approx57.3^\circ\). From now on, all angles in calculus are in radians unless stated otherwise.

Why this is not obvious. Putting \(\theta=0\) gives \(\frac{0}{0}\), which means nothing. The Quotient Rule fails because the bottom limit is 0. We cannot cancel anything either. The table shows what happens numerically:

\(\theta\) (radians)10.50.10.010.001
\(\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\)0.841470.958850.998330.999981.00000

A table suggests the answer but does not prove it. The proof traps \(\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\) between \(\cos\theta\) and \(1\) using areas in a unit circle (a circle of radius 1 centred at the origin). Drag the slider to see it.

Unit-circle picture behind sin x / x → 1

triangle OAPsector OAPtriangle OAT

The three shapes are nested: the small triangle sits inside the sector, which sits inside the big triangle. So their areas are in order. As \(x\) shrinks, \(\cos x\to1\) and the ratio \(\frac{\sin x}{x}\) is squeezed to 1.

Going further: proof that sin θ / θ → 1

Here is the standard proof. Take \(0\lt\theta\lt\frac\pi2\). On the unit circle let \(O\) be the centre, \(A=(1,0)\), \(P=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\), and \(T=(1,\tan\theta)\), where the line \(OP\) meets the vertical line \(x=1\).

  • Triangle \(OAP\): base \(OA=1\), height \(\sin\theta\), area \(\frac12\sin\theta\).
  • Sector \(OAP\): it is \(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\) of the whole circle of area \(\pi\), so its area is \(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\cdot\pi=\frac12\theta\).
  • Triangle \(OAT\): base \(OA=1\), height \(AT=\tan\theta\), area \(\frac12\tan\theta\).

The shapes are nested, so

\[\frac12\sin\theta\le\frac12\theta\le\frac12\tan\theta.\]

Multiply by 2 and divide by \(\sin\theta\gt0\):We want \(\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) together in one fraction in the middle; dividing by a positive number keeps the signs the same way round.

\[1\le\frac{\theta}{\sin\theta}\le\frac{1}{\cos\theta}.\]

Take reciprocals of positive numbers, which reverses the inequalities:This flips the middle into \(\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\), the function we want, with officers \(\cos\theta\) below and 1 above.

\[\cos\theta\le\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\le1.\]

In words: cosine of the angle ≤ sine ÷ angle ≤ 1.

As \(\theta\to0^{+}\), \(\cos\theta\to1\) (on the unit circle, \(P\) slides down to \(A=(1,0)\), so its \(x\)-coordinate \(\cos\theta\) tends to 1), and the constant 1 stays at 1. By the Sandwich Theorem, \(\lim_{\theta\to0^{+}}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}=1\).

For \(\theta\lt0\): \(\frac{\sin(-\theta)}{-\theta}=\frac{-\sin\theta}{-\theta}=\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\), so the graph is symmetric about the \(y\)-axis and the left-hand limit is also 1. Both one-sided limits are 1, so the two-sided limit is 1.

The graph of sin x / x, squeezed between cos x and 1

sin x / xcos x (lower bound)y = 1 (upper bound)

The function is not defined at \(x=0\) (open circle), but its graph heads to height 1 from both sides. Near 0 it is trapped between \(\cos x\) and 1, which meet at height 1.

Going further: degrees break it

If \(x\) were in degrees, \(\sin(x^\circ)=\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{180}\right)\), and then \(\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin(x^\circ)}{x}=\frac{\pi}{180}\), not 1. This is one reason calculus always uses radians.

You can now: quote \(\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}=1\) and say it needs radians; explain why direct substitution fails; and outline the unit-circle proof that squeezes the ratio between \(\cos\theta\) and 1.

4Corollary: the limit of a reciprocal ~3 min

What is this?

If a car's speed settles at 4 km per minute, the time it takes per km settles at a quarter of a minute: time per km is one over the speed. This only works if the speed settles at something other than 0. If the car creeps to a stop, the time per km grows without limit. Turning a limit upside down is safe exactly when the limit is not 0.

Start with an observation. Take \(f(x)=x+2\). Then \(\lim_{x\to2}f(x)=4\), and \(\lim_{x\to2}\frac{1}{f(x)}=\frac14\). See the picture below.

f(x) = x + 2 and 1/f(x) = 1/(x + 2) near x = 2

f(x) = x + 21/f(x) = 1/(x + 2)

As \(x\to2\) the line heads to height 4 and the reciprocal curve heads to height \(\frac14\).

Corollary: Quotient Rule

If \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\), then

\[\lim_{x\to c}\frac{1}{f(x)}=\frac1L,\]

provided \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\) exists and \(L\ne0\).

In words: the limit of one over a function = one over the limit of the function, as long as that limit is not 0.

Why it is true. Use the Quotient Rule (from the limit laws above) with the top function equal to the constant 1 and the bottom function equal to \(f\): \(\lim\frac{1}{f(x)}=\frac{\lim 1}{\lim f(x)}=\frac1L\). The condition \(L\ne0\) is the Quotient Rule's condition that the bottom limit is not 0.

Note: in the Quotient Rule as written above, \(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\), \(g\) is the bottom function, so setting \(g=1\) would give nothing new. The right move is to set the top function to 1 and keep the given function on the bottom, which is what is done above.

Why it is needed now. Turning the key limit upside down gives

\[\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\theta}{\sin\theta}=\frac{1}{1}=1.\]

In words: angle ÷ sine of the angle also tends to 1.

This is used in Examples 5 and 6.

You can now: find \(\lim\frac{1}{f(x)}\) from \(\lim f(x)\) when that limit is not 0; explain where the condition \(L\ne0\) comes from; and use \(\frac{\theta}{\sin\theta}\to1\).

5Examples 4–6: make the boxes match ~6 min

What is this?

The key limit says: for a very short walk around the unit circle, the height climbed is almost the distance walked. It does not care what the walk is called. If the walk is \(3x\), then \(\sin3x\) is almost \(3x\), not almost \(x\). So each sine must be compared with its own inside. The algebra in this section just rearranges the expression until every sine sits over exactly its own inside, and the inside is shrinking to 0.

The key limit works for any expression in place of \(\theta\), as long as that expression tends to 0. Think of \(\theta\) as a box:

\[\lim\frac{\sin\Box}{\Box}=1\quad\text{whenever }\Box\to0.\]

In words: sine of a box ÷ the same box → 1, provided the box shrinks to 0 (and is never exactly 0 along the way).

The contents of the two boxes must be identical. Most of the work is rearranging so they match. To be precise, this is a change of variable: set \(\theta=\Box\), check that \(\theta\to0\) (and \(\theta\ne0\)) as \(x\) approaches its target, then use the key limit in the variable \(\theta\).

Reading the formula
\(\sin\Box\) on top
the height climbed on a walk of length \(\Box\).
\(\Box\) underneath
the length of that same walk; it must be the identical expression.
\(\Box\to0\), \(\Box\ne0\)
the walk gets very short but is never exactly zero, so the fraction always makes sense.
\(=1\)
height and distance walked become equal in the limit.

The method, step by step:

  1. Do: Find the box inside each sine (and inside each tan, after writing \(\tan=\frac{\sin}{\cos}\)). Each box is a separate short walk, and each sine needs to be compared with its own walk.
  2. Do: Check that each box tends to 0 (and is not 0) as \(x\) approaches its target. The key limit is only about very short walks; if a box does not shrink to 0, the key limit says nothing about it.
  3. Do: Insert the missing boxes and compensate: multiply and divide by the same thing, so that every \(\sin\Box\) sits over the same \(\Box\). Multiplying and dividing by the same non-zero thing changes nothing, but it puts each height next to its own walk.
  4. Do: Set \(\theta=\Box\) and use \(\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\to1\) or \(\frac{\theta}{\sin\theta}\to1\) for each matched pair. Find the limit of whatever is left over. Every matched pair is now just "height over distance" for a short walk; only the leftover factor decides the answer.
  5. Do: Combine with the Product Rule. Every factor now has a limit, so the limit laws can be used again.
Example 4

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin kx}{x}\), where \(k\in\mathbb R\) (\(k\) is any real constant).

Case \(k=0\). Then \(\sin kx=\sin0=0\), so \(\frac{\sin kx}{x}=0\) for every \(x\ne0\), and the limit is \(0\).

Case \(k\ne0\). The box on top is \(kx\), but the bottom is only \(x\). Multiply top and bottom by \(k\) to make the boxes match:

\[\frac{\sin kx}{x}=k\cdot\frac{\sin kx}{kx}.\]

Let \(\theta=kx\). As \(x\to0\), \(\theta\to0\), and \(\theta\ne0\) when \(x\ne0\). So

\[\begin{aligned}\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin kx}{x}&=k\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\\&=k\cdot1=k.\end{aligned}\]

Answer. In both cases the limit is \(k\) (when \(k=0\), \(k=0\) agrees).

\[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin kx}{x}=k\]

In words: for small \(x\), \(\sin kx\approx kx\), so dividing by \(x\) leaves \(k\).

sin(kx)/x heads to height k at x = 0

sin(kx)/xheight k

The open circle at \((0,k)\) marks the limit: the function is undefined at \(x=0\) itself.

Example 5

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\tan3x}{\sin8x}\).

Step 1: write tan in terms of sin and cos. \(\tan3x=\frac{\sin3x}{\cos3x}\), so

\[\frac{\tan3x}{\sin8x}=\frac{\sin3x}{\cos3x\,\sin8x}.\]

Step 2: make the boxes match. Next to \(\sin3x\) we want \(3x\); next to \(\sin8x\) we want \(8x\). Insert them and compensate:Putting \(3x\) and \(8x\) in and taking them out again in \(\frac{3x}{8x}\) multiplies by 1 overall, so the expression is unchanged, but now each sine has its own box.

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\tan3x}{\sin8x}&=\frac{\sin3x}{3x}\cdot\frac{8x}{\sin8x}\\&\quad\cdot\frac{1}{\cos3x}\cdot\frac{3x}{8x}.\end{aligned}\]

(Check: the \(3x\) and \(8x\) we inserted cancel with \(\frac{3x}{8x}\).) For \(x\ne0\), \(\frac{3x}{8x}=\frac38\).

Step 3: limit of each factor.

  • \(\frac{\sin3x}{3x}\to1\) (key limit with \(\theta=3x\to0\)).
  • \(\frac{8x}{\sin8x}\to1\) (the reciprocal corollary of Section 4 with \(\theta=8x\to0\)).
  • \(\frac{1}{\cos3x}\to\frac{1}{\cos0}=1\) (\(\cos3x\to\cos0=1\), then the reciprocal corollary).

Step 4: Product Rule (all limits exist):

\[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\tan3x}{\sin8x}=1\cdot1\cdot1\cdot\frac38=\frac38.\]

In words: for small \(x\), \(\tan3x\approx3x\) and \(\sin8x\approx8x\), so the ratio is about \(\frac{3x}{8x}=\frac38\).

Example 6

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^{+}}\frac{x}{\sin\sqrt x}\).

Why one-sided? \(\sqrt x\) needs \(x\ge0\).

Make the box match. The box inside sine is \(\sqrt x\). Split \(x=\sqrt x\cdot\sqrt x\) (true for \(x\gt0\)):One \(\sqrt x\) goes on top of \(\sin\sqrt x\) to make a matched pair; the other \(\sqrt x\) is the leftover, and it decides the answer.

\[\frac{x}{\sin\sqrt x}=\sqrt x\cdot\frac{\sqrt x}{\sin\sqrt x}.\]

Let \(\theta=\sqrt x\). As \(x\to0^{+}\), \(\theta\to0^{+}\) and \(\theta\ne0\). By the reciprocal corollary, \(\frac{\theta}{\sin\theta}\to1\). Also \(\sqrt x\to0\). By the Product Rule,

\[\lim_{x\to0^{+}}\frac{x}{\sin\sqrt x}=0\cdot1=0.\]

In words: for small positive \(x\), \(\sin\sqrt x\approx\sqrt x\), so the expression is about \(\frac{x}{\sqrt x}=\sqrt x\), which goes to 0.

Trap

It is tempting to write \(\frac{x}{\sin\sqrt x}\to1\) because “it looks like \(\frac{\theta}{\sin\theta}\)”. It does not: the top is \(x\), the box is \(\sqrt x\). The boxes must match exactly.

You can now: rewrite tan as sin over cos; insert and compensate so that every sine sits over its own box; check that each box tends to 0; and finish with the key limit, the reciprocal corollary and the Product Rule.

6Summary

The summary, point by point:

  • The Sandwich Theorem is a powerful tool to evaluate limits for oscillating functions (functions that keep swinging up and down, like \(\sin\frac1x\) near 0) or limits where direct substitution fails (where putting \(x=c\) into the formula gives nonsense such as \(\frac00\), as in \(\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\)).
  • Choose bounds for the given function whose limits are simpler to compute, not harder, and whose limits are equal. (In Examples 1–3, \(\pm x^2\), \(\pm|x|\), \(\pm\sqrt x\) all have obvious limits.)
  • The Sandwich Theorem does not apply to unbounded functions (no floor and ceiling exist) or when the limits of the bounds are not equal.
  • Ensure that the conditions of the Sandwich Theorem are satisfied, to avoid logical and computational errors: the inequality \(g\le f\le h\) must really hold near \(c\), and \(\lim g=\lim h\).
Key formulas of Lecture 2 \[g\le f\le h,\ \ \lim g=\lim h=L\ \Rightarrow\ \lim f=L\] \[\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}=1,\qquad\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\theta}{\sin\theta}=1\] \[\lim_{x\to c}\frac{1}{f(x)}=\frac1L\ \ (L\ne0)\]

7Common mistakes

Four common mistakes, each explained.

1. Choosing incorrect bounds

✗\(-x\le x\sin\frac1x\le x\ \Rightarrow\ \lim_{x\to0}x\sin\frac1x=0\)

The inequality is false when \(x\lt0\). For example, at \(x=-\frac{2}{\pi}\): \(\sin\frac1x=\sin\left(-\frac\pi2\right)=-1\), so \(x\sin\frac1x=\frac2\pi\gt0\), but the “upper bound” \(x=-\frac2\pi\) is negative. The final answer 0 happens to be right, but the argument is wrong and loses the marks.

✓\(-|x|\le x\sin\frac1x\le|x|\) (Example 2).

2. Choosing bounds with unequal limits

✗\(-1\le x^2\sin\frac1x\le1\) near 0, but \(\lim_{x\to0}(-1)\ne\lim_{x\to0}1\)

The inequality is true, but useless: the bounds tend to \(-1\) and \(1\), so all we learn is that the function stays between \(-1\) and \(1\). The bounds must close in on a single value.

✓\(-x^2\le x^2\sin\frac1x\le x^2\), both bounds → 0 (Example 1).

3. Writing strict inequality for the bounds

✗\(-\sqrt x\lt\sqrt x\sin\frac1x\lt\sqrt x\ \Rightarrow\ \lim_{x\to0}\sqrt x=0\)

The strict signs \(\lt\) are false: whenever \(\sin\frac1x=1\) (for instance at \(x=\frac{2}{\pi}\), \(\frac{2}{5\pi}\), \(\frac2{9\pi}\), … arbitrarily close to 0) the middle equals the upper bound. Use \(\le\), which is what the theorem asks for. Two further slips in this line: the limit should be \(x\to0^{+}\) (\(\sqrt x\) needs \(x\ge0\)), and the conclusion should be about the function itself, \(\lim_{x\to0^{+}}\sqrt x\sin\frac1x=0\), not just about the bound.

4. Omitting the substitution for a change of variable

✗\(\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin(x^2)}{x^2}=1\) since \(\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1\)

The answer is right but the reason is incomplete: \(\frac{\sin(x^2)}{x^2}\) is not the same function as \(\frac{\sin x}{x}\). Write the substitution: let \(\theta=x^2\); as \(x\to0\), \(\theta\to0\) and \(\theta\ne0\) for \(x\ne0\); so the limit is \(\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}=1\).

More traps

✗“\(\lim x^2\sin\frac1x=\lim x^2\cdot\lim\sin\frac1x=0\cdot(\text{something})=0\).” The Product Rule needs both limits to exist, and \(\lim_{x\to0}\sin\frac1x\) does not. Use the Sandwich Theorem. ✗“\(\frac{\sin3x}{\sin8x}=\frac{3x}{8x}\), cancel the sin.” Sine is not a number you can cancel. Make the boxes match instead (Example 5). ✗“\(\frac{\sin kx}{x}\to1\).” The boxes \(kx\) and \(x\) do not match; the limit is \(k\) (Example 4). ✗Using degrees. The key limit is 1 only in radians.

8Find the mistake

Here is a student's handwritten answer to this question:

3. Let \(f(x)=\sin x\cdot e^{\cos\left(\frac{1}{x^3}\right)}\). Apply the Sandwich Theorem to prove

\[\lim_{x\to0^{+}}\left(\sin x\cdot e^{\cos\left(\frac{1}{x^3}\right)}\right)=0.\]

The student's work, copied line by line (as written, including the student's shorthand):

\[-1\lt\cos\frac{1}{x^3}\lt1\] \[e^{-1}\lt e^{\cos\frac{1}{x^3}}\lt e^{1}\] \[\frac1e\sin x\lt fx\lt e\sin x\] \[\lim\frac{\sin x}{e}=0\] \[\lim e\sin x=0\] \[\lim f(x)=0\]

(Here \(e\approx2.718\) is the base of the natural exponential, and “fx” means \(f(x)\).) Try to find what is wrong before opening the hints. You can also see this function in the squeeze explorer of Section 2 (last option).

Hint 1

Does \(\cos\frac{1}{x^3}\) ever equal exactly 1 or −1 for \(x\) near 0? Compare with mistake 3 in Section 7.

Hint 2

Line 3 multiplies an inequality by \(\sin x\). When is that allowed without flipping the signs? Why does the question say \(x\to0^{+}\)?

Answer

The conclusion (limit 0) is correct, but the argument has these problems:

  1. Strict inequalities are false. \(\cos\frac1{x^3}=1\) whenever \(\frac1{x^3}=2n\pi\), i.e. \(x=(2n\pi)^{-1/3}\), and such \(x\) come arbitrarily close to 0. So line 1 must use \(\le\): \(-1\le\cos\frac1{x^3}\le1\), and every later line must use \(\le\) too.
  2. Unexplained multiplication by \(\sin x\). Multiplying by \(\sin x\) keeps the inequality signs only if \(\sin x\ge0\). This is true for \(0\lt x\lt\pi\), which is why the limit is one-sided (\(x\to0^{+}\)). The student must say so; for \(x\lt0\) the inequality would flip and the bounds would be wrong.
  3. The limits are not labelled. Every “lim” should say \(x\to0^{+}\), and the conclusion should name the Sandwich Theorem.
  4. Line 2 uses that \(e^{t}\) is an increasing function (bigger input, bigger output), so applying it keeps the inequality. Worth a few words in an exam.

A correct write-up:

\[-1\le\cos\frac{1}{x^3}\le1\quad(x\ne0)\] \[\Rightarrow\ e^{-1}\le e^{\cos(1/x^3)}\le e\]

since \(e^t\) is increasing. For \(0\lt x\lt\pi\), \(\sin x\gt0\), so multiplying by \(\sin x\) keeps the signs:

\[\frac{\sin x}{e}\le f(x)\le e\sin x.\]

Since \(\lim_{x\to0^{+}}\frac{\sin x}{e}=\frac{0}{e}=0\) and \(\lim_{x\to0^{+}}e\sin x=0\), the Sandwich Theorem gives \(\lim_{x\to0^{+}}f(x)=0\).

9Group work

Six limits to try. Decide first which tool fits: the Sandwich Theorem (a bounded wiggle times something going to 0), or the key limit (match the boxes).

1. Find

\[\lim_{x\to0}\left[\sin x\cdot\sin\left(\frac{1}{x^2}\right)\right].\]
Hint

\(\sin\frac1{x^2}\) is a bounded wiggle with no limit. Bound the size: \(\left|\sin x\sin\frac1{x^2}\right|\le|\sin x|\). What does \(|\sin x|\) tend to?

Answer

For \(x\ne0\), \(\left|\sin\frac1{x^2}\right|\le1\), so

\[-|\sin x|\le\sin x\sin\frac1{x^2}\le|\sin x|.\]

As \(x\to0\), \(\sin x\to\sin0=0\), so both bounds tend to 0. By the Sandwich Theorem the limit is 0.

2. Find

\[\lim_{x\to0}\sqrt{x^3+x^2}\,\sin\frac{\pi}{x}.\]

The square root covers \(x^3+x^2\) only; \(\sin\frac\pi x\) is outside it.

Hint

\(\sin\frac\pi x\) is between −1 and 1, and \(\sqrt{x^3+x^2}\ge0\), so no sign problem. Find \(\lim_{x\to0}\sqrt{x^3+x^2}\) with the Root Rule.

Answer

Near 0 (for \(x\ge-1\), \(x\ne0\)), \(\sqrt{x^3+x^2}\ge0\), so multiplying \(-1\le\sin\frac\pi x\le1\) by it gives

\[\begin{aligned}-\sqrt{x^3+x^2}&\le\sqrt{x^3+x^2}\sin\frac\pi x\\&\le\sqrt{x^3+x^2}.\end{aligned}\]

\(\lim_{x\to0}\sqrt{x^3+x^2}=\sqrt{0}=0\) (Root Rule). Both bounds → 0, so the limit is 0 by the Sandwich Theorem.

3. Find

\[\lim_{x\to0^{+}}\sqrt x\left[1+\sin^2\left(\frac{2\pi}{x}\right)\right].\]
Hint

A square is never negative and \(\sin^2\le1\), so \(0\le\sin^2(\ldots)\le1\). Add 1, then multiply by \(\sqrt x\gt0\).

Answer

\(0\le\sin^2\frac{2\pi}{x}\le1\) gives \(1\le1+\sin^2\frac{2\pi}{x}\le2\). For \(x\gt0\) multiply by \(\sqrt x\gt0\):

\[\sqrt x\le\sqrt x\left[1+\sin^2\frac{2\pi}x\right]\le2\sqrt x.\]

Both \(\sqrt x\) and \(2\sqrt x\) tend to 0 as \(x\to0^{+}\). By the Sandwich Theorem the limit is 0. (The bounds need not be mirror images; they just need the same limit.)

4. Find

\[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin\left(x^2\right)}{x}.\]
Hint

The box is \(x^2\). Multiply top and bottom by \(x\) so that \(x^2\) appears underneath.

Answer
\[\frac{\sin(x^2)}{x}=x\cdot\frac{\sin(x^2)}{x^2}.\]

Let \(\theta=x^2\): as \(x\to0\), \(\theta\to0\) with \(\theta\ne0\), so \(\frac{\sin(x^2)}{x^2}\to1\). Also \(x\to0\). By the Product Rule the limit is \(0\cdot1=\) 0.

5. Find

\[\lim_{x\to0^{+}}\frac{\sin x}{\sin\sqrt x}.\]
Hint

Two boxes: \(x\) and \(\sqrt x\). Give each sine its own matching box, then see what is left over.

Answer

For \(x\gt0\) (small),

\[\frac{\sin x}{\sin\sqrt x}=\frac{\sin x}{x}\cdot\frac{\sqrt x}{\sin\sqrt x}\cdot\frac{x}{\sqrt x}.\]

The first factor → 1 (key limit), the second → 1 (reciprocal corollary with \(\theta=\sqrt x\to0^{+}\)), and \(\frac{x}{\sqrt x}=\sqrt x\to0\). So the limit is \(1\cdot1\cdot0=\) 0.

6. Find

\[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin(2x)}{x\cos(3x)}.\]
Hint

Separate the cosine: \(\frac{\sin2x}{x}\cdot\frac{1}{\cos3x}\). The first factor is Example 4 with \(k=2\).

Answer
\[\frac{\sin2x}{x\cos3x}=2\cdot\frac{\sin2x}{2x}\cdot\frac{1}{\cos3x}.\]

\(\frac{\sin2x}{2x}\to1\) and \(\frac1{\cos3x}\to\frac1{\cos0}=1\). So the limit is \(2\cdot1\cdot1=\) 2.

10Concept checks

1. True or false: “Since \(-1\le\sin\frac1x\le1\), the limit \(\lim_{x\to0}\sin\frac1x\) is some number between −1 and 1.”

Answer

False. The limit does not exist: \(\sin\frac1x\) keeps hitting both 1 and −1 at points arbitrarily close to 0, so it never settles. Being bounded is not the same as having a limit. The bounds −1 and 1 have different limits, so the Sandwich Theorem gives nothing.

2. Suppose \(1-\frac{x^2}{4}\le u(x)\le1+\frac{x^2}{2}\) for all \(x\ne0\). Find \(\lim_{x\to0}u(x)\). Do you need a formula for \(u\)?

Answer

Both bounds tend to \(1\) as \(x\to0\), so by the Sandwich Theorem \(\lim_{x\to0}u(x)=\) 1. No formula for \(u\) is needed: the bounds alone decide the limit.

3. Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}x\cos\frac1x\).

Answer

\(\left|x\cos\frac1x\right|\le|x|\), so \(-|x|\le x\cos\frac1x\le|x|\), and both bounds → 0. The limit is 0 (Sandwich Theorem). Using \(\pm x\) as the bounds would be mistake 1 of Section 7.

4. Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin5x}{\sin2x}\).

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\sin5x}{\sin2x}&=\frac{\sin5x}{5x}\cdot\frac{2x}{\sin2x}\cdot\frac52\\&\to1\cdot1\cdot\frac52=\frac52.\end{aligned}\]

5. Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\frac{\sin(x-1)}{x^2-1}\).

Hint

The box is \(x-1\), which tends to 0 as \(x\to1\). Factorise \(x^2-1\).

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\sin(x-1)}{x^2-1}&=\frac{\sin(x-1)}{x-1}\cdot\frac{1}{x+1}\\&\to1\cdot\frac12=\frac12.\end{aligned}\]

With \(\theta=x-1\to0\). The key limit works at any point, as long as the box tends to 0.

6. Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2}\).

Hint

Multiply top and bottom by \(1+\cos x\) and use \(1-\cos^2x=\sin^2x\).

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2}&=\frac{\sin^2x}{x^2(1+\cos x)}\\&=\left(\frac{\sin x}{x}\right)^2\cdot\frac{1}{1+\cos x}\end{aligned}\]

which tends to \(1^2\cdot\frac12=\frac12\). This trick (rewriting \(1-\cos x\) with \(\sin^2x\)) is useful in the exam taster below.

May 2025 Q1(a)Q1 total 10%Guide · L2.2 sin of a box over the same box: make the boxes match

Evaluate the limit

\[\lim_{x\to0}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{x^2}}\cdot\sin x\]

or explain carefully why the limit does not exist.

Hint

Combine under the root: \(\sqrt{1+\frac1{x^2}}=\frac{\sqrt{x^2+1}}{\sqrt{x^2}}=\frac{\sqrt{x^2+1}}{|x|}\), because \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\), not \(x\). So the expression is \(\sqrt{x^2+1}\cdot\frac{\sin x}{|x|}\). Treat \(x\to0^{+}\) and \(x\to0^{-}\) separately.

Answer

The limit does not exist: the right-hand limit is \(1\) and the left-hand limit is \(-1\).
