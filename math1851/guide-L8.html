The short version

What to expect

This chapter is small but word-for-word. Only 5 recent parts (May 2019–May 2026) come from it, but two of them simply say "State the Mean Value Theorem", and the others are short proofs that use the theorem in one line. The marks go to stating the hypotheses ("continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\)") and naming the theorem you use. Learn the two statements in the Ready answer box exactly; with those, every type below is a short fixed routine.

Absolute maximum and minimum questions (L8.1) have not been asked on their own since 2018, but the same check (critical points and end points) is inside many curve-sketching questions in the L9 guide.

How to practise: use Start here to find the type from the command word. Work the question with the type's procedure open, check with the Hint if stuck and the Solution at the end. Newest papers come first; 2013–2018 papers are tagged older format. For the ideas, use the Chapter note links. All answers checked with sympy.

Exam format

  • Where: Part I (calculus). Recent: May 2026 Q4 (10 points, all three parts on the MVT), May 2019 Q5(a), Dec 2023 Q4 (6%). Older papers put it in Q2 (absolute extrema) or Q5 (Rolle and MVT), 10–18% per question.
  • Speed: Dec 2025 moved part of Part I to multiple choice, and May 2026 made Part I Q1–3 answers only. This chapter sits in the long questions: May 2026 says "Questions 4–5 are long questions, provide detailed arguments", and Dec 2023 says "Identify the theorem(s) you are using". Write the theorem's name and check its hypotheses on paper.
  • Formula sheet: papers print trigonometric identities (and, on most, derivatives of \(\sin^{-1}\), \(\cos^{-1}\), \(\tan^{-1}\)); nothing else. No paper prints Rolle's Theorem, the Mean Value Theorem or the Intermediate Value Theorem, or the derivatives of \(e^x\) and \(\ln x\). You must know the statements yourself.
  • Wording: "absolute" extreme value means the largest or smallest value over the whole interval (not just a local peak). "State without proof" means statement only. Older papers write "the point \((1,2)\)" for "at \(x=1\), with value 2".

L8.1Absolute maximum and minimum on an interval

Chapter note · absolute extrema and the Extreme Value Theorem · Chapter note · critical points

Frequency: 0 of 13 recent papers; 4 of 10 older papers (May 2018, May 2017, May 2016, May 2013). Not asked on its own since 2018, but absolute extrema reappear inside L9 questions (see the L9 guide).

Recognise it

The words "absolute maximum / minimum" or "absolute extreme values" of a given \(f\), on a stated interval or domain. A variant gives the maximum point and asks for unknown constants.

Standard procedure

A critical point is an \(x\) inside the interval (not an end) where \(f'(x)=0\) or \(f'(x)\) does not exist. The Extreme Value Theorem (EVT): a function continuous on a closed interval \([a,b]\) has an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum there.

  1. Look at the interval. Closed \([a,b]\) and \(f\) continuous: say "by the EVT both exist". Open or infinite (\((0,\infty)\), \((-\infty,\infty)\)): they may not exist; you will need step 4b.
  2. Differentiate and list the critical points inside the interval: solve \(f'(x)=0\), and look for points where \(f'\) is undefined (a zero in a denominator, \(x^{-1/3}\), \(|x|\)). Drop any outside the interval.
  3. Make a table of values: \(f\) at every critical point and at each end point that belongs to the interval (for an open or infinite end, its limit).
  4. Decide.
    • 4a. Closed interval: the largest value in the table is the absolute maximum, the smallest the absolute minimum. Say where each occurs. Asked: May 2017, May 2013.
    • 4b. Open or infinite interval: use the sign of \(f'\) (decreasing, then increasing) and the end limits. A value the function only approaches is not attained, so it is not a maximum or minimum; if \(f\to\infty\) there is no absolute maximum. Asked: May 2016.
    • 4c. Reverse question (constants unknown, the maximum point \((x_0,y_0)\) given): write \(f(x_0)=y_0\) and \(f'(x_0)=0\), solve, then check the point really is the absolute maximum. Asked: May 2018.
Worked example · the procedure in action

May 2013 Q2(b), Q2: 10%. Consider the function \(f(x)=x^{2/3}-\frac23x\). Find the absolute extreme values of \(f(x)\) on \(\left[-1,\frac32\right]\), saying where they occur. [Hint: The value \(f(\frac32)=(\frac32)^{2/3}-1\) is approximately \(0.310\).]

Step 1 \(x^{2/3}=\left(\sqrt[3]{x}\right)^2\) is defined and continuous for every real \(x\), so \(f\) is continuous on the closed interval \(\left[-1,\frac32\right]\). By the EVT, an absolute maximum and minimum exist.

One line, but it is the reason the "table of values" method is allowed at all.

Step 2 Differentiate:

\[f'(x)=\frac23x^{-1/3}-\frac23=\frac23\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{x}}-1\right).\]

\(f'(x)=0\) when \(\sqrt[3]{x}=1\), i.e. \(x=1\). \(f'(x)\) is undefined at \(x=0\) (division by zero), and \(0\) is inside the interval. Critical points: \(x=0\) and \(x=1\).

This is the trap of the question: solving only \(f'(x)=0\) loses \(x=0\), which turns out to be the minimum.

Step 3 Table of values (ends and critical points):

\[\begin{aligned}f(-1)&=1+\tfrac23=\tfrac53\\f(0)&=0\\f(1)&=1-\tfrac23=\tfrac13\\f\!\left(\tfrac32\right)&\approx0.310\end{aligned}\]

\((-1)^{2/3}=\left(\sqrt[3]{-1}\right)^2=(-1)^2=1\), not \(-1\). Calculators often get this wrong, so work it out by hand.

Step 4a Largest value \(\frac53\) at \(x=-1\); smallest value \(0\) at \(x=0\).

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{abs max } \tfrac53 \text{ at } x=-1\\&\text{abs min } 0 \text{ at } x=0\end{aligned}}\]

"Saying where they occur" is part of the answer: give both the value and the \(x\). The point \(x=1\) is only a local maximum (\(\frac13\lt\frac53\)).

Traps
  • Forgetting "\(f'\) undefined". May 2013 Q2(b): the minimum is at \(x=0\), where \(f'\) does not exist.
  • Treating an open interval like a closed one. May 2016 Q2(c)(i) is on \((0,\infty)\) and says "if exist": there is no absolute maximum, and \(T\to0\) at the left end is not a value of \(T\).
  • Stopping at \(f'(x_0)=0\) in a reverse question (May 2018 Q4(b)): \(f'=0\) only gives a candidate; one line must show it is the absolute maximum.

May 2018 Q4(b)Q4: 10%; part marks not shownolder format

Determine the values of constants \(a\) and \(b\) so that the function \(v(x)=ax^2+bx\) (whose domain is \((-\infty,\infty)\)) has an absolute maximum at the point \((1,2)\).

Hint

Type L8.1, reverse question (step 4c). First step: the point \((1,2)\) gives two equations, \(v(1)=2\) and \(v'(1)=0\).

Solution

Step 4c Two conditions. The graph passes through \((1,2)\): \(v(1)=a+b=2\). An absolute maximum inside \((-\infty,\infty)\) is a critical point, and \(v\) is differentiable, so \(v'(1)=2a+b=0\).

Step 4c Solve. Subtract: \(a=-2\), then \(b=4\).

Step 4c Check it is absolute. \(v(x)=-2x^2+4x=2-2(x-1)^2\le2\) for all \(x\), with equality only at \(x=1\). So \((1,2)\) is the absolute maximum.

\[\boxed{a=-2,\quad b=4}\]

Marker: the check line (completing the square, or "\(a\lt0\), so the parabola opens downward and its vertex is the highest point") is what turns a candidate into an answer.

May 2017 Q2(d)Q2: 12%; part marks not shownolder format

Find the absolute minimum and maximum of \(g(x)=\ln(x^2+x+1)\) over the interval \([-1,1]\).

Hint

Type L8.1, closed interval. First step: \(x^2+x+1\gt0\) for all \(x\), so \(g\) is continuous on \([-1,1]\); differentiate with the chain rule and solve \(g'(x)=0\).

Solution

Step 1 \(x^2+x+1=\left(x+\frac12\right)^2+\frac34\gt0\), so \(g\) is continuous on the closed interval \([-1,1]\); by the EVT the extrema exist.

Step 2 Chain rule:

\[g'(x)=\frac{2x+1}{x^2+x+1}.\]

The bottom is never 0, so \(g'\) exists everywhere; \(g'(x)=0\) only at \(x=-\frac12\), which is inside \([-1,1]\).

Step 3 Table:

\[\begin{aligned}g(-1)&=\ln1=0\\g\!\left(-\tfrac12\right)&=\ln\tfrac34\approx-0.288\\g(1)&=\ln3\approx1.099\end{aligned}\]

Step 4a Compare the three values.

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{abs min } \ln\tfrac34 \text{ at } x=-\tfrac12\\&\text{abs max } \ln3 \text{ at } x=1\end{aligned}}\]

Equivalent forms. \(\ln\frac34=\ln3-2\ln2\). Shortcut: \(\ln\) is increasing, so \(g\) is smallest and largest where \(x^2+x+1\) is.

May 2016 Q2(c)(i)Q2: 18%; part marks not shownolder format

Let \(T(x)=x\ln x\) for \(x\gt0\).
i. Evaluate, if exist, the absolute minimum value and the absolute maximum value of \(T(x)\).

Part (ii) of (c) is in the L16 guide.

Hint

Type L8.1 on an infinite interval \((0,\infty)\) (step 4b). First step: solve \(T'(x)=0\) and find the sign of \(T'\) on each side; then find \(T\) as \(x\to0^+\) and as \(x\to\infty\).

Solution

Step 1 The domain \((0,\infty)\) is not closed, so the EVT does not apply: existence must be checked.

Step 2 Product rule: \(T'(x)=\ln x+1\), defined for all \(x\gt0\). \(T'(x)=0\iff x=\frac1e\).

Step 3 \(T\!\left(\frac1e\right)=\frac1e\ln\frac1e=-\frac1e\). End behaviour: \(x\ln x\to0\) as \(x\to0^+\) (L'Hôpital on \(\frac{\ln x}{1/x}\); see the L10 guide), and \(x\ln x\to\infty\) as \(x\to\infty\).

Step 4b \(T'\lt0\) on \(\left(0,\frac1e\right)\) and \(T'\gt0\) on \(\left(\frac1e,\infty\right)\): \(T\) decreases, then increases. So \(T(x)\ge T\!\left(\frac1e\right)\) for every \(x\gt0\): absolute minimum \(-\frac1e\). Since \(T\to\infty\), \(T\) has no largest value.

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{abs min } -\tfrac1e \text{ at } x=\tfrac1e\\&\text{no absolute maximum}\end{aligned}}\]

Marker: "if exist" invites the answer "does not exist"; justify it with \(T(x)\to\infty\) as \(x\to\infty\).

Equivalent forms. \(-\frac1e=-e^{-1}\approx-0.368\).

L8.2State or prove Rolle's Theorem or the Mean Value Theorem

Chapter note · Rolle's Theorem · Chapter note · the Mean Value Theorem

Frequency: 1 of 13 recent papers (May 2026, two parts); 2 of 10 older papers (May 2015, two parts; Dec 2016).

Recognise it

"State Rolle's Theorem / the Mean Value Theorem", "prove it", "sketch a function that does not satisfy the conclusion", or "show there is a number \(w\) with \(f'(w)=g'(w)\)" (a derivative equal to something, at an unknown point).

Ready answer · the two statements, word for word

Rolle's Theorem. Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\), and \(f(a)=f(b)\). Then there is at least one number \(c\in(a,b)\) such that

\[f'(c)=0.\]

In words: if a smooth curve starts and ends at the same height, somewhere in between its tangent is horizontal.

Mean Value Theorem (MVT). Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\). Then there is at least one number \(c\in(a,b)\) such that

\[f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.\]

In words: slope of the tangent at \(c\) = (change in height) ÷ (change in \(x\)) = slope of the chord joining the two end points. Same statement, multiplied out: \(f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)\).

Proof of the MVT from Rolle (May 2015 Q5(b)): put \(s=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\) and

\[g(x)=f(x)-f(a)-s\,(x-a).\]

Check Rolle's three hypotheses for \(g\), get \(g'(c)=f'(c)-s=0\). Full write-up in the May 2015 Q5(b) card.

Standard procedure
  1. Write the statement you need from the Ready answer: name, both hypotheses (closed interval for continuity, open interval for differentiability), conclusion with \(c\in(a,b)\).
  2. Follow the command word.
    • 2a. "State": the statement is the whole answer. Stop. Asked: May 2026, May 2015.
    • 2b. "Prove the MVT using Rolle": define \(g(x)=f(x)-f(a)-s(x-a)\) with \(s\) the chord slope; check \(g\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\), \(g(a)=g(b)=0\); apply Rolle; rearrange \(g'(c)=0\). Asked: May 2015.
    • 2c. "Sketch (or give) a function that does not satisfy the conclusion": keep the hypotheses the question insists on and break another one. Continuous but not differentiable: put a corner in the middle, e.g. \(|x-m|\). Then show the conclusion fails: compute the chord slope and show no \(f'(c)\) equals it. Asked: May 2026.
    • 2d. "Show there is \(w\) with \(f'(w)=g'(w)\)": set \(h=f-g\), so you need \(h'(w)=0\). Find two points \(u\lt v\) with \(h(u)=h(v)\) (often two zeros of \(h\), each from the IVT on a sign change), check \(h\) is continuous on \([u,v]\) and differentiable on \((u,v)\), then apply Rolle. Asked: Dec 2016.
  3. Name each theorem as you use it, with its hypotheses checked in one line ("\(h\) is continuous on \([u,v]\) as a difference of continuous functions…").
Worked example · the procedure in action

May 2026 Q4(b), Q4: 10 points (long question, "provide detailed arguments"). Sketch a function on \([1,3]\) which is continuous, but does not satisfy the conclusion of the Mean Value Theorem.

Step 1 MVT: if \(f\) is continuous on \([1,3]\) and differentiable on \((1,3)\), there is \(c\in(1,3)\) with \(f'(c)=\dfrac{f(3)-f(1)}{3-1}\).

The question keeps "continuous", so the only hypothesis left to break is "differentiable on \((1,3)\)". A function that satisfies both hypotheses always satisfies the conclusion, so it cannot be an answer.

Step 2c Take a corner in the middle: \(f(x)=|x-2|\) on \([1,3]\). It is continuous (no jumps or gaps).

Step 2c Chord slope: \(\dfrac{f(3)-f(1)}{3-1}=\dfrac{1-1}{2}=0\).

Derivative: \(f'(x)=-1\) on \((1,2)\), \(f'(x)=1\) on \((2,3)\), and \(f'(2)\) does not exist (the left and right slopes differ). So \(f'(c)=0\) for no \(c\in(1,3)\): the conclusion fails.

Showing the failure with the two numbers (chord slope 0, tangent slopes \(\pm1\)) is the "detailed argument"; a picture alone does not prove anything.

Step 3 This does not contradict the MVT: \(f\) is not differentiable at \(x=2\), so the hypotheses are not all met.

\[\boxed{f(x)=|x-2|,\ 1\le x\le3}\]

Any continuous graph with a sharp corner works if no tangent is parallel to the chord, e.g. a "V" or an upside-down "V". Label the end points and the corner on your sketch.

Traps
  • Swapping the brackets. Continuous on the closed \([a,b]\), differentiable on the open \((a,b)\), and \(c\) strictly inside, \(c\in(a,b)\). May 2026 Q4(a) and May 2015 Q5(a),(b) mark exactly this.
  • Breaking the wrong hypothesis in May 2026 Q4(b): a function with a jump is not continuous, so it does not answer the question.
  • Using Rolle with only one zero in Dec 2016 Q5(c): Rolle needs two points with equal values, so the IVT must be used twice (on \([0,1]\) and \([1,2]\)).

Dec 2016 Q5(c)Q5: 12%; part marks not shownolder format

Let \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) be two continuous and differentiable functions defined on the interval \([0,2]\). Suppose \(f(0)\gt g(0)\) and \(f(1)\lt g(1)\), show that there exists a number \(u\) in the interval \((0,1)\) such that \(f(u)=g(u)\). Suppose further that \(f(2)\gt g(2)\). Show that there is a number \(w\) in the interval \((0,2)\) such that \(f'(w)=g'(w)\). (Hint: consider \(h(x)=f(x)-g(x)\).)

Parts (a) and (b) of this question are in the L10 and L4 guides. The first half of this part is an IVT step (L4.4).

Hint

Type L8.2, apply Rolle (step 2d). First step: \(h(0)\gt0\) and \(h(1)\lt0\); what does the IVT give? Then look at \([1,2]\) the same way.

Solution

Step 2d Set \(h=f-g\). \(h\) is continuous and differentiable on \([0,2]\) (difference of two such functions), and \(h'=f'-g'\).

Step 2d First zero (IVT). \(h(0)=f(0)-g(0)\gt0\) and \(h(1)=f(1)-g(1)\lt0\). \(h\) is continuous on \([0,1]\), so by the Intermediate Value Theorem there is \(u\in(0,1)\) with \(h(u)=0\), i.e. \(f(u)=g(u)\).

Step 2d Second zero (IVT). \(h(1)\lt0\) and \(h(2)=f(2)-g(2)\gt0\), so likewise there is \(v\in(1,2)\) with \(h(v)=0\).

Step 3 Rolle on \([u,v]\). \(0\lt u\lt1\lt v\lt2\). \(h\) is continuous on \([u,v]\), differentiable on \((u,v)\), and \(h(u)=h(v)=0\). By Rolle's Theorem there is \(w\in(u,v)\subset(0,2)\) with \(h'(w)=0\), i.e.

\[\boxed{f'(w)=g'(w)\ \text{for some } w\in(0,2)}\]

Marker: name the IVT (twice) and Rolle's Theorem, and say why \(w\) lies in \((0,2)\): because \(u\gt0\) and \(v\lt2\).

May 2015 Q5(b)Q5: 10%; part marks not shownolder format

(b) State the Mean Value Theorem and prove it by using Rolle's Theorem.

Hint

Type L8.2, proof (step 2b). First step: subtract the chord from \(f\): build a function \(g\) with \(g(a)=g(b)\) so that Rolle applies to \(g\).

Solution

Step 1 Statement. If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\), then there exists \(c\in(a,b)\) with \(f'(c)=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\).

Step 2b Auxiliary function. Let \(s=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\) and

\[g(x)=f(x)-f(a)-s\,(x-a).\]

Step 2b Rolle's hypotheses for \(g\). \(f\) and the polynomial \(f(a)+s(x-a)\) are continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\), so \(g\) is too. And

\[\begin{aligned}g(a)&=0,\\g(b)&=f(b)-f(a)-s(b-a)=0.\end{aligned}\]

Step 3 Apply Rolle. There is \(c\in(a,b)\) with \(g'(c)=0\). Since \(g'(x)=f'(x)-s\), this says \(f'(c)=s\).

\[\boxed{f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a},\ c\in(a,b)}\]

Marker: the three Rolle checks for \(g\) (continuous, differentiable, \(g(a)=g(b)\)) are the proof; do not skip \(g(b)=0\).

Equivalent forms. Any \(g(x)=f(x)-s\,x\) also works (then \(g(a)=g(b)=\frac{bf(a)-af(b)}{b-a}\)).

L8.3Show an equation has exactly one root

Chapter note · counting roots with Rolle's Theorem · Chapter note · the Intermediate Value Theorem

Frequency: 1 of 13 recent papers (Dec 2023); 1 of 10 older papers (Dec 2015). May 2015 Q2(b) (how many times the tangent meets the curve) is solved in the L6 guide; part (ii) is this type.

Recognise it

"Show that … has one and exactly one (real) solution / root", or "how many roots does \(h\) have in \([a,b]\)?". Two jobs: show a root exists, and show there cannot be two.

Standard procedure
  1. Write it as \(f(x)=0\) and say \(f\) is continuous and differentiable (sums and products of polynomials, \(\sin\), \(\cos\), \(e^x\) are).
  2. Existence (IVT). Find two numbers \(p\lt q\) where \(f(p)\) and \(f(q)\) have opposite signs (try \(0\), \(\pm1\), the ends of the interval). By the Intermediate Value Theorem there is a root in \((p,q)\).
  3. Uniqueness (sign of \(f'\)).
    • 3a. \(f'(x)\gt0\) for all \(x\) (or \(f'\lt0\) for all \(x\)): \(f\) is strictly increasing, so it crosses 0 at most once. Rolle version: two roots \(r\lt s\) would give \(f(r)=f(s)=0\), so \(f'(c)=0\) for some \(c\in(r,s)\), impossible. Asked: Dec 2023.
    • 3b. \(f'\) changes sign: split the interval at the critical points (\(f'=0\)) into pieces where \(f\) only increases or only decreases. On each piece: values at the two ends of opposite sign → exactly one root; same sign → none. Add up. Asked: Dec 2015.
  4. Conclude with the number of roots and the theorems used (the question often says "identify the theorem(s)").

This procedure solves both past L8.3 parts.

Worked example · the procedure in action

Dec 2023 Q4, 6%. Show that \(2x-\sin(x)+x^3+2=0\) has one and exactly one solution. Identify the theorem(s) you are using.

Step 1 Let \(f(x)=2x-\sin x+x^3+2\). \(f\) is continuous and differentiable on \(\mathbb{R}\) (a polynomial minus \(\sin x\)).

Step 2 Try easy points:

\[\begin{aligned}f(0)&=2\gt0,\\f(-1)&=-2+\sin1-1+2\\&=\sin1-1\lt0,\end{aligned}\]

since \(\sin1\lt1\). \(f\) is continuous on \([-1,0]\), so by the Intermediate Value Theorem there is a root in \((-1,0)\).

Note \(\sin(-1)=-\sin1\), so \(-\sin(-1)=+\sin1\). Explain why the sign is negative (\(\sin1\lt1\)); a decimal (\(\approx-0.159\)) is fine as support.

Step 3a Derivative:

\[f'(x)=2-\cos x+3x^2\ge2-1+0=1\gt0,\]

because \(\cos x\le1\) and \(x^2\ge0\). Suppose there were two roots \(r\lt s\). Then \(f(r)=f(s)=0\), and by Rolle's Theorem \(f'(c)=0\) for some \(c\in(r,s)\). This contradicts \(f'\gt0\).

The bound \(-\cos x\ge-1\) is the key line: it shows \(f'\) is never 0 without solving anything.

Step 4 Exactly one solution, and it lies in \((-1,0)\).

\[\boxed{\text{exactly one real root, in }(-1,0)}\]

Theorems named: Intermediate Value Theorem (existence) and Rolle's Theorem (uniqueness). Numerically the root is \(x\approx-0.963\).

Traps
  • Doing only half. Dec 2023 Q4 needs both "at least one" (IVT) and "at most one" (Rolle or \(f'\gt0\)); each half carries marks.
  • Counting sign changes at the ends only. Dec 2015 Q2(c): \(h(0)\lt0\lt h(4)\) suggests one root, but that alone does not rule out three; the monotonic pieces do.

L8.4Use the Mean Value Theorem to bound a function

Chapter note · the Mean Value Theorem · Chapter note · consequences of the MVT

Frequency: 2 of 13 recent papers (May 2026, May 2019); 0 of 10 older papers.

Recognise it

You know something about \(f'\) (an inequality like \(f'(x)\lt3\), or a formula for \(F'\)) and must show something about the values of \(f\): "show \(f(2)\lt13\)", "show \(F(x)\to\infty\)".

Standard procedure
  1. Choose the interval \([a,b]\): its ends are the points where \(f\) is given and where it is wanted. For "\(\to\infty\)" use \([1,x]\) (or \([k,x]\)) with \(x\) free.
  2. Check the hypotheses: \(f\) continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\). If \(f'\) exists everywhere, both hold (differentiable implies continuous).
  3. Write the MVT: \(f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)\,(b-a)\) for some \(c\in(a,b)\).
  4. Bound \(f'(c)\) and multiply by \(b-a\gt0\) (the inequality keeps its direction).
    • 4a. Fixed interval: rearrange to the inequality asked. Asked: May 2026.
    • 4b. Interval growing with \(x\): find a lower bound \(m\gt0\) for \(f'(c)\) that does not depend on \(x\); then \(f(x)\ge f(k)+m(x-k)\to\infty\). Asked: May 2019.

This procedure solves both past L8.4 parts.

Worked example · the procedure in action

May 2026 Q4(c), Q4: 10 points (long question, "provide detailed arguments"). Given that \(f(-2)=1\) and that \(f'(x)\) exists and is less than 3 for all real numbers. Show that \(f(2)\lt13\), using the Mean Value Theorem.

Step 1 Information is at \(x=-2\) and wanted at \(x=2\): use \([-2,2]\).

Step 2 \(f'(x)\) exists for every \(x\), so \(f\) is differentiable on \((-2,2)\) and, being differentiable, continuous on \([-2,2]\).

The question only gives "\(f'\) exists"; the marker wants you to get continuity from it out loud.

Step 3 By the MVT there is \(c\in(-2,2)\) with

\[f(2)-f(-2)=f'(c)\,\big(2-(-2)\big)=4f'(c).\]

Step 4a \(f'(c)\lt3\) and \(4\gt0\), so \(4f'(c)\lt12\). Hence

\[f(2)\lt f(-2)+12=1+12.\]
\[\boxed{f(2)\lt13}\]

Check of the idea: if \(f'\) were exactly 3, \(f(x)=3x+7\) would give \(f(2)=13\); a slope always below 3 must end lower.

Traps
  • Writing \(f'(c)=3\). In May 2026 Q4(c) you never know \(f'(c)\); you only know \(f'(c)\lt3\). Keep it as an inequality.
  • Choosing \([0,x]\) in May 2019 Q5(a). Near \(0\), \(F'(c)=\sqrt{\ln(c+1)}\) is close to 0, so there is no useful lower bound. Start the interval at 1.

May 2019 Q5(a)Q5: 10%; part marks not shown

Let \(F(x)\) be a continuous function on \([0,\infty)\) such that \(F(0)=0\) and its derivative \(F'(x)=\sqrt{\ln(x+1)}\) for all \(x\gt0\).
(a) Show that \(F(x)\) tend to infinity as \(x\to+\infty\).

Part (b) is in the L4 guide and part (c) in the L12 guide.

Hint

Type L8.4, growing interval (step 4b). First step: for \(x\gt1\) apply the MVT on \([1,x]\); what is the smallest \(F'(c)\) can be when \(c\gt1\)?

Solution

Step 1–2 Take \(x\gt1\). \(F\) is continuous on \([1,x]\) and differentiable on \((1,x)\) (\(F'\) exists for all \(x\gt0\)).

Step 3 MVT: for some \(c\in(1,x)\),

\[F(x)-F(1)=(x-1)\sqrt{\ln(c+1)}.\]

Step 4b \(c\gt1\), and \(\ln\) and \(\sqrt{\ }\) are increasing, so \(\sqrt{\ln(c+1)}\gt\sqrt{\ln2}\). Hence

\[F(x)\gt F(1)+(x-1)\sqrt{\ln2}.\]

Step 3–4b \(F(1)\gt0\): by the MVT on \([0,1]\), \(F(1)-F(0)=1\cdot\sqrt{\ln(c_1+1)}\gt0\) for some \(c_1\in(0,1)\).

So \(F(x)\gt(x-1)\sqrt{\ln2}\), and the right side tends to \(\infty\) since \(\sqrt{\ln2}\approx0.833\gt0\).

\[\boxed{F(x)\to+\infty\ \text{as}\ x\to+\infty}\]

Marker: the bound must not depend on \(x\): \(\sqrt{\ln2}\) is a fixed positive number, which is why the interval starts at 1.

Equivalent forms. Any fixed start \(k\gt0\) works, with \(\sqrt{\ln(k+1)}\) in place of \(\sqrt{\ln2}\).
