ODEs by Substitution

0Where this part sits

Think of an ODE as a scene filmed by a camera. From the usual angle (the unknown \(y\) against \(x\)) most equations in this part look nonlinear or too hard, and most nonlinear ODEs cannot be solved exactly. A substitution (a change of unknown, like \(u=y^{-1}\) or \(y=vx\)) changes the camera angle: the same solution curves, described by a new unknown, until the equation looks like a type from Part O1 (linear or separable). Solve it in that view, then switch back to \(y\). It is the same idea as integration by substitution (Lecture 13).

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 Bernoulli: a power change of the unknown straightens the extra power of \(y\), and the equation becomes linear. Asked most often
  2. §2 Riccati: ride along one known solution and watch 1 ÷ the gap; that view is linear. You must be given (or guess) that solution.
  3. §3 Separable is the view the next substitutions aim for. Reminder from Part O1
  4. §4 Degree 0 means only the direction from the origin matters, that is, only \(\frac yx\).
  5. §5 Homogeneous: describe each point by the slope \(\frac yx\) of its line from the origin, and the equation separates.
  6. §6 Shift: name the repeated combination, or move the origin to where two lines cross. Going further
  7. §7 Reduction of order: measure the unknown solution in units of a known one, and one derivative drops out.

You need: Part O1 (separable equations and the integrating factor), the chain and product rules (Lecture 6), and integration by substitution and partial fractions (Lectures 13–14). In the exam: almost every recent paper has one 5-mark "Solve the differential equation" part that needs Bernoulli, homogeneous, Riccati or a shift, and recent papers no longer print the substitution; reduction of order was last asked in May 2017. See the O2 exam guide.

Reminders from Part O1

A differential equation is an equation that links an unknown function \(y(x)\) with its derivatives, for example \(\frac{dy}{dx}=y-y^2\). It is an ordinary differential equation (ODE) when there is only one independent variable (here \(x\)). Solving it means finding the function \(y(x)\) itself. A general solution contains arbitrary constants (\(C\), \(c_1\), \(c_2\), …) and describes every solution at once, a whole family of curves.

Part O1 (the first part of the ODE half) set up the language and three direct methods. One-line reminders:

  • Order: the order of the highest derivative in the equation (\(y''\) appears → second order).
  • Linear vs nonlinear: an ODE is linear if the unknown \(y\) and its derivatives appear only to the first power, never multiplied together and never inside a nonlinear function (no \(y^2\), \(\sin y\), \(e^y\), \(y\,y'\), …). Nonlinear functions of \(x\) are allowed. Linear ODEs obey the superposition principle: if the right side is zero, the sum of two solutions is again a solution.
  • Separable: \(\frac{dy}{dx}=X(x)\,Y(y)\). Put all the \(y\)'s on one side, all the \(x\)'s on the other, and integrate.
  • Linear first order, integrating factor: write the equation as \(\frac{dy}{dx}+P(x)\,y=Q(x)\) (coefficient of \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) equal to 1, the \(y\) term on the same side). Multiply by the integrating factor \(e^{\int P\,dx}\). The left side becomes one derivative: \[ \frac{d}{dx}\Big(e^{\int P\,dx}\,y\Big)=Q(x)\,e^{\int P\,dx}, \] and you integrate both sides. This method always works for a first-order linear ODE.
  • Exact: \(M\,dx+N\,dy=0\) with \(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\); the solution is \(F(x,y)=\text{constant}\).

Notation. A prime means a derivative with respect to \(x\): \(y'=\frac{dy}{dx}\), \(y''=\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\). The functions \(p(x)\), \(q(x)\), \(r(x)\) are given functions of \(x\) only.

1Bernoulli's equation ~7 min

What is this?

A Bernoulli equation is a linear equation with one extra power of the unknown on the right. Seen from the usual angle, that power bends the picture: the equation is nonlinear, and the integrating factor cannot grip it. The fix is to change the ruler on the vertical axis. Keep the same solution curves, but measure their height with a power of \(y\) instead of \(y\) itself, much as log graph paper makes an exponential curve look straight. With the right power, the bend disappears and a linear equation appears.

Definition

A Bernoulli equation is a first-order ODE of the form

\[ \frac{dy}{dx}+p(x)\,y=q(x)\,y^n , \]

where \(n\) is any real number: it need not be an integer (\(n=\frac12\), \(n=-3\), \(n=\frac23\) are all allowed).

In words: rate of change of \(y\) + (function of \(x\)) × \(y\) = (function of \(x\)) × (a power of \(y\)).

It looks exactly like the linear equation of Part O1, except for the extra power \(y^n\) on the right. For \(n\ne0,1\) that power makes the equation nonlinear, so the integrating factor cannot be used directly. It is a special nonlinear first-order ODE that can still be solved exactly, which means we get a formula for \(y\), not just a numerical approximation.

What is this? (the power change)

The right power is \(1-n\): one minus the power on the right. Dividing by \(y^n\) moves all of the bend onto the left side, and the new ruler \(u\) is chosen so that the chain rule turns that left side into a plain rate of change of \(u\). The new view only exists where we did not divide by zero, so the curve \(y=0\) has to be checked by hand.

Method

To solve \(\frac{dy}{dx}+p(x)\,y=q(x)\,y^n\) (with \(n\ne1\)), use the substitution

\[ u=y^{1-n}. \]

In words: new unknown = old unknown raised to the power (1 − the power on the right).

This converts the Bernoulli equation into a linear first-order equation in \(u\):

\[ \frac{du}{dx}+(1-n)\,p(x)\,u=(1-n)\,q(x). \]

In words: rate of change of \(u\) + (1 − n) × p × \(u\) = (1 − n) × q. Both p and q get multiplied by the same factor (1 − n).

Reading the formula
\(u=y^{1-n}\)
the new ruler: the height of the same curve, read on a power scale.
\(\frac{du}{dx}\)
how fast the curve climbs, measured on the new ruler.
\((1-n)\)
the conversion factor the chain rule brings in when you change ruler; it multiplies both \(p\) and \(q\).
\((1-n)\,q(x)\)
the right side no longer contains the unknown: this is why the new view is linear.

Why the substitution works

  1. Do: divide the whole equation by \(y^n\) (assume \(y\ne0\) for now): \[ y^{-n}\frac{dy}{dx}+p(x)\,y^{1-n}=q(x). \] Now the right side has no \(y\) at all, and \(y^{1-n}\) appears in the middle term. That suggests calling \(u=y^{1-n}\). The bend now sits only on the left, and the right side is a function of \(x\) alone, as a linear equation needs.
  2. Do: differentiate \(u\) with the chain rule (Lecture 6), remembering \(y\) is a function of \(x\): \[ \frac{du}{dx}=(1-n)\,y^{-n}\,\frac{dy}{dx}. \] So the first term \(y^{-n}\frac{dy}{dx}\) is exactly \(\frac{1}{1-n}\frac{du}{dx}\). This tells us how the awkward first term looks on the new ruler: it is just a rate of change of \(u\).
  3. Do: substitute: \[ \frac{1}{1-n}\frac{du}{dx}+p(x)\,u=q(x). \] Multiply by \((1-n)\) to get the linear equation in the box. Solve it with the integrating factor \(e^{\int(1-n)p\,dx}\) (Part O1), then return to \(y\) using \(y=u^{1/(1-n)}\). Every \(y\) has been replaced by \(u\) to the first power, so Part O1 can finish the job; the last move switches the camera back.
Special values of n and a lost solution
  • \(n=0\): the equation is \(y'+py=q\), already linear. Nothing to do.
  • \(n=1\): the equation is \(y'+(p-q)y=0\), linear (and separable). The substitution \(u=y^0=1\) is useless, so the method needs \(n\ne1\).
  • When \(n\gt0\), the constant function \(y=0\) is a solution (both sides are 0). Dividing by \(y^n\) throws it away, so state it separately if the question wants all solutions.
  • When \(n\) is not an integer, \(y^n\) may only make sense for \(y\ge0\) (for example \(\sqrt y\)); the answer is then valid only where \(y\ge0\).
Example 1

Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}+y=y^2\).

Spot the type. \(p(x)=1\), \(q(x)=1\), \(n=2\). So \(u=y^{1-2}=y^{-1}=\frac1y\).

Transform. Divide by \(y^2\): \(y^{-2}y'+y^{-1}=1\). Since \(u'=-y^{-2}y'\), the first term is \(-u'\): the chain rule brings in the factor \(1-n=-1\); this minus sign is the one students drop.

\[ -u'+u=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u'-u=-1. \]

(Check against the box: \((1-n)p=-1\), \((1-n)q=-1\). ✓)

Integrating factor \(e^{\int(-1)dx}=e^{-x}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx}\big(e^{-x}u\big)&=-e^{-x}\\ e^{-x}u&=e^{-x}+C\\ u&=1+Ce^{x}. \end{aligned} \]

Back to y:

\[ y=\frac{1}{1+Ce^{x}},\qquad\text{and also } y=0. \]

In words: y = 1 ÷ (1 + constant × eˣ); the zero function is a separate solution lost when we divided by y².

Compare the logistic equation \(y'=y(1-y)\) used for population growth (a refinement of the growth model in Part O1). Our equation is its mirror image, \(y'=y^2-y=-y(1-y)\): solutions between 0 and 1 move away from \(y=1\) and towards \(y=0\) as \(x\) increases.

chosen solutionother membersy = 1 (C = 0)y = 0 (lost solution)

Drag C. For \(C\gt0\) the curve falls from near 1 (far left) to near 0 (far right). For \(C\lt0\) there is a vertical asymptote at \(x=\ln(-1/C)\): to its left the curve lies above 1 and shoots up; to its right the curve is negative and climbs back towards 0. No value of \(C\) gives the red line \(y=0\); it only appears as the limit \(C\to\infty\), which is why it must be listed separately.

Example 2: a non-integer power

Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}+\dfrac{y}{x}=x\sqrt y\) for \(x\gt0\), \(y\ge0\).

Spot the type. \(\sqrt y=y^{1/2}\), so \(n=\frac12\), \(p=\frac1x\), \(q=x\). Substitution \(u=y^{1-\frac12}=y^{1/2}=\sqrt y\).

Transform (using the boxed formula, \(1-n=\frac12\)):

\[ u'+\frac{1}{2x}\,u=\frac x2. \]

Integrating factor \(e^{\int\frac1{2x}dx}=e^{\frac12\ln x}=x^{1/2}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx}\big(x^{1/2}u\big)&=\frac{x^{3/2}}{2}\\ x^{1/2}u&=\frac{x^{5/2}}{5}+C\\ u&=\frac{x^2}{5}+\frac{C}{\sqrt x}. \end{aligned} \]

Back to y (\(y=u^2\)):

\[ y=\left(\frac{x^2}{5}+\frac{C}{\sqrt x}\right)^{2},\qquad\text{and also } y=0, \]

valid where \(\frac{x^2}{5}+\frac{C}{\sqrt x}\ge0\) (because \(u=\sqrt y\) cannot be negative).

In words: y = (x²/5 + constant/√x)², as long as the bracket is not negative.

You can now: recognise a Bernoulli equation and read off \(p\), \(q\) and \(n\) after tidying it; turn it into a linear equation with \(u=y^{1-n}\) and solve it with an integrating factor; switch back to \(y\), state where the answer is valid, and list \(y=0\) when \(n\gt0\).

2Riccati's equation ~5 min

What is this?

A Riccati equation has a \(y^2\) term plus a term with no \(y\) at all, so no change of ruler on its own straightens it. But if you already know one solution curve \(Y\), you can put the camera on it, like filming from a car driving along a known road, and watch only the gap \(y-Y\) between your curve and the known one. Turning that gap upside down (the new unknown is 1 ÷ the gap) gives a linear equation. Without a known solution there is nothing to ride on, which is why the method needs \(Y\) first.

Definition

A Riccati equation is a first-order ODE of the form

\[ \frac{dy}{dx}=p(x)\,y^2+q(x)\,y+r(x). \]

In words: rate of change of y = a quadratic expression in y whose coefficients are functions of x.

It is nonlinear (because of \(y^2\)) and is not one of the classical types: it is not linear, separable, exact or Bernoulli in general, and there is no recipe that always works from scratch. You need one extra piece of information.

Method

If one particular solution \(Y(x)\) is known (one specific function, with no arbitrary constant, that satisfies the equation), substitute

\[ y=Y(x)+\frac1u . \]

In words: unknown solution = the known solution + 1 ÷ (new unknown).

The equation for \(u\) is then linear:

\[ \frac{du}{dx}+\big(2p(x)Y(x)+q(x)\big)\,u=-p(x). \]

In words: rate of change of u + (2 × p × known solution + q) × u = −p.

Reading the formula
\(Y(x)\)
the known solution: the road the camera rides on.
\(u\)
1 ÷ the gap between the unknown solution and the known one; a large \(u\) means the two curves are close.
\(2p(x)Y(x)+q(x)\)
how strongly the equation pushes nearby curves towards or away from the known one (it is the derivative of the right side with respect to \(y\), taken at \(y=Y\)).
\(-p(x)\)
what is left of the \(y^2\) term once the known solution has absorbed the rest.

Why it works

  1. Do: differentiate \(y=Y+u^{-1}\): \(y'=Y'-\dfrac{u'}{u^2}\). This is the slope of the unknown curve as seen from the camera riding on \(Y\): the known slope plus the change in the gap.
  2. Do: expand the right side: \[ \begin{aligned} &p\Big(Y+\frac1u\Big)^2+q\Big(Y+\frac1u\Big)+r\\ &=\underbrace{pY^2+qY+r}_{=\,Y'}+\frac{2pY+q}{u}+\frac{p}{u^2}. \end{aligned} \] The underbraced part equals \(Y'\) because \(Y\) is a solution. Grouping by powers of \(\frac1u\) separates what the known road does on its own from what the gap does.
  3. Do: so \(Y'\) cancels from both sides: \[ -\frac{u'}{u^2}=\frac{2pY+q}{u}+\frac{p}{u^2}. \] Only the behaviour of the gap is left in the picture.
  4. Do: multiply by \(-u^2\): \(u'=-(2pY+q)u-p\), which is the boxed linear equation. The nasty \(y^2\) disappeared because the known solution already "uses up" the quadratic part. Clearing the fractions leaves \(u\) and \(u'\) only to the first power, so the new view is linear and Part O1 can finish it.
Going further
  • Links with §1. If \(r(x)=0\), the Riccati equation \(y'-qy=py^2\) is a Bernoulli equation with \(n=2\) (and \(Y=0\) is a particular solution, so both methods agree). If \(p(x)=0\), it is just linear.
  • Finding \(Y\). Exam questions give the substitution or the particular solution. If not, try simple shapes suggested by the equation: a constant, \(Y=ax\), or \(Y=\frac ax\) (good when the equation contains \(\frac1{x^2}\)), and choose \(a\) to make it work. Always check \(Y\) by substituting it back.
  • The known solution \(Y\) itself is not included in \(Y+\frac1u\) for any finite \(u\); it corresponds to "\(u=\infty\)" (constant \(\to\infty\)). Mention it separately if all solutions are wanted.
Example 3

Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=y^2-\dfrac yx-\dfrac1{x^2}\), given that \(Y=\dfrac1x\) is a particular solution.

Check \(Y\). \(Y'=-\frac1{x^2}\). Right side: \(\frac1{x^2}-\frac1{x^2}-\frac1{x^2}=-\frac1{x^2}\). ✓

Read off \(p=1\), \(q=-\frac1x\), so \(2pY+q=\frac2x-\frac1x=\frac1x\). With \(y=\frac1x+\frac1u\) the linear equation is

\[ u'+\frac1x\,u=-1. \]

Integrating factor \(e^{\int\frac1x dx}=x\) (take \(x\gt0\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} (xu)'&=-x\\ xu&=-\frac{x^2}{2}+C\\ u&=\frac{2C-x^2}{2x}. \end{aligned} \]

Back to y (write \(K=2C\)):

\[ y=\frac1x+\frac{2x}{K-x^2},\qquad\text{and also } y=\frac1x. \]

the known solution itself is the case "gap = 0", that is \(u=\infty\), so the formula cannot produce it and it is listed separately.

In words: y = the known solution 1/x + a correction 2x/(K − x²) that contains the arbitrary constant.

You can now: recognise a Riccati equation and read off \(p\), \(q\), \(r\); check a given particular solution \(Y\) by substituting it; use \(y=Y+\frac1u\) to get a linear equation in \(u\), solve it and switch back; and list \(Y\) itself as an extra solution.

3Separable equations: a reminder ~2 min

What is this?

Separable is the camera angle that §5 and §6 are trying to reach. The slope is a product of two pieces, one that only knows where you are left to right and one that only knows your height. So the \(x\)-information and the \(y\)-information can be sorted into two piles, one on each side, and each pile integrated on its own.

This is the separable equation from Part O1, repeated here because two of the substitutions in this part (homogeneous §5 and shift §6) end in a separable equation.

Definition (from Part O1)

A separable equation is a first-order equation of the form

\[ \frac{dy}{dx}=X(x)\,Y(y). \]

By observation, the \(x\)'s can be moved to one side and the \(y\)'s to the other:

\[ \frac{1}{Y(y)}\,dy=X(x)\,dx, \]

and the solution is obtained by simple integration: \(\displaystyle\int\frac{dy}{Y(y)}=\int X(x)\,dx+C\).

In words: derivative = (function of x only) × (function of y only); divide by the y-part, then integrate each side with respect to its own variable.

Here \(X\) and \(Y\) are just names for "a function of \(x\) only" and "a function of \(y\) only" (this \(Y\) has nothing to do with the particular solution \(Y\) of §2). If \(Y(y_0)=0\) for some number \(y_0\), the constant \(y=y_0\) is also a solution that dividing by \(Y(y)\) loses. Full treatment and examples: Part O1.

You can now: recognise a separable equation; separate and integrate it; and spot the constant solutions \(y=y_0\) lost when dividing by \(Y(y)\).

4Homogeneous functions ~3 min

What is this?

Picture enlarging a photo about the origin: every point moves the same number of times further out along its own line from the origin. A homogeneous function of degree \(N\) responds to every such enlargement in the same simple way, like a length, an area, or (for degree 0) an angle, which does not change at all. A degree-0 function therefore only sees which direction a point lies in from the origin. That direction is the slope \(\frac yx\) of the line from the origin to the point, and it is the camera angle §5 uses.

Definition

A function \(g(x,y)\) of two variables is homogeneous of degree \(N\) if, for every scale factor \(k\),

\[ g(kx,ky)=k^N\,g(x,y). \]

In words: stretch both inputs by the same factor k, and the output gets multiplied by k to the power N.

Reading the formula
\((kx,ky)\)
the same point after the enlargement: \(k\) times as far from the origin, on the same line through the origin.
\(k^N\)
how the value grows: like a length for \(N=1\), like an area for \(N=2\), not at all for \(N=0\).
\(N\)
the degree: for a polynomial, the total power of \(x\) and \(y\) shared by every term.

Analogy: in a photo enlarged \(k\) times, every length grows by \(k\) (degree 1), every area by \(k^2\) (degree 2), and every angle stays the same (degree 0). Each term of a homogeneous polynomial has the same total power of \(x\) and \(y\) (the powers in each term add up to the same number \(N\)).

Three examples, checked by replacing \(x\to kx\), \(y\to ky\):

\(x^2+3xy+y^2\): degree 2\(k^2x^2+3k^2xy+k^2y^2\)
\(=k^2(x^2+3xy+y^2)\). Every term has total power 2.
\(x^3+y^3\): degree 3\(k^3x^3+k^3y^3=k^3(x^3+y^3)\).
\(x^2+y^3\): NOT homogeneous\(k^2x^2+k^3y^3\). The two terms grow at different rates, so no single \(k^N\) can be factored out.
Key fact: degree 0 means "depends only on y/x"

If \(g\) is homogeneous of degree 0, then \(g(kx,ky)=g(x,y)\). Choose \(k=\frac1x\) (\(x\ne0\)):

\[ g(x,y)=g\Big(1,\frac yx\Big)=G\Big(\frac yx\Big). \]

In words: a degree-0 function does not care about size, only about the ratio y/x (the slope of the line from the origin to the point).

A quotient of two homogeneous functions of the same degree is homogeneous of degree 0, e.g. \(\frac{x^2+3xy+y^2}{x^2}=1+3\frac yx+\big(\frac yx\big)^2\).

You can now: test whether a function is homogeneous by replacing \(x\to kx\), \(y\to ky\), and give its degree; spot that a quotient of two homogeneous functions of the same degree has degree 0; and rewrite such a quotient as a function of \(\frac yx\).

5Homogeneous equations: the substitution y = vx ~7 min

What is this?

Stand at the origin and look out along one line through it. Every point on that line has the same slope \(\frac yx\), and for a homogeneous equation every point on it also has the same slope mark (the figure below shows this). So describe a point not by its height \(y\) but by which line it is on, \(w=\frac yx\), and how far along it is, \(x\). In that view the \(w\)-part and the \(x\)-part separate. The method needs \(g\) of degree 0, and \(x\ne0\): points on the vertical axis have no slope \(\frac yx\).

Method

The equation \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=g(x,y)\), where \(g\) is homogeneous (of degree 0), can be solved by the substitution

\[ w=\frac yx,\qquad\text{i.e. } y=wx . \]

In words: new unknown = y ÷ x, the slope of the line from the origin to the point (x, y).

Exam papers and many textbooks call the new unknown \(v\) and write \(y=vx\); it is the same thing.

Note: for the method to work, \(g\) must be homogeneous of degree 0 (as in §4's key fact). Equivalently, if the equation is written as \(M(x,y)\,dx+N(x,y)\,dy=0\), then \(M\) and \(N\) must be homogeneous of the same degree.

Why it works

  1. Do: degree 0 means \(g(x,y)=G(w)\) with \(w=\frac yx\) (§4). In the new view the right side only asks which line through the origin you are on.
  2. Do: from \(y=wx\), the product rule (Lecture 6) gives \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=w+x\frac{dw}{dx}. \]

    In words: derivative of y = w + x × (derivative of w). The "+ w" is the part students forget.

    Moving along a solution changes both the line you are on (\(w\)) and the distance along it (\(x\)), so the slope has two parts.
  3. Do: so the equation becomes \[ w+x\frac{dw}{dx}=G(w)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x\frac{dw}{dx}=G(w)-w, \] which is separable (§3): \[ \int\frac{dw}{G(w)-w}=\int\frac{dx}{x}=\ln|x|+C. \]The \(w\)-pieces and the \(x\)-pieces now sit in separate factors: the camera has reached the separable view of §3.
  4. Do: finally replace \(w\) by \(\frac yx\). If \(G(w_0)=w_0\) for some number \(w_0\), the straight line \(y=w_0x\) is an extra solution lost by dividing. Where \(G(w_0)=w_0\) the slope mark points straight along the line itself, so a solution can run along that line without turning.
Reading the formula
\(w\)
the slope of the line from the origin to the point: which line the camera is looking along.
\(G(w)\)
the slope of the solution as it crosses that line (the same at every point of the line).
\(G(w)-w\)
how much the solution's direction differs from the line's; where it is 0, the line itself is a solution.
\(x\dfrac{dw}{dx}\)
how quickly the solution swings onto a new line as it moves along.

Why only y/x matters: the slope field is constant along every ray

slope marks y′ = g(x, y)marks on the chosen line through O

A slope field draws, at many points \((x,y)\), a short dash with slope \(g(x,y)\): the direction a solution curve must have when it passes through that point. For a homogeneous equation, every point on the line \(y=mx\) has the same \(w=\frac yx=m\), hence the same slope \(G(m)\): the highlighted dashes are all parallel. Switch to the non-homogeneous equation and they are not.

Example 4 (using a function from §4)

Solve \(x^2\dfrac{dy}{dx}=x^2+3xy+y^2\) for \(x\ne0\).

Spot the type. Dividing by \(x^2\):

\[ \frac{dy}{dx}=1+3\frac yx+\Big(\frac yx\Big)^2 \]

so \(G(w)=1+3w+w^2\).

(Degree 2 on top over degree 2 below: degree 0.)

Substitute \(y=wx\), \(y'=w+xw'\):

\[ \begin{aligned} w+xw'&=1+3w+w^2\\ xw'&=w^2+2w+1=(w+1)^2. \end{aligned} \]

Separate (for \(w\ne-1\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} \int(w+1)^{-2}\,dw&=\int\frac{dx}{x}\\ -\frac{1}{w+1}&=\ln|x|+C\\ w&=-1-\frac{1}{\ln|x|+C}. \end{aligned} \]

Back to y (\(y=wx\)):

\[ y=-x-\frac{x}{\ln|x|+C},\qquad\text{and also } y=-x \]

at \(w=-1\) the slope mark points along the line \(y=-x\) itself, so that line is a solution the division by \((w+1)^2\) threw away.(the line from \(w=-1\), where \(G(w)-w=0\); check: \(x^2(-1)=x^2-3x^2+x^2\) ✓).

Example 5: spiral solutions

Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{x+y}{x-y}\).

Spot the type. Degree 1 over degree 1. Divide top and bottom by \(x\): \(G(w)=\frac{1+w}{1-w}\).

Substitute:

\[ \begin{aligned} w+xw'&=\frac{1+w}{1-w}\\ xw'&=\frac{1+w}{1-w}-w=\frac{1+w^2}{1-w}. \end{aligned} \]

Separate and integrate, splitting the fraction:

\[ \int\Big(\frac{1}{1+w^2}-\frac{w}{1+w^2}\Big)dw=\int\frac{dx}{x}. \]

The first piece is \(\tan^{-1}w\) (Lecture 7); the second is \(\frac12\ln(1+w^2)\) by the substitution \(t=1+w^2\) (Lecture 13):

\[ \tan^{-1}w-\tfrac12\ln(1+w^2)=\ln|x|+C. \]

Back to x, y. Since \(\ln|x|+\frac12\ln\big(1+\frac{y^2}{x^2}\big)=\ln\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\):

\[ \tan^{-1}\frac yx=\ln\sqrt{x^2+y^2}+C . \]

In polar coordinates (Lecture 15: \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), \(\theta\) the angle, \(\tan\theta=\frac yx\)) this is \(\theta=\ln r+C\), i.e.

\[ r=Ke^{\theta}\quad(K=e^{-C}\gt0). \]

In words: distance from the origin = constant × e^(angle): every solution curve is a logarithmic spiral.

The formula with \(\tan^{-1}\frac yx\) describes the solutions piece by piece (for \(x\gt0\), \(\tan^{-1}\frac yx\) is exactly \(\theta\)); the polar form joins the pieces into whole spirals.

The spiral follows the slope marks everywhere: it is a solution curve. Changing \(K\) gives another member of the family (it is the same spiral, turned). The second option is the shifted equation of §6: the same picture moved so that its centre is \((1,1)\).

Two meanings of "homogeneous"

Be warned: the word homogeneous is used again later in the course with a completely different meaning:

  • Here (Part O2): \(y'=g(x,y)\) with \(g\) depending only on \(\frac yx\). Solve with \(y=vx\).
  • Later (Part O3): a linear equation whose right side is zero, e.g. \(y''-2y'+y=0\) (no term without \(y\)). Solve with a characteristic equation.

Decide which meaning from the shape of the equation, not from the word.

You can now: recognise a homogeneous first-order equation, including one written as \(M\,dx+N\,dy=0\); substitute \(y=vx\) with \(\frac{dy}{dx}=v+x\frac{dv}{dx}\); separate, integrate and switch back to \(x\) and \(y\); and list the straight-line solutions where \(G(w)=w\).

6Shift substitutions ~3 min

What is this?

Two more camera moves. If the right side only ever sees one combination, such as \(x+y\), give that combination its own name and watch it instead of \(y\). If the right side is a fraction of two straight-line expressions, slide the camera so that the origin sits where the two lines cross; sliding does not change any slope, and from the new origin the equation looks homogeneous. (If the two lines are parallel they never cross, but then top and bottom share one combination \(ax+by\), and the first move works.)

Going further

This type of question appears in recent exams (May 2025, for example). It uses only the ideas above.

Type A: the right side depends only on one combination \(ax+by+c\)

  1. Do: if the equation is \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=f(ax+by+c)\), put \[ u=ax+by+c, \] The right side then depends on one unknown, \(u\), instead of on \(x\) and \(y\) together.
  2. Do: differentiate: \[ \begin{aligned}\frac{du}{dx}&=a+b\,\frac{dy}{dx}\\ &=a+b\,f(u). \end{aligned} \] The right side is a function of \(u\) alone, so the equation is separable (§3); solve it and put \(u\) back.

In words: name the repeated combination u; its derivative is a + b × (the right side), which is separable.

Recognition cue: after expanding, the right side contains a perfect square like \(y^2-2xy+x^2=(y-x)^2\) or \((x+y)^2\), or a function such as \(\sin(x-y)\). Try \(u=y-x\), \(u=x+y\), … Watch the extra constant: for \(u=y-x\), \(u'=y'-1\), not \(u'=y'\).

Example 6

Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=(x+y)^2\).

Put \(u=x+y\), so \(u'=1+y'=1+u^2\). Separate: the derivative of \(x\) is 1, which gives the extra 1; without it the equation would not match.

\[ \begin{aligned} \int\frac{du}{1+u^2}&=\int dx\\ \tan^{-1}u&=x+C\\ u&=\tan(x+C). \end{aligned} \]

Back to \(y=u-x\):

\[ y=\tan(x+C)-x. \]

Type B: shifting the origin

  1. Do: for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{a_1x+b_1y+c_1}{a_2x+b_2y+c_2}\), find the point \((h,k)\) where the two lines \(a_1x+b_1y+c_1=0\) and \(a_2x+b_2y+c_2=0\) meet. That crossing is where top and bottom are both zero: the natural centre of the picture.
  2. Do: put \(x=X+h\), \(y=Y+k\). The constants disappear and the new equation in \(X,Y\) is homogeneous (§5). Sliding the camera does not change slopes (\(\frac{dY}{dX}=\frac{dy}{dx}\)); it only removes the constants that spoilt the degree.
  3. Do: solve with \(Y=vX\), then put \(X=x-h\), \(Y=y-k\) back. This slides the camera back to the original origin.
Example 7

Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{x+y-2}{x-y}\).

The lines \(x+y=2\) and \(x=y\) meet at \((1,1)\). With \(X=x-1\), \(Y=y-1\) (so \(\frac{dY}{dX}=\frac{dy}{dx}\)):

\[ \frac{dY}{dX}=\frac{X+Y}{X-Y}, \]

which is Example 5. So the solutions are the same spirals, centred at \((1,1)\):

\[ \begin{aligned}&\tan^{-1}\frac{y-1}{x-1}\\ &\quad=\ln\sqrt{(x-1)^2+(y-1)^2}+C. \end{aligned} \]

(Second option of the spiral figure above.)

Every solution is increasing (\(y'=(x+y)^2\ge0\)) and has vertical asymptotes where \(x+C=\frac\pi2+n\pi\): each solution only lives on an interval of length \(\pi\). Changing \(C\) slides the tangent pattern sideways.

You can now: spot a right side that depends only on one combination such as \(x+y\) or \(y-x\) (often a perfect square), substitute it with the correct \(u'\), and separate; and move the origin to where two lines meet to make a linear-over-linear equation homogeneous.

7Reduction of order ~8 min

What is this?

If you already know one solution \(y_1\) of a linear equation, use it as a measuring stick: describe any other solution as \(y_1\) times a new unknown \(u\), the way you would measure a length in multiples of a stick. A constant \(u\) just gives a multiple of \(y_1\), which is already a solution, so it costs nothing; only how \(u\) changes is left to find. Watching \(u'\) instead of \(u\) drops the order by one. This needs the equation to be linear, so that multiples of a solution are solutions.

Background from Part O3, in brief

Reduction of order is about second-order linear equations. Here is the minimum you need (Part O3 covers it properly):

  • A second-order linear equation (with zero right side) has the form \[ y''+P(x)\,y'+Q(x)\,y=0 . \] (This is "homogeneous" in the second meaning of §5.)
  • By superposition (Part O1), if \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) are solutions then so is \(c_1y_1+c_2y_2\). If \(y_2\) is genuinely different from \(y_1\), i.e. not just a constant multiple of it (linearly independent, Part O3), then \[ y=c_1y_1+c_2y_2 \] is the general solution: two arbitrary constants for a second-order equation.
  • For constant coefficients, e.g. \(y''-2y'+y=0\), Part O3 tries \(y=e^{mx}\). Since \(y'=me^{mx}\), \(y''=m^2e^{mx}\), this gives the characteristic equation \(m^2-2m+1=0\), i.e. \((m-1)^2=0\). There is only one (repeated) root \(m=1\), so only one solution \(y_1=e^x\). Where is the second one? Reduction of order finds it.
The idea: dividing out a known factor

For the quadratic \(x^2-5x+6=0\), if we know one root \(x=2\), we divide by the factor \((x-2)\):

\[ \frac{x^2-5x+6}{x-2}=x-3, \]

which is simpler (degree 1 instead of 2) and gives the other root \(x=3\).

In words: knowing one answer lets you reduce the problem to a smaller one.

Similarly, for an ODE, if \(y_1\) is a known solution we look at \(\frac{y}{y_1}\), i.e. write \(y=y_1u\) and find the equation for \(u\).

Method

When one solution \(y_1\) of a linear differential equation in \(y\) is known, substituting

\[ y=y_1(x)\,u(x) \]

reduces the order of the differential equation by one.

In words: unknown solution = known solution × new unknown function; the new problem has one derivative fewer.

Why it works

  1. Do: differentiate \(y=y_1u\) twice with the product rule: \[ \begin{aligned} y'&=y_1'u+y_1u'\\ y''&=y_1''u+2y_1'u'+y_1u''. \end{aligned} \] Each derivative splits into a part where the measuring stick \(y_1\) changes and a part where the count \(u\) changes.
  2. Do: substitute into \(y''+Py'+Qy=0\) and group by \(u\), \(u'\), \(u''\): \[ \begin{aligned} &y_1u''+\big(2y_1'+Py_1\big)u'\\ &\quad+\underbrace{\big(y_1''+Py_1'+Qy_1\big)}_{=\,0\text{ since }y_1\text{ is a solution}}u=0. \end{aligned} \] The \(u\) term always vanishes. The bracket in front of \(u\) is the equation applied to the stick \(y_1\) itself, which is zero because \(y_1\) is a solution.
  3. Do: only \(u''\) and \(u'\) remain, so put \(w=u'\): \[ y_1\,w'+\big(2y_1'+Py_1\big)\,w=0, \]

    In words: known solution × (derivative of w) + (2 × derivative of known solution + P × known solution) × w = 0.

    With no \(u\) left, the equation only sees \(u'\); naming it \(w\) makes the order one lower.
  4. Do: this is a first-order linear (and separable) equation for \(w\): solve it (Part O1), integrate once more to get \(u\), and multiply by \(y_1\). The same works for higher orders: a third-order linear equation becomes second order in \(w\), and so on. Integrating \(w\) brings in the second constant, and multiplying by \(y_1\) turns the count back into a solution \(y\).
Reading the formula
\(u\)
how many "sticks" \(y_1\) the unknown solution is worth at each \(x\).
\(w=u'\)
how fast that count changes; \(w=0\) just gives a constant multiple of \(y_1\).
\(2y_1'+Py_1\)
the only coefficient left once the \(u\) term has gone (in Example 8 it is 0 as well).
Example 8

For \(y''-2y'+y=0\) with \(y=y_1u\), where \(y_1=e^x\), the resulting equation for \(u\) has only \(u''\) and \(u'\) terms and no \(u\) term. Defining \(w=u'\) gives a first-order ODE in \(w\): reduction of order from second to first.

Check \(y_1\). \(e^x-2e^x+e^x=0\) ✓.

Differentiate \(y=e^xu\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y'&=e^x(u+u')\\ y''&=e^x(u+2u'+u''). \end{aligned} \]

Substitute and divide by \(e^x\neq0\): \(e^x\) is never zero, so dividing by it loses no solutions.

\[ \begin{aligned} (u+2u'+u'')-2(u+u')+u&=0\\ u''&=0. \end{aligned} \]

In this example even the \(u'\) term cancels (\(2y_1'+Py_1=2e^x-2e^x=0\)), so the equation is as simple as possible. In general a \(u'\) term remains.

Reduce: \(w=u'\) gives \(w'=0\), so \(w=c_2\). Then \(u=c_2x+c_1\), and

\[ y=e^xu=(c_1+c_2x)\,e^x . \]

In words: the second solution is x times the first one: \(y_2=xe^x\). This "multiply by x" rule for a repeated root of the characteristic equation is used throughout Part O3.

General solution of y″ − 2y′ + y = 0

y = (c₁ + c₂x)eˣy₁ = eˣy₂ = xeˣ (found by reduction of order)

\(e^x\) never crosses zero, but \(xe^x\) does (at \(x=0\)), so \(y_2\) is not a constant multiple of \(y_1\): they are linearly independent. With both sliders you can match any starting height \(y(0)=c_1\) and starting slope \(y'(0)=c_1+c_2\), which a single \(c\,e^x\) could not.

Example 9: variable coefficients

Given that \(y_1=x^2\) solves \(x^2y''-3xy'+4y=0\) (\(x\gt0\)), find the general solution.

Check \(y_1\). \(x^2(2)-3x(2x)+4x^2=0\) ✓.

Substitute \(y=x^2u\) directly into the equation as given (no need to divide by \(x^2\) first):

\[ \begin{aligned} y'&=2xu+x^2u'\\ y''&=2u+4xu'+x^2u''. \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} &x^2(2u+4xu'+x^2u'')\\ &-3x(2xu+x^2u')+4x^2u\\ &=x^4u''+x^3u'+0\cdot u=0. \end{aligned} \]

Reduce: divide by \(x^3\) and put \(w=u'\): \(xw'+w=0\), i.e. \((xw)'=0\), so \(w=\frac{c_2}{x}\). Integrate: \(u=c_2\ln x+c_1\).

\[ y=x^2\,(c_1+c_2\ln x). \]

In words: the second solution is \(x^2\ln x\); the general solution is any combination of \(x^2\) and \(x^2\ln x\).

Going further

Solving \(y_1w'+(2y_1'+Py_1)w=0\) once and for all (separate variables) gives the ready-made formula

\[ w=u'=\frac{e^{-\int P\,dx}}{y_1^{\,2}}, \] \[ y_2=y_1\int\frac{e^{-\int P\,dx}}{y_1^{\,2}}\,dx, \]

where \(P\) is read from the standard form \(y''+Py'+Qy=0\) (coefficient of \(y''\) equal to 1). In Example 9, \(P=-\frac3x\): \(e^{-\int P}=x^3\), \(w=\frac{x^3}{x^4}=\frac1x\), \(y_2=x^2\ln x\) ✓. Exams expect the substitution working, so show the steps rather than quoting this.

You can now: substitute \(y=y_1u\) into a second-order linear equation and check that the \(u\) term vanishes; reduce with \(w=u'\), solve for \(w\), integrate to get \(u\); and write the general solution \(y=c_1y_1+c_2y_2\) with two constants.

8Summary

  • Bernoulli \(y'+py=qy^n\) (\(n\) any real, \(n\ne0,1\)): \(u=y^{1-n}\) gives the linear equation \(u'+(1-n)pu=(1-n)q\). For \(n\gt0\), \(y=0\) is an extra solution.
  • Riccati \(y'=py^2+qy+r\): needs one particular solution \(Y\); \(y=Y+\frac1u\) gives the linear equation \(u'+(2pY+q)u=-p\).
  • Separable \(y'=X(x)Y(y)\) (Part O1): separate and integrate. The homogeneous and shift substitutions end here.
  • Homogeneous function of degree \(N\): \(g(kx,ky)=k^Ng(x,y)\). Degree 0 means \(g\) depends only on \(\frac yx\).
  • Homogeneous equation \(y'=G\big(\frac yx\big)\): \(y=wx\) (or \(vx\)), \(y'=w+xw'\), then \(xw'=G(w)-w\) is separable. "Homogeneous" means something else for linear equations (Part O3).
  • Shift (going further): \(y'=f(ax+by+c)\) → \(u=ax+by+c\); a linear-over-linear right side → move the origin to where the two lines meet.
  • Reduction of order: if \(y_1\) solves a linear equation, \(y=y_1u\) kills the \(u\) term; \(w=u'\) gives an equation of one lower order. For \(y''-2y'+y=0\), \(y_1=e^x\) leads to \(y=(c_1+c_2x)e^x\).

9Common mistakes

✗Bernoulli: writing \(u'=y^{-n}y'\) and forgetting the factor \((1-n)\). ✓ \(u'=(1-n)y^{-n}y'\); e.g. for \(u=y^{-1}\), \(u'=-y^{-2}y'\) (minus sign). Both \(p\) and \(q\) get multiplied by \((1-n)\). ✗Bernoulli: reading \(p\), \(q\), \(n\) before tidying the equation. ✓ First make the coefficient of \(y'\) equal to 1 and put the \(y\) term on the left, as for the integrating factor. ✗Using \(u=y^{n-1}\) or \(u=y^n\). ✓ The power is \(1-n\); only then does the equation become linear. ✗Dividing by \(y^n\), \(G(w)-w\) or \(Y(y)\) and never mentioning what was divided by zero. ✓ Note the lost solutions (\(y=0\); lines \(y=w_0x\); the particular solution \(Y\) of a Riccati equation) when a question asks for all solutions. ✗Homogeneous: writing \(\frac{dy}{dx}=x\frac{dw}{dx}\) or \(\frac{dy}{dx}=w\). ✓ Product rule: \(\frac{dy}{dx}=w+x\frac{dw}{dx}\). ✗Calling an equation homogeneous because each function is a polynomial. ✓ Test \(g(kx,ky)=g(x,y)\); e.g. \(\frac{x^2+y^3}{x^2}\) fails. ✗Leaving the answer in \(w\) or \(u\). ✓ Substitute back (\(w=\frac yx\), \(u=y^{1-n}\), …) and give \(y\) (explicitly if easy, otherwise as an equation in \(x\) and \(y\)). ✗Riccati: \(\frac{d}{dx}\big(\frac1u\big)=\frac1{u'}\). ✓ \(\frac{d}{dx}\big(\frac1u\big)=-\frac{u'}{u^2}\) (chain rule). ✗Shift: \(u=y-x\) but \(u'=y'\). ✓ \(u'=y'-1\). ✗Reduction of order: giving only \(y_2\), or only \(u\). ✓ The answer is \(y=y_1u=c_1y_1+c_2y_2\), with two constants. ✗Mixing up the two meanings of "homogeneous". ✓ First order with \(\frac yx\): use \(y=vx\). 10Find the mistake

Each piece of student work below contains one error. Find it before opening the answer.

A. Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}+2y=e^xy^2\).

Bernoulli with \(n=2\): let \(u=y^{-1}\), so \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=y^{-2}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\).

Divide by \(y^2\): \(y^{-2}y'+2y^{-1}=e^x\), so \(u'+2u=e^x\).

Integrating factor \(e^{2x}\): \((e^{2x}u)'=e^{3x}\), so \(e^{2x}u=\frac13e^{3x}+C\).

\[ u=\tfrac13e^x+Ce^{-2x},\qquad y=\frac{1}{\frac13e^x+Ce^{-2x}}. \]
Hint

Differentiate \(u=y^{-1}\) again carefully with the chain rule.

Answer

The first line is wrong: \(\frac{du}{dx}=-y^{-2}\frac{dy}{dx}\) (the factor \(1-n=-1\) is missing). The correct linear equation is \(-u'+2u=e^x\), i.e. \(u'-2u=-e^x\). With integrating factor \(e^{-2x}\): \((e^{-2x}u)'=-e^{-x}\), \(e^{-2x}u=e^{-x}+C\), \(u=e^x+Ce^{2x}\):

\[ y=\frac{1}{e^x+Ce^{2x}}\quad(\text{and } y=0). \]

Substituting the student's answer back into the equation does not give an identity, which is a quick way to catch such errors.

B. Solve \(x^2\dfrac{dy}{dx}=y^2+2xy\).

Homogeneous: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=w^2+2w\) where \(w=\frac yx\). Let \(y=wx\), so \(x\dfrac{dw}{dx}=w^2+2w\).

\[ \int\frac{dw}{w(w+2)}=\int\frac{dx}{x} \] \[ \tfrac12\ln\left|\frac{w}{w+2}\right|=\ln|x|+C \] \[ \frac{w}{w+2}=Kx^2\;\Rightarrow\; y=\frac{2Kx^3}{1-Kx^2}. \]
Hint

What is \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) when \(y=wx\)?

Answer

The student used \(\frac{dy}{dx}=x\frac{dw}{dx}\) and lost the \(+w\). Correctly, \(w+xw'=w^2+2w\), so \(xw'=w^2+w=w(w+1)\). Partial fractions (Lecture 14): \(\frac1{w(w+1)}=\frac1w-\frac1{w+1}\), so \(\ln\left|\frac{w}{w+1}\right|=\ln|x|+C\), \(\frac{w}{w+1}=Kx\), \(w=\frac{Kx}{1-Kx}\):

\[ y=\frac{Kx^2}{1-Kx}\quad(\text{also } y=-x \text{ from } w=-1). \]

(\(y=0\) is the case \(K=0\).)

11Group work

Practice questions, one per method. Try each before opening the hints.

1. Solve \(x\dfrac{dy}{dx}+y=x^2y^2\) for \(x\gt0\).

Hint 1

Divide by \(x\) first: \(y'+\frac1xy=xy^2\). Bernoulli with \(n=2\).

Hint 2

\(u=y^{-1}\) gives \(u'-\frac1xu=-x\). Integrating factor \(\frac1x\).

Answer

\(\big(\frac ux\big)'=-1\), so \(\frac ux=-x+C\), \(u=Cx-x^2\):

\[ y=\frac{1}{Cx-x^2}\quad(\text{and } y=0). \]

2. Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=e^{-x}y^2+y-e^x\), given that \(Y=e^x\) is a particular solution.

Hint 1

Riccati with \(p=e^{-x}\), \(q=1\), \(r=-e^x\). Check \(Y\): \(e^{-x}e^{2x}+e^x-e^x=e^x=Y'\).

Hint 2

\(2pY+q=3\), so \(y=e^x+\frac1u\) gives \(u'+3u=-e^{-x}\).

Answer

Integrating factor \(e^{3x}\): \(e^{3x}u=-\frac12e^{2x}+C\), \(u=Ce^{-3x}-\frac12e^{-x}\). Writing \(K=2C\):

\[ y=e^x+\frac{2e^{3x}}{K-e^{2x}}\quad(\text{and } y=e^x). \]

3. Solve \(x\dfrac{dy}{dx}=y+xe^{y/x}\) for \(x\gt0\).

Hint 1

Divide by \(x\): \(y'=w+e^w\) with \(w=\frac yx\). Homogeneous.

Hint 2

\(w+xw'=w+e^w\) gives \(xw'=e^w\); separate as \(e^{-w}dw=\frac{dx}{x}\).

Answer

\(-e^{-w}=\ln x+C\), so \(e^{-w}=K-\ln x\) (\(K=-C\)) and \(w=-\ln(K-\ln x)\):

\[ y=-x\ln(K-\ln x),\qquad 0\lt x\lt e^K. \]

4. Given that \(y_1=x\) solves \(x^2y''+xy'-y=0\) (\(x\gt0\)), use reduction of order to find the general solution.

Hint 1

Put \(y=xu\): \(y'=u+xu'\), \(y''=2u'+xu''\).

Hint 2

The equation becomes \(x^3u''+3x^2u'=0\); with \(w=u'\), \(xw'+3w=0\).

Answer

\(w=cx^{-3}\), \(u=-\frac{c}{2x^2}+c_1\), so

\[ y=c_1x+\frac{c_2}{x}. \]

12Concept checks

1. Is \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{x^3+y^3}{xy^2}\) a homogeneous equation? If so, what is \(G(w)\)?

Answer

Yes: top and bottom are both degree 3, so the quotient is degree 0. Dividing top and bottom by \(x^3\): \(G(w)=\dfrac{1+w^3}{w^2}\).

2. Which substitution turns \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}-\dfrac yx=x\,y^{1/3}\) into a linear equation? What is that linear equation?

Answer

Bernoulli with \(n=\frac13\): \(u=y^{2/3}\). Since \(1-n=\frac23\): \(u'-\frac{2}{3x}u=\frac23x\).

3. A Riccati equation has \(r(x)=0\). Which other type is it, and which particular solution is then obvious?

Answer

It is \(y'-qy=py^2\): a Bernoulli equation with \(n=2\). The obvious particular solution is \(Y=0\), and then \(y=0+\frac1u\) is exactly the Bernoulli substitution \(u=y^{-1}\).

4. Which substitution would you try for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\sin(x-y)\)? What equation does it give?

Answer

\(u=x-y\). Then \(u'=1-y'=1-\sin u\): separable.

5. Is \(g(x,y)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) homogeneous? Of what degree?

Answer

Yes, of degree 1 for positive scale factors: \(\sqrt{k^2x^2+k^2y^2}=k\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) when \(k\gt0\) (for \(k\lt0\) you get \(|k|\)). The definition is usually read with \(k\gt0\).

6. \(y_1=e^{3x}\) solves \(y''-6y'+9y=0\). Without calculation, what does reduction of order give as a second solution, and why is the "no \(u\) term" property guaranteed?

Answer

\(y_2=xe^{3x}\) (same pattern as Example 8: the characteristic equation \((m-3)^2=0\) has a repeated root). The coefficient of \(u\) is \(y_1''-6y_1'+9y_1\), which is zero precisely because \(y_1\) is a solution.

\[ x^2\frac{dy}{dx}=(x^3+x^2e^x)\,y^2+xy \]
Hint

Bernoulli. Divide by \(x^2\) and move the \(y\) term left: \(y'-\frac1xy=(x+e^x)y^2\), so \(n=2\). Put \(u=y^{-1}\): the linear equation is \(u'+\frac1xu=-(x+e^x)\), integrating factor \(x\).

Answer
\[ y=\frac{x}{C-\frac{x^3}{3}-(x-1)e^x}\quad(\text{and } y=0) \]

Equivalently \(\frac xy=C-\frac{x^3}{3}-(x-1)e^x\).

Dec 2023 Q6(b)5%Guide · O2.1 Homogeneous (y = vx)

Solve the differential equation:

\[ (x^2+2y^2)\,dx-2xy\,dy=0\qquad(x\gt0) \]
Hint

Homogeneous: \(\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{x^2+2y^2}{2xy}\) is degree 2 over degree 2. Put \(y=vx\): \(v+xv'=\frac{1+2v^2}{2v}\), so \(xv'=\frac1{2v}\), which is separable.

Answer
\[ y^2=x^2(\ln x+C) \]

Equivalently \(y=\pm x\sqrt{\ln x+C}\).

May 2022 Q6(b)5%Guide · O2.3 Riccati

Solve the Riccati's equation: (Hint: use a substitution of \(y=-\frac1x+\frac1u\).)

\[ \frac{dy}{dx}=-y^2+\frac{2}{x^2} \]
Hint

First check that \(Y=-\frac1x\) is a solution. Then \(p=-1\), \(q=0\), so \(2pY+q=\frac2x\) and the linear equation is \(u'+\frac2xu=1\); integrating factor \(x^2\).

Answer
\[ y=-\frac1x+\frac{3x^2}{x^3+K}\quad(\text{and } y=-\tfrac1x) \]

Equivalently \(u=\frac x3+\frac{C}{x^2}\), or \(y=\dfrac{2x^3-K}{x(x^3+K)}\).

May 2025 Q6(b)Q6 total 10%Guide · O2.4 Shift substitution

Solve the differential equation

\[ \frac{dy}{dx}=y^2-2xy+x^2+1. \]
Hint

The right side is \((y-x)^2+1\). Put \(u=y-x\); then \(u'=y'-1=u^2\), which is separable.

Answer
\[ y=x-\frac{1}{x+C}\quad(\text{and } y=x) \]

May 2017 Q7(b)Q7 total 10%older formatGuide · O2.5 Reduction of order

Observe that \(y_1=x\) is one solution to the differential equation

\[ x^2y''+x(x-2)y'-(x-2)y=0. \]

Find the general solution using the method of reduction of order. (Hint: you may consider to attempt \(y_2=c(x)y_1\) to get another independent solution.)

Hint

Put \(y=x\,c(x)\): \(y'=c+xc'\), \(y''=2c'+xc''\). The \(c\) terms cancel and you get \(x^3c''+x^3c'=0\), i.e. \(c''+c'=0\). Let \(w=c'\).

Answer
\[ y=c_1x+c_2xe^{-x} \]
