Extreme Values and the Mean Value Theorem

0Where this lecture sits

Lectures 5 to 7 computed derivatives; from here the course uses them. Picture a walk along a hilly trail between two fence posts: this lecture finds the highest and lowest spots of the walk, then, with a road trip, links the speed at one moment to the average speed over the whole trip. The Mean Value Theorem is the engine behind Lectures 9 to 12.

1Absolute (global) extrema ~4 min

What is this?

Picture a walk along a hilly trail from one fence post to another. The input is how far along the trail you are; the output is the height of the ground there. The absolute maximum is the highest spot you actually stand on during the whole walk, and the absolute minimum is the lowest. If the trail stops just short of the top, you never stand on it, and the walk has no highest spot at all.

Definition

The maximum and minimum values of a function \(f\) are called its extreme values (or extrema; one of them is an extremum). Extrema are classified as absolute or local.

Definition

Let \(c\) be a number in the domain \(D\) of a function \(f\). Then \(f(c)\) is

  • the absolute maximum value of \(f\) at \(c\) if \(\;f(c)\ge f(x)\;\) for all \(x\in D\);
  • the absolute minimum value of \(f\) at \(c\) if \(\;f(c)\le f(x)\;\) for all \(x\in D\).

These are also called the global maximum and minimum values.

Reading the formula
\(f(c)\)
the height at one particular spot \(c\) on the trail.
\(\ge f(x)\)
at least as high as the height at spot \(x\) (ties are allowed, so a flat top still counts).
for all \(x\in D\)
compared with every spot of the walk, not just the nearby ones. That is what makes it absolute.
\(c\) in \(D\)
the spot must be on the walk itself: a height you only get close to does not count.

In words: the absolute maximum is the single highest output the function ever actually reaches on its whole domain; the absolute minimum is the single lowest output it ever actually reaches. (\(x\in D\) means "\(x\) is in the domain \(D\)".)

Two details matter in exams:

  • Value versus location. The maximum value is the height \(f(c)\); the place where it happens is \(x=c\). A question saying "find the absolute maximum and where it occurs" wants both.
  • It must be reached. If the outputs creep closer and closer to 4 but never equal 4, then 4 is not a maximum. The domain matters, including whether each endpoint is included.

Take the same formula \(f(x)=x^2\) on four different domains. Try them below. A filled dot means the endpoint is included; an open circle means it is not.

Same formula, different domains

Analogy. The absolute maximum is the summit of a hiking trail. If the trail stops just short of the summit (an open endpoint), you never stand on it, so the trail has no highest point, only points that get closer and closer.

You can now: say what the absolute maximum and minimum values of a function are; give both the value and where it occurs; and explain, using the domain and its endpoints, why a function such as \(x^2\) on \(0\lt x\le2\) has no minimum.

2Local (relative) extrema ~3 min

What is this?

Back on the trail. A local maximum is a hilltop: stand there and every spot within a few steps is lower or level, even if a bigger mountain rises further along. A local minimum is a valley floor. The walk can have many hilltops; the absolute maximum is simply the highest spot of all.

Definition

Let \(D\) be the domain of \(f\). Then \(f\) has

  • a local maximum value at a number \(c\) in \(D\) if \(\;f(c)\ge f(x)\;\) for all \(x\in D\) near \(c\);
  • a local minimum value at \(c\) if \(\;f(c)\le f(x)\;\) for all \(x\in D\) near \(c\).

These are also called relative maximum and minimum values.

In words: a local maximum is a hilltop: higher than everything in a small neighbourhood around it, though there may be taller hills further away. A local minimum is the bottom of a valley.

"Near \(c\)" means: there is some small interval around \(c\), however small, on which the inequality holds. Picture a downward parabola (its top is a local max) and an upward parabola (its bottom is a local min).

Every absolute extremum is the best over the whole domain, so in particular it is the best nearby: an absolute maximum is also a local maximum. The reverse is false: a hilltop need not be the highest point overall.

Example 1

Determine the extreme values of \(y=\sin x\).

Solution. The outputs of \(\sin x\) always satisfy \(-1\le\sin x\le1\), and both bounds are actually reached.

  • Absolute maximum value \(1\), reached at \(x=\frac\pi2+2k\pi\) for every integer \(k\) (…, \(-\frac{3\pi}2\), \(\frac\pi2\), \(\frac{5\pi}2\), …).
  • Absolute minimum value \(-1\), reached at \(x=-\frac\pi2+2k\pi\) (…, \(-\frac\pi2\), \(\frac{3\pi}2\), …).
  • Each of these points is also a local maximum or local minimum, and there are no other local extrema: between a peak and the next trough, \(\sin x\) only goes one way.From a peak, \(\sin x\) falls all the way to the next trough without turning, so there is no hilltop or valley floor in between.

Lesson: an absolute maximum value is unique (there is only one highest height) but it can be reached at many places.

You can now: tell a local extremum from an absolute one; explain why an absolute maximum is also a local one but not the other way round; and list the extreme values of \(\sin x\) and where they occur.

3When do extreme values exist? The Extreme Value Theorem ~4 min

What is this?

The EVT is a guarantee for the walk. If the ground has no gaps or sudden jumps (continuous) and both fence posts are part of the walk (closed interval), you must pass a highest spot and a lowest spot. Take away either condition and the guarantee is gone: the trail could climb towards a fence post you may not stand on, or jump up at a gap to a height it never quite reaches.

Theorem: Extreme Value Theorem, EVT

If \(f\) is continuous on a closed interval \([a,b]\), then \(f\) attains both an absolute maximum value \(M\) and an absolute minimum value \(m\) in \([a,b]\). That is, there are numbers \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) in \([a,b]\) with

\[ \begin{gathered} f(x_1)=m,\qquad f(x_2)=M,\\ m\le f(x)\le M \ \text{ for every other } x \text{ in } [a,b]. \end{gathered} \]
Reading the formula
continuous on \([a,b]\)
the trail has no gaps or jumps anywhere, including right at the two fence posts, and both posts are part of the walk.
\(f(x_1)=m\)
the lowest height \(m\) is actually stood on, at the spot \(x_1\).
\(f(x_2)=M\)
the highest height \(M\) is actually stood on, at the spot \(x_2\).
\(m\le f(x)\le M\)
every other spot of the walk lies between these two heights.

In words: if the graph has no breaks (continuous) and you include both ends (closed interval), then there is a lowest point and a highest point, and the function actually reaches them.

Terms: continuous (Lecture 4) means no jumps, holes or blow-ups: \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=f(c)\) at every point. A closed interval \([a,b]\) includes both endpoints; an open interval \((a,b)\) excludes both. The proof is omitted as technically difficult (it needs properties of the real numbers beyond this course).

EVT is an existence theorem

The EVT tells you that the extrema exist. It does not tell you where they are (it does not give the values \(x_1\), \(x_2\)). That job belongs to the Closed Interval Method in section 5. A continuous function on \([a,b]\) can reach its extrema in the interior, at the endpoints, or at several places at once. The conditions are sufficient but not necessary:

  • Sufficient: if the conditions hold, extrema are guaranteed.
  • Not necessary: if a condition fails (a discontinuity, or an open interval), extrema may still exist, or may not. The theorem simply says nothing.

EVT gallery

Analogy. A continuous function on a closed interval is like a walk along a mountain ridge from one marked post to another without ever leaving the ground. You must pass a highest and a lowest spot. Remove an end post (open interval) and the walk could climb forever towards a height it never reaches; allow teleporting (a jump) and the same can happen in the middle.

You can now: state the EVT with both conditions (continuous, closed interval); say that it guarantees existence but not location; and use the gallery cases to show why each condition matters, while remembering that extrema may still exist when a condition fails.

4Where to look: critical points ~3 min

What is this?

Where on the trail can a hilltop be? Inside the fence, a smooth hilltop is flat at the very top: you have stopped climbing and not yet started down. The only other way is a sharp peak, like a roof ridge, where there is no single slope. The fence posts are different: the walk just stops there, so the ground can still be sloping at your highest spot.

Theorem: first derivative theorem for local extrema

If \(f\) has a local maximum or minimum at an interior point \(c\) of its domain, and if \(f'(c)\) exists, then

\[ f'(c)=0. \]

In words: at the top of a smooth hill or the bottom of a smooth valley, the tangent line is horizontal (slope zero).

An interior point is a point that is not an endpoint: there is room on both sides of it inside the domain.

Going further: why it is true

Suppose \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\). For small \(h\gt0\), \(f(c+h)\le f(c)\), so the difference quotient \(\dfrac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}\le0\) (a number at most zero divided by a positive number). For small \(h\lt0\), the top is still at most zero but now we divide by a negative number, so the quotient is \(\ge0\). As \(h\to0\) both one-sided limits equal \(f'(c)\), so \(f'(c)\le0\) and \(f'(c)\ge0\), which forces \(f'(c)=0\). A local minimum works the same way with the inequalities reversed.

Where extrema may occur

Turning the theorem around: an extremum can only happen at

  1. a point where \(f'(c)=0\);
  2. a point where \(f'(c)\) does not exist (DNE), such as a sharp corner or a vertical tangent;
  3. an endpoint of the domain.

These are candidates, not guarantees. The gallery below shows four cases: in each row, one function has an extremum at \(x=0\) and the other does not.

Candidates that work, and candidates that don't

What is this? (critical point)

A critical point is a spot inside the fence that is either flat or sharp. It is the shortlist of places where a hilltop or a valley floor could be. Being on the shortlist does not make it one: a flat ledge on a steady climb is flat but is neither a top nor a bottom.

Definition: critical point

An interior point \(c\) in the domain of \(f\) where

\[ f'(c)=0 \quad\text{or}\quad f'(c)\ \text{does not exist} \]

is a critical point of \(f\).

In words: a critical point is an inside point where the graph is either flat or has no well-defined slope. Endpoints are never called critical points; they are checked separately.

You can now: explain why an interior extremum with a derivative must have \(f'(c)=0\); list the three kinds of candidate points; find the critical points of a function, including where \(f'\) does not exist; and give \(x^3\) and \(|x|\) as warnings.

5The Closed Interval Method ~5 min

What is this?

The method is a search for the highest and lowest spots of the fenced walk. The EVT says they exist, as long as the trail is unbroken and both fence posts are included. Section 4 says each one is at a flat spot, a sharp point or a fence post. So visit every one of those, measure the height, and pick the biggest and the smallest. You never need to work out the shape at each spot: the heights decide.

Theorem: Closed Interval Method

To find the absolute maximum and minimum values of a continuous function \(f\) on a closed interval \([a,b]\):

  1. Do: Evaluate \(f\) at all critical points and at the endpoints \(a\) and \(b\). The highest and lowest spots exist (EVT) and can only be at flat spots, sharp points or fence posts, so this list cannot miss them.
  2. Do: The largest of these values is the absolute maximum; the smallest is the absolute minimum. Comparing heights picks the winners directly, so no test of the shape at each spot is needed.

In words: the EVT says the highest and lowest points exist; the first derivative theorem says they can only be at critical points or endpoints. So list every candidate, work out its height, and pick the biggest and smallest.

Why this works: by the EVT the absolute max is reached somewhere in \([a,b]\). If it is reached at an interior point, that point is also a local maximum, so by section 4 it is a critical point. Otherwise it is at an endpoint. Either way it is on your list. You do not need to decide whether each critical point is a max or a min; comparing values does that for you.

Exam layout that earns full marks:

  1. Do: (1) say \(f\) is continuous on the closed interval; this is the EVT's condition, and it is what guarantees there is a highest and a lowest spot to find.
  2. Do: (2) compute \(f'\); the slope is what shows where the trail is flat or sharp.
  3. Do: (3) solve \(f'(x)=0\) and note where \(f'\) does not exist, keeping only points inside \((a,b)\); these are the flat spots and sharp points inside the fence; a point outside \([a,b]\) is not on the walk at all.
  4. Do: (4) table of candidates and values; the heights, not the shapes, decide which spot is the top and which the bottom, and the table shows the marker every candidate was checked.
  5. Do: (5) state the absolute max and min and where they occur. The answer has two parts, the height and the place (section 1), and each earns marks.
Example 2

Find the absolute extrema of \(f(x)=x^3+x^2-x\) on \([-1,2]\).

Solution. \(f\) is a polynomial, so it is continuous on \([-1,2]\) and differentiable everywhere.

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x)&=3x^2+2x-1\\ &=(3x-1)(x+1)=0\\ &\iff x=\tfrac13 \ \text{ or } \ x=-1. \end{aligned} \]

\(x=-1\) is an endpoint, so the only critical point inside \((-1,2)\) is \(x=\frac13\). There are no points where \(f'\) fails to exist.\(x=-1\) is still checked, but as a fence post in the table, not as a critical point.

Candidate \(x\)Type\(f(x)\)
\(-1\)endpoint\(-1+1+1=1\)
\(\frac13\)critical point\(\frac1{27}+\frac19-\frac13=-\frac5{27}\)
\(2\)endpoint\(8+4-2=10\)

Answer: absolute maximum \(10\) at \(x=2\); absolute minimum \(-\frac5{27}\) at \(x=\frac13\).

Example 3

Find the absolute extrema of \(g(t)=|3t-4|\) on \([0,3]\).

Solution. \(g\) is continuous (absolute value of a polynomial). Write it without the absolute value:

\[ \begin{gathered} g(t)=\begin{cases}4-3t, & t\lt\frac43\\[2pt] 3t-4, & t\ge\frac43\end{cases}\\[8pt] g'(t)=\begin{cases}-3, & t\lt\frac43\\[2pt] 3, & t\gt\frac43.\end{cases} \end{gathered} \]

\(g'\) is never 0. At \(t=\frac43\) the slope jumps from \(-3\) to \(3\) (a sharp corner), so \(g'(\frac43)\) does not exist. The only critical point is \(t=\frac43\).The bottom of this V-shaped valley is a sharp point, not a flat spot, so solving \(g'=0\) alone cannot find it.

Candidate \(t\)Type\(g(t)\)
\(0\)endpoint\(4\)
\(\frac43\)critical point (\(g'\) DNE)\(0\)
\(3\)endpoint\(5\)

Answer: absolute maximum \(5\) at \(t=3\); absolute minimum \(0\) at \(t=\frac43\). Had you only solved \(g'(t)=0\), you would have missed the minimum entirely.

6Rolle's Theorem ~4 min

What is this?

Throw a ball straight up and catch it at the same height you let it go. While it rises its vertical velocity is positive; while it falls, negative. So at some moment in between, the top of the throw, the velocity is exactly zero. Each condition is part of the picture: the ball's height changes without jumps (continuous), it has a definite velocity at every moment in flight (differentiable), and it ends at the height it started (equal end values). The theorem promises only that such a moment exists, not when it happens.

Theorem: Rolle's Theorem

Suppose \(f\) satisfies:

  1. \(f\) is continuous on the closed interval \([a,b]\),
  2. \(f\) is differentiable on the open interval \((a,b)\), and
  3. \(f(a)=f(b)\).

Then there is at least one number \(c\) in \((a,b)\) at which \(f'(c)=0\).

In words: if a smooth, unbroken curve starts and ends at the same height, then somewhere strictly in between its tangent is horizontal.

Differentiable on \((a,b)\) means \(f'(x)\) exists at every point strictly between \(a\) and \(b\): no corners, cusps or vertical tangents inside. Notice the theorem asks for continuity on the closed interval but differentiability only on the open one; the ends are allowed to be steep or pointed.

Like the EVT, Rolle's Theorem is an existence theorem: it says some \(c\) exists but does not say where. It is the special case of the Mean Value Theorem (section 7) where the average slope \(\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\) is \(0\).

Analogy. You throw a ball and catch it at the same height you released it. At some moment in between, the ball was neither rising nor falling: its vertical velocity was zero (the top of the throw).

Proof of Rolle's Theorem

Here is the standard proof. It uses only the EVT and the first derivative theorem.

  1. Case 1: \(f\) is constant on \([a,b]\). Then \(f'(x)=0\) for every \(x\) in \((a,b)\), so any \(c\) works.
  2. Case 2: \(f(x)\gt f(a)\) for some \(x\) in \((a,b)\). By condition (i) and the EVT, \(f\) has an absolute maximum on \([a,b]\). That maximum is bigger than \(f(a)=f(b)\), so it is not at an endpoint; it is at some interior \(c\). An absolute max at an interior point is a local max, and \(f'(c)\) exists by condition (ii), so \(f'(c)=0\) by the first derivative theorem. The ball went above the hands, so the top of the throw happens in the air, strictly between \(a\) and \(b\), where the curve is smooth and must be flat at its top.
  3. Case 3: \(f(x)\lt f(a)\) for some \(x\) in \((a,b)\). Same argument with the absolute minimum.

One of the three cases always happens, so the theorem is proved. \(\blacksquare\)

The conditions are sufficient but not necessary

If any of the three conditions fails, a horizontal tangent may still exist, or may not. The explorer in section 7 lets you test examples of each (choose \(|x|\) on \([-1,1]\), for example).

Discontinuous at \(x=0\)A curve with a jump at 0 can still have flat tops on each side, or none at all.
Not differentiable at \(x=0\)\(f(x)=1-(|x|-1)^2\) on \([-2,2]\) has a corner at 0 but flat tops at \(x=\pm1\); but \(f(x)=|x|\) on \([-1,1]\) has \(f(-1)=f(1)\) and its slope is only ever \(\pm1\), never 0.
\(f(-1)\ne f(1)\)\(f(x)=x^3\) still has \(f'(0)=0\); \(f(x)=x\) has slope 1 everywhere.

You can now: state Rolle's Theorem with all three conditions; prove it from the EVT and the first derivative theorem; and give an example where a condition fails and no horizontal tangent exists, such as \(|x|\) on \([-1,1]\).

7The Mean Value Theorem ~7 min

What is this?

A road trip. Your position along the road is a function of time, and the speedometer shows its derivative. If you cover 120 km in 1.5 hours, your average speed is 80 km/h, so at some moment strictly during the trip the speedometer read exactly 80 km/h. The conditions are part of the picture: the car moves without teleporting, from departure to arrival included (continuous on the closed interval), and it has a definite speed at every moment in between (differentiable on the open interval).

Theorem: Mean Value Theorem, MVT

Suppose \(f\) satisfies:

  1. \(f\) is continuous on the closed interval \([a,b]\), and
  2. \(f\) is differentiable on the open interval \((a,b)\).

Then there is at least one number \(c\) in \((a,b)\) at which

\[ f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. \]
Reading the formula
\(f(b)-f(a)\)
distance covered between departure time \(a\) and arrival time \(b\).
\(b-a\)
how long the trip took.
\(\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\)
your average speed: the slope of the straight chord from start to finish.
\(f'(c)\)
the speedometer reading at the moment \(c\).
\(c\) in \((a,b)\)
the moment is strictly during the trip, not at departure or arrival; you are not told when.
continuous on \([a,b]\)
no teleporting, including at departure and arrival.
differentiable on \((a,b)\)
a definite speed at every moment in between. A sudden corner in the position graph, such as the bounce in \(|x|\), has no single speed.

In words: slope of the tangent at \(c\) = slope of the straight line joining the two ends, i.e. the instantaneous rate of change at \(c\) = the average rate of change over \([a,b]\).

Rolle's Theorem is the "level" version (\(f(a)=f(b)\), so the average slope is 0). The MVT is the "tilted" version: tilt the picture so the two ends are at different heights, and the horizontal tangent becomes a tangent parallel to the chord.

Geometry. Join \((a,f(a))\) to \((b,f(b))\) with a straight line (a secant, or chord). Its slope is \(m=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\). The MVT says that somewhere between \(a\) and \(b\) the curve has a tangent line with exactly this slope, i.e. parallel to the secant.

Analogy. You drive 120 km in 1.5 hours, an average of 80 km/h. However you sped up and slowed down, at some instant your speedometer read exactly 80 km/h. (Your position is a smooth function of time, and speed is its derivative.) This is how point-to-point speed cameras can prove you were speeding without catching you at the moment you did it.

Try: sin x with a = 0, b = π (Rolle's Theorem: the secant is flat). √x with a = 0, b = 4 (Group work 2). |x| with a = −1, b = 1 (no c exists: the conditions fail and so does the conclusion). |x| with a = −1, b = 2 (a condition fails, yet no c exists here either; then try 1/x² on a = −1, b = 3: a condition fails, but c ≈ 2.08 exists anyway).

The MVT conditions are sufficient but not necessary

If \(f\) is discontinuous on \([a,b]\) or not differentiable somewhere in \((a,b)\), a point \(c\) with \(f'(c)=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\) may still exist, or may not. Two "may not" examples are a jump function with flat pieces (its slope is always 0, but the secant across the jump is not flat) and \(|x|\) on a symmetric interval (secant slope 0, but slope is only \(\pm1\)). Check both in the explorer.

Why the MVT matters

The MVT is used to build

  • curve sketching, the link between the sign of \(f'\) and whether \(f\) increases (Lecture 9);
  • L'Hôpital's Rule (Lecture 10);
  • antiderivatives: two functions with the same derivative differ by a constant (Lecture 11);
  • the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Lecture 12).
Going further (examined): proof of the MVT from Rolle's Theorem

May 2015 Q5(b) asked for exactly this proof, so learn it. The idea is to subtract the secant line so the two ends become level, then apply Rolle.

Let \(m=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\) and let \(L(x)=f(a)+m(x-a)\) be the secant line. Define

\[ \begin{aligned} h(x)&=f(x)-L(x)\\ &=f(x)-f(a)-m(x-a). \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(h(x)\) = height of the curve minus height of the secant at the same \(x\), i.e. the vertical gap between them.

  1. \(h\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\), because \(f\) is and \(L\) is a straight line.
  2. \(h(a)=f(a)-f(a)-0=0\), and Subtracting the secant levels the two ends, so the gap \(h\) is a ball caught at the height it was thrown from, and Rolle applies. \[ \begin{aligned} h(b)&=f(b)-f(a)-m(b-a)\\ &=f(b)-f(a)-\big(f(b)-f(a)\big)\\ &=0. \end{aligned} \] So \(h(a)=h(b)\).
  3. By Rolle's Theorem there is \(c\) in \((a,b)\) with \(h'(c)=0\). Since \(h'(x)=f'(x)-m\), this says \(f'(c)=m=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\). \(\blacksquare\)

You can now: state the MVT word for word with both conditions and \(c\) strictly between \(a\) and \(b\); explain it as a tangent parallel to the secant; find \(c\) for a given function; and prove the MVT from Rolle's Theorem.

8Using the MVT: bounds, growth and uniqueness ~4 min

What is this?

Back on the road trip. Usually you do not know the moment \(c\), but you may know that the speedometer never showed less than 2, or always showed less than 3. Then the average speed obeys the same limit, and distance equals average speed times time. A limit on the speed at every moment becomes a limit on the total distance.

Most exam uses of the MVT come from rearranging it as

\[ \begin{gathered} f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)\,(b-a)\\ \text{for some } c \text{ in } (a,b). \end{gathered} \]
Reading the formula
\(f(b)-f(a)\)
the total change: distance covered on the trip.
\(f'(c)\)
the speedometer at some unknown moment \(c\).
\((b-a)\)
the length of the trip, always positive when \(a\lt b\).
for some \(c\) in \((a,b)\)
you do not know when, so you use what you know about \(f'\) at every moment.

In words: total change in \(f\) = (slope somewhere in between) × (length of the interval).

You usually do not know \(c\), but you often know something about \(f'\) everywhere (for example "\(f'(x)\ge2\)" or "\(f'(x)\lt3\)"). Then you know it at \(c\) too, and the equation turns into an inequality. This is how the group work questions 4 and 5 and several past paper questions work.

How to bound with the MVT:

  1. Do: Check \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\) (differentiable on the closed interval covers both). Without these the MVT promises nothing, and markers look for them.
  2. Do: Write \(f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)\,(b-a)\) for some \(c\) in \((a,b)\). This turns the unknown total change into a speed times a time.
  3. Do: Replace \(f'(c)\) by the bound you know for every \(x\), and rearrange for the value you want. The bound holds at every moment, so it holds at the unknown \(c\) too; multiplying by \(b-a\gt0\) keeps the inequality the same way round.
Idea, using Group work 4

If \(f(1)=10\) and \(f'(x)\ge2\) on \([1,4]\), then \(f(4)-f(1)=f'(c)\cdot3\ge2\cdot3=6\), so \(f(4)\ge16\). The graph of \(f\) can never fall below the line through \((1,10)\) with slope 2.

Beyond Lecture 8: three consequences used constantly later

Each follows from the MVT in one line (apply it to any two points \(x_1\lt x_2\) in the interval):

  • If \(f'(x)=0\) for all \(x\) in an interval, then \(f\) is constant there. (\(f(x_2)-f(x_1)=0\cdot(x_2-x_1)=0\).)
  • If \(f'(x)\gt0\) on an interval, then \(f\) is increasing there: \(x_1\lt x_2\) gives \(f(x_2)\gt f(x_1)\). If \(f'(x)\lt0\), \(f\) is decreasing. (Lecture 9 uses this for curve sketching.)
  • If \(f'(x)\ne0\) everywhere on an interval, then \(f\) has at most one root there. If it had two roots \(r_1\lt r_2\), Rolle's Theorem on \([r_1,r_2]\) would give \(f'(c)=0\). (Example 5 below.)

You can now: rewrite the MVT as total change = slope × length; turn a bound on \(f'\) into a bound on \(f(b)\); and explain why \(f'=0\) gives a constant, \(f'\gt0\) gives increasing, and \(f'\ne0\) gives at most one root.

9IVT vs MVT, and "exactly one root" ~5 min

What is this?

Two different guarantees on the road trip. The IVT is about positions: if you were at kilometre 10 at one time and at kilometre 50 later, then at some moment in between you passed kilometre 30, because the car cannot teleport. The MVT and Rolle are about speeds: at some moment the speedometer showed your average speed. "Exactly one root" uses both: the IVT finds a crossing of the axis, and Rolle rules out a second one.

Recall the Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT, Lecture 4): if \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(N\) is any number between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\), then \(f(c)=N\) for some \(c\) in \((a,b)\). Compare:

TheoremNeedsGuarantees a value of
IVT\(f\) continuous on \([a,b]\)\(f\) itself: every height between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\) is hit
MVT / Rollecontinuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\)the derivative \(f'\): the average slope is hit
Example 4

A car travels on a straight track. During \(0\le t\le60\) seconds, its velocity \(v(t)\) (m/s) and acceleration \(a(t)\) (m/s²) are continuous functions. Selected values:

\(t\)0152530355060
\(v(t)\)−20−30−20−14−10010
\(a(t)\)1521242

(a) For \(0\lt t\lt60\), must there be a time \(t\) when \(v(t)=-5\)?
(b) For \(0\lt t\lt60\), must there be a time \(t\) when \(a(t)=0\)?

(a) Yes, by the IVT. \(v\) is continuous on \([35,50]\), and \(-5\) lies between \(v(35)=-10\) and \(v(50)=0\). So \(v(t)=-5\) for some \(t\) in \((35,50)\subset(0,60)\).

(b) Yes, by Rolle's Theorem (the MVT). Acceleration is the derivative of velocity, \(a(t)=v'(t)\), so \(v\) is differentiable, and continuous. Since \(v(0)=v(25)=-20\), Rolle's Theorem on \([0,25]\) gives some \(t\) in \((0,25)\) with \(v'(t)=a(t)=0\).The velocity came back to its starting value, like the ball back at the hands, so its rate of change, the acceleration, was zero at some moment.

Lesson: the table's \(a\) values are all positive, but that only tells you about 7 moments. The theorems tell you about the moments in between. The IVT is about the function's values; the MVT is about its slopes.

Example 5

Show that \(x^3+2x-5=0\) has exactly one real root.

The same argument works for \(x^3+2x+5=0\), using \(f(-2)=-7\lt0\lt f(-1)=2\).

"Exactly one" is two claims, so prove two things.

At least one root (IVT). Let \(f(x)=x^3+2x-5\), a polynomial, so continuous on \([1,2]\). \(f(1)=-2\lt0\) and \(f(2)=7\gt0\), so by the IVT there is \(r\) in \((1,2)\) with \(f(r)=0\).

At most one root (Rolle). Suppose there were two roots \(r_1\lt r_2\). \(f\) is continuous and differentiable everywhere and \(f(r_1)=f(r_2)=0\), so Rolle's Theorem gives \(c\) in \((r_1,r_2)\) with \(f'(c)=0\). But \(f'(x)=3x^2+2\ge2\gt0\) for all \(x\). Contradiction. So there cannot be two roots.Two roots are two equal heights (both 0), which would force a flat moment in between; but \(f\) is always climbing, so it can never be flat.

Conclusion: exactly one real root (it is about \(1.328\)).

Template: exists (IVT: sign change) + unique (Rolle: two roots would force \(f'(c)=0\), but \(f'\) is never 0). Name both theorems; markers look for them.

Method for "exactly one root":

  1. Do: Move everything to one side, call it \(f(x)\), and say why \(f\) is continuous and differentiable. Both theorems need these conditions, so state them first.
  2. Do: Find two points where \(f\) has opposite signs and quote the IVT. An unbroken graph that starts below the axis and ends above it must cross: at least one root.
  3. Do: Suppose two roots \(r_1\lt r_2\), apply Rolle's Theorem on \([r_1,r_2]\) to get \(f'(c)=0\), and show \(f'\) is never 0. Two roots would force a flat moment in between, which a function whose slope is never 0 does not have: at most one root.
  4. Do: Conclude "exactly one real root", naming both theorems. "At least one" and "at most one" together make "exactly one".

You can now: say which theorem gives a value of \(f\) (IVT) and which gives a value of \(f'\) (MVT, Rolle); answer table questions like Example 4; and prove an equation has exactly one real root with the IVT plus Rolle.

10Summary

  • EVT: a continuous function on a closed interval \([a,b]\) has an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum.
  • Critical point: an interior number \(c\) with \(f'(c)=0\) or \(f'(c)\) undefined.
  • Absolute maximum and minimum values occur at critical points or endpoints (Closed Interval Method).
  • MVT: for \(f\) continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\), the instantaneous rate of change at some \(c\) equals the average rate of change: \(f'(c)=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\). Rolle is the case \(f(a)=f(b)\).
  • Consequences of the MVT include curve sketching, L'Hôpital's Rule, antiderivatives and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
  • The conditions of these value theorems are sufficient, but not necessary.

11Common mistakes

✗"\(f(x)=|x|\) does not have a minimum value." Why wrong: it ignores critical points where \(f'\) is undefined. \(f'(0)\) does not exist, and \(f(0)=0\) is the absolute minimum. ✓ Always list points where \(f'\) DNE. ✗"\(f(x)=x^2\) on \([-1,2]\) has no absolute maximum" (because \(f'(x)=2x=0\) only gives the minimum at 0). Why wrong: endpoints were not checked. ✓ \(f(-1)=1\), \(f(2)=4\): absolute maximum 4 at \(x=2\). ✗"The absolute maximum value of \(f(x)=x^2\) is \(\infty\)." Why wrong: \(\infty\) is not a value the function takes. On all real numbers \(x^2\) is unbounded, so it has no absolute maximum. The EVT does not apply because the domain is not a closed interval. ✓ Say "no absolute maximum". ✗"The absolute minimum value of \(f(x)=1/x\) is \(-\infty\)." Why wrong: same error; \(1/x\) is unbounded below near \(0^-\). ✓ No absolute minimum (and no absolute maximum either). ✗"For \(f(x)=1/x^2\) there is \(c\in(-1,1)\) with \(f'(c)=0\)" (by Rolle, since \(f(-1)=f(1)\)). Why wrong: \(1/x^2\) is not continuous on \([-1,1]\) (undefined at 0), so Rolle's conditions fail. In fact \(f'(x)=-2/x^3\) is never 0. ✓ Check every condition before quoting a theorem. ✗ Keeping a critical point that lies outside the interval. 12Find the mistake

5. Let \(f(x)=px^2+qx+r\). Apply the Mean Value Theorem for the interval \([a,b]\). What is the value of \(c\) guaranteed by the Theorem?

A student's work:

\[ \begin{gathered} f(a)=pa^2+qa+r,\\ f(b)=pb^2+qb+r \end{gathered} \]

According to the Mean Value Theorem: \(\because f(x)\) is continuous, \(\therefore\) there exists \(c\) with

\[ f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}=p(b+a)+q. \] \[ \begin{gathered} \because f'(x)=2px+q,\\ \therefore 2pc+q=p(b+a)+q,\\ \therefore c=\frac{b+a}{2}. \end{gathered} \]
Hint 1

The MVT has two conditions. How many did the student check?

Hint 2

Look at the last step: the student divided both sides by something. Could it be zero?

Answer

Mistake 1: only continuity is mentioned. The MVT also needs \(f\) differentiable on \((a,b)\). (It is, being a polynomial, but it must be stated.)

Mistake 2: going from \(2pc=p(b+a)\) to \(c=\frac{a+b}2\) divides by \(p\), which needs \(p\ne0\). If \(p=0\), \(f(x)=qx+r\) is a straight line, \(f'(x)=q\) equals the average slope everywhere, and every \(c\) in \((a,b)\) works, so "the value of \(c\)" is not unique.

Correct conclusion: if \(p\ne0\), \(c=\frac{a+b}2\), the midpoint (for every parabola!). If \(p=0\), any \(c\in(a,b)\). (A minor point: the derivation \(\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}=p(b+a)+q\) uses \(b^2-a^2=(b-a)(b+a)\); it is correct.)

13Group work

1. Find the absolute extrema of

(a) \(f(x)=x^3+x^2+x\)   (b) \(f(t)=t^{3/4}-2t^{1/4}\) on \([0,1]\)   (c) \(g(x)=x\ln x\) on \([\frac12,2]\)   (d) \(f(\theta)=\cos\theta+\sin\theta\) on \([0,\pi]\)

Hints

(a) No interval is given, so the domain is all real numbers and the EVT does not apply. What is the sign of \(f'(x)=3x^2+2x+1\)? (Look at the discriminant.) What happens as \(x\to\pm\infty\)?

(b) Factor \(f'(t)\) as \(t^{-3/4}\times(\ldots)\). Where is it 0, and where does it not exist? (Is that point interior?)

(c) Product rule. Is the solution of \(g'(x)=0\) inside the interval?

(d) \(f'(\theta)=0\) means \(\tan\theta=1\). Which solution lies in \([0,\pi]\)?

Answers

(a) \(f'(x)=3x^2+2x+1\) has discriminant \(4-12=-8\lt0\) and positive leading coefficient, so \(f'(x)\gt0\) always: \(f\) is increasing, with \(f\to\pm\infty\) as \(x\to\pm\infty\). No absolute maximum or minimum.

(b) \(f'(t)=\frac34t^{-1/4}-\frac12t^{-3/4}\) \(=\frac14t^{-3/4}\big(3t^{1/2}-2\big)\). Zero at \(\sqrt t=\frac23\), i.e. \(t=\frac49\); \(f'(0)\) does not exist but \(0\) is an endpoint. Values: \(f(0)=0\), \(f(\frac49)=\big(\tfrac23\big)^{3/2}-2\big(\tfrac23\big)^{1/2}\) \(=-\frac{4\sqrt6}9\approx-1.089\), \(f(1)=-1\). Max \(0\) at \(t=0\); min \(-\frac{4\sqrt6}{9}\) at \(t=\frac49\).

(c) \(g'(x)=\ln x+1=0\) \(\iff x=e^{-1}\approx0.368\), not in \([\frac12,2]\); no critical points. \(g(\frac12)=-\frac12\ln2\approx-0.347\), \(g(2)=2\ln2\approx1.386\). Max \(2\ln2\) at \(x=2\); min \(-\frac12\ln2\) at \(x=\frac12\).

(d) \(f'(\theta)=\cos\theta-\sin\theta=0\iff\theta=\frac\pi4\) in \([0,\pi]\). \(f(0)=1\), \(f(\frac\pi4)=\sqrt2\), \(f(\pi)=-1\). Max \(\sqrt2\) at \(\theta=\frac\pi4\); min \(-1\) at \(\theta=\pi\).

2. Verify the conditions of the MVT for \(f(x)=\sqrt x\) on \([0,4]\) and find all \(c\) in \([0,4]\) guaranteed by the MVT.

Hint

\(\sqrt x\) is not differentiable at \(0\). Does the MVT require differentiability there? Then solve \(\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt c}=\dfrac{f(4)-f(0)}{4-0}\).

Answer

\(\sqrt x\) is continuous on \([0,4]\) and differentiable on \((0,4)\) with \(f'(x)=\frac1{2\sqrt x}\) (only \(x=0\) is a problem, and it is an endpoint, which is allowed). Average slope \(\frac{2-0}{4}=\frac12\). \(\frac1{2\sqrt c}=\frac12\iff c=1\). \(c=1\) is the only value.

3. For \(k\gt0\), show that if \(f(x)=e^x-kx\) has exactly two roots, then \(k\gt e\).

Hint

Rolle between the two roots tells you where \(f'=0\). Then use that \(f\) is decreasing before that point and increasing after it, so the value there is below 0.

Answer

Let the roots be \(r_1\lt r_2\). By Rolle there is \(c\in(r_1,r_2)\) with \(f'(c)=e^c-k=0\), so \(c=\ln k\). Since \(f'(x)=e^x-k\) is negative for \(x\lt\ln k\) and positive for \(x\gt\ln k\), \(f\) is strictly decreasing on \([r_1,\ln k]\), so \(f(\ln k)\lt f(r_1)=0\). But \(f(\ln k)=k-k\ln k=k(1-\ln k)\). With \(k\gt0\), \(1-\ln k\lt0\), so \(\ln k\gt1\), i.e. \(k\gt e\).

4. If \(f(1)=10\) and \(f'(x)\ge2\) for \(1\le x\le4\), how small can \(f(4)\) possibly be?

Hint

See section 8. Is the bound actually reached by some function?

Answer

\(f\) is differentiable on \([1,4]\), hence continuous there. MVT: \(f(4)-f(1)=3f'(c)\ge6\), so \(f(4)\ge16\). The line \(f(x)=2x+8\) satisfies everything with \(f(4)=16\). Smallest possible value: 16.

5. For \(\frac13\le y\lt x\le3\), show that \(\ln x-\ln y\lt3(x-y)\).

Hint

Apply the MVT to \(\ln t\) on \([y,x]\). Where does \(c\) lie, and how big can \(\frac1c\) be?

Answer

\(\ln t\) is continuous on \([y,x]\) and differentiable on \((y,x)\) (all positive numbers). MVT: \(\ln x-\ln y=\frac1c(x-y)\) for some \(c\in(y,x)\). Then \(c\gt y\ge\frac13\), so \(\frac1c\lt3\); and \(x-y\gt0\). Hence \(\ln x-\ln y\lt3(x-y)\).

14Concept checks

Short questions to test understanding, not computation.

C1. If \(f'(c)=0\), must \(f\) have a local extremum at \(c\)? If \(f\) has a local extremum at an interior \(c\), must \(f'(c)=0\)?

Answer

No to both. \(x^3\) has \(f'(0)=0\) but no extremum. \(|x|\) has a minimum at 0 but \(f'(0)\) does not exist. The correct statement: an interior extremum forces \(f'(c)=0\) or \(f'(c)\) DNE, i.e. \(c\) is a critical point.

C2. A function is continuous on the open interval \((0,1)\). Must it have an absolute maximum? Give an example.

Answer

No. \(f(x)=x\) on \((0,1)\) gets as close to 1 as you like but never reaches it. The EVT needs a closed interval. (But it might have one anyway: \(-(x-\frac12)^2\) on \((0,1)\) has a max at \(\frac12\).)

C3. Why are endpoints not counted as critical points, yet still checked in the Closed Interval Method?

Answer

At an endpoint there is only one side, so the derivative argument (slope 0 at a hilltop) does not apply: \(x^2\) on \([0,2]\) has its max at \(x=2\) where the slope is 4. The theorem only constrains interior points, so endpoints must be checked by hand.

C4. Which theorem would you use to show (a) \(f(c)=0\) for some \(c\); (b) \(f'(c)=0\) for some \(c\); (c) \(f(b)\le f(a)+5(b-a)\) given \(f'\le5\)?

Answer

(a) IVT (sign change of \(f\)). (b) Rolle (two equal values of \(f\)). (c) MVT (total change = slope × length).

C5. \(f(x)=|x|\) on \([-1,1]\) satisfies \(f(-1)=f(1)\). Why doesn't Rolle's Theorem give a horizontal tangent?

Answer

Condition (ii) fails: \(|x|\) is not differentiable at \(0\in(-1,1)\). Without that condition the theorem says nothing, and indeed the slope is only ever \(\pm1\).

C6. If \(f'(x)=0\) for every \(x\) in \((a,b)\) and \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), what can you say about \(f\)? Why?

Answer

\(f\) is constant on \([a,b]\). For any \(x\) in \((a,b]\), the MVT on \([a,x]\) gives \(f(x)-f(a)=f'(c)(x-a)=0\).
