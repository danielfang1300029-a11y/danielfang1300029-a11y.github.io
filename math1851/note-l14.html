Techniques of Integration II

0Where this lecture sits

There is no single recipe for integration. Each technique of integration (a systematic way to find antiderivatives) rewrites an integrand (the function being integrated) that you cannot integrate into an equal one that you can. This lecture adds three: identities give the same curve a friendlier formula; a trigonometric substitution uses a right triangle so that a square root disappears; partial fractions un-add a fraction into simple pieces.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 Each basic trig integral is a derivative fact read backwards. \(\int\tan x\,dx\) needs a substitution; \(\int\sec x\,dx\) needs a trick.
  2. §2 An identity changes the formula, not the curve. Power reduction, perfect squares, product to sum.
  3. §3 Powers of sine and cosine: save one factor for \(du\), or halve the powers if both are even.
  4. §4 Trig substitution: make \(x\) a side of a right triangle, so the square root is just another side. \(a^2-x^2\) → sine, \(a^2+x^2\) → tangent, \(x^2-a^2\) → secant.
  5. §5 Partial fractions: un-add a fraction into simple pieces, each a log or an inverse tangent. Divide first if it is top-heavy. Most common type
  6. §6 Choose the method from the shape of the integrand, and check by differentiating. Some integrals, such as \(\int e^{x^2}dx\), have no formula at all.

Recall (slide 2)

Theorem · Substitution Rule (Lecture 13)

If \(u=g(x)\) is a differentiable function whose range is an interval \(I\), and \(f\) is continuous on \(I\), then

\[\int f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx=\int f(u)\,du.\]

For definite integrals the limits change too:

\[\int_a^b f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx=\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\,du.\]

In words: if the integrand is (a function of an inside expression) × (derivative of that inside expression), rename the inside expression \(u\); the "\(g'(x)\,dx\)" becomes "\(du\)", and the \(x\)-limits become the matching \(u\)-limits.

Theorem · Integration by Parts (Lecture 13)

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be differentiable functions. Then

\[\int f(x)\,g'(x)\,dx=f(x)\,g(x)-\int f'(x)\,g(x)\,dx.\]

For definite integrals:

\[\begin{aligned}\int_a^b f(x)\,g'(x)\,dx&=\Big[f(x)\,g(x)\Big]_a^b\\&\quad-\int_a^b f'(x)\,g(x)\,dx.\end{aligned}\]

In words: integral of (first × derivative of second) = first × second − integral of (derivative of first × second).

1Basic trigonometric integrals (slide 7) ~3 min

What is this?

Knowing that 6 × 7 = 42 lets you answer 42 ÷ 7 at once: you read the times table backwards. Integration works the same way. Every derivative you know, such as "the derivative of tan x is sec² x", is an integral answered in advance. One entry on the slide, the integral of tan x, is not on any derivative list, so it needs a substitution instead.

HKDSE Core uses only \(\sin\), \(\cos\) and \(\tan\). University calculus also uses their three reciprocals:

Definition · reciprocal trig functions \[\sec x=\frac{1}{\cos x},\qquad \csc x=\frac{1}{\sin x},\] \[\cot x=\frac{1}{\tan x}=\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}.\]

In words: secant = 1 ÷ cosine; cosecant = 1 ÷ sine; cotangent = 1 ÷ tangent = cosine ÷ sine. Each is undefined wherever the function in its denominator is 0.

Their derivatives (Lecture 6) are \(\frac{d}{dx}\tan x=\sec^2x\), \(\frac{d}{dx}\cot x=-\csc^2x\), \(\frac{d}{dx}\sec x=\sec x\tan x\), \(\frac{d}{dx}\csc x=-\csc x\cot x\). Reading each derivative backwards gives an integral:

Theorem · trigonometric integration formulas (slide 7) \[\begin{aligned} &1.\ \int\sin x\,dx=-\cos x+C\\ &2.\ \int\cos x\,dx=\sin x+C\\ &3.\ \int\tan x\,dx=\ln|\sec x|+C\\ &4.\ \int\sec^2x\,dx=\tan x+C\\ &5.\ \int\csc^2x\,dx=-\cot x+C\\ &6.\ \int\sec x\tan x\,dx=\sec x+C\\ &7.\ \int\csc x\cot x\,dx=-\csc x+C \end{aligned}\]

In words: each formula says "the derivative of the right-hand side is the integrand". For example, formula 6: the derivative of secant is secant × tangent, so the integral of secant × tangent is secant (plus a constant).

Formulas 1, 2 and 4 to 7 follow from the FTC: they are derivative facts read in reverse. Formula 3 needs a technique. Write \(\tan x=\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\) and put \(u=\cos x\), so \(du=-\sin x\,dx\):

\[\begin{aligned}\int\tan x\,dx&=\int\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\,dx=-\int\frac{du}{u}\\&=-\ln|u|+C=-\ln|\cos x|+C\\&=\ln\left|\frac{1}{\cos x}\right|+C=\ln|\sec x|+C.\end{aligned}\]

In words: integral of tangent = natural log of |secant|, because minus log of |cosine| is the log of |1 ÷ cosine|.

Slide note: slide 7 labels formula 3 "by IBP" (integration by parts). The standard derivation, shown above, uses the Substitution Rule; that is the reading used in these notes.

Beyond the handouts · integrals of sec x and csc x

Slide 7 has no formula for \(\int\sec x\,dx\), but it is needed later in this note (the Find-the-mistake slide, \(\int\sqrt{x^2+1}\,dx\)) and it was asked directly in May 2026. The trick is to multiply top and bottom by \(\sec x+\tan x\):

\[\begin{aligned}\int\sec x\,dx&=\int\frac{\sec x(\sec x+\tan x)}{\sec x+\tan x}\,dx\\&=\int\frac{\sec^2x+\sec x\tan x}{\sec x+\tan x}\,dx.\end{aligned}\]

The top is exactly the derivative of the bottom, so with \(u=\sec x+\tan x\), \(du=(\sec x\tan x+\sec^2x)\,dx\):

\[\int\sec x\,dx=\int\frac{du}{u}=\ln|\sec x+\tan x|+C.\]

In words: integral of secant = log of |secant + tangent|.

The same trick with \(\csc x-\cot x\) gives

\[\int\csc x\,dx=\ln|\csc x-\cot x|+C.\]

In words: integral of cosecant = log of |cosecant − cotangent|.

You can always check such a formula by differentiating the answer (the Summary of this lecture recommends exactly this).

You can now: write down the seven slide-7 integrals by reading derivatives backwards; derive \(\int\tan x\,dx\) with \(u=\cos x\); derive \(\int\sec x\,dx\) and \(\int\csc x\,dx\) with the multiply-top-and-bottom trick; and check any such formula by differentiating.

2Trigonometric identities (slides 8 to 11) ~10 min

What is this?

An identity gives the same curve a different formula, like the same person in a different outfit. The graphs of cos² x and ½(1 + cos 2x) are exactly the same curve, so they have the same area underneath. Only the second formula is on the list of things we can integrate. Choosing an identity means choosing the outfit that the integration rules recognise.

An identity is an equation that is true for every value of the variable, such as \(\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1\). In integration, identities let you replace an integrand you cannot integrate with an equal one you can.

The pictures on slide 8

Slide 8 shows four pictures that prove trig identities by geometry, with no words. Here is what each shows.

  • Picture 1: all six trig functions on one unit circle. Take a point on a circle of radius 1 at angle \(\theta\). Its horizontal distance from the centre is \(\cos\theta\) and its height is \(\sin\theta\). Draw the tangent line to the circle at that point. Measured along this tangent line, the length to the horizontal axis is \(\tan\theta\) and the length to the vertical axis is \(\cot\theta\). The tangent line meets the horizontal axis at distance \(\sec\theta\) from the centre and the vertical axis at distance \(\csc\theta\). (This is where the names come from: a "secant" line cuts the circle, a "tangent" line touches it.) The right triangle with legs \(1\) (radius) and \(\tan\theta\) has hypotenuse \(\sec\theta\), which is Pythagoras' theorem in the form \(1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta\).
  • Picture 2: a rectangle proving the angle-difference formulas. A right triangle with hypotenuse 1 and angle \(\alpha-\beta\) has legs \(\cos(\alpha-\beta)\) and \(\sin(\alpha-\beta)\). It is fitted into a rectangle together with three more right triangles: one sharing the same hypotenuse 1, with angle \(\beta\) and legs \(\cos\beta\) and \(\sin\beta\), and two with these legs as hypotenuses and angle \(\alpha\). The top edge of the rectangle is made of pieces \(\cos\alpha\cos\beta\) and \(\sin\alpha\sin\beta\), and must equal the bottom edge; the left edge \(\sin\alpha\cos\beta\) must equal the right edge, which is made of \(\cos\alpha\sin\beta\) and \(\sin(\alpha-\beta)\). So \[\begin{aligned}\cos(\alpha-\beta)&=\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta,\\ \sin(\alpha-\beta)&=\sin\alpha\cos\beta-\cos\alpha\sin\beta.\end{aligned}\]
  • Pictures 3 and 4: two views of one triangle proving \(\sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta\). An isosceles triangle has two sides of length 1 and angle \(2\theta\) between them. Split down the middle (left picture), it is two right triangles with hypotenuse 1, so each has base \(\sin\theta\) and height \(\cos\theta\): total area \(2\times\frac12\sin\theta\cos\theta=\sin\theta\cos\theta\). Lay the same triangle on one of its sides of length 1 (right picture): its height is now \(\sin2\theta\), so its area is \(\frac12\cdot1\cdot\sin2\theta\). The two areas are equal, so \(\sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta\). The figure below redraws this.

Example 1 · Double angle identity (slide 9)

Example 1 (slide 9)

Find \(\displaystyle\int\cos^2x\,dx\).

There is no "chain rule in reverse" here (there is no extra \(\sin x\) factor), and the Power Rule does not apply to a function of \(x\) (see Common mistakes). Instead use the double angle formula \(\cos2x=2\cos^2x-1\), rearranged:

\[\cos^2x=\tfrac12(1+\cos2x).\]
Reading the formula
\(\cos^2x\)
a wave that swings between 0 and 1 (a square is never negative), repeating every \(\pi\).
\(\tfrac12\)
the midline: the curve spends equal time above and below height \(\tfrac12\).
\(\tfrac12\cos2x\)
the swing: half a unit up and down from the midline; the \(2x\) makes it repeat every \(\pi\), twice as often as \(\cos x\).

Then

\[\begin{aligned}\int\cos^2x\,dx&=\frac12\int(1+\cos2x)\,dx\\&=\frac12\left(x+\frac{\sin2x}{2}\right)+C\\&=\frac{x}{2}+\frac{\sin2x}{4}+C.\end{aligned}\]

In words: cosine squared = half of (1 + cosine of double the angle), which is easy to integrate: half of \(x\), plus a quarter of \(\sin2x\).

(\(\int\cos2x\,dx=\frac12\sin2x\) by the substitution \(u=2x\).) Check: \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac x2+\frac{\sin2x}4\right)=\frac12+\frac{\cos2x}{2}=\cos^2x\). ✓

Example 2 · Double angle identity (slide 10)

Example 2 (slide 10)

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/4}\sqrt{1+\sin4x}\,dx\).

Step 1: make the inside a perfect square. Write \(1=\sin^22x+\cos^22x\) (fundamental identity with angle \(2x\)) and \(\sin4x=2\sin2x\cos2x\) (double angle with angle \(2x\)):together they give the pattern \(a^2+2ab+b^2\), a perfect square, and only a perfect square lets the root come off.

\[\begin{aligned}1+\sin4x&=\sin^22x+2\sin2x\cos2x+\cos^22x\\&=(\sin2x+\cos2x)^2.\end{aligned}\]

Step 2: take the square root carefully. \(\sqrt{A^2}=|A|\), not \(A\). For \(0\le x\le\frac\pi4\) we have \(0\le2x\le\frac\pi2\), so \(\sin2x\ge0\) and \(\cos2x\ge0\). Hence \(\sin2x+\cos2x\ge0\) and

\[\sqrt{1+\sin4x}=\sin2x+\cos2x\quad\text{on }\left[0,\tfrac\pi4\right].\]

Step 3: integrate.

\[\begin{aligned}&\int_0^{\pi/4}(\sin2x+\cos2x)\,dx\\&=\left[-\frac{\cos2x}{2}+\frac{\sin2x}{2}\right]_0^{\pi/4}\\&=\left(0+\frac12\right)-\left(-\frac12+0\right)=1.\end{aligned}\]

In words: 1 + sin 4x is the square of (sin 2x + cos 2x); on this interval that bracket is never negative, so the square root just removes the square, and the area is 1.

The second option in the figure below shows why Step 2 matters: beyond \(x=\frac{3\pi}8\) the bracket goes negative, and there \(\sqrt{1+\sin4x}\) equals \(-(\sin2x+\cos2x)\) instead.

Slide 11's advice: rewrite trigonometric integrands to reduce powers, eliminate square roots, separate products, and so on. How the rows connect:

  • Dividing \(\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1\) by \(\cos^2x\) gives \(\tan^2x+1=\sec^2x\); dividing by \(\sin^2x\) gives \(1+\cot^2x=\csc^2x\). These are used in trig substitution (Section 4).
  • The three forms of \(\cos2x\) come from \(\cos(\alpha+\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta\) with \(\alpha=\beta=x\), then replacing \(\sin^2x\) or \(\cos^2x\) using the fundamental identity.
  • Power reduction is the double angle formula solved for \(\sin^2x\) or \(\cos^2x\). It is called that because it turns a square (power 2) into a first power of \(\cos2x\).

    In words: sine squared = half of (1 − cos of double angle); cosine squared = half of (1 + cos of double angle).

  • Half angle is power reduction with \(x\) replaced by \(\frac x2\), then square-rooted. The sign \(\pm\) is chosen by the quadrant of \(\frac x2\): \(+\) if that sine or cosine is positive there, \(-\) if negative.
  • Product to sum comes from adding or subtracting two compound angle formulas. For example, \(\cos(A-B)-\cos(A+B)=2\sin A\sin B\); put \(A=mx\), \(B=nx\) and halve to get the first line.

    In words: a product of two sines or cosines with different frequencies equals half of a sum or difference of single sines or cosines, one at the difference of the frequencies and one at their sum. Single sines and cosines are easy to integrate.

Find \(\int\sin3x\cos x\,dx\). Using \(\sin mx\cos nx=\frac12[\sin(m-n)x+\sin(m+n)x]\) with \(m=3\), \(n=1\):

\[\begin{aligned}\int\sin3x\cos x\,dx&=\frac12\int(\sin2x+\sin4x)\,dx\\&=-\frac{\cos2x}{4}-\frac{\cos4x}{8}+C.\end{aligned}\]

You can now: rewrite \(\sin^2x\) and \(\cos^2x\) with power reduction; spot a perfect square under a root and remove the root with an absolute-value check on the interval; turn a product of sines and cosines into a sum with product to sum; and check a rewrite by differentiating.

3Powers of sine and cosine (slides 12 and 13) ~4 min

What is this?

Think of the substitution \(u=\cos x\) as a purchase: \(du=-\sin x\,dx\) must be paid for with exactly one factor of sin x. So you set one sine aside as the payment, and turn every other sine into cosines with sin² x = 1 − cos² x. That identity only converts sines in pairs, so what is left must be an even power. If both powers are even, there is no spare factor to pay with, and you halve the powers with the double angle identities instead.

Example 3 · Trigonometric identity (slide 12)

Example 3 (slide 12)

Find \(\displaystyle\int\sin^5x\cos^2x\,dx\).

The power of sine (5) is odd. Keep one \(\sin x\) aside and turn the remaining \(\sin^4x\) into cosines with \(\sin^2x=1-\cos^2x\):the single \(\sin x\) with \(dx\) is exactly what \(du=-\sin x\,dx\) needs, and \(\sin^4x\) is two pairs \(\sin^2x\), each of which converts.

\[\begin{aligned}\sin^5x\cos^2x&=(\sin^2x)^2\cos^2x\,\sin x\\&=(1-\cos^2x)^2\cos^2x\,\sin x.\end{aligned}\]

Now everything is a function of \(\cos x\), times \(\sin x\). Put \(u=\cos x\), \(du=-\sin x\,dx\):

\[\begin{aligned} &\int\sin^5x\cos^2x\,dx\\&=-\int(1-u^2)^2u^2\,du\\&=-\int(u^2-2u^4+u^6)\,du\\ &=-\frac{u^3}{3}+\frac{2u^5}{5}-\frac{u^7}{7}+C\\ &=-\frac{\cos^3x}{3}+\frac{2\cos^5x}{5}-\frac{\cos^7x}{7}+C. \end{aligned}\]

In words: save one sine to become part of \(du\); write every other sine in terms of cosine; then the integral is just a polynomial in \(u=\cos x\).

Slide 13's summary: rewrite an integrand of powers of sine and cosine so that only one sine factor or one cosine factor remains outside, ready to become \(du\).

  • Why isolate exactly one factor? Because \(du=-\sin x\,dx\) (or \(\cos x\,dx\)) uses up exactly one sine (or cosine). Why must the rest be an even power? Because only even powers can be rewritten with \(\sin^2x=1-\cos^2x\).
  • If both powers are odd, either row works; pick the one with the smaller odd power.
  • If both are even (including 0), there is no single factor to spare, so reduce powers with the double angle identities. Each round halves the powers; for \(\sin^4x\) you apply it twice (Group work 2).
Beyond the handouts · powers of sec and tan, and \(\int\sec^3x\,dx\)

The slides only treat sine and cosine powers, but the same idea works for \(\int\tan^mx\sec^nx\,dx\) using \(\frac{d}{dx}\tan x=\sec^2x\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}\sec x=\sec x\tan x\): if \(n\) is even, save \(\sec^2x\) and put \(u=\tan x\); if \(m\) is odd, save \(\sec x\tan x\) and put \(u=\sec x\).

One integral appears often after trig substitution: \(\int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta\). Use integration by parts with first \(=\sec\theta\), second's derivative \(=\sec^2\theta\) (so second \(=\tan\theta\)):

\[\begin{aligned}\int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta&=\sec\theta\tan\theta-\int\sec\theta\tan^2\theta\,d\theta\\&=\sec\theta\tan\theta-\int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta\\&\quad+\int\sec\theta\,d\theta,\end{aligned}\]

using \(\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta-1\). The unknown integral appears on both sides; move it across and use \(\int\sec\theta\,d\theta=\ln|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|\) (Section 1):

\[\begin{aligned}&\int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta\\&=\frac12\Big(\sec\theta\tan\theta+\ln|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|\Big)+C.\end{aligned}\]

In words: integral of secant cubed = half of (secant × tangent + log of |secant + tangent|).

You can now: pick the right row of the strategy table from whether \(m\) and \(n\) are odd or even; save one factor for \(du\) and convert the rest with the fundamental identity; and reduce even powers with the double angle identities, repeating as often as needed.

4Trigonometric substitution (slides 14 to 19) ~11 min

What is this?

Draw a right triangle with one side \(x\) and one side a fixed length \(a\). Pythagoras makes the third side \(\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\), \(\sqrt{a^2+x^2}\) or \(\sqrt{x^2-a^2}\), depending on which side is the hypotenuse. Trigonometric substitution names an angle θ of that triangle and writes \(x\) as a trig ratio of θ. The awkward root is then just another side, \(a\) times one trig function of θ, and the square root disappears. At the end the same triangle translates everything back into \(x\).

Why it is needed (slide 14)

We already know a definite integral involving \(\sqrt{1-x^2}\): the graph of \(y=\sqrt{1-x^2}\) is the upper half of the circle of radius 1, so

\[\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{2}\quad(\text{area of half a unit disc}).\]

But what about the indefinite integral \(\int\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx\)? No substitution \(u=g(x)\) from Lecture 13 helps, because there is no factor \(g'(x)\) next to the root. The idea is the opposite: put \(x\) equal to a trig function so that an identity removes the square root.

Let \(x=\sin\theta\) where \(-\frac\pi2\le\theta\le\frac\pi2\). By the fundamental identity,

\[\sqrt{1-x^2}=\sqrt{1-\sin^2\theta}=\sqrt{\cos^2\theta}=|\cos\theta|=\cos\theta,\]

where the last step holds because \(\cos\theta\ge0\) for \(-\frac\pi2\le\theta\le\frac\pi2\). Also \(dx=\cos\theta\,d\theta\). Then

\[\begin{aligned} \int\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx&=\int\cos\theta\cdot\cos\theta\,d\theta\\&\qquad\text{by }dx=\cos\theta\,d\theta\\ &=\int\cos^2\theta\,d\theta=\frac\theta2+\frac{\sin2\theta}{4}+C\\&\qquad\text{by Example 1}\\ &=\frac12\left(x\sqrt{1-x^2}+\sin^{-1}x\right)+C\\&\qquad\text{by }\theta=\sin^{-1}x. \end{aligned}\]

In the last line, \(\sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta=2x\sqrt{1-x^2}\), and \(\theta=\sin^{-1}x\) is the inverse sine from Lecture 7 (the angle in \(\left[-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2\right]\) whose sine is \(x\)).

In words: the area under the semicircle from 0 to \(x\) = half of (\(x\) × height of the curve at \(x\)) + half of the angle \(\sin^{-1}x\). The first part is a triangle and the second is a slice of the disc (a sector), as the figure shows.

Slide 14 ends: this is the Substitution Rule in reverse!

What the answer \(\frac12\left(x\sqrt{1-x^2}+\sin^{-1}x\right)\) means

y = √(1 − x²)triangle, area ½·x·√(1 − x²)sector, area ½·sin⁻¹x

The area under \(y=\sqrt{1-x^2}\) from 0 to \(x\) splits into a right triangle and a slice of the disc with angle \(\theta=\sin^{-1}x\) (measured from the vertical axis). A slice of a radius-1 disc with angle \(\theta\) has area \(\frac\theta2\). For negative \(x\) both parts are counted as negative, as the integral from 0 to a negative number should be. At \(x=1\) the total is \(\frac\pi4\), a quarter of the disc.

Substitution rule in reverse (slide 15)

What is this? (inverse substitution)

In Lecture 13 you gave a messy inside part a new name. Here you go the other way: you describe \(x\) itself by a new dial θ, the angle of the triangle. Turning the dial a little moves \(x\) a little, and \(dx=g'(\theta)\,d\theta\) says by how much. Each \(x\) must come from exactly one dial setting, so that you can read θ back off at the end; that is why θ is kept to a restricted range.

Proposition (slide 15)

Let \(g\) be a one-to-one function. If we replace \(u\) by \(x\) and \(x\) by \(\theta\) in the Substitution Rule, then

\[\int f(x)\,dx=\int f(g(\theta))\,g'(\theta)\,d\theta.\]

In words: to integrate in \(x\), write \(x\) as a function \(g(\theta)\) of a new variable; replace every \(x\) by \(g(\theta)\) and replace \(dx\) by \(g'(\theta)\,d\theta\).

Reading the formula
\(x=g(\theta)\)
\(x\) described by the dial θ, for example \(x=a\sin\theta\): \(x\) is a side of the triangle with angle θ.
\(f(g(\theta))\)
the same integrand with every \(x\) rewritten in θ; this is where the root becomes one side of the triangle.
\(g'(\theta)\,d\theta\)
the width \(dx\) of each thin strip, converted: a small turn \(d\theta\) moves \(x\) by \(g'(\theta)\,d\theta\).
\(g\) one-to-one
each \(x\) has exactly one θ, so the answer can be turned back into \(x\).
  • A function is one-to-one if different inputs always give different outputs (Lecture 7). This is needed so that at the end you can go back from \(x\) to a single \(\theta=g^{-1}(x)\). That is why each substitution comes with a restricted range of \(\theta\).
  • In Lecture 13 you set the new variable equal to a function of the old one (\(u=g(x)\)). Here you set the old variable equal to a function of the new one (\(x=g(\theta)\)). This is also known as inverse substitution.
  • In this course, the substitutions are \(x=a\tan\theta\), \(x=a\sin\theta\) or \(x=a\sec\theta\), where \(a\gt0\) is a constant.

Steps to apply trigonometric substitution (slide 17)

Given an indefinite integral with \(\sqrt{a^2+x^2}\), \(\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\) or \(\sqrt{x^2-a^2}\) in the integrand:

  1. Do: Choose a suitable substitution for \(x\) from the table. Compute the differential \(dx\) (for example \(x=a\sin\theta\) gives \(dx=a\cos\theta\,d\theta\)), and state the values allowed for \(\theta\). This fixes which side of the triangle \(x\) is; the θ-range makes the dial one-to-one and fixes the sign of the root.
  2. Do: Substitute the trig expression for \(x\) and the differential \(dx\) into the integrand, then simplify algebraically (the square root should disappear). The identity is Pythagoras on the triangle, so the root collapses into one side, \(a\) times a trig function.
  3. Do: Integrate the resulting trigonometric integral with respect to (WRT) \(\theta\), using Sections 1 to 3. With the root gone, what is left is the kind of trig integral those sections already handle.
  4. Do: Return to \(x\). Draw a reference right triangle to read off \(\sin\theta\), \(\cos\theta\), \(\tan\theta\) in terms of \(x\), and write \(\theta\) itself as an inverse trig function of \(x\). The question was about \(x\); the triangle's sides turn every trig ratio of θ back into an expression in \(x\).

(For a definite integral you may instead change the limits to \(\theta\)-values and skip step 4.)

Example 4 · Trigonometric substitution (slide 18)

Example 4 (slide 18)

Find \(\displaystyle\int x^2\sqrt{4-x^2}\,dx\).

Step 1. The root has the form \(\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\) with \(a=2\). Let \(x=2\sin\theta\), \(-\frac\pi2\le\theta\le\frac\pi2\). Then \(dx=2\cos\theta\,d\theta\).

Step 2. \(\sqrt{4-x^2}=\sqrt{4-4\sin^2\theta}=\sqrt{4\cos^2\theta}=2|\cos\theta|=2\cos\theta\).the absolute value can go because \(-\frac\pi2\le\theta\le\frac\pi2\), where \(\cos\theta\ge0\). So

\[\begin{aligned}&\int x^2\sqrt{4-x^2}\,dx\\&=\int(4\sin^2\theta)(2\cos\theta)(2\cos\theta)\,d\theta\\&=16\int\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta\,d\theta.\end{aligned}\]

Step 3. Both powers are even. Use \(\sin\theta\cos\theta=\frac12\sin2\theta\), so \(\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta=\frac14\sin^22\theta\), then power reduction \(\sin^22\theta=\frac12(1-\cos4\theta)\):

\[\begin{aligned}16\int\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta\,d\theta&=4\int\sin^22\theta\,d\theta\\&=2\int(1-\cos4\theta)\,d\theta\\&=2\theta-\frac{\sin4\theta}{2}+C.\end{aligned}\]

Step 4. From the triangle (hypotenuse 2, opposite \(x\), adjacent \(\sqrt{4-x^2}\)): \(\sin\theta=\frac x2\), \(\cos\theta=\frac{\sqrt{4-x^2}}{2}\), and \(\theta=\sin^{-1}\frac x2\). Write \(\sin4\theta\) using double angles twice:

\[\begin{aligned}\sin4\theta&=2\sin2\theta\cos2\theta\\&=2(2\sin\theta\cos\theta)(1-2\sin^2\theta)\\&=4\cdot\frac x2\cdot\frac{\sqrt{4-x^2}}{2}\left(1-\frac{x^2}{2}\right)\\&=x\sqrt{4-x^2}\left(1-\frac{x^2}{2}\right).\end{aligned}\]

Therefore

\[\begin{aligned}&\int x^2\sqrt{4-x^2}\,dx\\&=2\sin^{-1}\frac x2-\frac{x}{2}\sqrt{4-x^2}\left(1-\frac{x^2}{2}\right)+C\\&=2\sin^{-1}\frac x2+\frac{x(x^2-2)}{4}\sqrt{4-x^2}+C.\end{aligned}\]

In words: substitute so the root becomes \(2\cos\theta\); the integral becomes a sine-cosine power integral; integrate; then use the triangle to turn every \(\theta\)-expression back into \(x\).

Example 5 · Complete the square (slide 19)

Example 5 (slide 19)

Find \(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{x^2+2x+5}\,dx\).

There is no square root, but the denominator can be turned into "something squared + a constant squared" by completing the square (HKDSE Core):a sum of two squares is the \(a^2+x^2\) side of the tangent triangle, so \(1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta\) can then simplify it.

\[x^2+2x+5=(x+1)^2+4=(x+1)^2+2^2.\]

This has the form \(u^2+a^2\) with \(u=x+1\), \(a=2\), so use the tangent substitution \(x+1=2\tan\theta\), \(-\frac\pi2\lt\theta\lt\frac\pi2\). Then \(dx=2\sec^2\theta\,d\theta\) and \((x+1)^2+4=4\tan^2\theta+4=4\sec^2\theta\):

\[\begin{aligned}\int\frac{dx}{(x+1)^2+4}&=\int\frac{2\sec^2\theta}{4\sec^2\theta}\,d\theta=\int\frac12\,d\theta\\&=\frac\theta2+C\\&=\frac12\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{x+1}{2}\right)+C.\end{aligned}\]

In words: complete the square to see a "sum of two squares"; a tangent substitution turns the whole fraction into a constant.

Equivalently, put \(u=x+1\) and use formula 4 of slide 22 (Section 5): \(\int\frac{du}{a^2+u^2}=\frac1a\tan^{-1}\frac ua+C\). Completing the square works the same way inside a root, for example \(\sqrt{x^2+2x+5}=\sqrt{(x+1)^2+2^2}\).

You can now: pick \(x=a\sin\theta\), \(a\tan\theta\) or \(a\sec\theta\) from the shape of the root; state the θ-range and use it to drop the absolute value; carry the integral through in θ, including \(dx\); convert back to \(x\) with a reference triangle; and complete the square first when the quadratic is not yet a sum or difference of squares.

5Partial fractions (slides 20 to 26) ~11 min

What is this?

Adding fractions is easy: put them over a common denominator and they merge into one fraction. Partial fractions runs this backwards. You are handed the merged fraction and asked which simple fractions were added to make it, like working out which slices made up the whole pizza. Each simple piece has only one factor of the bottom in its own denominator, so it integrates to a log, a power or an inverse tangent.

The decomposition concept (slide 20)

Slide 20 shows a whole pizza cut into 6 slices: 2 meat slices and 4 vegetable slices.

\[\text{entire pizza}=\text{2 meat slices}+\text{4 veg slices},\] \[\frac66=\frac26+\frac46.\]

The slide asks: is it simpler to integrate the entire pizza or the slices? The point: adding fractions is easy (find a common denominator), and partial fractions means doing it backwards: split one complicated fraction into a sum of simple pieces ("slices"), each of which you can integrate on its own. For example, \(\frac{2}{x-2}+\frac{3}{x+1}=\frac{5x-4}{x^2-x-2}\). Integrating the left side is easy (two logarithms); integrating the right side directly is not.

Rational functions (slide 21)

What is this? (proper and improper)

A top-heavy number fraction hides a whole number: seven thirds is 2 and one third left over. A rational function whose top degree is at least the bottom degree likewise hides a polynomial part. Un-adding only works on the proper leftover, because simple pieces with their small numerators always add up to a proper fraction. So a top-heavy fraction must be divided first.

Definition (slide 21)

A polynomial is a function that is a sum of (constant multiples of) powers of \(x\):

\[P(x)=a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0,\]

with coefficients \(a_i\in\mathbb R\) (real numbers) for \(0\le i\le n\).

  • Degree: if \(a_n\ne0\), the degree of \(P\) is \(\deg(P)=n\), the highest power present.
  • A rational function is a quotient \(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\) of two polynomials.
  • Proper / improper: \(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\) is proper if \(\deg(P)\lt\deg(Q)\), and improper if \(\deg(P)\ge\deg(Q)\).
  • For an improper fraction, divide \(Q\) into \(P\) (polynomial long division) to obtain a quotient \(S\) and a remainder \(R\) with \(\deg(R)\lt\deg(Q)\), so that \(\frac{R(x)}{Q(x)}\) is proper and \[\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}=S(x)+\frac{R(x)}{Q(x)}.\]

In words: top-heavy fraction = whole-polynomial part + proper leftover fraction, exactly like \(\frac73=2+\frac13\).

Reading the formula
\(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\)
the top-heavy fraction you were given.
\(S(x)\)
the "whole number" part, a polynomial you can integrate term by term at once.
\(\frac{R(x)}{Q(x)}\)
the proper leftover, with \(\deg(R)\lt\deg(Q)\): the only part that needs un-adding.

Example: \(\frac{x^3+2}{x^2-4}\) is improper (degree 3 over degree 2). Dividing, \(x^3+2=x(x^2-4)+(4x+2)\), so \(\frac{x^3+2}{x^2-4}=x+\frac{4x+2}{x^2-4}\). The quotient \(x\) integrates directly and only the proper part needs partial fractions (Group work 10).

Rational integration formulas (slide 22)

Theorem (slide 22) \[\begin{aligned} &1.\ \int u^n\,du=\frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}+C,\ n\ne-1\\ &2.\ \int\frac1u\,du=\ln|u|+C\\ &3.\ \int\frac{1}{1+u^2}\,du=\tan^{-1}u+C\\ &4.\ \int\frac{1}{a^2+u^2}\,du=\frac1a\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac ua\right)+C\\ &5.\ \int\frac{u}{1+u^2}\,du=\frac12\ln(1+u^2)+C \end{aligned}\]

In words: (1) raise the power by one and divide by the new power; (2) one over \(u\) integrates to log of \(|u|\); (3, 4) one over (constant squared + \(u\) squared) integrates to an inverse tangent; (5) \(u\) over (1 + \(u\) squared) integrates to half the log of the denominator.

Formulas 1 to 3 come from the FTC (derivative facts from Lectures 6 and 7 read backwards). Formulas 4 and 5 come from the Substitution Rule:

  • Formula 4: \(a^2+u^2=a^2\left(1+\left(\frac ua\right)^2\right)\); put \(v=\frac ua\), \(du=a\,dv\): \(\int\frac{a\,dv}{a^2(1+v^2)}=\frac1a\tan^{-1}v+C\).
  • Formula 5: put \(w=1+u^2\), \(dw=2u\,du\): \(\int\frac{u\,du}{1+u^2}=\frac12\int\frac{dw}{w}=\frac12\ln(1+u^2)+C\) (no absolute value needed since \(1+u^2\gt0\)).

Together these integrate every "slice" that partial fractions can produce from linear and simple quadratic factors.

The method of partial fractions (slide 23)

Two words first. A factor of \(Q(x)\) is a polynomial that divides \(Q(x)\) exactly. A factor is irreducible if it cannot be factorised further into real polynomials of lower degree. Every linear factor \(ax-b\) is irreducible; a quadratic \(ax^2+bx+c\) is irreducible exactly when its discriminant \(b^2-4ac\lt0\) (no real roots), for example \(x^2+1\) or \(x^2+x+4\).

What is this? (the PFD form)

Before un-adding you write down the shape of the pieces, with unknown numbers on top: one piece for each factor of the bottom, and for a repeated factor one piece for each power. A linear factor gets a constant on top and a quadratic factor gets a linear top, so that every piece is proper. The rest of the method only finds the unknown numbers.

Method (slide 23)

Given \(\displaystyle\int\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\,dx\) with \(\deg(P)\lt\deg(Q)\):

  1. Do: Equate \(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\) with its partial fraction decomposition (PFD) form. Factorise \(Q(x)\) into irreducible factors; each factor contributes terms as follows:
    Irreducible factor of \(Q(x)\)Term(s) in the PFD
    \(ax-b\)\(\dfrac{A}{ax-b}\)
    \((ax-b)^m\)\(\dfrac{A_1}{ax-b}+\dfrac{A_2}{(ax-b)^2}+\cdots+\dfrac{A_m}{(ax-b)^m}\)
    \(ax^2+bx+c\)\(\dfrac{Ax+B}{ax^2+bx+c}\)
    \((ax^2+bx+c)^m\)\(\dfrac{A_1x+B_1}{ax^2+bx+c}+\dfrac{A_2x+B_2}{(ax^2+bx+c)^2}+\cdots+\dfrac{A_mx+B_m}{(ax^2+bx+c)^m}\)
    This lists every simple piece that could have been added to make the fraction, one for each power of each factor.
  2. Do: Multiply both sides by the least common denominator (which is \(Q(x)\) itself), so there are no fractions left. This is the "adding" step done on the unknown pieces: what remains is two polynomials that must agree for every \(x\).
  3. Do: Equate the coefficients of each power of \(x\) on the two sides.* Two polynomials agree for every \(x\) only if every power of \(x\) has the same coefficient on both sides.
  4. Do: Solve the resulting system of equations for the unknown constants.* There are as many unknowns as equations (both equal \(\deg(Q)\)), so the pieces are pinned down.
  5. Do: Rewrite \(\int\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\,dx\) as the integral of its PFD and evaluate it term by term. Each simple piece is one of the slide-22 formulas: a log, a power or an inverse tangent.

* Alternative to steps 3 and 4: assign numerical values to \(x\), or differentiate.

In words: factorise the bottom; write one unknown-numerator fraction for each power of each factor (constant on top of a linear factor, linear on top of a quadratic factor); clear denominators; find the unknowns; integrate each piece.

  • Why these forms? The numerator of each piece must be "one degree lower" than its own irreducible factor, which keeps every piece proper. With these forms, the number of unknown constants always equals \(\deg(Q)\), which is exactly the number of equations you get by comparing coefficients.
  • Why every power for repeated factors? Adding fractions with denominators \((x-1)\), \((x-1)^2\), \((x-1)^3\) can produce any proper numerator over \((x-1)^3\); using only \(\frac{A}{(x-1)^3}\) would not be enough (see Common mistakes).
  • Assigning values (the footnote): after step 2 the equation holds for every \(x\), so substitute convenient values, especially the roots of the linear factors, where most terms become 0. This is usually the fastest route.
  • Differentiating (the footnote): for a repeated factor \((x-r)^2\), substituting \(x=r\) only gives one constant. Differentiating both sides of the cleared equation and then putting \(x=r\) gives another. Comparing coefficients always works as a fallback.

Example 6 · PFD (slide 24)

Example 6 (slide 24)

Find \(\displaystyle\int\frac{5x-4}{x^2-x-2}\,dx\).

The fraction is proper (degree 1 over degree 2). Factorise: \(x^2-x-2=(x-2)(x+1)\). Two distinct linear factors, so

\[\frac{5x-4}{(x-2)(x+1)}=\frac{A}{x-2}+\frac{B}{x+1}.\]

Multiply by \((x-2)(x+1)\): \(5x-4=A(x+1)+B(x-2)\).

  • By values: \(x=2\): \(6=3A\Rightarrow A=2\). \(x=-1\): \(-9=-3B\Rightarrow B=3\).at \(x=2\) the term \(B(x-2)\) is zero, so only \(A\) is left; at \(x=-1\) the \(A\) term vanishes instead.
  • By coefficients (check): \(x\): \(A+B=5\); constant: \(A-2B=-4\). Solving gives \(A=2\), \(B=3\) again.
\[\begin{aligned}&\int\frac{5x-4}{x^2-x-2}\,dx\\&=\int\left(\frac{2}{x-2}+\frac{3}{x+1}\right)dx\\&=2\ln|x-2|+3\ln|x+1|+C.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the fraction is two simple "slices"; each slice integrates to a constant times a log.

Example 7 · PFD with an irreducible quadratic (slide 25)

Example 7 (slide 25)

Find \(\displaystyle\int\frac{x^2-2x+1}{x^3+x}\,dx\).

Proper (degree 2 over degree 3). Factorise: \(x^3+x=x(x^2+1)\), where \(x^2+1\) is irreducible (discriminant \(-4\lt0\)). So

\[\frac{x^2-2x+1}{x(x^2+1)}=\frac{A}{x}+\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+1}.\]

Multiply by \(x(x^2+1)\): \(x^2-2x+1=A(x^2+1)+(Bx+C)x=(A+B)x^2+Cx+A\). Compare coefficients:

\[\begin{aligned}x^2&:\ A+B=1,\\ x&:\ C=-2,\\ \text{constant}&:\ A=1\ \Rightarrow\ B=0.\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}\int\frac{x^2-2x+1}{x^3+x}\,dx&=\int\left(\frac1x-\frac{2}{x^2+1}\right)dx\\&=\ln|x|-2\tan^{-1}x+C.\end{aligned}\]

In words: one log from the linear factor, one inverse tangent from the quadratic factor (formula 3 of slide 22).

Example 8 (slide 26)

Set up the PFD of

6Summary (slide 27)

  • Techniques of integration so far: the Substitution Rule and Integration by Parts (Lecture 13); trigonometric identities, trigonometric substitution and partial fraction decomposition (this lecture).
  • The best strategy for integration is practice. There is no single algorithm; you learn to recognise the shape of an integrand: a root of \(a^2\pm x^2\) or \(x^2-a^2\) suggests trig substitution, a polynomial over a polynomial suggests (division and) partial fractions, powers of sine and cosine suggest the strategy table, a function times its inside derivative suggests ordinary substitution.
  • To check an indefinite integral, differentiate your answer and see whether you get the integrand back. This catches almost every slip (try it on the Find-the-mistake answer below).
  • Differentiation always works, integration may not. An elementary function is one built from polynomials, roots, exponentials, logarithms, trig and inverse trig functions using \(+,-,\times,\div\) and composition (putting one function inside another). If \(f(x)\) is elementary, then \(f'(x)\) is also elementary. But \(\int f(x)\,dx\) need not be elementary: for example, \(f(x)=e^{x^2}\) does not have an elementary antiderivative. It certainly has an antiderivative (by the FTC, \(F(x)=\int_0^x e^{t^2}\,dt\) is one), it just cannot be written as a formula using the functions above, so no technique in this course will find it.

Choosing a method

The recognition rules above, as an order to try them in:

  1. Do: look first for an ordinary substitution: a function next to the derivative of its inside. It is the quickest route when it exists; a trig substitution may also work but takes much longer (Concept check 5).
  2. Do: a polynomial over a polynomial: divide if it is top-heavy, then use partial fractions. Only a proper fraction can be un-added into simple pieces.
  3. Do: a root of \(a^2-x^2\), \(a^2+x^2\) or \(x^2-a^2\) (completing the square if needed): trig substitution. The right triangle turns the root into one of its sides.
  4. Do: powers or products of sines and cosines: the strategy table of Section 3 or an identity from Section 2. An identity changes the formula, not the curve, into one the basic list covers.
  5. Do: differentiate your answer. Getting the integrand back proves the antiderivative is right, and differentiation always works.
Beyond the handouts

Other common integrands with no elementary antiderivative include \(e^{-x^2}\) (central to statistics: the normal distribution), \(\frac{\sin x}{x}\), \(\frac{1}{\ln x}\) and \(\sqrt{1+x^3}\). Their antiderivatives are given new names (for example the "error function") or computed numerically.

7Common mistakes (slide 29)

Trigonometric identities

✗Incorrectly applying the Power Rule: \(\displaystyle\int\cos^2x\,dx=\frac13\cos^3x+C\).
The Power Rule \(\int u^n\,du=\frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}\) needs \(du\), the derivative of the inside, to be present. Here \(u=\cos x\) would need \(du=-\sin x\,dx\), which is missing. Differentiating the wrong answer gives \(-\cos^2x\sin x\), not \(\cos^2x\) (third option of the identities figure).
✓ \(\displaystyle\int\cos^2x\,dx=\frac x2+\frac{\sin2x}{4}+C\) by power reduction (Example 1). ✗Getting stuck in a loop of identities: applying power reduction instead of the fundamental identity to odd powers. For example, writing \(\sin^3x=\sin x\cdot\frac12(1-\cos2x)\) creates a product \(\sin x\cos2x\) that needs yet another identity.
✓ For an odd power, peel off one factor and use the fundamental identity: \(\int\sin^3x\,dx=\int(1-\cos^2x)\sin x\,dx=-\cos x+\frac{\cos^3x}{3}+C\) (with \(u=\cos x\)).

Trigonometric substitution

✗Incorrectly applying the Power Rule: \(\displaystyle\int\sqrt{x^2+1}\,dx=\frac23(x^2+1)^{3/2}+C\).
Differentiating the wrong answer gives \(2x\sqrt{x^2+1}\): the factor \(2x\) (the derivative of \(x^2+1\)) was never there.
✓ The root has the form \(\sqrt{a^2+x^2}\) with \(a=1\): put \(x=\tan\theta\), \(dx=\sec^2\theta\,d\theta\), \(\sqrt{x^2+1}=\sec\theta\). Then \(\int\sqrt{x^2+1}\,dx=\int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta\), and with the \(\sec^3\) formula from the beyond box in Section 3 and the triangle (\(\tan\theta=x\), \(\sec\theta=\sqrt{x^2+1}\)): \[\begin{aligned}&\int\sqrt{x^2+1}\,dx\\&=\frac12\left(x\sqrt{x^2+1}+\ln\left(x+\sqrt{x^2+1}\right)\right)+C.\end{aligned}\] ✗Forgetting the differential: setting \(x=3\sin\theta\) but forgetting \(dx=3\cos\theta\,d\theta\). Replacing \(dx\) by \(d\theta\) is wrong: \(x\) and \(\theta\) change at different rates.
✓ Always write the line "\(dx=g'(\theta)\,d\theta\)" before substituting (step 1 of slide 17).
Beyond the handouts · the hyperbolic alternative for \(\sqrt{x^2+1}\)

Some solutions use the hyperbolic functions \(\sinh t=\frac{e^t-e^{-t}}{2}\) and \(\cosh t=\frac{e^t+e^{-t}}{2}\), which satisfy \(\cosh^2t-\sinh^2t=1\), \(\frac{d}{dt}\sinh t=\cosh t\), and \(\cosh^2t=\frac12(1+\cosh2t)\). These are not in the MATH1851 handouts. With \(x=\sinh t\), \(dx=\cosh t\,dt\), \(\sqrt{x^2+1}=\cosh t\):

\[\begin{aligned}\int\sqrt{x^2+1}\,dx&=\int\cosh^2t\,dt\\&=\frac12\left(t+\sinh t\cosh t\right)+C,\end{aligned}\]

and \(t=\sinh^{-1}x=\ln\left(x+\sqrt{x^2+1}\right)\), which gives the same answer as the tangent route. The trig substitution in the slides is always enough.

Partial fraction decomposition

✗Applying PFD to an improper fraction: \(\displaystyle\int\frac{x^3+2}{x^2-4}\,dx=\int\left(\frac{A}{x+2}+\frac{B}{x-2}\right)dx\).
The right side is always proper (adding the two pieces gives degree at most 1 over degree 2), so it can never equal a degree-3-over-degree-2 fraction. No values of \(A\), \(B\) work.
✓ Divide first: \(\frac{x^3+2}{x^2-4}=x+\frac{4x+2}{x^2-4}\), then split the proper part (Group work 10). ✗Incorrect PFD setup: \(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{(x+1)^2(x^2+1)}\,dx=\int\left(\frac{A}{x+1}+\frac{B}{x+1}+\frac{C}{x^2+1}\right)dx\).
Two errors: the repeated factor needs the powers \((x+1)\) and \((x+1)^2\), and the quadratic factor needs a linear numerator \(Cx+D\). (The wrong form also has only 3 unknowns for a degree-4 denominator.)
✓ \(\displaystyle\frac{1}{(x+1)^2(x^2+1)}=\frac{A}{x+1}+\frac{B}{(x+1)^2}+\frac{Cx+D}{x^2+1}\), which works out as \(A=\frac12\), \(B=\frac12\), \(C=-\frac12\), \(D=0\).

More traps Written for these notes

✗Writing \(\sqrt{\cos^2\theta}=\cos\theta\) without saying why. It is \(|\cos\theta|\); it equals \(\cos\theta\) only because the chosen \(\theta\)-range makes \(\cos\theta\ge0\). Markers look for the stated range. ✗Dropping the absolute value in \(\int\frac{dx}{x-2}=\ln|x-2|+C\). Without it the answer is wrong for \(x\lt2\). ✗Leaving the answer in \(\theta\) after a trig substitution, or keeping the \(x\)-limits after changing variables in a definite integral.

8Find the mistake (slide 28)

The slide shows a student's handwritten solution to this question:

Make a substitution and use trigonometric substitution to evaluate

\[\int\frac{e^x}{\sqrt{e^{2x}-4}}\,dx.\]

Write your result in terms of exponential and logarithmic functions only.

The student's work, reproduced line by line:

let \(u=e^x\), \(du=e^x\,dx\)

\[\int\frac{u}{\sqrt{u^2-2^2}}\,du\]

for \(u=2\sec\theta\), \(du=2\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta\)

\[\begin{aligned} &\int\frac{2\sec\theta}{\sqrt{(2\sec\theta)^2-2^2}}\,du\\ &=\int\frac{2\sec\theta}{\sqrt{4(\sec^2\theta-1)}}\,du\\ &=\int\frac{2\sec\theta}{\sqrt{4\tan^2\theta}}\,2\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta\\ &=\int\frac{2\sec\theta}{2\tan\theta}\,2\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta\\ &=\int2\sec^2\theta\,d\theta=2\int\sec^2\theta\,d\theta=2\tan\theta+C \end{aligned}\]

for \(u=2\sec\theta\): \(\sec\theta=\frac u2\); we have

\[\begin{aligned}\tan\theta&=\sqrt{\sec^2\theta-1}=\sqrt{\left(\frac u2\right)^2-1}\\&=\sqrt{\frac{u^2}{4}-1}=\sqrt{\frac{u^2-4}{4}}\\&=\frac{\sqrt{u^2-4}}{2}\end{aligned}\]

for \(u=e^x\) we have

\[2\left(\frac{\sqrt{u^2-4}}{2}\right)+C=\sqrt{e^x-4}+C\]
Hint 1

After \(u=e^x\), which parts of \(\frac{e^x}{\sqrt{e^{2x}-4}}\,dx\) does "\(du\)" replace? Is anything left over in the numerator?

Hint 2

Check the last line: if \(u=e^x\), what is \(u^2\)? Then differentiate the final answer and compare with the integrand.

Answer

Mistake 1 (line 2, the main error). The numerator \(e^x\) and the \(dx\) together make \(du\); nothing else is left on top. The integral should be

\[\int\frac{e^x\,dx}{\sqrt{(e^x)^2-4}}=\int\frac{du}{\sqrt{u^2-4}},\]

not \(\int\frac{u}{\sqrt{u^2-4}}\,du\). The student used \(e^x\) twice: once as \(u\) and once inside \(du\).

Mistake 2 (notation, lines 4 and 5). After writing everything in \(\theta\), the integrals still end in "\(du\)". It is corrected in line 6, but mixing variables is how differentials get forgotten.

Mistake 3 (last line). \(u^2=(e^x)^2=e^{2x}\), so \(\sqrt{u^2-4}=\sqrt{e^{2x}-4}\), not \(\sqrt{e^x-4}\).

Also missing: the \(\theta\)-range. Since \(\sqrt{e^{2x}-4}\) needs \(e^x\gt2\), we have \(u\gt2\), so \(\theta\in\left[0,\frac\pi2\right)\) and \(\sqrt{4\tan^2\theta}=2\tan\theta\) is justified.

Correct solution. With \(u=e^x\) and \(u=2\sec\theta\), \(0\le\theta\lt\frac\pi2\), \(du=2\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta\), \(\sqrt{u^2-4}=2\tan\theta\):

\[\begin{aligned}\int\frac{du}{\sqrt{u^2-4}}&=\int\frac{2\sec\theta\tan\theta}{2\tan\theta}\,d\theta=\int\sec\theta\,d\theta\\&=\ln|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|+C.\end{aligned}\]

(This uses \(\int\sec\theta\,d\theta\), from the beyond box in Section 1.) From the triangle, \(\sec\theta=\frac u2\) and \(\tan\theta=\frac{\sqrt{u^2-4}}{2}\):

\[\begin{aligned}&\ln\left|\frac{u+\sqrt{u^2-4}}{2}\right|+C\\&=\ln\left(e^x+\sqrt{e^{2x}-4}\right)-\ln2+C\\&=\ln\left(e^x+\sqrt{e^{2x}-4}\right)+C',\end{aligned}\]

where the constant \(-\ln2\) is absorbed into the new constant \(C'\). This uses only exponentials and logarithms, as the question asks.

In words: after the first substitution the integrand is 1 over the root, which becomes a plain secant; the integral of secant is a logarithm.

The student's answer \(\sqrt{e^x-4}\) has derivative \(\frac{e^x}{2\sqrt{e^x-4}}\), which is not the integrand: the "differentiate to check" rule from the Summary would have caught it.

9Group work (slide 30)

Evaluate each integral.

1. \(\displaystyle\int\sin^2x\cos^2x\,dx\)

Hint

Both powers are even. Use \(\sin x\cos x=\frac12\sin2x\) first, then power reduction on \(\sin^22x\).

Answer
\[\sin^2x\cos^2x=\tfrac14\sin^22x=\tfrac18(1-\cos4x),\] \[\int\sin^2x\cos^2x\,dx=\frac x8-\frac{\sin4x}{32}+C.\]

2. \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4x\,dx\)

Hint

Even power: \(\sin^4x=\left(\frac12(1-\cos2x)\right)^2\). Expand; the \(\cos^22x\) term needs power reduction a second time.

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}\sin^4x&=\tfrac14\left(1-2\cos2x+\cos^22x\right)\\&=\tfrac14\left(1-2\cos2x+\tfrac12(1+\cos4x)\right)\\&=\tfrac38-\tfrac12\cos2x+\tfrac18\cos4x.\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4x\,dx&=\left[\frac{3x}{8}-\frac{\sin2x}{4}+\frac{\sin4x}{32}\right]_0^{\pi/2}\\&=\frac{3\pi}{16}.\end{aligned}\]

3. \(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{x^2-6x+18}\,dx\)

Hint

Complete the square as in Example 5: \(x^2-6x+18=(x-3)^2+9\).

Answer

With \(x-3=3\tan\theta\) (or formula 4 of slide 22 with \(u=x-3\), \(a=3\)):

\[\int\frac{dx}{(x-3)^2+3^2}=\frac13\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{x-3}{3}\right)+C.\]

4. \(\displaystyle\int\frac{\cos x}{\sin x(\sin x-1)}\,dx\)

Hint

Put \(u=\sin x\) first; you get a rational function of \(u\). Then use partial fractions.

Answer

With \(u=\sin x\), \(du=\cos x\,dx\): \(\displaystyle\int\frac{du}{u(u-1)}\). Partial fractions: \(\frac{1}{u(u-1)}=-\frac1u+\frac1{u-1}\). So

\[\begin{aligned}&\int\frac{\cos x}{\sin x(\sin x-1)}\,dx\\&=\ln|\sin x-1|-\ln|\sin x|+C\\&=\ln\left|\frac{\sin x-1}{\sin x}\right|+C\\&=\ln(1-\sin x)-\ln|\sin x|+C.\end{aligned}\]

(Since \(\sin x\le1\), \(|\sin x-1|=1-\sin x\).)

5. \(\displaystyle\int\frac{4x+6}{(x+1)^2(x^2+1)}\,dx\)

Hint

Form: \(\frac{A}{x+1}+\frac{B}{(x+1)^2}+\frac{Cx+D}{x^2+1}\). Clear denominators; \(x=-1\) gives \(B\) at once; compare coefficients for the rest.

Answer

\(4x+6=A(x+1)(x^2+1)+B(x^2+1)+(Cx+D)(x+1)^2\). At \(x=-1\): \(2=2B\), so \(B=1\). Comparing \(x^3\), \(x\) and constant terms: \(A+C=0\), \(A+C+2D=4\), \(A+B+D=6\), giving \(D=2\), \(A=3\), \(C=-3\). (Check with \(x^2\): \(A+B+2C+D=3+1-6+2=0\) ✓.)

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{4x+6}{(x+1)^2(x^2+1)}&=\frac{3}{x+1}+\frac{1}{(x+1)^2}\\&\quad+\frac{-3x+2}{x^2+1}.\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}&\int\frac{4x+6}{(x+1)^2(x^2+1)}\,dx\\&=3\ln|x+1|-\frac{1}{x+1}\\&\quad-\frac32\ln(x^2+1)+2\tan^{-1}x+C.\end{aligned}\]

(The piece \(\frac{-3x}{x^2+1}\) uses formula 5 and \(\frac{2}{x^2+1}\) uses formula 3 of slide 22.)

6. \(\displaystyle\int_4^6\frac{\sqrt{x^2-4}}{x}\,dx\)

Hint

\(\sqrt{x^2-a^2}\) with \(a=2\) and \(x\ge2\): \(x=2\sec\theta\), \(0\le\theta\lt\frac\pi2\). Change the limits: \(x=4\) gives \(\sec\theta=2\), \(x=6\) gives \(\sec\theta=3\).

Answer

\(dx=2\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta\), \(\sqrt{x^2-4}=2\tan\theta\):

\[\begin{aligned}&\int\frac{2\tan\theta}{2\sec\theta}\,2\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta\\&=2\int\tan^2\theta\,d\theta=2\int(\sec^2\theta-1)\,d\theta\\&=2\tan\theta-2\theta.\end{aligned}\]

Limits: at \(x=4\), \(\theta=\frac\pi3\), \(\tan\theta=\sqrt3\); at \(x=6\), \(\theta=\sec^{-1}3=\cos^{-1}\frac13\), \(\tan\theta=\sqrt{9-1}=2\sqrt2\).

\[\begin{aligned}&\int_4^6\frac{\sqrt{x^2-4}}{x}\,dx\\&=4\sqrt2-2\sqrt3+\frac{2\pi}{3}-2\cos^{-1}\frac13\\&\approx1.8252.\end{aligned}\]

(\(\sec^{-1}\) is the inverse secant: \(\sec^{-1}y=\cos^{-1}\frac1y\).)

7. \(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{x\sqrt{x^2-9}}\,dx\)

Hint

\(x=3\sec\theta\). The integrand is defined for \(x\gt3\) and for \(x\lt-3\), so both \(\theta\)-ranges of the table matter.

Answer

For \(x\gt3\): \(\theta\in\left[0,\frac\pi2\right)\), \(dx=3\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta\), \(\sqrt{x^2-9}=3\tan\theta\):

\[\begin{aligned}\int\frac{3\sec\theta\tan\theta}{3\sec\theta\cdot3\tan\theta}\,d\theta&=\frac\theta3+C\\&=\frac13\sec^{-1}\frac x3+C.\end{aligned}\]

For \(x\lt-3\): \(\theta\in\left(\frac\pi2,\pi\right]\), \(\sqrt{x^2-9}=-3\tan\theta\), giving \(-\frac13\sec^{-1}\frac x3+C\). Both cases together:

\[\int\frac{dx}{x\sqrt{x^2-9}}=\frac13\sec^{-1}\frac{|x|}{3}+C.\]

8. \(\displaystyle\int\frac{x^3}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx\)

Hint

\(x=\sin\theta\) gives \(\int\sin^3\theta\,d\theta\): an odd power, so peel off one \(\sin\theta\). (The substitution \(u=1-x^2\) also works.)

Answer

With \(x=\sin\theta\), \(dx=\cos\theta\,d\theta\), \(\sqrt{1-x^2}=\cos\theta\): \(\int\sin^3\theta\,d\theta=-\cos\theta+\frac{\cos^3\theta}{3}+C\), and \(\cos\theta=\sqrt{1-x^2}\):

\[\begin{aligned}&\int\frac{x^3}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx\\&=-\sqrt{1-x^2}+\frac{(1-x^2)^{3/2}}{3}+C\\&=-\frac{(x^2+2)\sqrt{1-x^2}}{3}+C.\end{aligned}\]

9. \(\displaystyle\int\frac{e^{2x}}{e^{2x}+3e^x+2}\,dx\)

Hint

\(u=e^x\), \(du=e^x\,dx\); one factor \(e^x\) of \(e^{2x}\) goes into \(du\). The bottom factorises as \((u+1)(u+2)\).

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}&\int\frac{u}{(u+1)(u+2)}\,du\\&=\int\left(-\frac{1}{u+1}+\frac{2}{u+2}\right)du,\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}&\int\frac{e^{2x}}{e^{2x}+3e^x+2}\,dx\\&=2\ln(e^x+2)-\ln(e^x+1)+C.\end{aligned}\]

(No absolute values needed: \(e^x+1\) and \(e^x+2\) are positive.)

10. \(\displaystyle\int\frac{x^3+2}{x^2-4}\,dx\)

Hint

Improper: divide first. \(x^3+2=x(x^2-4)+(4x+2)\).

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}\frac{x^3+2}{x^2-4}&=x+\frac{4x+2}{(x-2)(x+2)}\\&=x+\frac{5/2}{x-2}+\frac{3/2}{x+2},\end{aligned}\]

(by values: \(x=2\) gives \(10=4A\); \(x=-2\) gives \(-6=-4B\)), so

\[\begin{aligned}&\int\frac{x^3+2}{x^2-4}\,dx\\&=\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac52\ln|x-2|+\frac32\ln|x+2|+C.\end{aligned}\]

10Concept checks Written for these notes

1. Which row of the sine-cosine strategy table applies to \(\int\sin^3x\cos^4x\,dx\), and what is the answer?

Answer

\(m=3\) is odd: keep one \(\sin x\), write \(\sin^2x=1-\cos^2x\), and put \(u=\cos x\): \(-\int(1-u^2)u^4\,du\), giving

\[\int\sin^3x\cos^4x\,dx=-\frac{\cos^5x}{5}+\frac{\cos^7x}{7}+C.\]

2. Which substitution suits \(\displaystyle\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{9+4x^2}}\)?

Answer

\(9+4x^2=3^2+(2x)^2\) has the form \(a^2+u^2\) with \(u=2x\), so \(2x=3\tan\theta\), i.e. \(x=\frac32\tan\theta\). It leads to \(\frac12\int\sec\theta\,d\theta\) and

\[\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{9+4x^2}}=\frac12\ln\left(2x+\sqrt{9+4x^2}\right)+C.\]

3. Is \(\dfrac{x^3+1}{x^3-x}\) proper? Decompose it.

Answer

No: the degrees are equal (3 and 3), so divide first: \(\frac{x^3+1}{x^3-x}=1+\frac{x+1}{x^3-x}\). Since \(x^3-x=x(x-1)(x+1)\), the factor \(x+1\) cancels: \(\frac{x+1}{x^3-x}=\frac{1}{x(x-1)}\). So

\[\frac{x^3+1}{x^3-x}=1-\frac1x+\frac{1}{x-1}.\]

4. Write the PFD form of \(\dfrac{1}{x^2(x^2+4)}\), then find the constants.

Answer

\(x^2\) is the repeated linear factor \(x\) (power 2), and \(x^2+4\) is an irreducible quadratic:

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{1}{x^2(x^2+4)}&=\frac Ax+\frac B{x^2}+\frac{Cx+D}{x^2+4}\\&=\frac{1/4}{x^2}-\frac{1/4}{x^2+4}\end{aligned}\] \[(A=0,\ B=\tfrac14,\ C=0,\ D=-\tfrac14).\]

5. Do you need a trigonometric substitution for \(\int x\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx\)?

Answer

No. The factor \(x\) is (up to a constant) the derivative of \(1-x^2\), so the ordinary substitution \(u=1-x^2\) gives \(-\frac13(1-x^2)^{3/2}+C\) at once. Check for an ordinary substitution before reaching for a trig one.

6. Why does the substitution \(x=a\sin\theta\) restrict \(\theta\) to \(-\frac\pi2\le\theta\le\frac\pi2\)?

Answer

Two reasons. On this interval \(\sin\theta\) is one-to-one, so each \(x\in[-a,a]\) gives exactly one \(\theta=\sin^{-1}\frac xa\) for converting back. And \(\cos\theta\ge0\) there, so \(\sqrt{a^2-x^2}=a|\cos\theta|=a\cos\theta\) with no sign trouble.
