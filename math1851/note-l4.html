Continuity

0Where this lecture sits

Lectures 1 to 3 were about limits. This lecture uses them to make precise a picture you already know: a graph you can draw without lifting the pen, with no breaks, holes or jumps. That idea is called continuity.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 Continuous at a point means the pen passes through without lifting. Three checks: a dot exists, the graph heads to one height, the dot sits at that height.
  2. §2 On an interval: no lifting inside, and each end dot is reached from inside.
  3. §3 Four ways to lift the pen: a hole, a jump, a blow-up to infinity, an endless wiggle.
  4. §4 A single missing dot can be drawn in at the limit. That is the continuous extension.
  5. §5 Combine unbroken graphs and you get an unbroken graph (no dividing by zero). For pieces, check the joins.
  6. §6 A continuous function of a continuous function is continuous.
  7. §7 Move a limit inside a continuous outer function: direct substitution.
  8. §8 IVT: a hiker going from below sea level to above must cross sea level, if the path has no jumps.

You need: limits and one-sided limits (Lecture 1, recalled below), the Sandwich Theorem and the key limit \(\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1\) (Lecture 2), and limits involving infinity, i.e. vertical and horizontal asymptotes (Lecture 3). In the exam: 22 question parts in 23 papers (2013 to 2026): limits and continuity read off a graph (8), the IVT (7), constants that make a piecewise function continuous (5), classifying a discontinuity (2). See the L4 exam guide.

Recall from Lecture 1 (slide 2) · Limit, intuitive definition

Suppose \(f(x)\) is defined on an open interval about \(x_0\) (an interval \((p,q)\) with \(x_0\) strictly inside it; \(f\) may or may not be defined at \(x_0\) itself). We write

\[\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=L\]

and say "the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(x_0\) is \(L\)" if we can make the values of \(f(x)\) arbitrarily close to \(L\) for all \(x\) sufficiently close to \(x_0\) but not equal to \(x_0\).

In words: the limit is the height the graph is heading to as you slide towards \(x_0\); what happens exactly at \(x_0\) does not matter.

The definition can be modified for one-sided limits (\(x\to x_0^-\): approach from the left, using only \(x\lt x_0\); \(x\to x_0^+\): approach from the right, using only \(x\gt x_0\)) and for limits at infinity (\(x\to\pm\infty\), Lecture 3). The two-sided limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and are equal.

1Continuity at a point ~5 min

What is this?

Imagine drawing a graph with a pen, from left to right. A continuous graph is one you can draw in a single stroke, without ever lifting the pen off the paper. You would have to lift it at a hole (a missing dot), at a jump, where the curve shoots off to infinity, or where it wiggles so fast you could never get through. Continuity at a point asks a smaller question: can the pen pass through this one point without lifting?

Continuous curves (slide 7)

Slide 7 shows three smooth curves \(f\), \(g\), \(h\) drawn on the same axes: \(f\) wiggles up and down, \(g\) is a single hump, and \(h\) is an S-shaped curve that rises and levels off. What they share: each one can be drawn without lifting the pen off the paper. That is the picture to keep in mind for "continuous". The slide's picture is redrawn below (the exact formulas are not given on the slide; the ones used here only reproduce the shapes).

Three continuous curves (slide 7 redrawn)

f (wiggly)g (hump)h (S-shape)

No breaks, no holes, no jumps, no vertical asymptotes: each curve is one unbroken stroke.

"Without lifting the pen" is a good picture but not a definition you can calculate with. The definition uses limits.

What is this? (continuity at a point)

Watch the pen as it closes in on \(x=c\) from both sides. The limit is the height the pen is heading to. The value \(f(c)\) is where the dot actually is. Continuous at \(c\) means the pen arrives exactly on the dot, so it can carry straight on without lifting.

Definition · Continuity at a point (slide 8)

A function \(f(x)\) is continuous at an interior point \(c\) if

\[\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=f(c).\]

In words: the height the graph is heading to near \(c\) = the height the graph actually has at \(c\).

Reading the formula
\(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\)
the height the pen is heading to as it closes in on \(c\), from the left and from the right.
\(f(c)\)
where the dot at \(x=c\) actually sits.
\(=\)
the pen lands exactly on the dot, so no lift is needed.

If \(f(x)\) does not satisfy this condition at \(c\), then \(f\) is discontinuous at \(x=c\), and \(c\) is called a point of discontinuity.

An interior point of the domain is a point \(c\) with some room on both sides: \(f\) is defined on a whole open interval around \(c\) (except possibly at \(c\)). Points at the edge of the domain (endpoints) are handled with one-sided limits in Section 2.

Theorem · Continuity Test (slide 8)

A function \(f(x)\) is continuous at \(x=c\) if and only if (meaning: exactly when; each side guarantees the other) it meets all three conditions:

  1. Do: check that \(f(c)\) is defined; The pen needs a dot to pass through at \(x=c\). With no dot there is a hole, and you would have to lift the pen over it.
  2. Do: check that \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\) exists; From the left and from the right the pen must head to one single height. Otherwise it has to jump, shoot off to infinity, or never settle.
  3. Do: check that \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=f(c)\). The dot must sit exactly where the pen arrives. A dot placed above or below that height means lifting the pen to reach it.

In words: (i) there is a dot on the graph at \(x=c\); (ii) the graph heads to one single height from both sides; (iii) the dot sits exactly at that height.

The test is how you prove continuity or discontinuity at a point: check the three conditions in order and stop at the first one that fails. Each kind of failure gives a different picture, which is what the "types of discontinuity" in Section 3 describe.

Quick illustration

Let \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1}\). At \(c=1\): \(f(1)=\frac00\) is not defined, so condition (i) fails and \(f\) is discontinuous at \(1\), even though \(\lim_{x\to1}f(x)=\lim_{x\to1}(x+1)=2\) exists. The graph is the line \(y=x+1\) with one dot missing at \((1,2)\): the pen heads to height 2 but finds no dot there. At \(c=3\): \(f(3)=4\), \(\lim_{x\to3}f(x)=4\), equal, so \(f\) is continuous at \(3\).

You can now: state the definition of continuity at a point; run the three-condition Continuity Test to prove a function is continuous or discontinuous at a point; and say which condition fails.

2Continuity on an interval ~8 min

What is this?

On an interval you only draw the part of the graph above that stretch of the \(x\)-axis. Inside the stretch the pen must never lift. At an end, the pen can only come from one side (from inside the interval), so we only ask that it arrives on the end dot from that side. Whatever the graph does outside the interval does not count.

At an endpoint of an interval you can only approach from one side, so the definition uses one-sided limits there.

Definition · Continuity on a closed interval (slide 9)

A function \(f(x)\) is continuous on the interval \([a,b]\) if it meets the following three conditions.

  1. Continuity at every \(c\in(a,b)\) (every point strictly between \(a\) and \(b\)): \[\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=f(c)\]
  2. Continuity at the left endpoint \(a\) (approach from the right only): \[\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=f(a)\]
  3. Continuity at the right endpoint \(b\) (approach from the left only): \[\lim_{x\to b^-}f(x)=f(b)\]

In words: no breaks inside the interval, and at each end the graph runs right up to the dot that is there, coming from inside the interval.

The symbol \(\in\) means "is an element of" (belongs to). A point where only one of the one-sided conditions holds is called right-continuous (\(\lim_{x\to c^+}f(x)=f(c)\)) or left-continuous (\(\lim_{x\to c^-}f(x)=f(c)\)). The same idea applies to half-open intervals such as \([a,b)\): check the interior points and the included endpoint only.

What is this? (a continuous function)

Here the pen picture needs care. The graph of \(1/x\) is drawn in two separate strokes, yet \(1/x\) counts as a "continuous function". The reason: the convention only checks points where the function exists, and at every one of those the pen passes through without lifting. The gap at \(0\) is not in the domain, so it is never checked.

Convention (slide 9)

A continuous function is continuous on its domain. That is, when we simply call \(f\) "a continuous function", we mean it is continuous at every point where it is defined (using one-sided limits at any endpoints of the domain). The domain is the set of all \(x\) for which \(f(x)\) is defined.

In words: "continuous function" is judged only where the function exists. For example, \(1/x\) is a continuous function (it has no break anywhere on its domain), even though it is discontinuous at \(x=0\), where it is not defined.

Example 1 · Solution

(a) Domain. \(f\) is defined from \(x=-2\) (filled dot, included) up to but not including \(x=2\) (open circle), except at \(x=-1\) (open circle with no filled dot above or below it) and \(x=0\) (no point at all). At \(x=1\) there is a filled dot at height 2, so \(f(1)=2\) is defined.

\[\text{Domain}=[-2,-1)\cup(-1,0)\cup(0,2)\]

In words: every \(x\) from \(-2\) up to \(2\), including \(-2\), excluding \(-1\), \(0\) and \(2\). (\(\cup\) means "union": put the pieces together.)

(b) Intervals of continuity. The graph is unbroken on each of these pieces:

\[[-2,-1),\quad(-1,0),\quad(0,1),\quad[1,2)\]

At \(x=-2\) the curve runs into the filled dot from the right, so \(\lim_{x\to-2^+}f(x)=f(-2)\) (right-continuous); that is why \(-2\) is included. At \(x=1\), \(\lim_{x\to1^+}f(x)=2=f(1)\), so \(f\) is right-continuous at \(1\) and \([1,2)\) includes \(1\); but \(\lim_{x\to1^-}f(x)=1\ne f(1)\), so the piece to the left stops at \((0,1)\), open at \(1\).

(c) Points of discontinuity: \(x=-1\), \(x=0\), \(x=1\). These are the places inside the domain's span where the pen must lift; each fails a different condition of the Continuity Test.

  • \(x=-1\): \(f(-1)\) is not defined (test condition (i) fails), although the limit exists (both sides head to the hole at about \(-0.8\)). A removable discontinuity (Section 3).
  • \(x=0\): \(f(0)\) is not defined; from the left the graph oscillates (no limit), from the right \(f(x)\to+\infty\). Oscillating on the left, infinite on the right.
  • \(x=1\): \(f(1)=2\) is defined, but \(\lim_{x\to1^-}f(x)=1\) and \(\lim_{x\to1^+}f(x)=2\) differ, so \(\lim_{x\to1}f(x)\) does not exist (condition (ii) fails). A jump discontinuity.

About \(x=2\): the graph simply ends there (there is nothing to the right of \(x=2\)), so \(x=2\) is an end of the picture rather than a break inside it, and the usual answer does not list it. Since \(f(2)\) is undefined, some readers would also call \(f\) discontinuous at \(x=2\); if you include it, say why. The slide gives no printed answer, so this is the reading used here.

You can now: check continuity on a closed or half-open interval with one-sided limits at the ends; recognise right- and left-continuity; and read the domain, the intervals of continuity and the points of discontinuity off a graph.

3Types of discontinuity (slide 11) ~3 min

What is this?

There are four ways the pen can be forced off the paper at a point. One dot is missing or put in the wrong place (removable). The graph steps to a different height (jump). The graph shoots off to infinity (infinite). The graph wiggles faster and faster and never settles (oscillating). Only the first can be repaired by adding or moving a single dot.

Slide 11 shows four kinds of discontinuity, all at \(x=0\). Each matches a different failure in the Continuity Test. Use the selector to step through them; the readout runs the test.

The four types of discontinuity at x = 0 (slide 11 redrawn)

Open circle = hole (not on the graph). Filled dot = the actual value f(0).

Removable\(\lim_{x\to0}f(x)\) exists, but either \(f(0)\) is undefined (slide's left picture: the line \(y=x+1\) with a hole at \((0,1)\)) or \(f(0)\) is defined with the wrong value (slide's second picture: the hole at \((0,1)\) and a stray dot at \((0,2)\)). It is "removable" because redefining the single value \(f(0)\) as the limit (here \(1\)) repairs it.
JumpBoth one-sided limits exist but are different, so the graph jumps. Slide's picture: \(f(x)=0\) for \(x\lt0\) and \(f(x)=1\) for \(x\ge0\): \(\lim_{x\to0^-}f(x)=0\), \(\lim_{x\to0^+}f(x)=1=f(0)\). No single value of \(f(0)\) can fix it.
InfiniteThe function blows up to \(\pm\infty\) at the point (a vertical asymptote, Lecture 3). Slide's picture: \(f(x)=\dfrac{1}{x^2}\), with \(\lim_{x\to0}f(x)=+\infty\).
OscillatingThe function wiggles between values infinitely often near the point and never settles, so no limit exists. Slide's picture: \(y=\sin\dfrac1x\), which swings between \(-1\) and \(1\) faster and faster as \(x\to0\).
Trap

"Removable" does not mean the function is continuous. It is still discontinuous at that point; it just could be made continuous by changing or adding one value. Only removable discontinuities can be fixed this way; jump, infinite and oscillating ones cannot.

Beyond the handouts

Jump, infinite and oscillating discontinuities are sometimes grouped together as non-removable (or "essential" in some books). Also, a function can mix types at one point, as in Example 1 at \(x=0\) (oscillating from the left, infinite from the right). Exams ask you to name the type, so describe each side if they differ.

You can now: name the type of a discontinuity (removable, jump, infinite, oscillating) from a graph or from one-sided limits; describe each side when they differ; and explain why only a removable one can be fixed.

4Continuous extension (slide 12) ~3 min

What is this?

A removable discontinuity is a graph with one dot missing. The continuous extension draws that dot in exactly where the pen was heading (the limit) and changes nothing else. If the pen was not heading to one height (a jump, a blow-up, a wiggle), there is no right place to put the dot, so no extension exists.

Definition · Continuous extension (slide 12)

Suppose \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\) exists, but \(f(c)\) is not defined. The continuous extension of \(f\) to \(x=c\) is the function

\[F(x)=\begin{cases}f(x), & \text{if } x \text{ is in the domain of } f\\ L, & \text{if } x=c\end{cases}\]

In words: keep \(f\) exactly as it is everywhere it already exists, and plug the hole at \(c\) with the limit value \(L\).

Reading the formula
\(F\)
the repaired function: same graph, one more dot.
\(f(x)\) in the domain of \(f\)
everywhere the graph already exists, leave it alone.
\(L\) at \(x=c\)
draw the missing dot at the height the pen was heading to, so the pen now lands on it.

\(F\) is then continuous at \(c\) because \(\lim_{x\to c}F(x)=\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L=F(c)\). "Extension" means the domain is extended (made bigger) by one point.

Example 2 (slide 12)

Find the continuous extension of \(f(x)=\dfrac{\sin x}{x}\).

Solution. \(f\) is defined for every \(x\ne0\); at \(x=0\) it gives \(\frac00\), undefined. From Lecture 2 (the Sandwich Theorem result),

\[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1.\]

So the limit exists and \(f\) has a removable discontinuity at \(0\). The continuous extension is

\[F(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\sin x}{x}, & x\ne0\\[4pt] 1, & x=0\end{cases}\]

In words: \(F\) = \(\frac{\sin x}{x}\) wherever that makes sense, and \(1\) at \(x=0\).

Plugging the hole in sin x / x

The two graphs are identical except at the single point x = 0.

Not every hole can be plugged: if the limit at \(c\) does not exist (jump, infinite, oscillating), there is no continuous extension to \(c\). Group Work 1 (Section 12) asks about this.

You can now: find the continuous extension of a function with a removable discontinuity, written as a two-line piecewise function; and explain why there is none at a jump, infinite or oscillating discontinuity.

5Properties of continuous functions ~5 min

What is this?

If two graphs can each be drawn through \(x=c\) without lifting the pen, then their sum, difference, product or a multiple can be too: a small change in \(x\) gives small changes in each, and so a small change in the result. Dividing also works, as long as the bottom is not zero at \(c\); near a zero bottom the result can blow up. This is how you see at a glance that a formula built from familiar pieces is continuous.

Theorem · Combinations (slide 13)

If functions \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous at \(x=c\), then the following combinations are continuous at \(x=c\).

#CombinationFormulaCondition
1Sums\(f+g\)
2Differences\(f-g\)
3Constant multiples\(kf\)\(k\in\mathbb R\) (any real constant)
4Products\(fg\)
5Quotients\(\dfrac fg\)\(g(c)\ne0\)
6Powers\(f^n\)\(n\in\mathbb Z^+\) (positive integer)
7Roots\(\sqrt[n]{f}\)\(n\in\mathbb Z^+\)

In words: add, subtract, scale, multiply, divide (by something non-zero at \(c\)), raise to a power or take a root of continuous functions, and you get a continuous function.

Why they hold: each one follows from the matching limit law of Lecture 1. For example, for sums, \(\lim_{x\to c}(f+g)=\lim_{x\to c}f+\lim_{x\to c}g=f(c)+g(c)=(f+g)(c)\). The condition \(g(c)\ne0\) for quotients is needed because \(\frac{f(c)}{0}\) is not defined.

Trap · roots

For an even root (\(n=2,4,\dots\)) the root must be defined near \(c\), so you need \(f(c)\gt0\) (if \(f(c)=0\) you only get one-sided continuity, on the side where \(f\ge0\)). For example \(\sqrt{x}\) is continuous on \([0,\infty)\), right-continuous at \(0\). Odd roots such as \(\sqrt[3]{x}\) work for negative values too.

Theorem · Standard continuous functions (slide 14)

The following types of functions are continuous on their domains.

  1. Polynomial functions, e.g. \(3x^4-2x+7\): domain all real numbers, so continuous everywhere.
  2. Rational functions (one polynomial divided by another), e.g. \(\dfrac{x+1}{x^2-4}\): continuous wherever the denominator is not zero.
  3. Root functions, e.g. \(\sqrt{x}\), \(\sqrt[3]{x}\): continuous where defined.
  4. Trigonometric functions: \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) everywhere; \(\tan x\) and \(\sec x\) except at odd multiples of \(\frac\pi2\); \(\cot x\) and \(\csc x\) except at multiples of \(\pi\).

In words: the familiar functions have no breaks anywhere they are defined; their only discontinuities are where they are undefined.

The slide notes that more function types (exponential, logarithmic and inverse trigonometric functions) are added to this list in a later lecture (Lecture 7).

Together, the two theorems let you see continuity at a glance: build the function out of standard pieces, and the only places to check are where a denominator is zero, a root is of a negative number, or (for piecewise functions) where the formula changes.

What is this? (joining pieces)

A piecewise function is drawn with one formula on one part of the line and another formula on the next part. Each piece is unbroken on its own, so the only place you might have to lift the pen is the join. Continuity there means the first piece ends exactly where the second piece starts, and the dot at the join is at that same height.

Example 3 (slide 15)

For what value of \(b\) is

\[f(x)=\begin{cases}x, & \text{if } x\lt -2\\ bx^2, & \text{if } x\ge -2\end{cases}\]

continuous for every \(x\)?

Solution. A piecewise function uses different formulas on different parts of the number line. For \(x\lt-2\), \(f(x)=x\) is a polynomial, so continuous. For \(x\gt-2\), \(f(x)=bx^2\) is a polynomial, so continuous. The only point to check is the join \(x=-2\).

  • \(f(-2)=b(-2)^2=4b\) (the second formula applies since \(-2\ge-2\)). The dot at the join belongs to the piece whose condition includes \(-2\).
  • Left limit: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-2^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to-2^-}x=-2\). Arriving from the left, the pen is drawing the first piece, \(y=x\), which is a polynomial, so just substitute.
  • Right limit: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-2^+}f(x)=\lim_{x\to-2^+}bx^2=4b\).

For the limit to exist and equal \(f(-2)\) we need

\[4b=-2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b=-\frac12.\]

In words: the height where the left piece ends = the height where the right piece starts.

Example 3: slide b until the pieces join

y = x (x < −2)y = bx² (x ≥ −2)

The open circle is where the left piece ends; the filled dot is f(−2) = 4b. They meet only at b = −1/2.

You can now: use the combination rules (with \(g(c)\ne0\) for quotients and the even-root condition) and the list of standard functions to say where a formula is continuous; and find a constant that makes a piecewise function continuous at its join.

6Composites (slide 16) ~3 min

What is this?

A composite feeds the output of one function into another. A small nudge to \(x\) gives a small nudge to \(f(x)\). Because \(g\) is unbroken at that output, a small nudge to its input gives a small nudge to its output. So the pen tracing \(g(f(x))\) never has to lift either.

A composite function applies one function and then another. \(g\circ f\) (read "\(g\) of \(f\)" or "\(g\) composed with \(f\)") means \((g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x))\): first apply \(f\), then feed the result into \(g\). For example if \(f(x)=x^2+1\) and \(g(u)=\sqrt u\), then \(g(f(x))=\sqrt{x^2+1}\).

Theorem · Composite of continuous functions (slide 16)

If \(f\) is continuous at \(c\) and \(g\) is continuous at \(f(c)\), then the composite \(g\circ f\) is continuous at \(c\).

In words: a continuous function of a continuous function is a continuous function. Note \(g\) must be continuous at the output \(f(c)\), not at \(c\).

Analogy: \(f\) is a dimmer dial that turns smoothly, and \(g\) converts the dial reading to brightness smoothly. Small turns of the knob give small changes of reading, which give small changes of brightness, so the whole chain is smooth.

Example 4 (slide 16)

Find the interval for which \(y=\sqrt{\dfrac{x-1}{x+2}}\) is continuous.

Solution. This is a composite: inside \(f(x)=\dfrac{x-1}{x+2}\) (rational, continuous for \(x\ne-2\)), outside \(g(u)=\sqrt u\) (continuous for \(u\ge0\), one-sided at \(u=0\)). So \(y\) is continuous wherever it is defined, which is where

\[\frac{x-1}{x+2}\ge0\quad\text{and}\quad x\ne-2.\]

Sign table: the numerator is zero at \(x=1\), the denominator at \(x=-2\). The outer square root only accepts inputs \(\ge0\), so we need to know where the inside is positive, zero or negative.

In words: every \(x\) less than \(-2\), together with every \(x\) from \(1\) upwards. At \(x=1\) the function is only right-continuous (it is not defined just to the left of \(1\)), which is exactly continuity at a left endpoint.

7Limits of continuous functions (slide 17) ~2 min

What is this?

If the inside heads to some value \(b\), and the outer function's graph is unbroken at \(b\), then the outer function heads to its own value at \(b\). The inside may even have a hole at \(c\): that does not matter, because a limit never looks at \(c\) itself, only at where the pen is heading.

Theorem · Limits of continuous functions (slide 17)

If \(g\) is continuous at \(b\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=b\), then

\[\lim_{x\to c}g(f(x))=g\Big(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\Big)=g(b).\]

In words: limit of (continuous function of something) = continuous function of (limit of that something). You may move the limit inside a continuous outer function.

Reading the formula
\(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=b\)
the inside heads to \(b\); its own value at \(c\) does not matter.
\(g\) continuous at \(b\)
the outer graph is unbroken at \(b\), so inputs near \(b\) give outputs near \(g(b)\).
\(g\Big(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\Big)\)
first find where the inside heads, then plug that into \(g\).

This is stronger than Section 6: \(f\) itself need not be continuous at \(c\) (it need not even be defined there); only its limit \(b\) matters, and \(g\) must be continuous at \(b\). Together with Section 5 it justifies the slogan "limits of continuous functions can be evaluated by direct substitution": if \(h\) is continuous at \(c\), then \(\lim_{x\to c}h(x)=h(c)\), so just plug in.

Example 5 (slide 17)

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\cos\Big(\frac{\sin x}{x}\Big)\).

Solution. Inside: \(f(x)=\dfrac{\sin x}{x}\) is not defined at \(0\), but \(\lim_{x\to0}\dfrac{\sin x}{x}=1\) (Lecture 2). Outside: \(g(u)=\cos u\) is continuous everywhere, in particular at \(b=1\). By the theorem,

\[\begin{aligned}\lim_{x\to0}\cos\Big(\frac{\sin x}{x}\Big)&=\cos\Big(\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}\Big)\\&=\cos 1\approx0.5403.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the inside heads to 1, and cosine is smooth at 1, so the whole thing heads to cos 1 (1 radian, not 1 degree).

You can now: move a limit inside a continuous outer function, after checking the outer function is continuous at the inside's limit; and evaluate limits of continuous functions by direct substitution.

8The Intermediate Value Theorem (slides 18–19) ~6 min

What is this?

A hiker sets off from a point below sea level and finishes at a point above sea level. However her path twists, at some moment she was exactly at sea level, provided the path has no jumps: she cannot teleport over the shoreline. Read \(x\) as time and \(f(x)\) as her altitude. Continuity is the "no jumps" condition, and the closed interval \([a,b]\) means she really stands at the start and at the finish. Sea level is height 0, which is why the IVT finds roots.

Theorem · Intermediate Value Theorem, IVT (slide 18)

A function \(y=f(x)\) that is continuous on a closed interval \([a,b]\) takes on every value between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\). In other words, if \(y_0\) is any value between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\), then there exists \(c\in[a,b]\) such that

\[f(c)=y_0.\]

In words: if an unbroken graph starts at height \(f(a)\) and ends at height \(f(b)\), it must pass through every height in between at least once.

Reading the formula
\(f\) continuous on \([a,b]\)
the hike has no jumps, from the start \(a\) to the finish \(b\), both included.
\(y_0\) between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\)
any altitude between the starting and finishing heights.
\(c\in[a,b]\)
some moment during the hike. The theorem does not say when, and it may happen more than once.
\(f(c)=y_0\)
at that moment she is at exactly that altitude.

Analogy: you hike from a village at 200 m altitude to a peak at 800 m. However winding the path, at some moment you were at exactly 500 m, because you cannot teleport. The IVT says the same for graphs, and continuity is the "no teleporting" condition. Three things to notice:

  • Both conditions matter: continuous and on a closed interval \([a,b]\). Drop continuity and the graph can jump over \(y_0\) (try the jump case in the figure).
  • The IVT says a \(c\) exists; it does not say where, and there may be several.
  • It says nothing about values outside the range from \(f(a)\) to \(f(b)\): the graph may or may not reach those.

IVT: pick a target height N

y = f(x)y = Npoints with f(c) = N

For the continuous function, every N between f(0) and f(4) is hit at least once (sometimes three times). Heights above f(4) may still be hit, but the IVT does not promise it. For the function with a jump, heights inside the gap are skipped.

The most common use is finding roots. A root (or zero) of \(f\) is a value \(c\) with \(f(c)=0\), i.e. where the graph meets the \(x\)-axis. If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(f(a)\), \(f(b)\) have opposite signs, then \(0\) lies between them, so the IVT gives a root \(c\) in \([a,b]\) (in fact in \((a,b)\), since \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\) are not zero).

Example 6 (slide 19)

Show that \(f(x)=\sqrt[3]{x}+x-1\) has a root in \([0,1]\).

Solution.

  1. Do: Continuity. \(\sqrt[3]{x}\) is a root function and \(x-1\) is a polynomial; both are continuous for all real \(x\), so their sum \(f\) is continuous on \([0,1]\) (slides 13–14). This is the "no jumps" condition. Without it the graph could jump over zero, as \(|x|/x\) does in Common Mistake 3.
  2. Do: End values. \(f(0)=0+0-1=-1\lt0\) and \(f(1)=1+1-1=1\gt0\). The hike starts below sea level and finishes above it.
  3. Do: Conclude. \(0\) lies between \(f(0)=-1\) and \(f(1)=1\). By the Intermediate Value Theorem there exists \(c\in[0,1]\) with \(f(c)=0\), i.e. \(f\) has a root in \([0,1]\). Height 0 lies between the start and finish heights, so the IVT promises a moment at sea level. Name the theorem and its conditions: Section 11 shows an answer that loses marks for skipping continuity.

In words: continuous, negative at one end, positive at the other, so it crosses zero in between.

Beyond the handouts · Bisection: actually finding the root

The IVT only proves a root exists. Repeating it pins the root down: check the sign at the midpoint, keep the half where the sign still changes, and repeat. Each step halves the interval. For Example 6 this homes in on \(c\approx0.3177\) (checked numerically). Drag the slider to watch.

9Summary (slide 20)

  • "The graph of a continuous function can be drawn without removing a pen from the paper." Precisely: \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=f(c)\) at every point (one-sided at endpoints).
  • Types of discontinuity include removable, jump, infinite and oscillating.
  • "A continuous function of a continuous function is a continuous function" (composites).
  • Limits of continuous functions can be evaluated by direct substitution.
  • The IVT asserts that a closed interval on which a continuous function changes sign contains a root.

10Common mistakes (slide 22)

1. Assuming that if \(f\) is discontinuous at \(x=c\), then \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\) does not exist

Slide's example: suppose

\[f(x)=\begin{cases}1 & \text{if } x\ne0\\ 0 & \text{if } x=0.\end{cases}\]
✗"Since \(f(x)\) is discontinuous at \(x=0\), \(\lim_{x\to0}f(x)\) does not exist." ✓Near \(0\) (but not at \(0\)) every value is \(1\), so \(\lim_{x\to0}f(x)=1\) exists. The discontinuity comes from condition (iii) of the Continuity Test: the limit \(1\ne f(0)=0\). This is a removable discontinuity. Discontinuity means at least one of the three conditions fails; it need not be the limit one.

2. Assuming algebraic operations preserve discontinuities

✗"Since \(f(x)=\frac1x\) and \(g(x)=-\frac1x\) are discontinuous at \(x=0\), \(f+g\) is also discontinuous at \(x=0\)" (meaning: it inherits their infinite discontinuity). ✓For every \(x\ne0\), \(f(x)+g(x)=\frac1x-\frac1x=0\). The two blow-ups cancel, so \(\lim_{x\to0}(f+g)(x)=0\) exists and \(f+g\) extends continuously to the zero function. The theorem in Section 5 only goes one way: continuous + continuous = continuous. It says nothing about discontinuous + discontinuous, which can be continuous (or have a removable discontinuity only).

Being strict: \(f+g\) is still undefined at \(0\) (because \(f\) and \(g\) are), so by the Continuity Test it is technically not continuous there; but its discontinuity is now only removable, not infinite. A cleaner example with no such fine print is given next.

Example Written for these notes

Let \(H(x)=0\) for \(x\lt0\) and \(H(x)=1\) for \(x\ge0\) (the jump from slide 11), and \(K(x)=1-H(x)\). Both have a jump discontinuity at \(0\), but \(H(x)+K(x)=1\) for every \(x\), which is continuous everywhere.

3. Applying the IVT when its conditions are not satisfied

✗"\(f(x)=\dfrac{|x|}{x}\) has a root in \([-1,1]\)" (because \(f(-1)=-1\lt0\) and \(f(1)=1\gt0\)). ✓\(\dfrac{|x|}{x}=-1\) for \(x\lt0\) and \(1\) for \(x\gt0\), and it is undefined at \(0\). It is not continuous on \([-1,1]\), so the IVT does not apply. In fact it never equals \(0\): no root at all. Always check continuity on the whole closed interval first.

|x|/x changes sign without a root

The graph jumps from −1 to 1 at x = 0 without ever touching the x-axis.

Two more Written for these notes

✗Checking a piecewise function only by plugging the join point into both formulas, without asking which formula defines \(f\) at that point. ✓You need left limit = right limit = \(f(c)\), where \(f(c)\) comes from the piece whose condition includes \(c\) (the one with \(\le\) or \(\ge\)). ✗Concluding "no root" when \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\) have the same sign. ✓The IVT gives no information then; there may still be roots (see Group Work 4).

12Group work (slide 23)

1. Find the continuous extension of \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^3+2x^2-1}{5-3x}\).

Hint

Where is \(f\) undefined? Does the limit exist there? (Plug that point into the numerator.)

Answer

\(f\) is a rational function, continuous everywhere except where \(5-3x=0\), i.e. \(x=\frac53\). At that point the numerator is

\[\Big(\tfrac53\Big)^3+2\Big(\tfrac53\Big)^2-1=\frac{248}{27}\ne0,\]

so near \(x=\frac53\) the function behaves like (non-zero)/(tiny): \(\lim_{x\to5/3^-}f(x)=+\infty\) and \(\lim_{x\to5/3^+}f(x)=-\infty\). This is an infinite discontinuity; the limit does not exist, so no continuous extension to \(x=\frac53\) exists. \(f\) is already continuous on its whole domain, so as printed, there is nothing to extend: the "extension" is \(f\) itself.

Possible typo on the slide: the numerator factorises as \((x+1)(x^2+x-1)\), which suggests the intended denominator may have contained a factor \(x+1\). For example, if the function were \(\frac{x^3+2x^2-1}{x+1}\), its continuous extension would be \(F(x)=x^2+x-1\) for all \(x\) (so \(F(-1)=-1\)). The answer above is for the question as printed.

2. For what values of \(a\) and \(b\) is

\[f(x)=\begin{cases}ax+2b, & \text{if } x\le0\\ x^2+3a-b, & \text{if } 0\lt x\le2\\ 3x-5, & \text{if } x\gt2\end{cases}\]

continuous for every \(x\)?

Hint

Each piece is a polynomial, so only the joins \(x=0\) and \(x=2\) need checking. At each join set left limit = right limit = value. You get two equations in \(a\) and \(b\).

Answer

At \(x=0\): \(f(0)=2b\), left limit \(2b\), right limit \(0+3a-b=3a-b\). Need \(2b=3a-b\), i.e. \(a=b\).

At \(x=2\): \(f(2)=4+3a-b\), left limit \(4+3a-b\), right limit \(3(2)-5=1\). Need \(4+3a-b=1\), i.e. \(3a-b=-3\).

Substituting \(a=b\): \(2a=-3\), so

\[a=b=-\frac32.\]

3. Find the interval for which \(y=\sqrt[3]{\sin(x^{20}+5)}\) is continuous.

Hint

Build it from the inside out: polynomial, then sine, then cube root. Where is each one continuous? Is a cube root of a negative number a problem?

Answer

\(x^{20}+5\) is a polynomial (continuous everywhere); \(\sin u\) is continuous everywhere; \(\sqrt[3]{v}\) is an odd root, defined and continuous for every real \(v\), including negatives. By the composite theorem (twice), \(y\) is continuous on

\[(-\infty,\infty),\]

i.e. for all real \(x\).

4. Does \(f(x)=x^{100}-9x^2+1\) have a root in \([0,2]\)?

Hint

\(f(0)\) and \(f(2)\) are both positive, so the IVT on \([0,2]\) directly gives nothing. Try a point in between, such as \(x=1\).

Answer

Yes. \(f\) is a polynomial, so continuous everywhere. \(f(0)=1\gt0\), \(f(1)=1-9+1=-7\lt0\), \(f(2)=2^{100}-36+1\gt0\).

By the IVT on \([0,1]\) there is a root in \((0,1)\), and on \([1,2]\) another root in \((1,2)\). So \(f\) has at least two roots in \([0,2]\). (Numerically they are \(x\approx0.3333\) and \(x\approx1.0215\).)

Lesson: the same sign at both ends does not mean "no root"; split the interval.

5. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pi/4}\tan\Big(\frac{\cos x-\sin x}{\cos 2x}\Big)\).

Hint

At \(x=\frac\pi4\) the inside is \(\frac00\). Use \(\cos2x=\cos^2x-\sin^2x=(\cos x-\sin x)(\cos x+\sin x)\) to cancel, then move the limit inside \(\tan\) (Section 7).

Answer

For \(x\) near \(\frac\pi4\) with \(\cos x\ne\sin x\),

\[\cos2x=(\cos x-\sin x)(\cos x+\sin x),\] \[\text{so}\quad\frac{\cos x-\sin x}{\cos2x}=\frac{1}{\cos x+\sin x}.\]

So the inside tends to \(\dfrac{1}{\frac{\sqrt2}{2}+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}=\dfrac1{\sqrt2}=\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\). Since \(\tan\) is continuous at \(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\) (which is not an odd multiple of \(\frac\pi2\)),

\[\begin{aligned}&\lim_{x\to\pi/4}\tan\Big(\frac{\cos x-\sin x}{\cos 2x}\Big)\\&\quad=\tan\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\approx0.8545.\end{aligned}\]

In words: simplify the inside, find its limit, then apply tan to that limit (in radians).

13Concept checks Written for these notes

1. Can \(f\) be continuous at \(c\) if \(f(c)\) is not defined?

Answer

No. Condition (i) of the Continuity Test requires \(f(c)\) to be defined. If the limit exists, you can build the continuous extension, but that is a new function \(F\).

2. True or false: if \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\) exists, then \(f\) is continuous at \(c\).

Answer

False. The limit must also equal \(f(c)\). Common Mistake 1 is a counterexample: the limit at \(0\) is \(1\) but \(f(0)=0\).

3. \(f\) is continuous on \([0,1]\) with \(f(0)=2\) and \(f(1)=5\). Must \(f\) take the value 3? The value 6?

Answer

Value 3: yes, by the IVT, since \(3\) is between \(2\) and \(5\). Value 6: not necessarily. The IVT says nothing about values outside \([2,5]\); for example \(f(x)=3x+2\) never reaches 6 on \([0,1]\), while another function could rise above 6 and come back down to 5.

4. Is \(f(x)=\dfrac1x\) a continuous function?

Answer

Yes, as a "continuous function" in the sense of slide 9: it is a rational function, continuous at every point of its domain (all \(x\ne0\)). It is discontinuous at \(x=0\) (an infinite discontinuity), but \(0\) is not in its domain. On the interval \([-1,1]\), however, it is not continuous.

5. Can the product of two functions that are both discontinuous at \(0\) be continuous at \(0\)?

Answer

Yes. Take \(s(x)=1\) for \(x\ge0\) and \(s(x)=-1\) for \(x\lt0\) (jump at \(0\)). Then \(s(x)\cdot s(x)=1\) for every \(x\), which is continuous. The product rule for continuity only works in the direction "continuous \(\times\) continuous \(\Rightarrow\) continuous".

6. \(f\) is continuous on \([-1,1]\) with \(f(-1)=f(1)=3\). Does the IVT tell you that \(f\) has no root in \([-1,1]\)?

Answer

No. The IVT only ever promises that values are reached; it never rules a value out. For example \(f(x)=4x^2-1\) has \(f(\pm1)=3\) but roots at \(x=\pm\frac12\).
