MATH1851 Exam Guide · Limits Involving Infinity

All 6 question parts on infinite limits, limits at infinity and asymptotes from the past papers (2013–2026), sorted into three types, each with a fixed procedure and every question with a hint and a full worked solution.

The short version

What to expect

Infinity questions are a small topic: 6 parts in 23 papers, but 5 of them are on recent papers. Expect at most one or two short parts: a Q1 limit "as \(x\to\infty\)" or "with a \(0\) in the bottom", or an "all the asymptotes" question (Dec 2020 Q1, Dec 2025 multiple choice). Each of the three types below has a short standard procedure; the marks go to the sign (\(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\)) and to handling each side (\(x\to+\infty\) and \(x\to-\infty\)) separately.

How to practise: use Start here to name the type, work the question with the procedure open, then check with the Hint and the Solution. Newest papers come first; 2013–2018 papers are tagged older format. For the ideas behind each step, use the Chapter note links. All answers checked with sympy.

Exam format

  • Where: parts of Q1 "Find each of the following limits" (May 2022 Q1(a),(b), May 2023 Q1(b), May 2016 Q1(a)(iii)); asymptotes inside a graph question (Dec 2020 Q1(b)(II), which uses a given graph) or as a Part I multiple-choice question (Dec 2025 MC6).
  • Speed: Dec 2025 moved part of Part I to multiple choice, and May 2026 made Q1–3 answers only ("a number, \(\infty\), \(-\infty\), or non-existence"). On written papers the stem says "If the limit involves infinity explain whether it is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\)": the sign argument carries the marks.
  • Formula sheet: trigonometric identities on every paper; the derivatives of \(\sin^{-1}\), \(\cos^{-1}\), \(\tan^{-1}\) on most (including Dec 2025, which helps with MC6). Nothing else for this topic: no limit laws, no definition of an asymptote, no \(\frac1{x^n}\to0\) facts.
  • Wording to watch: "find all the asymptotes" means vertical, horizontal and oblique, each side separately; \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\), which is \(x\) only for \(x\gt0\).

L3.1Nonzero number over something heading to 0: infinite limits and their sign

Chapter note · infinite limits

Frequency: 1 of 13 recent papers (May 2022); 0 of 10 older papers. The same sign argument is also the last step of several L'Hôpital questions.

Recognise it

Substituting gives \(\frac{c}{0}\) with \(c\ne0\). This is not indeterminate: the answer is \(+\infty\), \(-\infty\), or "does not exist", and only the signs decide which.

Standard procedure
  1. Substitute and confirm: top \(\to c\ne0\), bottom \(\to0\).
  2. Sign of the top near the point (the sign of \(c\)).
  3. Sign of the bottom on the side you approach from. Write \(0^+\) (small and positive) or \(0^-\) (small and negative).
  4. Conclude. Same signs: \(+\infty\). Opposite signs: \(-\infty\). Asked: May 2022.
    • 4a. Two-sided limit with the bottom changing sign at the point: the left and right answers differ, so the limit does not exist (it is neither \(\infty\) nor \(-\infty\)).
Traps
  • L'Hôpital on \(\frac{c}{0}\). In May 2022 Q1(a), differentiating gives \(\frac{-\sin x}{1}\to0\), a wrong answer: the rule needs \(\frac00\) or \(\frac\infty\infty\).
  • No sign given. "The limit is \(\infty\)" without the sign reasoning loses marks when the stem says "explain whether it is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\)".

May 2020 Q1(a) and May 2023 Q1(a) (one-sided \(\frac00\) limits that use this sign idea as a second step) are in the L10 guide.

Past-paper questions

May 2022 Q1(a)3% (Q1: 12%)

Find the following limit, or explain why one does not exist, showing all the reasoning. If the limit involves infinity explain whether it is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\). \[\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{\cos x}{x}\]

Other parts of this question: (b) is under L3.2; (c) and (d) are in the L10 guide.

Hint

Type L3.1, number over 0. First step: substitute \(x=0\) into \(\cos x\) and into \(x\) separately, then find the sign of each for small \(x\gt0\).

Solution

Step 1 Form. \(\cos x\to\cos0=1\ne0\) and \(x\to0\): form \(\frac10\), not indeterminate.

Step 2–3 Signs. Top \(\to1\gt0\). For \(x\to0^+\) the bottom \(x\) is small and positive: \(0^+\).

Step 4 Positive over \(0^+\): the quotient grows without bound through positive values.

\[\boxed{\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{\cos x}{x}=+\infty}\]

Marker: the reason for the sign ("top \(\to1\gt0\), bottom \(\to0^+\)") is what the stem asks for.

Equivalent forms. "\(\infty\)". (From the left it would be \(-\infty\), so the two-sided limit does not exist; not asked.)

L3.2x → ±∞: divide by the dominant power, or rationalise a difference of roots

Chapter note · limits at infinity · Chapter note · conjugates

Frequency: 2 of 13 recent papers (May 2023, May 2022); 1 of 10 older papers (May 2016). The most common L3 type: 3 parts in all.

Recognise it

\(x\to\infty\) or \(x\to-\infty\), and the expression is built from powers of \(x\) and square roots: a quotient (form \(\frac\infty\infty\)) or a difference of two roots (form \(\infty-\infty\)).

Standard procedure

The dominant power is the highest power of \(x\) that a piece behaves like for large \(x\); \(\sqrt{x^2+6x}\) behaves like \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\).

  1. Name the form. A difference of roots (\(\infty-\infty\)): go to step 2. A quotient (\(\frac\infty\infty\)): go to step 3.
  2. Rationalise. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate (\(\sqrt A-B\) by \(\sqrt A+B\)). The top becomes \(A-B^2\), which has no root and usually a lower power; the result is a quotient. Asked: May 2023, May 2016.
  3. Divide top and bottom by the dominant power of the bottom. Inside a root, divide by its square: \(\frac{\sqrt{x^2+\dots}}{x}=\sqrt{\frac{x^2+\dots}{x^2}}\), which is true only for \(x\gt0\). For \(x\to-\infty\), \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|=-x\), and a minus sign appears.
  4. Take limits. \(\frac{k}{x^n}\to0\) for \(n\gt0\); finish with the limit laws (and continuity of \(\sqrt{\;}\)). Asked: May 2023, May 2022, May 2016.
Worked example · the procedure in action

May 2023 Q1(b), 4% (Q1: 12%). Find the following limit, or explain why one does not exist, showing all the reasoning. If the limit involves infinity explain whether it is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\).

\[\lim_{x\to\infty}\sqrt{x^2+6x}-x\]

Step 1 Both \(\sqrt{x^2+6x}\) and \(x\) tend to \(\infty\): form \(\infty-\infty\), a difference with a root.

\(\infty-\infty\) has no fixed value, so "the answer is 0" or "\(\infty\)" are both guesses. The marker wants the form named.

Step 2 Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate \(\sqrt{x^2+6x}+x\):

\[\begin{aligned}\sqrt{x^2+6x}-x&=\frac{(x^2+6x)-x^2}{\sqrt{x^2+6x}+x}\\&=\frac{6x}{\sqrt{x^2+6x}+x}.\end{aligned}\]

The difference of squares removes the root from the top. The top is now a single power, \(6x\).

Step 3 The dominant power of the bottom is \(x\). Divide top and bottom by \(x\); since \(x\gt0\), \(x=\sqrt{x^2}\):

\[\frac{6x}{\sqrt{x^2+6x}+x}=\frac{6}{\sqrt{1+\frac6x}+1}.\]

Write "\(x\gt0\)" when you move \(x\) inside the root. For \(x\to-\infty\) this step changes.

Step 4 \(\frac6x\to0\), and \(\sqrt{\;}\) is continuous, so the bottom \(\to\sqrt1+1=2\) and, by the Quotient Law, the limit is \(\frac62\).

\[\boxed{3}\]

Common slip: dividing by \(x^2\) instead of \(x\), which gives \(\frac00\) again. Check: sympy gives 3; as \(x\to-\infty\) the same expression tends to \(+\infty\) (no cancellation there).

Traps
  • \(\sqrt{x^2}=x\) for negative \(x\). Fine in May 2022 Q1(b) because \(x\to+\infty\); for \(x\to-\infty\) the same quotient tends to \(-1\), not \(1\).
  • Subtracting "infinities". In May 2023 Q1(b) and May 2016 Q1(a)(iii), \(\infty-\infty\) is not 0: the answers are 3 and \(\frac12\).
Ready answer · difference of square roots Beyond the handouts

Steps 2–4 done once in general: as \(x\to+\infty\),

\[\sqrt{x^2+bx+c}-\sqrt{x^2+dx+e}\to\frac{b-d}{2},\]

and as \(x\to-\infty\) the limit is \(\frac{d-b}{2}\).

May 2023 Q1(b) is the case \(b=6\), \(d=c=e=0\): \(\frac62=3\). The same idea with \(x^4\) in place of \(x^2\) gives May 2016: \(\sqrt{x^4+0x^2+3x}-\sqrt{x^4-x^2}\to\frac{0-(-1)}{2}=\frac12\) (the \(3x\) is too small to count). Use it to check; on written papers show the steps.

Coverage: the procedure solves all 3 past parts: May 2023 Q1(b) and May 2016 Q1(a)(iii) use steps 2–4, May 2022 Q1(b) steps 3–4. A limit as \(x\to-\infty\) has never been asked in this type, but step 3's sign rule covers it.

May 2022 Q1(b)3% (Q1: 12%)

Find the following limit, or explain why one does not exist, showing all the reasoning. If the limit involves infinity explain whether it is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\). \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt{x^2-6}}{x+6}\]

Other parts of this question: (a) is under L3.1; (c) and (d) are in the L10 guide.

Hint

Type L3.2, quotient (step 3). First step: divide top and bottom by \(x\), using \(x=\sqrt{x^2}\) because \(x\gt0\).

Solution

Step 1 Form. Top and bottom both \(\to\infty\): \(\frac\infty\infty\), a quotient.

Step 3 Divide by \(x\). For \(x\gt0\), \(x=\sqrt{x^2}\), so

\[\frac{\sqrt{x^2-6}}{x+6}=\frac{\sqrt{1-\frac6{x^2}}}{1+\frac6x}.\]

Step 4 \(\frac6{x^2}\to0\), \(\frac6x\to0\); by continuity of \(\sqrt{\;}\) and the Quotient Law the limit is \(\frac{\sqrt1}{1}\).

\[\boxed{1}\]

Marker: state \(x\gt0\) when moving \(x\) inside the root; that is the only real reasoning step.

Equivalent forms. As \(x\to-\infty\) the same quotient tends to \(-1\) (not asked).

Older format (2013–2018)

May 2016 Q1(a)(iii)Q1: 10%; part marks not shownolder format

Find \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{x^4+3x}-\sqrt{x^4-x^2}\right).\]

Other parts of this question: (i) is in the L1 guide, (ii) in the L10 guide.

Hint

Type L3.2, difference of roots. First step: multiply top and bottom by \(\sqrt{x^4+3x}+\sqrt{x^4-x^2}\) and simplify the top.

Solution

Step 1 Form. Both roots \(\to\infty\): \(\infty-\infty\).

Step 2 Conjugate. The top becomes \((x^4+3x)-(x^4-x^2)=x^2+3x\):

\[\frac{x^2+3x}{\sqrt{x^4+3x}+\sqrt{x^4-x^2}}.\]

Step 3 Divide by \(x^2\) (dominant power of the bottom; \(x^2=\sqrt{x^4}\)):

\[\frac{1+\frac3x}{\sqrt{1+\frac3{x^3}}+\sqrt{1-\frac1{x^2}}}.\]

Step 4 Every fraction \(\to0\), so the limit is \(\dfrac{1}{1+1}\).

\[\boxed{\tfrac12}\]

Equivalent forms. \(0.5\). Sympy gives \(\frac12\).

L3.3Find all the asymptotes

Chapter note · asymptotes · Chapter note · asymptotes in curve sketching

Frequency: 2 of 13 recent papers (Dec 2025, Dec 2020); 0 of 10 older papers. New since 2020.

Recognise it

"Find all the asymptotes to the graph of \(y=f(x)\)", or a multiple-choice list of possible asymptotes. An asymptote is a straight line the graph gets closer and closer to: vertical \(x=a\), horizontal \(y=L\), or oblique (slanted) \(y=mx+c\).

Standard procedure
  1. Vertical. List the suspect points: where a bottom is 0, a \(\ln\) argument is 0, or two pieces join. At each, find the one-sided limits. \(x=a\) is a vertical asymptote only if a one-sided limit is \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\). If the function stays bounded near every point (or is continuous on \(\mathbb R\)), say "no vertical asymptotes". Asked: Dec 2025, Dec 2020.
  2. Horizontal, each side separately. Find \(\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x)\) and \(\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)\). A finite value \(L\) gives \(y=L\) on that side.
    • 2a. \(f\) built from a given graph, e.g. \(u(g(x))\): find what \(g(x)\) tends to and from which side, then read that one-sided limit of \(u\) off the graph. Asked: Dec 2020.
    • 2b. A \(0\cdot\infty\) or \(\frac00\) form: use L'Hôpital (L10 guide). Asked: Dec 2025.
  3. Oblique, only on a side where \(f\to\pm\infty\). Find \(m=\lim\frac{f(x)}{x}\) and then \(c=\lim\big(f(x)-mx\big)\) on that side. Both finite with \(m\ne0\) gives \(y=mx+c\). Asked: Dec 2025.
  4. List them all, saying which side each horizontal or oblique asymptote belongs to.
Traps
  • Doing only \(x\to+\infty\). In Dec 2025 MC6 the two sides give different asymptotes (horizontal on the right, oblique on the left); checking one side picks the wrong option.
  • The wrong side of a jump. In Dec 2020 Q1(b)(II), \(2-3e^x\) tends to 2 from below, so the value is read from the curve arriving at the open circle \((2,2)\), not from \(u(2)=3\) or the circle \((2,1)\).

Past-paper questions

Dec 2025 MC6Part I multiple choice: 2 pts

The function \(f(x)=x\arctan(x)-\frac\pi2x+1\) has which of the following asymptotes?
(A) Horizontal asymptote \(y=0\) only
(B) Oblique asymptote \(y=-\pi x\) only
(C) Both a horizontal asymptote \(y=0\) and an oblique asymptote \(y=-\pi x\)
(D) No asymptotes
(E) Vertical asymptote \(x=\pi/2\) only

Hint

Type L3.3, all the asymptotes. First step: \(f\) is continuous on \(\mathbb R\), so there is no vertical one; then use \(\arctan x\to\frac\pi2\) as \(x\to+\infty\) and \(\arctan x\to-\frac\pi2\) as \(x\to-\infty\), one side at a time.

Solution

Step 1 Vertical. \(f\) is continuous on \(\mathbb R\) (products and sums of continuous functions): none. This rules out (E).

Step 2b \(x\to+\infty\). \(f(x)=x\left(\arctan x-\frac\pi2\right)+1\), and the product is \(\infty\cdot0\). As a quotient, form \(\frac00\), L'Hôpital:

\[\begin{aligned}\lim\frac{\arctan x-\frac\pi2}{1/x}&=\lim\frac{\frac1{1+x^2}}{-\frac1{x^2}}\\&=\lim\frac{-x^2}{1+x^2}=-1.\end{aligned}\]

So \(f(x)\to-1+1=0\): horizontal asymptote \(y=0\) on the right.

Step 3 \(x\to-\infty\). \(\arctan x\to-\frac\pi2\), so \(f\to+\infty\); look for an oblique one.

\[\begin{aligned}m&=\lim\frac{f(x)}{x}\\&=\lim\Big(\arctan x-\frac\pi2+\frac1x\Big)\\&=-\frac\pi2-\frac\pi2=-\pi,\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}&f(x)+\pi x\\&=x\Big(\arctan x+\frac\pi2\Big)+1.\end{aligned}\]

The same L'Hôpital step gives \(x\left(\arctan x+\frac\pi2\right)\to-1\), so \(c=-1+1=0\): oblique asymptote \(y=-\pi x\) on the left.

\[\boxed{\text{(C)}}\]

Marker: multiple choice, so only the letter scores; the left side is the step most people skip.

Equivalent forms. Asymptotes \(y=0\) (as \(x\to+\infty\)) and \(y=-\pi x\) (as \(x\to-\infty\)). Sympy confirms both limits.

Dec 2020 Q1(b)(II)Q1: 15%; part marks not shown

Let \(u\) be a function whose graph is shown in Diagram I below.

Diagram I (redrawn). Dots and circles are where the paper puts them; the curve shapes between them are approximate.

(b) Let \(q\gt0\) be a constant, and

\[t(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac1x\sin\dfrac{x}{q} & \text{for } x\gt0,\\[2mm] u\big(2-3e^x\big) & \text{for } x\le0.\end{cases}\]

(I) For what value of \(q\) will the function \(t(x)\) be continuous on \(\mathbb R\)?
(II) Find all the asymptotes to the graph of \(y=t(x)\).

Diagram I, read from the paper: filled dot \((-1,0.5)\); a rounded max near \((1,2.5)\); open circle \((2,2)\); filled dot \((2,3)\); open circle \((2,1)\) rising steeply to open circle \((3,4)\); falling and levelling to open circle \((5,2.5)\); filled dot \((5,0)\) rising to a max near \((5.6,2)\), open circle \((6,1.5)\); open circle \((6,4)\) falling to about \((9.5,2.5)\). Parts (a) and (b)(I) are in the L4 guide (answer to (b)(I): \(q=2\)); parts (c) and (d) are in the L9 guide.

Hint

Type L3.3, all the asymptotes, built from a graph (step 2a). First step: as \(x\to-\infty\), find what \(2-3e^x\) tends to and whether it is above or below that value.

Solution

Step 1 Vertical. For \(x\gt0\), \(t\) is continuous, and as \(x\to0^+\), \(\frac{\sin(x/q)}{x}\to\frac1q\) (finite). For \(x\le0\), \(s=2-3e^x\) lies in \([-1,2)\), where \(u\) is defined and bounded (values between \(0.5\) and about \(2.5\)). So \(t\) is bounded near every point: no vertical asymptotes.

Step 2 \(x\to+\infty\). \(\left|\frac1x\sin\frac xq\right|\le\frac1x\to0\), so by the Sandwich Theorem \(t(x)\to0\): \(y=0\) on the right.

Step 2a \(x\to-\infty\). \(e^x\to0^+\), so \(s=2-3e^x\to2\), and \(s\lt2\) always: \(s\to2^-\). From Diagram I, the curve arriving at \(x=2\) from the left ends at the open circle \((2,2)\), so \(\lim_{s\to2^-}u(s)=2\). Hence

\[\lim_{x\to-\infty}t(x)=\lim_{s\to2^-}u(s)=2:\]

\(y=2\) on the left.

Step 3–4 Both ends have finite limits, so there is no oblique asymptote.

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&y=0\ (x\to+\infty),\\&y=2\ (x\to-\infty);\\&\text{no vertical or oblique.}\end{aligned}}\]

Marker: say from which side \(2-3e^x\) approaches 2; that is what fixes the value 2 rather than \(u(2)=3\) or the right-hand limit 1.

Equivalent forms. The answer is the same for every \(q\gt0\), including \(q=2\) from part (I).
