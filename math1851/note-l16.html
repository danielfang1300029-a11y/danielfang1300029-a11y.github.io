Area and Volume

0Where this lecture sits

This lecture uses one idea again and again: cut a shape into thin pieces, measure one piece, and add them all up with an integral. Flat regions are cut into thin strips; solids are cut into slices, like a loaf, or into thin tubes nested like cans.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 The area between two curves is a stack of thin strips, each one top minus bottom. Split the region where the curves cross.
  2. §2 Turn the strips sideways when the curves are easier as \(x=\) (something in \(y\)): each strip is right minus left.
  3. §3 A volume is a sliced loaf: add up (area of each slice) × (its thickness).
  4. §4 Spin a region about a line and every slice is a coin (disc) or a ring (washer). Most common exam type
  5. §5 Or cut the solid into nested cans (shells): each can wall is 2π × radius × height × thickness.
  6. §6 Choose the cut from the strip: across the axis gives discs or washers; along the axis gives shells.
  7. §7–§12 Summary table, common mistakes, the slides' practice questions, concept checks and an exam taster.

You need: Riemann sums (Lecture 11), the definite integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC, Lecture 12), and the integration techniques of Lectures 13 and 14 (substitution, integration by parts, trigonometric substitution, partial fractions). 1Area between curves ~6 min

What is this?

Picture the region between two curves as a shape cut out of card. Slice it into many thin vertical strips, like the slats of a fence standing side by side. Each strip is almost a rectangle: its height is the gap between the two curves at that point (top curve minus bottom curve), and its width is tiny. Add up the areas of all the strips and you have the area of the region. This needs curves without breaks, so that every strip has a definite top and bottom.

Slide 7 shows two wavy curves \(y=f(x)\) and \(y=g(x)\) that keep crossing each other, with the gaps between them shaded, and asks: how do we compute the area between \(f\) and \(g\) from \(a\) to \(b\)?

The idea: cut the region into thin vertical strips. A strip at position \(x\) of width \(\Delta x\) is (almost) a rectangle whose height is the gap between the curves, top curve minus bottom curve. Its area is about \([f(x)-g(x)]\,\Delta x\) when \(f\) is on top. Adding up all strips and letting the width shrink to zero turns the sum into an integral, exactly as in Lecture 11.

The slide's picture redrawn: which curve is on top changes at every crossing

f(x)g(x)strip

Blue shading: \(f\) is on top. Green shading: \(g\) is on top. Points \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) are crossing points. From \(a\) to \(b\), \(f(x)\ge g(x)\); from \(b\) to \(c\), \(g(x)\ge f(x)\). The strip's height is always "upper minus lower", whichever curve that is.

Theorem (slide 8)

The area \(A\) of the region bounded by the curves \(y=f(x)\), \(y=g(x)\) and the lines \(x=a\), \(x=b\), where \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous and \(f(x)\ge g(x)\) for all \(x\) in \([a,b]\), is

\[A=\int_a^b \big[f(x)-g(x)\big]\,dx.\]

In words: area between the curves = integral from left end to right end of (upper curve − lower curve).

Reading the formula
\(f(x)-g(x)\)
the height of the strip standing at position \(x\): top curve minus bottom curve.
\(dx\)
the strip's width, so height × width is the area of one strip.
\(\int_a^b\)
add up the strips from the left edge \(a\) of the region to the right edge \(b\).
\(f(x)\ge g(x)\)
\(f\) is the top all the way across, so every strip has a positive height and nothing cancels.
continuous
no breaks in either curve, so every strip has a definite top and bottom and the integral exists.

Terms used here: continuous (Lecture 4) means the graph has no breaks or jumps on \([a,b]\), which guarantees the integral exists. The condition \(f(x)\ge g(x)\) says \(f\) is the upper curve throughout. Note that it does not matter whether the curves are above or below the \(x\)-axis: the height of a strip is \(f(x)-g(x)\ge0\) either way.

What is this? (splitting at crossings)

Where two curves cross, top and bottom swap places. A strip's height must always be a positive length. If you keep subtracting in the same order after the crossing, the strips there come out negative and cancel some of the others. So cut the region at every crossing and treat each piece on its own, with its own top and bottom.

Slide 8 adds, for the picture: from \(a\) to \(b\), \(f(x)\ge g(x)\); from \(b\) to \(c\), \(g(x)\ge f(x)\). So the area from \(a\) to \(c\) must be split at \(b\):

\[A=\int_a^b\big[f(x)-g(x)\big]\,dx+\int_b^c\big[g(x)-f(x)\big]\,dx.\]

In words: on each piece, subtract the lower curve from the upper one; then add the pieces.

Beyond the handouts

Both cases can be written in one formula: \(A=\displaystyle\int_a^c |f(x)-g(x)|\,dx\). In practice you still have to split at the crossing points to remove the absolute value, so the split form above is what you actually compute.

Example 1 (slide 9)

Find the area of the region bounded by the curves \(y=x^3\) and \(y=x\).

Step 1: where do they cross? \(x^3=x\iff x^3-x=0\iff x(x-1)(x+1)=0\), so \(x=-1,\ 0,\ 1\). The curves enclose two pieces: one over \([-1,0]\) and one over \([0,1]\).

Step 2: which is on top on each piece? Test a point.Between two neighbouring crossings the curves cannot swap (both are continuous), so one test point decides the whole piece. At \(x=-\tfrac12\): \(x^3=-\tfrac18\) and \(x=-\tfrac12\), so \(x^3\) is on top on \([-1,0]\). At \(x=\tfrac12\): \(x^3=\tfrac18\lt\tfrac12=x\), so \(x\) is on top on \([0,1]\).

Step 3: integrate each piece.

\[\begin{aligned}A&=\int_{-1}^{0}(x^3-x)\,dx+\int_0^1(x-x^3)\,dx\\&=\Big[\tfrac{x^4}{4}-\tfrac{x^2}{2}\Big]_{-1}^{0}+\Big[\tfrac{x^2}{2}-\tfrac{x^4}{4}\Big]_0^1\\&=\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12.\end{aligned}\]

Shortcut: both functions are odd (\(h(-x)=-h(x)\)), so the picture is symmetric through the origin and the two pieces have equal area: \(A=2\int_0^1(x-x^3)\,dx=\tfrac12\).

Answer: \(A=\tfrac12\).

Why you must split at crossing points

counted as +counted as −

Colours show how the single, unsplit integral treats each piece. Red pieces are subtracted instead of added, so the unsplit integral is too small (even zero or negative). Splitting at the crossings and always using "upper − lower" makes every piece count positively.

You can now: write the area between two curves as an integral of (top − bottom); find where the curves cross and split the integral there; decide which curve is on top on each piece with one test point.

2Slicing sideways: integrating with respect to \(y\) ~5 min

What is this?

Now lay the strips sideways, like planks stacked one on top of another. Each horizontal strip runs from the left curve to the right curve, so its length is right minus left, and its thickness is a tiny step in height. Add them up from the bottom of the region to the top. This is worth doing when every horizontal strip meets the same two curves, while vertical strips would change their top or bottom part-way across.

Some regions are bounded by curves that are easier to describe as \(x=\) (something in \(y\)). Then it is easier to cut the region into thin horizontal strips. A horizontal strip at height \(y\) of thickness \(\Delta y\) has length "right curve − left curve", so

\[A=\int_c^d\big[x_{\text{right}}(y)-x_{\text{left}}(y)\big]\,dy.\]

In words: area = integral from bottom height to top height of (right curve − left curve).

Reading the formula
\(x_{\text{right}}(y)-x_{\text{left}}(y)\)
the length of the plank at height \(y\): where it ends minus where it starts.
\(dy\)
the plank's thickness, a tiny step in height.
\(\int_c^d\)
stack the planks from the lowest height \(c\) of the region to the highest height \(d\).
\(x\) as a function of \(y\)
each plank is measured across, so both curves must be written as \(x=\) (something in \(y\)).
Example 2 (slide 10)

Find the area enclosed by the line \(y=x-1\) and the parabola \(y^2=2x+6\) by evaluating an integral (a) with respect to \(x\), (b) with respect to \(y\).

Sketch first. \(y^2=2x+6\) means \(x=\tfrac{y^2}{2}-3\): a parabola opening to the right with its tip (vertex) at \((-3,0)\). Its upper half is \(y=\sqrt{2x+6}\) and its lower half is \(y=-\sqrt{2x+6}\).

Intersection points. Put \(x=y+1\) (from the line) into \(y^2=2x+6\): \(y^2=2y+8\iff y^2-2y-8=0\iff(y-4)(y+2)=0\). So \(y=4\) (point \((5,4)\)) or \(y=-2\) (point \((-1,-2)\)).

(a) With respect to \(x\) (vertical strips)

A vertical strip's top and bottom change at \(x=-1\):Left of \(x=-1\) both ends of the strip are on the parabola; to the right, the bottom end is on the line instead.

  • For \(-3\le x\le -1\): the strip runs from the lower half of the parabola to the upper half, height \(\sqrt{2x+6}-(-\sqrt{2x+6})=2\sqrt{2x+6}\).
  • For \(-1\le x\le 5\): the strip runs from the line up to the upper half, height \(\sqrt{2x+6}-(x-1)\).
\[\begin{aligned}A&=\int_{-3}^{-1}2\sqrt{2x+6}\,dx\\&\quad+\int_{-1}^{5}\big[\sqrt{2x+6}-x+1\big]\,dx.\end{aligned}\]

Using \(\int\sqrt{2x+6}\,dx=\tfrac13(2x+6)^{3/2}+C\) (substitution \(u=2x+6\), Lecture 13):

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-3}^{-1}2\sqrt{2x+6}\,dx&=\tfrac23\big[(2x+6)^{3/2}\big]_{-3}^{-1}\\&=\tfrac23(8-0)=\tfrac{16}{3},\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}&\int_{-1}^{5}\big[\sqrt{2x+6}-x+1\big]dx\\&=\tfrac13\big[(2x+6)^{3/2}\big]_{-1}^{5}-\Big[\tfrac{x^2}{2}-x\Big]_{-1}^{5}\\&=\tfrac13(64-8)-\big(\tfrac{15}{2}-\tfrac32\big)\\&=\tfrac{56}{3}-6=\tfrac{38}{3}.\end{aligned}\] \[A=\tfrac{16}{3}+\tfrac{38}{3}=18.\]

(b) With respect to \(y\) (horizontal strips)

Every horizontal strip, for \(-2\le y\le4\), runs from the parabola on the left, \(x=\tfrac{y^2}{2}-3\), to the line on the right, \(x=y+1\). One integral is enough:

\[\begin{aligned}A&=\int_{-2}^{4}\Big[(y+1)-\Big(\tfrac{y^2}{2}-3\Big)\Big]dy\\&=\int_{-2}^{4}\Big(-\tfrac{y^2}{2}+y+4\Big)dy\\&=\Big[-\tfrac{y^3}{6}+\tfrac{y^2}{2}+4y\Big]_{-2}^{4}\\&=\tfrac{40}{3}-\big(-\tfrac{14}{3}\big)=18.\end{aligned}\]

Answer: \(A=18\) both ways. The \(y\)-integral needed only one piece and no square roots: choosing the variable well saves work.

Example 2: vertical strips versus horizontal strips

parabola y² = 2x + 6line y = x − 1strip

With vertical strips, the bottom of the strip changes from the parabola to the line at \(x=-1\), so two integrals are needed. With horizontal strips, every strip goes from the parabola (left) to the line (right).

Steps to find area between curves (slide 11)
  1. Do: Sketch the region. You need to see the strips to know where each one starts and ends.
  2. Do: Choose the variable of integration (\(x\): vertical strips; \(y\): horizontal strips). This decides which way the strips lie; choose the direction in which every strip meets the same two curves, if there is one.
  3. Do: Find the limits of integration: find the points of intersection; list the intervals between intersection points and endpoints. The crossing points are where the region ends, or where a strip's top or bottom switches to another curve.
  4. Do: Determine the "top" and "bottom" functions (for \(dx\)) or the "left" and "right" functions (for \(dy\)) on each interval. A strip's length is only positive if you subtract the right way round.
  5. Do: Set up the integral(s). The total area is the sum of the integrals over all sub-regions. Each integral adds up the strips of one piece, and the pieces together make the whole region.
  6. Do: Evaluate the integral(s). The FTC turns the sum of infinitely many thin strips into an antiderivative taken at the two ends.

You can now: choose vertical or horizontal strips; rewrite curves as \(x=\) (something in \(y\)) when needed; set up and evaluate an area integral in \(y\) as right − left.

3Volume by cross-sections ~6 min

What is this?

Think of a loaf of bread. Cut it into thin slices. Each slice is almost a flat slab, so its volume is the area of its face times its thickness. Add up all the slices and you have the volume of the loaf. Where the loaf is fatter the faces are bigger, so the face area changes along the loaf; the integral adds up face area × thickness as the slices get thinner and thinner.

Slide 12 lists the volume formulas of common shapes (all within school geometry):

ShapeVolumeIn words
Triangular prism (triangle of base \(b\), height \(h\), length \(l\))\(V=\dfrac{b\cdot h\cdot l}{2}\)area of triangle × length
Cylinder (radius \(r\), height \(h\))\(V=\pi r^2h\)area of circle × height
Cone (radius \(r\), height \(h\))\(V=\dfrac{\pi r^2h}{3}\)one third of the matching cylinder

The prism, cylinder and box share one pattern: volume = (area of the cross-section) × (length), because every slice across them has the same shape. A cross-section is the flat shape you see when you cut straight through a solid, like the face of a slice of bread.

Example 3 (slide 13)

Find the volume of a cylinder with radius \(r\) and height \(h\).

Lay the cylinder along the \(x\)-axis from \(x=0\) to \(x=h\). Every cross-section perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis is a circle of radius \(r\), so its area is \(A(x)=\pi r^2\) (a constant). Adding up thin slices of thickness \(dx\):

\[\begin{aligned}V&=\int_0^h A(x)\,dx=\int_0^h\pi r^2\,dx\\&=\pi r^2\big[x\big]_0^h=\pi r^2h.\end{aligned}\]

Answer: \(V=\pi r^2h\), the familiar formula, now obtained by integration.

Definition: volume (slide 14)

Let \(S\) be a solid that lies between \(x=a\) and \(x=b\). If the cross-sectional area of \(S\) in the plane \(P_x\), through \(x\) and perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis, is \(A(x)\), where \(A\) is an integrable function, then the volume of \(S\) is

\[V=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}A(x_i^*)\,\Delta x=\int_a^b A(x)\,dx.\]

In words: volume = sum of (area of slice × thickness of slice), in the limit of infinitely thin slices = integral of the cross-sectional area.

Reading the formula
\(A(x_i^*)\)
the face area of the \(i\)-th slice of the loaf, measured at one point inside it.
\(\Delta x\)
the thickness of each slice.
\(A(x_i^*)\,\Delta x\)
the volume of one slice, treated as a flat slab.
\(\sum\), then \(n\to\infty\)
add up all the slices, then cut thinner and thinner until the slabs fit the loaf exactly.
\(\int_a^b A(x)\,dx\)
the same thing written as an integral along the loaf, from one end \(a\) to the other end \(b\).

Unpacking the symbols:

  • \(P_x\) is the flat plane that cuts through the solid at position \(x\), at right angles to the \(x\)-axis (like a knife cut).
  • \(A(x)\) is the area of the cross-section that this cut reveals.
  • \([a,b]\) is split into \(n\) equal pieces of width \(\Delta x=\frac{b-a}{n}\); \(x_i^*\) is any sample point in the \(i\)-th piece (Lecture 11).
  • Each slice is approximated by a thin slab (a "cylinder" in the general sense: a flat shape pushed straight through a thickness) of volume \(A(x_i^*)\,\Delta x\). Adding the slabs gives a Riemann sum; its limit is the integral.
  • "Integrable" means the limit exists, which is true for example whenever \(A\) is continuous (Lecture 12).

Slide 14's pictures show a lumpy solid \(S\) with one thin slice of width \(\Delta x\) taken at \(x_i\), and the same solid approximated by a stack of flat slabs. The figure below does the same for a solid of revolution.

You can now: explain a volume as a sum of thin slices; write \(V=\int_a^b A(x)\,dx\) once you know the area of a cross-section; rederive \(\pi r^2h\) for a cylinder.

4Solids of revolution: the disc and washer method ~11 min

What is this?

Spin a flat region about a line, and it sweeps out a solid, the way a profile on a potter's wheel sweeps out a pot. Now slice that solid like a loaf, straight across the axis. Because every point went round in a circle, every slice is round: a solid coin if the region touched the axis, or a coin with a hole if there was a gap. So the face area of each slice is easy to write down, and §3 does the rest.

Definition (slide 15)

A solid obtained by revolving (spinning) a flat region about a line is called a solid of revolution. The line is the axis of rotation.

Analogy: a potter's wheel. A flat profile spun around the vertical spindle sweeps out a pot. Every point of the region travels in a circle around the axis, so every cross-section perpendicular to the axis is a circle (or a ring). That makes \(A(x)\) easy to write down.

Slide 15 restates the volume formula for either direction of slicing:

\[V=\int_a^b A(x)\,dx\qquad\text{or}\qquad V=\int_c^d A(y)\,dy.\]

In words: slice perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis and integrate in \(x\), or slice perpendicular to the \(y\)-axis and integrate in \(y\).

The cross-sectional area is:

Disc (a solid coin, no hole): \[A=\pi(\text{radius})^2\] In words: area of a circle.
Washer (a coin with a hole, like a metal washer used with bolts): \[A=\pi(\text{outer radius})^2-\pi(\text{inner radius})^2\] In words: big circle minus the hole.

Slide 15's pictures: a sphere cut by a plane shows a disc cross-section; a region that does not touch the axis, spun around it, gives a solid with a tunnel through it, so its cross-sections are washers.

What is this? (disc method)

Slice a cucumber across: every slice is a solid round coin. When the region touches the axis, each thin strip across the axis spins into such a coin. Its radius is how far the curve is from the axis at that point, so the face area is π times that distance squared.

Definition: disc method (slide 16)

The volume of the solid formed when the region below \(y=f(x)\) (and above the \(x\)-axis), \(a\le x\le b\), is rotated about the \(x\)-axis is

\[V=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi\big[f(x_i^*)\big]^2\Delta x=\int_a^b\pi\big[f(x)\big]^2\,dx.\]

In words: volume = integral of π × (radius)², where the radius of each disc is the height of the curve above the axis.

What is this? (washer method)

If there is a gap between the region and the axis, the solid has a tunnel through the middle, like a bagel or a ring of tinned pineapple. Each slice is then a coin with a hole. Its face area is the whole circle minus the hole. The outer edge comes from the curve farther from the axis and the hole from the nearer curve.

Definition: washer method (slide 16)

The volume of the solid formed when the region between \(y=f(x)\) and \(y=g(x)\), where \(|f(x)|\ge|g(x)|\), is rotated about the \(x\)-axis is

\[\begin{aligned}V&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\pi\big[f(x_i^*)\big]^2-\pi\big[g(x_i^*)\big]^2\Big)\Delta x\\&=\int_a^b\Big(\pi\big[f(x)\big]^2-\pi\big[g(x)\big]^2\Big)dx.\end{aligned}\]

In words: volume = integral of π × [(outer radius)² − (inner radius)²]; the outer radius comes from the curve farther from the axis.

Reading the formula
\(\pi\big[f(x)\big]^2\)
the area of the whole ring's outer circle: its radius is the distance from the axis to the farther curve.
\(\pi\big[g(x)\big]^2\)
the area of the hole: its radius is the distance to the nearer curve.
difference, times \(dx\)
face area of the ring × its thickness: the volume of one slice of the bagel.
\(|f(x)|\ge|g(x)|\)
\(f\) is the farther curve, so the outer circle really is the bigger one.
\(g=0\)
no hole: the formula becomes the disc method above.

Why \(|f(x)|\ge|g(x)|\)? The radius is a distance from the axis, and distance from the \(x\)-axis is \(|y|\). The condition says \(f\) is the curve farther from the axis (outer radius) and \(g\) the nearer one (inner radius). Since the radii are squared, the signs do not matter, but the order does: outer² − inner², never \((\text{outer}-\text{inner})^2\).

What is this? (another axis)

Spinning about a different line, such as a horizontal line above the region, just moves the spindle of the potter's wheel. The slices are still coins or rings across the spindle. Every radius is measured from the spindle to a curve, not from the \(x\)-axis, so a curve that is lower can be the one farther away.

Slide 16 ends: the definition may be reformulated with respect to \(y\) and/or another axis of rotation. The general rule:

\[V=\int\pi\big[R^2-r^2\big]\,d(\text{variable along the axis}),\] \[R=\text{outer radius},\quad r=\text{inner radius}.\]

In words: slices go perpendicular to the axis; integrate along the axis; each radius is the distance from the axis to a boundary curve.

  • Axis horizontal (\(x\)-axis or \(y=k\)): slices are vertical, integrate in \(x\); radius of a point at height \(y\) is \(|y-k|\).
  • Axis vertical (\(y\)-axis or \(x=k\)): slices are horizontal, integrate in \(y\); radius of a point at position \(x\) is \(|x-k|\). The curves must be written as \(x=\) (function of \(y\)).
Example 4 (slide 17): disc method with respect to \(y\)

Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating the region bounded by \(y=\ln x\), \(y=1\), \(y=2\), \(x=0\) about the \(y\)-axis.

Sketch. The region sits between heights \(y=1\) and \(y=2\), from the \(y\)-axis (\(x=0\)) on the left to the curve \(y=\ln x\) on the right. Solving for \(x\): \(y=\ln x\iff x=e^y\).

Slice. The axis is vertical, so slice horizontally: at height \(y\) the slice runs from \(x=0\) to \(x=e^y\). It touches the axis, so it spins into a disc of radius \(e^y\), area \(\pi(e^y)^2=\pi e^{2y}\).

\[\begin{aligned}V&=\int_1^2\pi\big(e^{y}\big)^2\,dy=\pi\int_1^2e^{2y}\,dy\\&=\frac{\pi}{2}\big[e^{2y}\big]_1^2=\frac{\pi}{2}\big(e^4-e^2\big)\approx74.16.\end{aligned}\]

Answer: \(V=\dfrac{\pi(e^4-e^2)}{2}\).

Example 5 (slides 18–19): changing the axis

Find the volume of the solids obtained by rotating the region bounded by \(y=x\) and \(y=x^2\) about (a) the \(x\)-axis, (b) the \(y\)-axis, (c) \(y=2\), (d) \(x=-1\).

The region. \(x=x^2\iff x=0\) or \(1\): the curves meet at \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\). On \([0,1]\), \(x\ge x^2\) (e.g. at \(x=\tfrac12\): \(\tfrac12\gt\tfrac14\)), so the line is on top. Written in terms of \(y\) (for \(0\le y\le1\)): the line is \(x=y\) and the parabola is \(x=\sqrt y\); since \(\sqrt y\ge y\), the parabola is on the right.

(a) About the \(x\)-axis

Vertical slices; the region does not touch the axis (except at \(x=0\)), so washers. Outer radius = distance to the farther curve = \(x\); inner radius = \(x^2\).

\[\begin{aligned}V&=\pi\int_0^1\big[x^2-(x^2)^2\big]dx=\pi\int_0^1(x^2-x^4)\,dx\\&=\pi\Big(\tfrac13-\tfrac15\Big)=\frac{2\pi}{15}.\end{aligned}\]

(b) About the \(y\)-axis

Horizontal slices at height \(y\), from \(x=y\) to \(x=\sqrt y\): washers with outer radius \(\sqrt y\), inner radius \(y\).

\[\begin{aligned}V&=\pi\int_0^1\big[(\sqrt y)^2-y^2\big]dy=\pi\int_0^1(y-y^2)\,dy\\&=\pi\Big(\tfrac12-\tfrac13\Big)=\frac{\pi}{6}.\end{aligned}\]

(c) About the line \(y=2\)

The axis is horizontal, above the region. Vertical slices give washers. Distance from height \(y\) up to the axis is \(2-y\). The parabola \(y=x^2\) is lower, hence farther from \(y=2\): outer radius \(R=2-x^2\); inner radius \(r=2-x\).The spindle is above the region, so the outer edge of each ring is traced by the lower curve.

\[\begin{aligned}V&=\pi\int_0^1\big[(2-x^2)^2-(2-x)^2\big]dx\\&=\pi\int_0^1\big(x^4-5x^2+4x\big)dx\\&=\pi\Big(\tfrac15-\tfrac53+2\Big)=\frac{8\pi}{15}.\end{aligned}\]

(d) About the line \(x=-1\)

The axis is vertical, to the left of the region. Horizontal slices give washers. Distance from position \(x\) to the axis is \(x-(-1)=x+1\). The farther curve is the right one, \(x=\sqrt y\): \(R=1+\sqrt y\); \(r=1+y\).

\[\begin{aligned}V&=\pi\int_0^1\big[(1+\sqrt y)^2-(1+y)^2\big]dy\\&=\pi\int_0^1\big(2\sqrt y-y-y^2\big)dy\\&=\pi\Big(\tfrac43-\tfrac12-\tfrac13\Big)=\frac{\pi}{2}.\end{aligned}\]

Answers: (a) \(\dfrac{2\pi}{15}\) (b) \(\dfrac{\pi}{6}\) (c) \(\dfrac{8\pi}{15}\) (d) \(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\). Part (d) is quicker with shells (Section 5).

Trap

For an axis \(y=k\) or \(x=k\), the radius is distance to the axis, not the \(y\)- or \(x\)-value of the curve. In (c) the outer radius is \(2-x^2\), not \(x\) or \(x^2\). Always check which curve is farther from the axis; it can be the lower curve.

5The cylindrical shells method ~8 min

What is this?

Instead of slicing across the axis, cut the solid into thin tubes around it, like a set of tin cans of increasing size, without lids or bases, nested one inside another. Each can wall is a thin sheet: cut it down the side and unroll it, and you get an almost flat rectangle. Its length is the distance round the can, its height is the height of the can, and its thickness is the thickness of the wall. A thin strip of the region lying alongside the axis sweeps out exactly one such can wall, and adding up all the walls gives the solid.

Discs and washers need slices perpendicular to the axis. Sometimes that is awkward: for example, rotating the region under \(y=e^{-x^2}\) about the \(y\)-axis would need \(x\) in terms of \(y\) (\(x=\sqrt{-\ln y}\)), which is messy. The shell method uses strips parallel to the axis instead.

Definition: cylindrical shell

A cylindrical shell is a thin-walled hollow cylinder, like a toilet-paper tube or one ring of an onion (or a Swiss roll). Its radius is the distance from the axis to the wall, its height is the length of the wall, and its thickness is the tiny width of the wall.

Slide 20's pictures: a region under \(y=f(x)\) from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\) is rotated about the \(y\)-axis to form a dome-like solid. A thin vertical strip at distance \(x\) from the axis spins into a shell. Cutting the shell open and unrolling it gives a nearly flat slab: its length is the circumference \(2\pi x\), its height is \(f(x)\) and its thickness is \(\Delta x\). Finally the whole solid is shown as a set of nested shells, like Russian dolls.

\[\begin{aligned}&\text{volume of one shell}\\&\approx(\text{circumference})\times(\text{height})\times(\text{thickness})\\&=2\pi x\cdot f(x)\cdot\Delta x.\end{aligned}\]

In words: shell volume ≈ 2π × radius × height × thickness.

Definition: shell method (slide 21)

The volume of the solid formed when the region between \(y=f(x)\) and \(y=g(x)\) (where \(f(x)\ge g(x)\)), \(a\le x\le b\), is rotated about the \(y\)-axis is

\[\begin{aligned}V&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\pi x_i\big[f(x_i^*)-g(x_i^*)\big]\Delta x\\&=\int_a^b2\pi x\big[f(x)-g(x)\big]\,dx.\end{aligned}\]

In words: volume = integral of 2π × (shell radius) × (shell height), where shell radius = distance to the axis and shell height = upper curve − lower curve.

Reading the formula
\(2\pi x\)
the distance round the can at radius \(x\): the length of the unrolled sheet.
\(f(x)-g(x)\)
the height of the can: the length of the strip, top minus bottom.
\(dx\)
the thickness of the can wall.
\(\int_a^b\)
add up the cans from the innermost (radius \(a\)) to the outermost (radius \(b\)).

The definition may be reformulated with respect to \(y\) and/or the axis of rotation.

On the slide the radius in the sum is written \(x_i\) while the heights use \(x_i^*\). In the limit it makes no difference which point of the \(i\)-th piece is used, so the integral is the same.

Here we assume \(0\le a\lt b\) (the region is on one side of the axis), so the radius \(x\) is positive. General version:

\[V=2\pi\int(\text{radius})(\text{height})\,d(\text{variable across the axis}).\]
  • Axis vertical (\(y\)-axis or \(x=k\)): vertical strips, integrate in \(x\); radius \(|x-k|\); height = top − bottom.
  • Axis horizontal (\(x\)-axis or \(y=k\)): horizontal strips, integrate in \(y\); radius \(|y-k|\); height = right − left (the "height" is now a horizontal length).
Beyond the handouts: why 2πx·h·Δx is right

Exactly, a shell with inner radius \(x\), outer radius \(x+\Delta x\) and height \(h\) has volume \(\pi(x+\Delta x)^2h-\pi x^2h=2\pi x\,h\,\Delta x+\pi h(\Delta x)^2\). The last term is tiny compared with the others when \(\Delta x\) is small (it is "\(\Delta x\) times \(\Delta x\)"), and it disappears in the limit, so \(2\pi x\,h\,\Delta x\) is all that survives.

Example 6 (slide 22): shells

Find the volume obtained by rotating the region bounded by \(y=e^{-x^2}\), \(y=0\), \(x=0\), \(x=1\) about the \(y\)-axis.

Why shells? Vertical strips are parallel to the \(y\)-axis. A strip at \(x\) has height \(e^{-x^2}-0\) and sits at distance \(x\) from the axis. (Discs would need \(x=\sqrt{-\ln y}\) and two pieces; also \(e^{-x^2}\) alone has no elementary antiderivative, but \(x\,e^{-x^2}\) does.)

\[V=\int_0^1 2\pi x\,e^{-x^2}\,dx.\]

Substitute \(u=x^2\), \(du=2x\,dx\); \(x=0\Rightarrow u=0\), \(x=1\Rightarrow u=1\) (Lecture 13):

\[\begin{aligned}V&=\pi\int_0^1e^{-u}\,du=\pi\big[-e^{-u}\big]_0^1\\&=\pi\Big(1-\frac1e\Big)\approx1.986.\end{aligned}\]

Answer: \(V=\pi\big(1-e^{-1}\big)\).

What is this? (discs or shells?)

The same solid can be cut into loaf slices or into nested cans. Both ways add up the same volume, so the answers must agree. The choice is only about which integral is easier to write down and to work out.

Example 7 (slide 23): discs or shells?

Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating the region under the curve \(y=\sqrt x\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\) about the \(x\)-axis using (a) discs, (b) shells.

(a) Discs

Vertical slices at \(x\), from \(y=0\) to \(y=\sqrt x\), touch the axis: discs of radius \(\sqrt x\).

\[V=\pi\int_0^1(\sqrt x)^2\,dx=\pi\int_0^1x\,dx=\frac{\pi}{2}.\]

(b) Shells

The axis is horizontal, so shells come from horizontal strips, and we integrate in \(y\). For \(0\le y\le1\), the strip at height \(y\) runs from the curve \(x=y^2\) (since \(y=\sqrt x\iff x=y^2\) for \(y\ge0\)) to the line \(x=1\): length (shell height) \(1-y^2\). Its distance from the \(x\)-axis (shell radius) is \(y\).A strip alongside a horizontal axis must be horizontal, so it is measured across from left to right, and the region's right edge is the line \(x=1\).

\[V=\int_0^12\pi y\,(1-y^2)\,dy=2\pi\Big(\tfrac12-\tfrac14\Big)=\frac{\pi}{2}.\]

Answer: \(V=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\) either way. Here discs are simpler. Choose whichever gives the easier integral.

6Steps to compute the volume of a solid of revolution ~3 min

What is this?

Every volume question starts from one thin strip of the region. Hold it up against the axis. If the strip lies across the axis, spinning it makes a coin or a ring: one slice of the loaf. If it lies alongside the axis, spinning it makes one can wall. The steps below make that choice and then read the radius and height off the sketch.

Steps (slide 24)
  1. Do: Sketch the region and the solid. Draw the 2D region that will be rotated. Sketch the 3D solid, or at least one representative disc/washer/shell. Every radius is a distance you read off this picture, from the axis to a boundary curve.
  2. Do: Choose the axis of rotation and the method. (The axis is usually given; you choose discs/washers or shells.) A strip across the axis spins into a slice of the loaf; a strip alongside it spins into a can wall.
  3. Do: Choose the variable of integration. Discs/washers: the variable runs along the axis. Shells: the variable runs across (perpendicular to) the axis. Loaf slices are stacked along the axis, while nested cans grow outward from it.
  4. Do: Determine the radius/height and the limits. Find the points of intersection. These measure one typical slice or can, and the limits say where the first and last ones sit.
  5. Do: Set up the integral(s). The integral adds up the volumes of all the slices or cans.
  6. Do: Evaluate the integral(s). A volume must come out positive; zero or a negative answer means a radius, or top and bottom, is the wrong way round.
Axis horizontal (y = k)Washers: vertical strips, \(dx\), \(R,r=\) distances \(|y-k|\) to the curves.
Shells: horizontal strips, \(dy\), radius \(|y-k|\), height = right − left.
Axis vertical (x = k)Washers: horizontal strips, \(dy\), \(R,r=\) distances \(|x-k|\) to the curves.
Shells: vertical strips, \(dx\), radius \(|x-k|\), height = top − bottom.

Choosing discs/washers or shells

  1. Do: Look at the axis: horizontal (\(x\)-axis or \(y=k\)) or vertical (\(y\)-axis or \(x=k\)). The axis decides which strips make loaf slices and which make can walls.
  2. Do: With the curves given as \(y=f(x)\), draw a vertical strip of the region. Its length is top − bottom, read straight from the formulas, with no need to solve for \(x\).
  3. Do: If the axis is horizontal, use discs (the strip touches the axis) or washers (there is a gap), integrating in \(x\). The strip lies across the axis, so it spins into a coin or a ring.
  4. Do: If the axis is vertical, use shells, with radius = distance from the axis to the strip, integrating in \(x\). The strip lies alongside the axis, so it spins into one can wall.
  5. Do: Use horizontal strips (\(dy\)) instead only when the question asks for the other method, or when the \(x\)-integral is hard or needs many pieces. Horizontal strips need every curve rewritten as \(x=\) (something in \(y\)), which costs time.
  6. Do: If a strip's top or bottom (or left or right end) changes part-way across, split the integral there. Each piece of the region needs its own pair of boundary curves, exactly as for areas in §1.

You can now: follow the six steps for any solid of revolution; pick discs, washers or shells from the direction of the strip; read radius and height from the sketch for any horizontal or vertical axis.

7Summary

  • To compute areas between curves and volumes, geometric visualization is essential: draw the region first.
  • Both quantities are computed by Riemann sums (thin strips or thin slices) which turn into a definite integral in the limit.
  • Choose a method and variable of integration that makes the computation simpler.
  • Do not memorize formulas: draw a picture and the right formula will emerge naturally.

8Common mistakes

Area between curves

✗Forgetting to split the integral at intersection points. Area between \(y=x^3\) and \(y=x\): writing \(A=\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}(x^3-x)\,dx\) gives \(0\), because \(x^3-x\) is positive on \((-1,0)\) and negative on \((0,1)\), and the two pieces cancel.
✓ Split at \(x=0\) and use upper − lower on each piece: \(A=\int_{-1}^0(x^3-x)\,dx+\int_0^1(x-x^3)\,dx=\tfrac12\) (Example 1). ✗Identifying top/bottom functions incorrectly. Area between \(y=x\) and \(y=x^2\) for \(0\le x\le2\): writing \(A=\int_0^1(x-x^2)\,dx+\int_1^2(x-x^2)\,dx\) gives \(\tfrac16-\tfrac56=-\tfrac23\). The split is right, but after \(x=1\) the parabola is on top.
✓ \(A=\int_0^1(x-x^2)\,dx+\int_1^2(x^2-x)\,dx=\tfrac16+\tfrac56=1.\) A negative "area" is a sure sign of a mix-up.

Volume

✗Wrong radius in the disc/washer method. Rotate \(y=x^2\) from \(y=0\) to \(y=4\) about the \(y\)-axis: writing \(V=\pi\int_0^4[x^2]^2\,dy\). The slices are horizontal (we integrate in \(y\)), so the radius is the horizontal distance \(x\) from the \(y\)-axis to the curve, written in terms of \(y\): \(x=\sqrt y\). The integrand must also contain only \(y\).
✓ \(V=\pi\int_0^4(\sqrt y)^2\,dy=\pi\int_0^4y\,dy=8\pi.\) ✗Confusing disc and washer. Rotate the region between \(y=x^2\) and \(y=x\) about the \(x\)-axis: writing \(\pi\int_0^1[x]^2\,dx\) uses a disc, ignoring the hole left by the gap between the axis and \(y=x^2\).
✓ Washers: \(\pi\int_0^1\big(x^2-x^4\big)\,dx=\frac{2\pi}{15}\) (Example 5(a)). ✗Confusing shell radius and shell height. Radius = distance from the axis to the strip; height = length of the strip (difference of two curves). In \(2\pi\int x\,f(x)\,dx\) about the \(y\)-axis, \(x\) is the radius and \(f(x)\) the height, not the other way round. ✗Mixing up \(dx\) and \(dy\). Every part of the integrand and both limits must be in the integration variable. If you integrate in \(y\), write the curves as \(x=\) (function of \(y\)) and use \(y\)-limits. ✗Squaring the difference instead of taking the difference of squares. Written for these notes A washer has area \(\pi(R^2-r^2)\), not \(\pi(R-r)^2\). For \(R=2\), \(r=1\): \(\pi(4-1)=3\pi\), but \(\pi(2-1)^2=\pi\). ✗Using the \(y\)-value as the radius when the axis is \(y=k\). Written for these notes About \(y=2\), a point at height \(y\) has radius \(|2-y|\), not \(y\) (Example 5(c)).

2. Without integrating, explain why \(\int_{-1}^{1}(x^3-x)\,dx=0\) even though \(y=x^3\) and \(y=x\) enclose a region of area \(\tfrac12\).

Answer

The integrand is odd, so the piece on \([-1,0]\) (where \(x^3-x\gt0\)) exactly cancels the piece on \([0,1]\) (where it is negative). An integral adds signed heights; an area needs upper − lower on each piece.

3. The region between \(y=x\) and \(y=x^2\) (\(0\le x\le1\)) is rotated about the line \(y=1\). Which curve gives the outer radius? Set up and evaluate the washer integral.

Hint

Distance from height \(y\) up to \(y=1\) is \(1-y\). Which curve is lower?

Answer

The parabola \(y=x^2\) is lower, so farther from \(y=1\): \(R=1-x^2\), \(r=1-x\).

\[V=\pi\int_0^1\big[(1-x^2)^2-(1-x)^2\big]dx=\frac{\pi}{5}.\]

4. The triangle under \(y=x\), \(0\le x\le1\), is rotated (i) about the \(y\)-axis, (ii) about the line \(x=3\). Using shells, what changes between the two integrals?

Answer

Only the radius. The height is \(x\) in both. (i) radius \(x\): \(V=2\pi\int_0^1x\cdot x\,dx=\frac{2\pi}{3}\). (ii) radius \(3-x\): \(V=2\pi\int_0^1(3-x)\,x\,dx=\frac{7\pi}{3}\). Moving the axis farther away makes every shell longer, so the volume grows.

5. Use the disc method to check the sphere formula \(V=\frac43\pi r^3\) from slide 12.

Hint

A sphere is the solid made by rotating the upper half-disc \(y=\sqrt{r^2-x^2}\), \(-r\le x\le r\), about the \(x\)-axis.

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}V&=\pi\int_{-r}^{r}\big(r^2-x^2\big)\,dx=\pi\Big[r^2x-\tfrac{x^3}{3}\Big]_{-r}^{r}\\&=\pi\Big(\tfrac{2r^3}{3}+\tfrac{2r^3}{3}\Big)=\frac43\pi r^3.\end{aligned}\]

6. A strip is parallel to the axis of rotation. Does it sweep out a disc, a washer, or a shell? What if it is perpendicular and does not touch the axis?

Answer

Parallel: a shell. Perpendicular and not touching the axis: a washer (a disc with a hole). Perpendicular and touching the axis: a disc.
