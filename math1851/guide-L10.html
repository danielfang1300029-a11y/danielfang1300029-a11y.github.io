MATH1851 Exam Guide · L'Hôpital's Rule

The short version

What to expect

L'Hôpital limits are in 11 of the 13 recent papers, almost always as two or three parts of Q1 ("Find each of the following limits"). Unlike Laplace, the exact limits change every time, so you cannot memorise answers. What does not change is the method: every question is one of six types, and each type below comes with a standard procedure, numbered steps of the form "if you see this, do that; if that fails, do this". Follow the steps in order and you never have to invent an approach in the exam.

How to practise: first use Start here to name the form and find the type. Then work the question with the type's procedure open, check with the Hint if stuck and with the Solution at the end. Newest papers come first; 2013–2018 papers are tagged older format. For the ideas behind the rule, use the Chapter note links. All answers checked with sympy.

Exam format

  • Where: Q1 "Find each of the following limits" (10–12%, 3–4% per part) on most papers; also inside other Part I questions (May 2019 Q2, Dec 2020 Q4, May 2024 Q2). Dec 2025: Part I free-response Q11 (10 pts) plus multiple-choice limits.
  • Speed: Dec 2025 moved part of Part I to multiple choice, and May 2026 made Q1–3 answers only ("a number, \(\infty\), \(-\infty\), or non-existence"). Only the final value scores there, so a wrong form check costs the whole part. On written papers the opposite holds: the stem says "showing all the reasoning" or "indicate clearly the theorems you have used", and the steps carry the marks.
  • Formula sheet: no paper prints L'Hôpital's Rule, the list of indeterminate forms or the standard limits \(\frac{\sin x}{x}\to1\), \(\frac{e^x-1}{x}\to1\), \(\frac{\ln(1+x)}{x}\to1\), \(x\ln x\to0\). Learn them; they shorten most solutions.
  • Typical wording traps: "or explain why one does not exist", "explain whether it is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\)", and the May 2019 reminder "Check the conditions when using L'Hospital rule". Several questions are deliberately not indeterminate.

L10.10/0 or ∞/∞ quotient: L'Hôpital's Rule

Chapter note · L'Hôpital's Rule · Chapter note · classifying forms

Frequency: 3 of 13 recent papers (May 2019, May 2020, May 2023); 2 of 10 older papers. It is also the last step of most other L10 types and of many L12 questions.

Recognise it

One fraction \(\dfrac{\text{top}}{\text{bottom}}\), and substituting gives \(\frac00\) or \(\frac{\pm\infty}{\pm\infty}\). Watch for questions that only look like this but are not indeterminate.

Standard procedure

Write the limit as \(\lim\dfrac{T(x)}{B(x)}\): \(T\) is the top, \(B\) the bottom.

  1. Find the limits of \(T\) and \(B\) separately. Go one-sided if \(x\to a^{\pm}\) is given, or if there is an \(|x|\), \(\ln x\) or \(\frac1x\).
  2. Name the form.
    • \(\frac00\) or \(\frac{\pm\infty}{\pm\infty}\): indeterminate, go to step 3.
    • \(\frac{c}{d}\) with \(d\ne0\): not indeterminate. Quotient Law, answer \(\frac cd\), stop. Asked: May 2019.
    • \(\frac{c}{0}\) with \(c\ne0\): not indeterminate; the answer is \(\pm\infty\) by the sign of the bottom (or does not exist if the two sides differ).
  3. Split the sides if needed. If an \(|x|\) (or a formula that changes at \(a\)) is involved, remove it on each side (\(|x|=x\) for \(x\gt0\), \(|x|=-x\) for \(x\lt0\)) and do steps 4–5 for \(x\to a^+\) and \(x\to a^-\) separately. Same value: that is the limit. Different values: the limit does not exist. Asked: Dec 2016.
  4. Check the conditions, then apply L'Hôpital. \(T\) and \(B\) are differentiable near \(a\) and \(B'(x)\ne0\) near \(a\). Differentiate top and bottom separately (no quotient rule): look at \(\lim\dfrac{T'(x)}{B'(x)}\).
  5. Find \(\lim\dfrac{T'}{B'}\).
    • 5a. It is a number: that is the answer. Asked: May 2017, Dec 2016.
    • 5b. It is \(\frac{c}{0}\) with \(c\ne0\): find the sign of the bottom on the given side; the answer is \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\). Asked: May 2023 (= May 2020).
    • 5c. Still \(\frac00\) or \(\frac\infty\infty\): simplify (cancel common factors), then go back to step 2 and apply the rule again, or finish with a standard limit \(\frac{\sin u}{u}\to1\), \(\frac{e^u-1}{u}\to1\), \(\frac{\ln(1+u)}{u}\to1\).
Worked example · the procedure in action

May 2023 Q1(a), 4% (the same question was May 2020 Q1(a), 4 marks). Find the following limit, or explain why it does not exist, showing all the reasoning. If the limit involves infinity explain whether it is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\).

\[\lim_{x\to-\frac\pi2^+}\frac{x+\frac\pi2}{\sin x+1}\]

Step 1 Top \(T=x+\frac\pi2\to0\). Bottom \(B=\sin x+1\to\sin\left(-\frac\pi2\right)+1=0\).

Only the right-hand side is asked: \(x\) is slightly bigger than \(-\frac\pi2\).

Step 2 Form \(\frac00\): indeterminate, carry on.

Write the form down. On a written paper "showing all the reasoning" means the marker looks for it before any L'Hôpital.

Step 4 \(T\) and \(B\) are differentiable, and \(B'(x)=\cos x\ne0\) for \(x\) just to the right of \(-\frac\pi2\). So

\[\lim_{x\to-\frac\pi2^+}\frac{x+\frac\pi2}{\sin x+1}=\lim_{x\to-\frac\pi2^+}\frac{1}{\cos x}.\]

Step 3 is skipped: there is no \(|x|\) and only one side is asked.

Step 5b The new top is \(1\); the new bottom \(\cos x\to0\). Form \(\frac10\), so the answer is infinite; the sign decides which. For \(-\frac\pi2\lt x\lt\frac\pi2\), \(\cos x\gt0\), so \(\cos x\to0^+\) and \(\dfrac1{\cos x}\to+\infty\).

The question says "explain whether it is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\)": the sign of \(\cos x\) on the right of \(-\frac\pi2\) is the explanation the marker wants.

\[\boxed{+\infty}\]

Check without L'Hôpital: near \(-\frac\pi2\), \(\sin x+1\) behaves like \(\frac12\left(x+\frac\pi2\right)^2\), so the fraction behaves like \(\frac{2}{x+\pi/2}\), which is large and positive on the right.

Traps
  • Skipping step 2. May 2019 Q2(a)(i) carries the reminder "Check the conditions when using L'Hospital rule": the bottom tends to \(1\), not \(0\), so L'Hôpital is not allowed. Differentiating anyway gives a wrong answer.
  • Using the quotient rule. L'Hôpital differentiates top and bottom separately: \(\frac{T'}{B'}\), not \(\left(\frac TB\right)'\).
  • Forgetting the side. \(|x|\) (Dec 2016 Q5(a)) and \(\frac{c}{0}\) answers (May 2020/2023 Q1(a)) both depend on which side \(x\) comes from.

Dec 2016 Q5(a)Q5: 12%; part marks not shownolder format

For each of the following find the limit with clear justifications if it exists, or explain why it does not exist.

\[\begin{aligned}&\text{(i) }\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\ln(1+x)}{x}\\&\text{(ii) }\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\ln(1+|x|)}{x}\\&\text{(iii) }\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\ln(1+|x|)}{x}\sin x\end{aligned}\]
Hint

Type L10.1, \(\frac00\) quotient; (ii) and (iii) need step 3. First step: for (ii), replace \(|x|\) by \(x\) on the right and by \(-x\) on the left, and use (i) on each side.

Solution

Step 1–2 (i) Form \(\frac{\ln1}{0}=\frac00\).

Step 4–5a L'Hôpital (bottom's derivative \(1\ne0\)): \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\frac1{1+x}}{1}=1\).

Step 3 (ii) Split the sides. For \(x\gt0\): \(\dfrac{\ln(1+x)}{x}\to1\) by (i). For \(x\lt0\), put \(u=-x\to0^+\):

\[\frac{\ln(1+|x|)}{x}=-\frac{\ln(1+u)}{u}\to-1.\]

The one-sided limits \(1\) and \(-1\) differ, so the limit does not exist.

Step 3 (iii) On each side, \(\dfrac{\ln(1+|x|)}{x}\) tends to \(\pm1\) and \(\sin x\to0\). Product Law on each side: both one-sided limits are \(0\), so the limit is \(0\).

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{(i) }1\\&\text{(ii) does not exist}\\&\text{(iii) }0\end{aligned}}\]

Marker: in (ii) give both one-sided limits; "does not exist" without them scores little.

L10.2∞ − ∞: combine into one fraction first

Chapter note · ∞ − ∞ forms · Chapter note · classifying forms

Frequency: 3 of 13 recent papers (May 2022, Dec 2023, May 2026); 3 of 10 older papers. Almost always a difference of two reciprocals such as \(\frac1x-\frac1{\sin x}\).

Recognise it

A difference (or sum) of two terms that both blow up: \(\frac1{\text{small}}-\frac1{\text{small}}\), \(\cot x-\csc x\), \(\frac1{\sin^2x}-\frac1{x^2}\).

Standard procedure
  1. Find the limit of each term separately. Go one-sided if \(x\to a^{\pm}\) is given, or if a term is like \(\frac1x\) or \(\frac1{|y|}\) (then look at both sides).
  2. Name the form.
    • \((+\infty)-(+\infty)\) or \((-\infty)-(-\infty)\): indeterminate, go to step 3.
    • \((+\infty)-(-\infty)=+\infty\) or \((-\infty)-(+\infty)=-\infty\): not indeterminate; answer and stop. Asked: Dec 2015 (left side).
  3. Combine into one fraction over a common denominator (write \(\cot\), \(\csc\) in terms of \(\sin\), \(\cos\) first). Check the new form: it should be \(\frac00\).
  4. Find the limit of the fraction.
    • 4a. Apply L'Hôpital (L10.1 steps 4–5). Still \(\frac00\)? Simplify and apply it again; stop as soon as the bottom does not tend to 0. Asked: May 2026, Dec 2023, May 2022, Dec 2015, May 2014.
    • 4b. If the fraction would need three or more rounds, first factor it into pieces with known limits (\(\frac{\sin x}{x}\to1\), a difference of squares) and use L'Hôpital only on the hard piece; Product Law. Asked: May 2013.
  5. Two-sided questions: make sure steps 2–4 hold from both sides. Same value: that is the limit; different values: it does not exist.
Worked example · the procedure in action

Dec 2023 Q1(b), 4%. Find the following limit, or explain why it does not exist, showing all the reasoning. If the limit involves infinity explain whether it is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\).

\[\lim_{x\to0}\frac1x-\frac1{\sin x}\]

Step 1 From the right, \(\frac1x\to+\infty\) and \(\frac1{\sin x}\to+\infty\). From the left, both tend to \(-\infty\).

The question is two-sided and both terms change sign at 0, so look at each side.

Step 2 Form \(\infty-\infty\) from both sides: indeterminate.

"\(\infty-\infty=0\)" is a classic wrong move: the two infinities can grow at different speeds.

Step 3 Common denominator:

\[\frac1x-\frac1{\sin x}=\frac{\sin x-x}{x\sin x},\quad\text{form }\frac00.\]

Combining turns an unusable form into one L'Hôpital can handle. Write the new form down.

Step 4a L'Hôpital (top and bottom differentiable near 0):

\[\begin{aligned}&\lim\frac{\sin x-x}{x\sin x}\\&=\lim\frac{\cos x-1}{\sin x+x\cos x}.\end{aligned}\]

Still \(\frac00\) (top \(\to0\), bottom \(\to0\)), so apply the rule again:

\[\begin{aligned}&\lim\frac{\cos x-1}{\sin x+x\cos x}\\&=\lim\frac{-\sin x}{2\cos x-x\sin x}\\&=\frac{0}{2}=0.\end{aligned}\]

Name the form before each round. The second round stops because the bottom tends to \(2\), not 0.

Step 5 Nothing in steps 3–4 depended on the side, so the limit from both sides is 0.

\[\boxed{0}\]

May 2022 Q1(c) is the same question with \(x\to0^+\) only; May 2026 Q1(e) is the same idea with \(2x\) in place of \(x\).

Traps
  • Assuming every \(\infty-\infty\)-looking question is indeterminate. Dec 2015 Q1(a) from the left is \((-\infty)-(+\infty)=-\infty\) at once.
  • Applying L'Hôpital before combining. The rule needs one quotient; \(\frac1x-\frac1{\sin x}\) is not one.
  • Too many rounds. May 2013 Q1(b)(ii) needs four rounds of L'Hôpital done blindly; factoring first (step 4b) takes three lines.
Ready answer · reciprocal differences

These recur (as \(u\to0\), from either side):

\[\begin{aligned}\frac1u-\frac1{\sin u}&\to0\\ \frac1{\sin^2u}-\frac1{u^2}&\to\frac13\end{aligned}\]

The first is Dec 2023 Q1(b) and May 2022 Q1(c), and with \(u=2x\) (and the sign swapped) May 2026 Q1(e). The second is May 2013 Q1(b)(ii). On written papers show steps 1–5 anyway.

L10.30 · ∞: rewrite as a quotient first

Chapter note · 0 · ∞ forms · Chapter note · classifying forms

Frequency: 1 of 13 recent papers (Dec 2024); 2 of 10 older papers. The same rewrite also appears inside curve sketching (asymptotes of \(xe^{x}\)) and inside L10.4 (\(x\ln x\)).

Recognise it

A product where one factor tends to 0 and the other to \(\pm\infty\): \(xe^{-x}\) as \(x\to\infty\), \(x^{0.7}\ln(\cdots)\) as \(x\to0^+\), \(x\big(\text{something}\to e\big)-ex\).

Standard procedure
  1. Find the limit of each factor separately. If the question has an \(ex\) or similar term, first take out the common factor so it is one product.
  2. Name the form.
    • \(0\cdot(\pm\infty)\): indeterminate, go to step 3.
    • \(c\cdot(\pm\infty)\) with \(c\ne0\), or \((\pm\infty)\cdot(\pm\infty)\): not indeterminate; answer \(\pm\infty\) by the signs and stop. Asked: Dec 2017 (\(x\to-\infty\)).
  3. Move one factor to the bottom as its reciprocal. Keep \(\ln\) and other awkward pieces on top; move the power of \(x\) or the exponential down (\(x^{0.7}\to\frac1{x^{-0.7}}\), \(e^{-x}\to\frac1{e^{x}}\)).     • 4a. One round, then known limits finish it. Asked: Dec 2017, May 2016.

L10.4Variable power (1∞, 00, ∞0): take ln first

Chapter note · indeterminate powers · Chapter note · classifying forms

Frequency: 8 of 13 recent papers (every one except Dec 2019, Dec 2020, May 2023, Dec 2024, May 2025); 3 of 10 older papers. The most common L10 type.

Recognise it

The variable is in the exponent: \(\big(\text{base}\big)^{\text{exponent}}\) with \(x\) in both, an \(x\)-th root \(\sqrt[x]{\;\cdot\;}\), or \(x^{(\dots)}\).

Standard procedure

Call the expression \(y=B(x)^{E(x)}\): \(B\) is the base, \(E\) the exponent.

  1. Find the limits of \(B\) and \(E\) separately. Go one-sided if \(x\to a^{\pm}\) is given, or if \(E\) is like \(\frac1x\) (then look at both sides).
  2. Name the form.
    • \(1^{\pm\infty}\), \(0^0\) or \(\infty^0\): indeterminate, go to step 3.
    • Anything else is not indeterminate: answer straight away and stop. \(0^{+\infty}=0\), \(0^{-\infty}=+\infty\), \(\infty^{+\infty}=\infty\), and \(c^k\) for a positive number \(c\) is just \(c^k\). Asked: May 2019, Dec 2025.
    • If \(E\) has no limit at all (it keeps swinging), go to step 3 and expect step 5c.
  3. Take logs. \(B\gt0\) near the point, so \(\ln y=E\cdot\ln B\).
  4. Make \(\ln y\) a quotient by moving one factor to the bottom, usually \(\ln y=\dfrac{\ln B}{1/E}\). Write down that it is \(\frac00\) or \(\frac{\pm\infty}{\pm\infty}\).
  5. Find \(L=\lim\ln y\).
    • 5a. If \(\ln y\) splits into factors with known limits, \(\frac{e^x-1}{x}\to1\), \(\frac{\ln(1+u)}{u}\to1\), \(x\ln x\to0\), or a limit the question gives you, use the Product Law. Fastest. Asked: May 2020, Dec 2021.
    • 5b. Otherwise apply L'Hôpital's Rule and simplify. Still \(\frac00\)? Check again and apply it again, or finish with \(\frac{\sin u}{u}\to1\), \(\frac{\tan u}{u}\to1\). Asked: May 2026, May 2024, Dec 2023, and 3 older papers.
    • 5c. If \(\ln y\) has no limit, pick two lists of points tending to \(a\) that give different values: the limit does not exist. Asked: May 2014.
  6. Exponentiate. \(e^x\) is continuous, so \(\lim y=e^{L}\). If \(L=+\infty\) the answer is \(+\infty\); if \(L=-\infty\) it is \(0\).
Worked example · the procedure in action

May 2020 Q1(b), 4 marks. Find the following limit, showing all the reasoning. If there is an infinite limit, then explain whether it is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\).

\[\lim_{x\to0^+}x^{(\exp(x)-1)}\]

Step 1 Base \(B=x\to0^+\). Exponent \(E=e^x-1\to0^+\).

\(x\to0^+\) is given, so we only look at the right-hand side.

Step 2 Form \(0^0\): indeterminate, carry on.

\(0^0\) has no fixed value (\(0^x\to0\) but \(x^0\to1\)), so we can't just "plug in".

Step 3 \(x\gt0\), so logs are allowed: \(\ln y=(e^x-1)\ln x\).

Write "\(x\gt0\)" down: the marker wants to see that \(\ln B\) makes sense.

Step 4–5a \(\ln y\) is \(0\cdot(-\infty)\). Rather than force one quotient, split it into two known pieces:

\[\ln y=\frac{e^x-1}{x}\cdot x\ln x.\]

Multiplying and dividing by \(x\) is allowed because \(x\ne0\). This is the step-5a move: each factor is a limit we can find on its own.

First factor, form \(\frac00\), L'Hôpital: \(\dfrac{e^x}{1}\to1\).

Second factor, \(x\ln x=\dfrac{\ln x}{1/x}\), form \(\frac{-\infty}{\infty}\), L'Hôpital:

\[\frac{1/x}{-1/x^2}=-x\to0.\]

Product Law (both limits exist): \(L=\lim\ln y=1\cdot0=0\).

Name the form before each L'Hôpital: that is where "showing all the reasoning" earns its marks.

Step 6 \(e^x\) is continuous, so \(\lim y=e^{L}=e^0\).

\[\boxed{1}\]

Common slip: writing the answer as \(0\), which is \(L\), not the limit.

Traps
  • Skipping step 2. Two recent questions are not indeterminate at all: May 2019 Q2(a)(ii) is \(0^{+\infty}\) and Dec 2025 Q11(b) is \(2^{-2}\). Logs and L'Hôpital there waste time and can go wrong.
  • Stopping at \(L\). The answer is \(e^{L}\), not \(L\).
  • Using L'Hôpital on \(B^E\) itself. The rule only works on a quotient; that is what steps 3–4 are for.
Ready answer · the \(1^\infty\) shortcut Beyond the handouts

If \(B\to1\) and \(E\to\pm\infty\), then

\[\lim B^{E}=e^{\,\lim E\,(B-1)}.\]

Why: when \(B\) is close to 1, \(\ln B\approx B-1\) (because \(\frac{\ln(1+u)}{u}\to1\)). It gives all 7 of the \(1^\infty\) parts below in one line, e.g. \((e^x+x)^{1/x}\): \(\frac{e^x+x-1}{x}\to2\), so the limit is \(e^2\). Use it freely on answers-only papers; on written papers use it to check, and show steps 1–6.

\(f\) has no formula. You are told only "\(f\) is (twice) differentiable" and a few values like \(f(0)\), \(f'(0)\), \(f''(0)\), and asked for a limit built from \(f\).

Standard procedure
  1. List what the conditions give you. Differentiable at \(a\) means continuous at \(a\), so \(f(x)\to f(a)\). Twice differentiable at \(a\) means \(f'\) exists near \(a\) and is continuous at \(a\), so \(f'(x)\to f'(a)\). Nothing is known about \(f''\) near \(a\).
  2. Substitute and name the form using step 1. Every past question gives \(\frac00\).
  3. Turn the pieces into derivatives.
    • 3a. Divide top and bottom by \(h\) (or \(x\)). Each piece becomes a difference quotient, e.g. \(\frac{f(2h)-f(0)}{h}=2\cdot\frac{f(2h)-f(0)}{2h}\to2f'(0)\), or a value like \(f'(h)\to f'(0)\). Finish with the Quotient Law. Asked: Dec 2020.
    • 3b. Apply L'Hôpital once (allowed: \(f\) is differentiable near \(a\)). Then stop and spot the definition: \(\frac{f'(x)-f'(a)}{x-a}\to f''(a)\). Asked: Dec 2025.
  4. Check with a sample function (scratch work): a polynomial with the given values, e.g. \(f(x)=2+3x-2x^2\) for \(f(0)=2\), \(f'(0)=3\), \(f''(0)=-4\). Its limit must match your answer.
Traps
  • A second L'Hôpital. In Dec 2025 Q11(a), a second round would need \(f''\) near 0 and \(f''\) continuous at 0; neither is given. The marker wants step 3b's "definition of \(f''(0)\)" instead.
  • Treating \(hf''(h)\to0\) as obvious. In Dec 2020 Q4(b), L'Hôpital on the whole fraction produces \(hf''(h)\) terms whose limit is not known; step 3a avoids them.
Ready answer · the second-order quotient Beyond the handouts

If \(f\) is twice differentiable at 0, then

\[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{f(x)-f(0)-f'(0)\,x}{x^2}=\frac{f''(0)}{2}.\]

This is step 3b done once in general (it is the \(x^2\) coefficient of the Taylor polynomial, which the course does not cover). It gives Dec 2025 Q11(a) in one line: \(\frac{-4}{2}=-2\). Use it to check; on written papers show the steps.

Coverage: the procedure solves both past parts: Dec 2020 Q4(b) ends at step 3a, Dec 2025 Q11(a) at step 3b. Questions needing \(f'''\) or a limit as \(x\to\infty\) have never been asked.

L10.6Continuity or differentiability that needs L'Hôpital at the join

Chapter note · L'Hôpital at a join · Chapter note · continuity · Chapter note · derivative by definition

Frequency: 3 of 13 recent papers (May 2019, May 2024, Dec 2025); 2 of 10 older papers. Usually a later part of a longer Part I question, after a limit part.

Recognise it

A function defined in pieces (or with a filled-in value at one point), and the question asks "continuous at \(x=a\)?", "find \(c\) so that …", "differentiable at \(a\)?" or "find \(h'(a)\) if it exists".

Standard procedure

Call the function \(F\) and the special point (the join) \(a\).

  1. Read what is asked. Continuity ("continuous", "find \(c\)") → step 2. A derivative ("differentiable", "find \(F'(a)\)") → step 3.
  2. Continuity: \(F\) is continuous at \(a\) when \(\lim_{x\to a^-}F=\lim_{x\to a^+}F=F(a)\). Find each one-sided limit using the formula on that side; each is a form from L10.1–L10.5, so use that type.
    • 2a. Both one-sided limits are the same number: the constant must make \(F(a)\) equal to it. Say why it works. Asked: May 2013 (and Dec 2025 Q11(a), under L10.5).
    • 2b. A one-sided limit is infinite (or the two differ whatever the constant): no value works. Say which side fails. Asked: May 2014.
  3. Derivative: check continuity at \(a\) first. Not continuous means not differentiable. Usually an earlier part has done this.
  4. Choose the route.
    • 4a. \(F\) is one formula built from functions that are differentiable at \(a\) (e.g. \(f(x)^{g(x)}\)): use the derivative rules at \(a\); for a variable power, take \(\ln\) first. No L'Hôpital needed. Asked: Dec 2025.
    • 4b. \(F(a)\) is a filled-in value where the formula gives \(\frac00\): write the difference quotient \(\dfrac{F(x)-F(a)}{x-a}\) as one fraction (form \(\frac00\)) and use L'Hôpital, splitting off factors with non-zero limits first. Asked: May 2019.
    • 4c. The formula changes at \(a\) (pieces, or a composition whose inner function lands in different pieces): work out the formula for \(F(a+h)\) when \(h\gt0\) and when \(h\lt0\), then find the two one-sided difference quotients \(\dfrac{F(a+h)-F(a)}{h}\). Asked: May 2024.
  5. Conclude. The two one-sided values agree: that is \(F'(a)\). They differ, or one is infinite: \(F\) is not differentiable at \(a\).
Worked example · the procedure in action

May 2024 Q2(b), part of Q2 (10%). Let \(f\) and \(g\) be two functions defined as follows:

\[f(x)=\begin{cases}xe^x & \text{for } x\ge0,\\ -e^x & \text{for } x\lt0,\end{cases}\] \[g(x)=\begin{cases}\ln x & \text{for } x\gt0,\\ \ln(1-x) & \text{for } x\le0.\end{cases}\]

Determine the derivative \((f\circ g)'(0)\). [Hint: Use the definition of derivative.]

Step 1 A derivative is asked, so go to steps 3–4. Let \(F=f\circ g\), i.e. \(F(x)=f(g(x))\).

The chain rule is useless here: \(f\) is not even continuous at \(0\) (\(f(0)=0\) but \(-e^x\to-1\) from the left), and \(g\) blows up just right of 0. That is why the paper says "use the definition".

Step 3 \(F(0)=f(g(0))=f(\ln1)=f(0)=0\).

You need \(F(a)\) for the difference quotient. Continuity comes out of step 4 anyway: both formulas below tend to 0.

Step 4c Where does \(g(h)\) land? For small \(h\gt0\), \(g(h)=\ln h\lt0\), so \(f\) uses its left formula:

\[F(h)=-e^{\ln h}=-h.\]

For \(h\lt0\), \(g(h)=\ln(1-h)\gt0\), so \(f\) uses its right formula:

\[\begin{aligned}F(h)&=\ln(1-h)\,e^{\ln(1-h)}\\&=(1-h)\ln(1-h).\end{aligned}\]

This is the whole difficulty: the inner function sends the right side of 0 to the left piece of \(f\), and the left side to the right piece. Write the signs down.

Step 4c Right quotient: \(\dfrac{F(h)-F(0)}{h}=\dfrac{-h}{h}=-1\).

Left quotient: \(\dfrac{(1-h)\ln(1-h)}{h}=(1-h)\cdot\dfrac{\ln(1-h)}{h}\). The first factor \(\to1\); the second is \(\frac00\), and L'Hôpital gives

\[\lim_{h\to0^-}\frac{\ln(1-h)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0^-}\frac{-\frac1{1-h}}{1}=-1.\]

This is where L'Hôpital is needed "at the join". State the \(\frac00\) form first.

Step 5 Both one-sided quotients tend to \(-1\), so \(F\) is differentiable at 0 and

\[\boxed{(f\circ g)'(0)=-1}\]

Common slip: using \(f(x)=xe^x\) for \(h\gt0\) because "\(h\) is positive". The piece of \(f\) is chosen by the sign of \(g(h)\), not of \(h\).

Traps
  • Choosing the piece by the sign of \(h\) in a composition (May 2024 Q2(b)): look at the sign of the inner function's value.
  • Checking one side only. May 2014 Q1(b): the left limit of \(x^2e^{1/x}\) is a harmless \(0\); the right limit is \(+\infty\) and decides the answer.
  • Differentiating the formula instead of using the definition at a filled-in point (May 2019 Q2(c)): \(h(-1)\) is defined separately, so \(h'(-1)\) must come from the difference quotient.
