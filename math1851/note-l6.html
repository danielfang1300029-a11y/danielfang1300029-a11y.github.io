Differentiation Rules

0Where this lecture sits

Lecture 5 found each derivative from a limit, which is slow. This lecture builds a toolkit of differentiation rules: short formulas that build the slope of a complicated function from the slopes of its simple parts. Keep one picture in mind: a derivative is a rate, like a car's speedometer showing how fast the odometer reading is changing.

Recall

The derivative of a function \(f(x)\) with respect to the variable \(x\) is the function \(f'(x)\) whose value at \(x\) is

\[f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h},\]

provided this limit exists. (Lecture 5)

In words: derivative at \(x\) = the limit, as the step \(h\) shrinks to 0, of (change in output) ÷ (change in input) = slope of the tangent line at \(x\).

Differentiability implies continuity (Lecture 5): if \(f\) is differentiable at \(c\) (the limit above exists at \(x=c\)), then \(f\) is continuous at \(c\) (no jump or hole there). The proof of the Product Rule below uses this fact.

Learning objectives.

  • Derive formulas that simplify the process of differentiation: the Constant Rule, Sum/Difference Rules, Constant Multiple Rule, Product/Quotient Rules and the Chain Rule.
  • Apply a technique (implicit differentiation) to differentiate implicit equations, that is, equations in \(x\) and \(y\) that are not solved for \(y\).
Going further

The derivative as a rate of change is not a separate topic here, but it is the reason derivatives matter. If \(s(t)\) is the position of a car at time \(t\), then \(s'(t)\) is its velocity: how fast position changes per unit time. In general, \(f'(x)\) measures how fast the output \(f\) changes per unit change in the input \(x\). Keep this picture in mind for the Chain Rule (section 5).

Notation. The derivative of \(y=f(x)\) can be written \(f'(x)\), \(y'\), \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) or \(\dfrac{d}{dx}f(x)\). The symbol \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\) means "take the derivative with respect to \(x\) of whatever follows". \(\big(\ldots\big)'\) means the same thing.

1The basic differentiation rules ~5 min

What is this?

A derivative is a speedometer: it shows how fast a quantity is changing right now. In that picture the basic rules are common sense. A parked car's odometer never changes, so its speed is 0 (Constant Rule). Record the same trip in metres instead of kilometres and every reading, the speed included, is 1000 times bigger (Constant Multiple Rule). Walk along a moving walkway and your speed over the ground is your walking speed plus the walkway's speed (Sum Rule).

Throughout, \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) are differentiable functions (their derivatives exist) and \(c\) is a real number (\(c\in\mathbb{R}\)). This table collects every rule of the lecture:

It extends to every real power \(n\in\mathbb{R}\), for example \(x^{1/2}\) or \(x^{\pi}\), using logarithmic differentiation (Lecture 7). This note already uses it for \(x^{1/2}\) and \(x^{2/3}\).

The Product, Quotient and trig rules get their own sections below. Here is why the first four rules hold, straight from the definition.

Constant Rule \[\frac{d}{dx}(c)=0\]

In words: the slope of a flat (horizontal) line is zero, because a constant never changes.

From the definition: \(\dfrac{c-c}{h}=0\) for every \(h\ne0\), so the limit is 0.

Constant Multiple Rule \[\frac{d}{dx}\big(c\,f(x)\big)=c\,f'(x)\]

In words: stretching a graph vertically by a factor \(c\) multiplies every slope by \(c\).

From the definition: \(\dfrac{cf(x+h)-cf(x)}{h}=c\cdot\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\to c\,f'(x)\), since a constant factor comes out of a limit (limit laws, Lecture 1).

Sum/Difference Rule \[\frac{d}{dx}\big(f(x)\pm g(x)\big)=f'(x)\pm g'(x)\]

In words: the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives (and the same for a difference): you may differentiate term by term.

From the definition: the difference quotient of \(f\pm g\) splits into (difference quotient of \(f\)) \(\pm\) (difference quotient of \(g\)), and the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits.

What is this? (Power Rule)

Picture a square of side \(x\), with area \(x^2\). Let the side grow a little. The new area is two thin strips along two sides, each as long as \(x\), plus a tiny corner square that does not matter for a very small growth. So the area grows about \(2x\) times as fast as the side. A cube of side \(x\) grows by three thin slabs on three faces, each of area \(x^2\): rate \(3x^2\). That is where the power comes down in front and drops by one.

Power Rule \[\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^n\right)=n\,x^{n-1}\]

In words: bring the power down in front as a multiplier, then lower the power by one.

Why, for a positive integer \(n\). Expand \((x+h)^n\) with the binomial theorem:

\[\begin{aligned}(x+h)^n&=x^n+n x^{n-1}h\\&\quad+(\text{terms with }h^2,h^3,\ldots).\end{aligned}\]

So

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{(x+h)^n-x^n}{h}&=n x^{n-1}\\&\quad+(\text{terms with }h,h^2,\ldots)\end{aligned}\]

and every leftover term tends to 0 as \(h\to0\), leaving \(nx^{n-1}\). For \(n=0\), \(x^0=1\) is a constant with derivative 0, which agrees with \(0\cdot x^{-1}\). For a negative integer \(n=-m\), write \(x^{-m}=\dfrac{1}{x^m}\) and use the Quotient Rule (section 2): the result is \(-m\,x^{-m-1}\), which is again \(nx^{n-1}\).

Examples: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}x^5=5x^4\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}x=1\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac1x=\dfrac{d}{dx}x^{-1}=-x^{-2}=-\dfrac{1}{x^2}\).

Combining these, any polynomial can be differentiated term by term. For example

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dx}\left(4x^3-7x+2\right)&=4\cdot3x^2-7\cdot1+0\\&=12x^2-7.\end{aligned}\]

The Sum Rule lets each term be done on its own; the Constant Multiple Rule keeps the 4 and the 7 as multipliers; the constant 2 never changes, so it gives 0.

f(x)tangent line at x₀f′(x) (bottom)

Move the point. The slope of the red tangent line in the top graph is exactly the height of the green graph below it at the same \(x\). Where the top graph has a peak or a valley (horizontal tangent), the bottom graph crosses zero. Where the top graph rises, the bottom one is positive; where it falls, negative. The "sin x" option shows that the slopes of \(\sin x\) trace out \(\cos x\).

You can now: differentiate any polynomial term by term with the Constant, Constant Multiple, Sum/Difference and Power Rules; explain each rule as a statement about rates; and read off the derivative table for the six trigonometric functions.

2The Product Rule and the Quotient Rule ~7 min

What is this?

Picture a rectangle whose width is \(f(x)\) and height is \(g(x)\), so its area is the product. As \(x\) increases, both sides grow. The area grows for two reasons: the height grows, which adds a thin strip along the top, and the width grows, which adds a thin strip down the side. The Product Rule adds these two strips. Where the two strips overlap there is a tiny corner piece (extra width × extra height); for a small step it is far smaller than the strips, so it drops out. The wrong guess "slope × slope" keeps only that corner and throws away both strips.

A natural guess is "the derivative of a product is the product of the derivatives". It is false. For example \(x\cdot x=x^2\) has derivative \(2x\), but \((x)'\cdot(x)'=1\cdot1=1\). The correct rule has two terms.

Theorem: Product Rule

If \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) are differentiable functions, then

\[\frac{d}{dx}\big(f(x)g(x)\big)=f(x)g'(x)+f'(x)g(x).\]

In words: derivative of (first × second) = first × (derivative of second) + (derivative of first) × second.

Reading the formula
\(f(x)\,g'(x)\)
the top strip: the width as it is now × how fast the height grows.
\(f'(x)\,g(x)\)
the side strip: how fast the width grows × the height as it is now.
\(+\)
both strips are new area, so their rates add.
no \(f'g'\) term
that would be the corner piece, which is too small to count as the step shrinks.
Example 1

Find the derivative of \(f(x)=x\sin x\).

Solution. This is a product of \(x\) (derivative 1) and \(\sin x\) (derivative \(\cos x\), proved in section 3). By the Product Rule,

\[f'(x)=x\cdot\cos x+1\cdot\sin x=x\cos x+\sin x.\]

(Pick "x sin x" in the figure of section 1 to see this derivative drawn.)

Why it is true: the area picture

Think of \(u(x)\) and \(v(x)\) as the width and height of a rectangle, so the product \(u(x)v(x)\) is its area. Move from \(x\) to \(x+h\). The width grows by \(\Delta u=u(x+h)-u(x)\) and the height by \(\Delta v=v(x+h)-v(x)\) (the symbol \(\Delta\), "delta", means "change in"). The new rectangle is the old one plus two strips:

  • a top strip of width \(u(x+h)\) and height \(\Delta v\): area \(u(x+h)\Delta v\);
  • a right strip of width \(\Delta u\) and height \(v(x)\): area \(v(x)\Delta u\).

So the change in area is

\[\begin{aligned}\Delta(uv)&=u(x+h)v(x+h)-u(x)v(x)\\&=u(x+h)\,\Delta v+v(x)\,\Delta u.\end{aligned}\]

In words: extra area = (new width × extra height) + (old height × extra width).

Divide by \(h\) and let \(h\to0\):

\[\begin{aligned}\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\Delta(uv)}{h}&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{u(x+h)\Delta v}{h}+\lim_{h\to0}\frac{v(x)\Delta u}{h}\\&=uv'+vu'\end{aligned}\]

if \(u(x)\) and \(v(x)\) are differentiable, because \(\dfrac{\Delta v}{h}\to v'\), \(\dfrac{\Delta u}{h}\to u'\) and \(u(x+h)\to u(x)\).

The picture shows a positive \(h\), but the derivative needs the two-sided limit \(h\to0\); the algebra is the same for negative \(h\).

The Product Rule as growing area

u(x)v(x)top strip u(x+h)Δvright strip v(x)Δu

Here width \(u(x)=x\) and height \(v(x)=\tfrac14x^2+1\). Shrink \(h\): the two strips get thin, the tiny corner piece they overlap in becomes negligible, and (extra area) ÷ \(h\) settles on \(uv'+vu'\).

The proof, step by step

The trick is to add and subtract the same quantity \(f(x+h)g(x)\) in the numerator (this changes nothing, since it adds zero) so that the numerator splits into two familiar difference quotients.

\[(fg)'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)g(x+h)-f(x)g(x)}{h}.\]

Add and subtract \(f(x+h)g(x)\) in the numerator, then group the four terms in pairs:

\[\begin{aligned}&f(x+h)g(x+h)-f(x)g(x)\\ &=f(x+h)g(x+h)-f(x+h)g(x)\\&\quad+f(x+h)g(x)-f(x)g(x)\\ &=f(x+h)\big(g(x+h)-g(x)\big)\\&\quad+g(x)\big(f(x+h)-f(x)\big).\end{aligned}\]

The extra term splits the change in area into the two strips of the picture: new width × extra height (top strip), plus old height × extra width (side strip).

Divide by \(h\) and take the limit of each piece:

\[\begin{aligned}(fg)'(x)&=\lim_{h\to0}f(x+h)\cdot\lim_{h\to0}\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}\\&\quad+g(x)\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\\ &=f(x)g'(x)+g(x)f'(x).\end{aligned}\]

The last line uses three facts: the two difference quotients tend to \(g'(x)\) and \(f'(x)\) by definition; and \(\lim_{h\to0}f(x+h)=f(x)\) because \(f\) is differentiable, hence continuous (Lecture 5). That is why the Recall box at the start reminded you of that theorem.

What is this? (Quotient Rule)

Picture a pot of money \(f\) shared equally among \(g\) people, so each share is \(f/g\). The share rises when the pot grows, and falls when more people join. The Quotient Rule weighs these two effects against each other: the minus sign is the "more people" effect. You cannot share among nobody, which is the condition \(g(x)\ne0\).

Quotient Rule

If \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable and \(g(x)\ne0\), then

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right)=\frac{f'(x)g(x)-f(x)g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2}.\]

In words: derivative of (top ÷ bottom) = [(derivative of top) × bottom − top × (derivative of bottom)] ÷ bottom squared.

Memory aid: "low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared". The order matters because of the minus sign; the denominator never gets differentiated on its own.

Reading the formula
\(f'(x)\,g(x)\)
the pot is growing: this pushes each share up.
\(-\,f(x)\,g'(x)\)
more people are joining: this pushes each share down. Swap the order and a bigger pot would lower everyone's share, which is why order matters.
\([g(x)]^2\)
the common denominator: the pot effect alone is \(f'/g\) and the people effect alone is \(fg'/g^2\); over \(g^2\) they combine into the top line.
Going further: where the Quotient Rule comes from

The Quotient Rule above was stated without proof. Write \(Q=\dfrac fg\), so \(f=Q\,g\). If \(Q\) is differentiable, the Product Rule gives \(f'=Q'g+Qg'\), so

\[Q'=\frac{f'-Qg'}{g}=\frac{f'-\frac fg g'}{g}=\frac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}.\]

(That \(Q\) is differentiable can be shown from the definition in the same add-and-subtract way as the Product Rule.)

Quick example

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\dfrac{1}{x^m}\right)\) for a positive integer \(m\): top \(=1\) (derivative 0), bottom \(=x^m\) (derivative \(mx^{m-1}\)):

\[\frac{0\cdot x^m-1\cdot mx^{m-1}}{x^{2m}}=-m\,x^{-m-1},\]

which is the Power Rule for the negative power \(-m\), as promised in section 1.

You can now: differentiate a product or a quotient with the right terms in the right order; explain the Product Rule with the growing rectangle; and follow the add-and-subtract proof of the Product Rule.

3Derivatives of the trigonometric functions ~5 min

What is this?

Picture a point going round a circle of radius 1 at a steady speed of 1 (one unit of arc per second). After \(x\) seconds it has turned through \(x\) radians, and its height above the centre is \(\sin x\). At the far right the point is moving straight up at full speed; at the top it moves sideways, so its height is not changing; at the far left it moves straight down. The upward part of its velocity is always \(\cos x\). Its sideways position is \(\cos x\), and while it is in the upper half it moves left, which is the minus sign in \(-\sin x\). All this needs radians: only in radians is the angle the same as the distance travelled round the circle, so that the speed is exactly 1.

In calculus, angles are always measured in radians (\(\pi\) radians \(=180^\circ\)). The rules below are only true in radians.

Theorem \[\frac{d}{dx}(\sin x)=\cos x\]

In words: the slope of the sine graph at any \(x\) equals the value of cosine at that \(x\).

Look at the graph of \(f(x)=\sin x\) from \(-\tfrac{5\pi}{2}\) to \(\tfrac{5\pi}{2}\). Look at its slopes: steepest upward (slope 1) at \(x=0\), flat (slope 0) at the peak \(x=\tfrac\pi2\), steepest downward (slope \(-1\)) at \(x=\pi\). Those are exactly the values \(\cos0=1\), \(\cos\tfrac\pi2=0\), \(\cos\pi=-1\). The figure in section 1 (option "sin x") draws this.

Proof

It needs the compound angle formula \(\sin(A+B)=\sin A\cos B+\cos A\sin B\) (part of HKDSE M2, not Core; you can check it with \(A=B=\tfrac\pi4\): both sides equal 1) and two special limits.

\[\begin{aligned}f'(x)&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin(x+h)-\sin x}{h}\\ &=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(\sin x\cos h+\cos x\sin h)-\sin x}{h}\\ &=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin x(\cos h-1)+\cos x\sin h}{h}\\ &=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin x(\cos h-1)}{h}+\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos x\sin h}{h}\\ &=\sin x\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h-1}{h}+\cos x\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}\\ &=\sin x\cdot0+\cos x\cdot1=\cos x.\end{aligned}\]

\(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) come out of the limits because they do not depend on \(h\). The two special limits are:

\[\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1\qquad\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h-1}{h}=0\]

In words: for a tiny angle \(h\) (in radians), \(\sin h\) is almost exactly \(h\), and \(\cos h\) is so close to 1 that even after dividing the gap by \(h\) it still vanishes.

Reading the formula
\(\dfrac{\sin h}{h}\to1\)
after a tiny turn \(h\), the height gained (\(\sin h\)) is almost the whole arc travelled (\(h\)): at the start the point moves straight up.
\(\dfrac{\cos h-1}{h}\to0\)
the sideways drift \(1-\cos h\) is much smaller than the arc: at the start the point is not moving sideways at all.
\(h\) in radians
the arc length equals \(h\) only in radians; in degrees the first limit would not be 1.

The first was proved with the Sandwich Theorem (Lecture 2). For the second, multiply top and bottom by \(\cos h+1\) and use \(\cos^2h-1=-\sin^2h\):

\[\frac{\cos h-1}{h}=\frac{\cos^2h-1}{h(\cos h+1)}=-\frac{\sin h}{h}\cdot\frac{\sin h}{\cos h+1}\]

which tends to \(-1\cdot\dfrac{0}{2}=0\).Multiplying by \(\cos h+1\) turns \(\cos h-1\) into \(-\sin^2h\), so the known limit \(\frac{\sin h}{h}\to1\) can do the work.

The two special limits used in the proof

sin h / h → 1(cos h − 1) / h → 0

Neither expression is defined at \(h=0\) (open circles), but as \(h\) approaches 0 from either side the blue curve heads to height 1 and the red curve to height 0.

The other five trig derivatives

First the definitions of the three reciprocal trig functions (not in HKDSE Core):

\[\sec x=\frac{1}{\cos x},\qquad \csc x=\frac{1}{\sin x},\]\[\cot x=\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}=\frac{1}{\tan x}.\]

In words: secant = 1 ÷ cosine; cosecant = 1 ÷ sine; cotangent = cosine ÷ sine = 1 ÷ tangent.

Each derivative then follows from \(\sin x\) and the rules of section 2:

  • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos x=-\sin x\). Since \(\cos x=\sin\!\left(x+\tfrac\pi2\right)\), the Chain Rule (section 5) gives \(\cos\!\left(x+\tfrac\pi2\right)=-\sin x\). (It can also be proved from the definition exactly like \(\sin x\), using \(\cos(A+B)=\cos A\cos B-\sin A\sin B\).)
  • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan x=\sec^2x\): Quotient Rule on \(\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\) gives \(\dfrac{\cos x\cos x-\sin x(-\sin x)}{\cos^2x}=\dfrac{1}{\cos^2x}\) (Group work 3).
  • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sec x=\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{1}{\cos x}=\dfrac{0-1\cdot(-\sin x)}{\cos^2x}=\dfrac{1}{\cos x}\cdot\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}=\sec x\tan x\).
  • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\csc x=\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{1}{\sin x}=\dfrac{0-1\cdot\cos x}{\sin^2x}=-\csc x\cot x\).
  • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cot x=\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}=\dfrac{-\sin^2x-\cos^2x}{\sin^2x}=-\csc^2x\).

Pattern to remember: the three "co" functions (\(\cos\), \(\csc\), \(\cot\)) have a minus sign in their derivative.

You can now: state the six trigonometric derivatives with the right signs; explain why \(\sin\) differentiates to \(\cos\) and why radians are needed; and derive \(\tan\), \(\sec\), \(\csc\) and \(\cot\) from \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) with the Quotient Rule.

4Using the rules ~3 min

What is this?

Back to the growing rectangle of section 2. To know how fast its area is growing right now, you do not need its whole history. You need four numbers: the width and the height at this moment, and how fast each is growing. Example 3 is exactly this, read off two graphs. Example 2 is the other half of good practice: before choosing a rule, see whether algebra can turn the function into something simpler.

Example 2: simplify first

Find the derivative of

\[f(x)=\frac{(x^2+1)(x^3-2x)}{x}.\]

Solution. The Quotient Rule (with a Product Rule inside) would work, but it is much faster to simplify first. For \(x\ne0\), cancel the \(x\) in \(x^3-2x=x(x^2-2)\):

\[f(x)=(x^2+1)(x^2-2)=x^4-x^2-2.\]

A plain sum of powers needs only the Power Rule term by term, so there is nothing left to multiply or divide.

Now differentiate term by term with the Power Rule:

\[f'(x)=4x^3-2x\qquad(x\ne0).\]

(\(f\) is not defined at \(x=0\), so neither is \(f'\).)

Lesson: before reaching for the Product or Quotient Rule, check whether expanding or cancelling turns the function into a plain sum of powers.

Example 3: rules from a graph

The graphs of \(F\) and \(G\) are shown below. Find

\[\text{(a)}\ \big(F(x)G(x)\big)'(2)\qquad\text{(b)}\ \left(\frac{F(x)}{G(x)}\right)'(2).\]

The graphs of F and G

FGtangent lines

Read from the grid: \(F\) is the parabola \(F(x)=(x-2)^2+3\) up to its corner at \((3,4)\), then a straight line of slope \(\tfrac14\). \(G\) is a straight line of slope \(\tfrac12\) up to its peak \((4,3)\), then a line of slope \(-1\). At a corner there is no single tangent, so the derivative does not exist there (Lecture 5).

Reading the graphs at \(x=2\). \(F\) has its lowest point at \((2,3)\), so \(F(2)=3\) and the tangent is horizontal: \(F'(2)=0\). \(G\) is a straight line through \((0,1)\) and \((4,3)\), so \(G(2)=2\) and \(G'(2)=\) slope \(=\dfrac{3-1}{4-0}=\dfrac12\).

(a) Product Rule:

\[\begin{aligned}(FG)'(2)&=F(2)G'(2)+F'(2)G(2)\\&=3\cdot\tfrac12+0\cdot2=\tfrac32.\end{aligned}\]

In the rectangle picture: width 3 with height growing at \(\tfrac12\) gives the first strip; the width is momentarily not growing (\(F\) is at its lowest point), so the second strip contributes 0.

(b) Quotient Rule:

\[\begin{aligned}\left(\frac FG\right)'(2)&=\frac{F'(2)G(2)-F(2)G'(2)}{[G(2)]^2}\\&=\frac{0\cdot2-3\cdot\frac12}{2^2}=-\frac38.\end{aligned}\]

Lesson: to use the rules at one point you only need four numbers: the value and the slope of each function at that point. You never need a formula for the whole function.

The values are read from the grid; the readings \(F(2)=3\), \(F'(2)=0\), \(G(2)=2\), \(G'(2)=\tfrac12\) are clear from it (minimum point of \(F\); straight line \(G\) through grid points).

You can now: simplify before differentiating when algebra allows; and find the derivative of a product or quotient at one point from just the values and slopes of the two functions there, including values read from a graph.

5The Chain Rule ~7 min

What is this?

Picture a train of gears. You turn the first gear by hand (that is \(x\)); it drives a middle gear (\(u=g(x)\)), which drives the last gear (\(y=f(u)\)). Each pair has a gear ratio: how many turns the next gear makes per turn of the one before. The overall ratio is the product of the ratios along the train. A composite function works the same way, with one difference: a function's "gear ratio" (its derivative) can change from place to place, so the outer ratio must be read where the inner gear has put it. A negative ratio is like two meshing gears turning in opposite directions.

A composite function is "a function of a function": first apply \(g\), then apply \(f\) to the result. It is written \((f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x))\) (read "f of g of x", or "f composed with g"). For example \(\cos^8(3t+5)\) is built in layers: \(3t+5\), then \(\cos\) of that, then the 8th power of that.

The picture: a chain of changes

Picture three number lines: \(x\), then \(u=g(x)\), then \(y=f(u)=f(g(x))\). A small change in \(x\) causes a change in \(u\) that is about \(g'(x)\) times as big (\(g\) changes at rate \(g'(x)\)). That change in \(u\) causes a change in \(y\) about \(f'(u)=f'(g(x))\) times as big. So overall, \(y\) changes \(f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)\) times as fast as \(x\): rates of change multiply along the chain.

Analogy (gears). If gear B turns 3 times for each turn of gear A, and gear C turns 2 times for each turn of B, then C turns \(3\times2=6\) times for each turn of A.

How a small change travels along the chain

Here \(u=g(x)=x^2\) and \(y=f(u)=\sin u\), so \(y=\sin(x^2)\). The shaded interval of length \(\Delta x\) on the top line is carried by \(g\) to an interval of length \(\Delta u\), and then by \(f\) to an interval of length \(\Delta y\). The stretch factors \(\frac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}\) and \(\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta u}\) multiply to \(\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\). As \(\Delta x\) shrinks they approach \(g'(x)=2x\), \(f'(u)=\cos u\) and \(\cos(x^2)\cdot2x\). (A negative factor means the interval is flipped.)

Theorem: Chain Rule

If \(f(u)\) is differentiable at the point \(u=g(x)\) and \(g(x)\) is differentiable at \(x\), then the composite function \((f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x))\) is differentiable at \(x\) and

\[\frac{d}{dx}(f\circ g)(x)=f'(g(x))\,g'(x).\]

In words: derivative of (outer of inner) = (derivative of outer, evaluated at the inner) × (derivative of inner).

With \(y=f(u)\) and \(u=g(x)\) this is often written in Leibniz notation:

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}.\]

In words: (rate of \(y\) per \(x\)) = (rate of \(y\) per \(u\)) × (rate of \(u\) per \(x\)), like the gears.

Reading the formula
\(f'(g(x))\)
the ratio of the outer gear, read at the position \(g(x)\) that the inner gear has delivered.
\(g'(x)\)
the ratio of the inner gear: how fast \(u\) turns per turn of \(x\).
\(\times\)
ratios along a chain multiply, never add.
\(\dfrac{dy}{du}\cdot\dfrac{du}{dx}\)
the same statement in Leibniz notation; it looks like fractions cancelling \(du\), which is a good memory aid but not a proof.

Working method: "outside in".

  1. Do: Differentiate the outermost layer, leaving what is inside it untouched. This is the last gear's ratio, read at the position the inner gears have put it in: \(f'\) evaluated at \(g(x)\).
  2. Do: Then multiply by the derivative of what was inside. The inner gear passes its own ratio on, and ratios multiply along the chain.
  3. Do: Repeat until you reach plain \(x\). Each layer is one more gear; you stop at the gear you turn by hand.

The proof, explained

Multiply and divide by \(g(x+h)-g(x)\), which is allowed as long as it is not zero:

\[\begin{aligned}[(f\circ g)(x)]'&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(g(x+h))-f(g(x))}{h}\\ &=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(g(x+h))-f(g(x))}{g(x+h)-g(x)}\cdot\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}.\end{aligned}\]

This splits the overall ratio into the two gear ratios: change in \(y\) per change in \(u\), times change in \(u\) per change in \(x\).

Now name the change in the inside: let \(u=g(x)\) and \(k=g(x+h)-g(x)\), so \(g(x+h)=u+k\). Because \(g\) is differentiable, it is continuous (Lecture 5), so \(k\to0\) as \(h\to0\). Therefore

\[\begin{aligned}[(f\circ g)(x)]'&=\lim_{k\to0}\frac{f(u+k)-f(u)}{k}\cdot\lim_{h\to0}\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}\\&=f'(u)\,g'(x)=f'(g(x))\,g'(x).\end{aligned}\]

One more case: if \(g(x+h)-g(x)=0\), then \(g'(x)=[(f\circ g)(x)]'=0\). The idea: if the inside does not move, neither does the outside, so both rates are 0 and the formula \(f'(g(x))\cdot0=0\) still holds.

Going further: the gap in this proof

The division step needs \(g(x+h)-g(x)\ne0\) for all small \(h\ne0\). Some functions (for example \(g(x)=x^2\sin\frac1x\) with \(g(0)=0\)) hit the value \(g(x)\) again at infinitely many \(h\) close to 0, so the "one case or the other" split is not quite enough. A more careful proof fixes this by writing \(f(u+k)-f(u)=\big(f'(u)+\varepsilon(k)\big)k\), where \(\varepsilon(k)\to0\) as \(k\to0\) and \(\varepsilon(0)=0\); this form never divides by \(k\). The conclusion of the theorem is correct; only the proof needs this extra care. Exams ask you to use the Chain Rule, not to prove it.

Example 4

Find the derivative of \(f(t)=\cos^8(3t+5)\).

Solution. \(\cos^8(3t+5)\) means \(\big[\cos(3t+5)\big]^8\). Three layers, outside in:

  1. Do: Outer: \((\ldots)^8\). Derivative \(8(\ldots)^7\), so \(8\cos^7(3t+5)\). The power is the last gear; it acts on the value \(\cos(3t+5)\), which stays untouched inside.
  2. Do: Middle: \(\cos(\ldots)\). Derivative \(-\sin(\ldots)\), so \(-\sin(3t+5)\). The cosine gear's ratio, read at its own input \(3t+5\).
  3. Do: Inner: \(3t+5\). Derivative \(3\). The first gear: turning \(t\) turns \(3t+5\) three times as fast.

Multiply:

\[\begin{aligned}f'(t)&=8\cos^7(3t+5)\cdot\big(-\sin(3t+5)\big)\cdot3\\&=-24\cos^7(3t+5)\sin(3t+5).\end{aligned}\]
Example 5

Find the derivative of

\[g(x)=\sqrt{\sqrt{x+1}+1}.\]

Solution. Use \(\sqrt{w}=w^{1/2}\), whose derivative is \(\tfrac12w^{-1/2}=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt w}\) (Power Rule with \(n=\tfrac12\), see the footnote in section 1). Outside in:

  1. Do: Outer square root, inside \(\sqrt{x+1}+1\): gives \(\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{\sqrt{x+1}+1}}\). The outer gear's ratio \(\frac{1}{2\sqrt w}\), read at its input \(w=\sqrt{x+1}+1\).
  2. Do: Derivative of the inside \(\sqrt{x+1}+1\): the \(+1\) gives 0; \(\sqrt{x+1}\) is another chain, giving \(\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x+1}}\cdot1\). The inside is itself a two-gear train; adding 1 shifts the position but not the rate.
\[\begin{aligned}g'(x)&=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\sqrt{x+1}+1}}\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x+1}}\\&=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{x+1}\,\sqrt{\sqrt{x+1}+1}}\end{aligned}\]

for \(x\gt-1\). Check at \(x=3\): \(\sqrt{4}=2\), so \(g'(3)=\dfrac{1}{4\cdot2\cdot\sqrt3}=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{24}\approx0.0722\).

You can now: split a composite function into layers; differentiate it outside in, multiplying one factor per layer; explain the Chain Rule as gear ratios that multiply; and point out where the proof of the Chain Rule divides by \(g(x+h)-g(x)\).

6Implicit differentiation ~7 min

What is this?

Picture a path on a map given by a rule, such as "stay at distance 2 from the centre" (the circle \(x^2+y^2=4\)). You can walk along it and, at every step, ask which way you are heading, without ever writing the path as \(y=\) (a formula in \(x\)). Implicit differentiation does this: it follows the curve. The rule stays true at every point you pass, so both sides of the equation change at the same rate as you walk. As you step in \(x\), your \(y\) moves too, and that is where \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) comes in. The method gives no slope where the path runs straight up and down, and needs care at sharp tips and crossings.

So far every curve was given explicitly, as \(y=\) (a formula in \(x\)). Many curves are instead given implicitly: by an equation linking \(x\) and \(y\) that is not solved for \(y\). For example the circle \(x^2+y^2=4\). Solving gives \(y=\pm\sqrt{4-x^2}\), two functions (top half and bottom half). For other curves solving for \(y\) is messy or impossible, for example \(x^3+y^3=3xy\).

Definition

An implicit equation in \(x\) and \(y\) is an equation \(F(x,y)=\) constant (or any equation involving both letters) that is not written as \(y=f(x)\). Near most of its points, the curve still looks like the graph of some function \(y(x)\), even if we cannot write a formula for it; implicit differentiation finds the slope \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) of that function anyway.

In words: the curve is described by a rule that \(x\) and \(y\) must satisfy together, rather than a recipe "take \(x\), compute \(y\)".

Method: implicit differentiation

Given an implicit equation in terms of \(x\) and \(y\), compute \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) as follows.

  1. Do: Differentiate both sides of the implicit equation with respect to \(x\). The two sides are equal at every point of the curve, so as you walk along it they change at the same rate.
  2. Do: Apply the Chain Rule when differentiating \(y\): multiply by \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), since \(y\) is a function of \(x\). For example \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(y^3\right)=3y^2\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(xy)=x\dfrac{dy}{dx}+y\) by the Product Rule. As you step in \(x\), your height \(y\) on the path moves too: \(y\) is an inner gear driven by \(x\), with ratio \(\frac{dy}{dx}\).
  3. Do: Solve for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\): collect the terms containing \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) on one side and factor it out. \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) is the one unknown, your heading on the path, and it appears only to the first power, so it can always be solved for (unless its coefficient is 0).

In words: treat \(y\) as "some unknown function of \(x\)". Every time you differentiate something made of \(y\), the Chain Rule attaches a factor \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) (the derivative of the inner function \(y\)).

Compare: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(x^3\right)=3x^2\) but \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(y^3\right)=3y^2\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). The answer usually contains both \(x\) and \(y\): to get a number, substitute a point \((x,y)\) that lies on the curve.

Worked mini-example: the circle

For \(x^2+y^2=4\): differentiate both sides, \(2x+2y\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0\), soWalking along the circle the left side stays at 4, so its rate of change is 0; the \(y^2\) term picks up \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) because \(y\) moves as you walk.

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{x}{y}\qquad(y\ne0).\]

At \((\sqrt2,\sqrt2)\) the slope is \(-1\). Geometric check: the tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the radius; the radius to \((x,y)\) has slope \(\frac yx\), and \(-\frac xy\cdot\frac yx=-1\). At \((\pm2,0)\) the formula breaks down (\(y=0\)): the tangent there is vertical.

What is this? (tangent line)

The tangent line is the straight road that touches the curve at the point and heads in exactly the same direction as the curve there. Its slope is the heading that implicit differentiation found.

Tangent line

The tangent line to a curve at a point \((x_0,y_0)\) on it is the straight line through that point whose slope \(m\) is \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) evaluated at \((x_0,y_0)\):

\[y-y_0=m\,(x-x_0).\]

In words: (rise from the point) = slope × (run from the point). This is the point-slope form of a straight line from HKDSE Core.

Going further: tangent-line approximation

Close to the point of contact, a smooth curve and its tangent line almost coincide. So to estimate the \(y\)-value of the curve at a nearby \(x\), plug that \(x\) into the tangent line instead:

\[y\approx y_0+m\,(x-x_0)\quad\text{for }x\text{ near }x_0.\]

In words: new height ≈ old height + slope × (step in \(x\)).

This is called linear approximation (or linearisation). It is examined (see the May 2026 question in the exam taster) even though Lecture 6 does not name it. Example: on \(x^2+y^2=4\) near \((\sqrt2,\sqrt2)\approx(1.414,1.414)\), at \(x=1.5\) the tangent gives \(y\approx1.414-1\cdot(1.5-1.414)=1.328\); the true value \(\sqrt{4-2.25}\approx1.323\).

Example 6

Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) of

\[\frac{x}{y}=1+x^2y.\]

Solution (directly). Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\). Left: Quotient Rule, remembering that \(y\) has derivative \(y'=\frac{dy}{dx}\). Right: Product Rule on \(x^2y\).

\[\frac{1\cdot y-x\,y'}{y^2}=2xy+x^2y'.\]

Multiply both sides by \(y^2\):This clears the fraction, so the \(y'\) terms are easy to collect.

\[y-x\,y'=2xy^3+x^2y^2\,y'.\]

Collect the \(y'\) terms on one side and factor:

\[y-2xy^3=y'\left(x+x^2y^2\right)\] \[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{y-2xy^3}{x+x^2y^2}=\frac{y\,(1-2xy^2)}{x\,(1+xy^2)}.\]

Alternative (clear the fraction first). Multiply the equation by \(y\): \(x=y+x^2y^2\). Differentiate: \(1=y'+2xy^2+2x^2y\,y'\), so

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1-2xy^2}{1+2x^2y}.\]

The two answers look different but agree at every point of the curve (they are linked by the curve's own equation). For example at the point with \(y=\tfrac12\) and \(x=2-\sqrt2\approx0.586\), both give \(\approx0.526\). In an exam, either form is accepted.

You can now: find \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) for a curve given by an equation in \(x\) and \(y\); evaluate it at a point after checking the point is on the curve; write the tangent line \(y-y_0=m(x-x_0)\); and use it to approximate the curve nearby.

7Higher derivatives ~2 min

What is this?

Back to the car. The odometer reading is the position \(f\); the speedometer shows its rate of change \(f'\). Press the accelerator and the speedometer needle itself moves: the rate at which the speed changes is \(f''\), the acceleration. Higher derivatives keep asking the same question, "how fast is this changing?", one level up each time.

The derivative \(f'\) is itself a function, so it can be differentiated again.

Definition

The second derivative is the derivative of the derivative: \(f''(x)=\big(f'(x)\big)'\), also written \(y''\) or \(\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\). Continuing gives the third derivative \(f'''\), and in general the \(n\)th derivative \(f^{(n)}(x)\) or \(\dfrac{d^ny}{dx^n}\) (differentiate \(n\) times). The brackets in \(f^{(n)}\) distinguish it from the power \(f^n\).

In words: \(f''\) = rate of change of the slope. If \(f\) is position against time, \(f'\) is velocity and \(f''\) is acceleration.

Example 7

Find the derivatives of all orders of

\[y=\frac{x^4}{2}-\frac32x^2-x.\]

Solution. Differentiate repeatedly with the Power Rule:

\[\begin{aligned}y'&=2x^3-3x-1\\ y''&=6x^2-3\\ y'''&=12x\\ y^{(4)}&=12\\ y^{(n)}&=0\quad\text{for all }n\ge5.\end{aligned}\]

Each differentiation lowers the degree of a polynomial by one; after the degree reaches 0 (a constant), every further derivative is 0. In general a polynomial of degree \(n\) has \(f^{(n+1)}=0\).

You can now: find second and higher derivatives, including all derivatives of a polynomial; and read \(f''\) as the rate of change of the slope.

8Summary

  • The table of rules in section 1: Constant, Constant Multiple, Power (integer powers; all real powers after Lecture 7), Sum/Difference, Product, Quotient and the six trigonometric derivatives.
  • The Chain Rule \(\big(f(g(x))\big)'=f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\) is a powerful and efficient differentiation rule for composite functions, and it is what makes implicit differentiation work.
  • Use implicit differentiation to differentiate implicit equations: differentiate both sides, attach \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) whenever you differentiate something containing \(y\), then solve for \(\frac{dy}{dx}\).

9Common mistakes

1. Applying false differentiation "rules"

✗\(\big[(x^2)(3x+1)\big]'=(2x)(3)\). The derivative of a product is not the product of derivatives. ✓ Product Rule: \(x^2\cdot3+2x(3x+1)=9x^2+2x\). ✗\(\left(\dfrac{x^2}{3x+1}\right)'=\dfrac{2x}{3}\). Not the quotient of derivatives. ✓ Quotient Rule: \(\dfrac{2x(3x+1)-x^2\cdot3}{(3x+1)^2}=\dfrac{3x^2+2x}{(3x+1)^2}\). ✗\(\big[\sin(3x+1)\big]'=\cos(3x+1)\). The inner derivative was forgotten. ✓ Chain Rule: \(3\cos(3x+1)\).

2. Substituting values before taking the derivative

✗If \(f(x)=x^2\), then \(f(2)=4\) and \(f'(2)=0\) (the derivative of the constant 4). ✓ Differentiate first, then substitute: \(f'(x)=2x\), so \(f'(2)=4\). The number \(f(2)\) is one value; the slope depends on how \(f\) behaves near 2.

3. Attempting to solve for \(y\) in an implicit equation

✗Solving \(y^5+y+x=0\) for \(y\) is impossible with a formula. ✓ Differentiate implicitly instead: \(5y^4y'+y'+1=0\), so \(y'=-\dfrac{1}{5y^4+1}\).

4. Omitting the Product Rule and Chain Rule (multiplying by \(\frac{dy}{dx}\)) when differentiating \(y\) in implicit equations

✗Given \(xy=4\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(xy)=x+y=0\). ✓ Product Rule with \(y'\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(xy)=x\,y'+y=0\), so \(y'=-\dfrac yx\). ✗Given \(x^2+y^2=1\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(x^2+y^2\right)=2x+2y=0\). ✓ Chain Rule on \(y^2\): \(2x+2y\,y'=0\), so \(y'=-\dfrac xy\).

More traps

✗\(\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)'=\dfrac{f g'-f'g}{g^2}\) (order reversed): this gives the negative of the right answer. ✓ Top's derivative comes first: \(\dfrac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}\). ✗\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin^2x=2\sin x\). ✓ \(\sin^2x=(\sin x)^2\) is a chain: \(2\sin x\cos x\). ✗\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos x=\sin x\). ✓ \(-\sin x\): the "co" functions carry a minus sign. ✗Substituting a point that is not on an implicit curve. ✓ First check the point satisfies the equation; otherwise the slope formula means nothing there.

10Find the mistake

Question. Find the slope of the tangent line at the point \((0,1)\) to the curve

\[y+x\ln y-2x=\cos xy.\]

(Here \(\ln y\) is the natural logarithm, introduced properly in Lecture 7; you only need \(\dfrac{d}{dy}\ln y=\dfrac1y\) and \(\ln1=0\). \(\cos xy\) means \(\cos(xy)\).)

A student's handwritten work (reproduced line by line):

\[y+x\ln y-2x=\cos xy\] \[\frac{dy}{dx}+\ln y-2=-y\sin x\] \[\frac{dy}{dx}=-y\sin x-\ln y+2\]

Sub \(x=0\), \(y=1\):

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=-(1)\sin(0)-\ln(1)+2=2\]
Hint 1

Look at the term \(x\ln y\). It is a product of two things that both change with \(x\). What rule is needed, and what does differentiating \(\ln y\) produce?

Hint 2

Look at the right-hand side. What is the inner function of \(\cos(xy)\), and what is its derivative with respect to \(x\)?

Answer

Two mistakes in line 2.

  1. \(x\ln y\) needs the Product Rule and the Chain Rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x\ln y)=\ln y+x\cdot\dfrac1y\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). The student wrote only \(\ln y\).
  2. \(\cos(xy)\) needs the Chain Rule with inner function \(xy\), whose derivative is \(y+x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) (Product Rule): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos(xy)=-\sin(xy)\left(y+x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)\). The student wrote \(-y\sin x\), which is neither.

Correct working. First check \((0,1)\) is on the curve: \(1+0-0=1=\cos0\). ✓ Differentiating:

\[\begin{aligned}&\frac{dy}{dx}+\ln y+\frac xy\frac{dy}{dx}-2\\&\quad=-\sin(xy)\left(y+x\frac{dy}{dx}\right).\end{aligned}\]

Substitute \(x=0,\ y=1\): \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}+0+0-2=-\sin0\cdot(1+0)=0\), so

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=2.\]

The student's final answer happens to be right, but only by luck: at \(x=0\) every term they lost is multiplied by \(x\) or by \(\sin0\), so it vanishes. At any other point the student's formula would give a wrong slope, and a marker would not award the method marks. The tangent line is \(y-1=2(x-0)\), that is \(y=2x+1\).

Product Rule with first factor \(x^2+3x-1\) (derivative \(2x+3\)) and second factor \(5x+1\) (derivative 5). Or expand first and use the Power Rule.

Hint

Product Rule; the first factor \(\sin^3\theta=(\sin\theta)^3\) needs the Chain Rule: its derivative is \(3\sin^2\theta\cos\theta\).

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}&3\sin^2\theta\cos\theta\cdot\cos\theta+\sin^3\theta\cdot(-\sin\theta)\\&=3\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta-\sin^4\theta.\end{aligned}\]

Equivalent form: \(\sin^2\theta\left(3\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta\right)\).

Hint

Write \(\tan x=\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\) and use the Quotient Rule, then \(\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1\).

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}(\tan x)'&=\frac{\cos x\cdot\cos x-\sin x\cdot(-\sin x)}{\cos^2x}\\&=\frac{\cos^2x+\sin^2x}{\cos^2x}\\&=\frac{1}{\cos^2x}=\sec^2x.\end{aligned}\]

Hint 2

Find \(y'\) with the Quotient Rule and solve \(y'=\tfrac12\). There are two solutions.

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}y'&=\frac{1\cdot(x+1)-(x-1)\cdot1}{(x+1)^2}\\&=\frac{2}{(x+1)^2}.\end{aligned}\]

  • \(x=1\): \(y=0\). Tangent \(y-0=\tfrac12(x-1)\), i.e. \(y=\tfrac12x-\tfrac12\) (or \(x-2y=1\)).
  • \(x=-3\): \(y=\dfrac{-4}{-2}=2\). Tangent \(y-2=\tfrac12(x+3)\), i.e. \(y=\tfrac12x+\tfrac72\) (or \(x-2y=-7\)).

9. For \(f(x)=\cos(2x)\), find \(f^{(50)}(x)\).

Hint

Work out the first four derivatives. Each one multiplies by 2, and the trig function cycles with period 4. Then find the remainder of 50 ÷ 4.

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}f'&=-2\sin2x,& f''&=-4\cos2x,\\ f'''&=8\sin2x,& f^{(4)}&=16\cos2x.\end{aligned}\]

The pattern repeats every 4 derivatives (with an extra factor \(2^4\)). Since \(50=4\cdot12+2\), \(f^{(50)}\) looks like \(f''\) with factor \(2^{50}\):

\[f^{(50)}(x)=-2^{50}\cos(2x).\]

10. Find a cubic function \(ax^3+bx^2+cx+d\) whose graph has horizontal tangents at the points \((-2,6)\) and \((2,0)\).

Hint 1

"Horizontal tangent" means the derivative is 0. That gives two equations \(f'(-2)=0\), \(f'(2)=0\); the points give two more, \(f(-2)=6\), \(f(2)=0\).

Hint 2

\(f'(x)=3ax^2+2bx+c\). Subtracting \(f'(2)=0\) and \(f'(-2)=0\) gives \(b=0\) quickly.

Answer

\(f'(\pm2)=12a\pm4b+c=0\) gives \(b=0\) and \(c=-12a\). Then \(f(2)=8a+2c+d=-16a+d=0\) and \(f(-2)=-8a-2c+d=16a+d=6\). Adding: \(2d=6\), \(d=3\), \(a=\tfrac{3}{16}\), \(c=-\tfrac94\).

\[f(x)=\frac{3}{16}x^3-\frac94x+3.\]

12Concept checks

1. A classmate says \((fg)'=f'g'\). Give the simplest example showing this is false.

Answer

Take \(f(x)=g(x)=x\). Then \(fg=x^2\) with derivative \(2x\), but \(f'g'=1\cdot1=1\). These differ (for example at \(x=1\): 2 versus 1).

2. Differentiate \((x^2+1)^{100}\) without expanding. Why would expanding be a bad idea?

Answer

Chain Rule: \(100(x^2+1)^{99}\cdot2x=200x(x^2+1)^{99}\). Expanding would produce 101 terms; the Chain Rule handles the whole power at once.

3. Differentiate \(\sin^2x+\cos^2x\) with the rules. Explain why the answer had to come out that way.

Answer

\(2\sin x\cos x+2\cos x(-\sin x)=0\). It had to be 0 because \(\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1\) is a constant, and the Constant Rule gives 0. This is a good self-check that the rules for \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) (with the minus sign) are consistent.

4. On the circle \(x^2+y^2=25\), find the slope at \((3,4)\). At which points is the tangent horizontal, and at which is it vertical?

Answer

\(y'=-\dfrac xy\), so the slope at \((3,4)\) is \(-\tfrac34\). Horizontal tangent when \(y'=0\), i.e. \(x=0\): points \((0,5)\) and \((0,-5)\). Vertical tangent where the denominator \(y=0\): points \((5,0)\) and \((-5,0)\).

5. Suppose \(f(1)=2\) and \(f'(1)=3\). Find the derivative at \(x=1\) of (a) \(f(x^2)\) and (b) \([f(x)]^2\).

Answer

(a) Chain Rule with inner \(x^2\): \(f'(x^2)\cdot2x\); at \(x=1\): \(f'(1)\cdot2=6\). (b) Chain Rule with outer "square": \(2f(x)f'(x)\); at \(x=1\): \(2\cdot2\cdot3=12\). The two look alike but put the square in different places.

6. Why must angles be in radians for \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin x=\cos x\)?

Answer

The proof uses \(\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1\), which holds only when \(h\) is in radians. If \(x\) is in degrees, \(\sin(x^\circ)=\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi x}{180}\right)\), and the Chain Rule gives the derivative \(\frac{\pi}{180}\cos(x^\circ)\): an unwanted extra factor.

7. Without computing, what is the 7th derivative of a polynomial of degree 6? What about its 6th derivative, if the leading term is \(ax^6\)?

Answer

The 7th derivative is 0 (each differentiation lowers the degree by one, as in Example 7). The 6th derivative is the constant \(6!\,a=720a\) (\(6\cdot5\cdot4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1\) from bringing the power down six times).
