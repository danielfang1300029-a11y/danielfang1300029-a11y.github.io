L'Hôpital's Rule

0Where this lecture sits

Some limits are a race between the top and the bottom of a fraction: both head to 0, or both run off to infinity, and the answer depends on who gets there faster. Lectures 1 to 3 settled such races with algebra (factorise, rationalise, divide by the highest power, squeeze). Lecture 10 settles them with derivatives, the runners' speeds.

Chapter on one page

  2. §2 When both effects pull the same way, the form is determinate and the answer is known at once.

  8. §8 Summary; §9–§10 the mistakes that cost marks; §11–§12 practice; §13 exam taster.

1Indeterminate forms ~4 min

What is this?

Picture a tug-of-war between two teams whose strengths you have not been told. Knowing only that both teams are pulling tells you nothing about where the rope ends up. An indeterminate form is that situation for a limit: in \(\frac00\) a shrinking top pulls the fraction towards 0 while a shrinking bottom pulls it towards infinity. The form names the two teams; it does not name the winner. Deciding the winner needs a closer look at how hard each side pulls.

History

Guillaume de l'Hôpital (1661–1704), a French nobleman, wrote the first calculus textbook, Analyse des Infiniment Petits ("Analysis of the infinitely small", 1696). The rule named after him appears in it, with this example:

\[ \begin{gathered}\lim_{x\to a}\frac{\sqrt{2a^3x-x^4}-a\sqrt[3]{a^2x}}{a-\sqrt[4]{ax^3}}=\ ?\\ (a\gt0\text{ a constant})\end{gathered} \]

Substituting \(x=a\) gives \(\dfrac{\sqrt{a^4}-a\cdot a}{a-a}=\dfrac{0}{0}\). No factorising trick works here. We solve it in section 4.

Going further

The rule was actually discovered by l'Hôpital's teacher, Johann Bernoulli. L'Hôpital paid Bernoulli for his mathematical results and published them in his book, so the rule carries the pupil's name. The spelling "L'Hospital" (used in some exam papers) is the older French spelling of the same name.

Definition

An indeterminate form is a limit that cannot be determined by the Limit Laws alone and requires additional methods to evaluate.

In words: plugging in gives a shape like \(\frac00\) that does not tell you the answer. The answer could be any number, \(\pm\infty\), or "does not exist" (DNE), depending on the actual functions.

The seven indeterminate forms are written symbolically as

\[ \begin{gathered}\frac00,\qquad \frac{\infty}{\infty},\qquad \infty-\infty,\qquad 0\cdot\infty,\\ \infty^0,\qquad 1^\infty,\qquad 0^0.\end{gathered} \]

These symbols are shorthand, not arithmetic. "\(\frac00\)" means "the top tends to 0 and the bottom tends to 0"; it does not mean you divide 0 by 0. Likewise "\(1^\infty\)" means "the base tends to 1 while the exponent grows without bound". (The Limit Laws of Lecture 1 say, for example, that the limit of a quotient is the quotient of the limits; they need the separate limits to be finite, and the bottom one non-zero, so they fail on every form above.)

Why "indeterminate"? Each form describes two effects pulling in opposite directions. In \(\frac00\), a shrinking top pushes the fraction to 0 while a shrinking bottom pushes it to \(\infty\); which wins depends on how fast each shrinks. In \(1^\infty\), a base near 1 pulls towards 1, while a huge exponent magnifies any tiny difference from 1. Analogy: a tug-of-war between two teams whose strengths you have not been told. Knowing that "both teams are pulling" does not tell you who wins.

Additional methods

The methods available are algebraic manipulation, the Sandwich Theorem, L'Hôpital's Rule and the logarithmic transformation. Here is one limit of each type that you have met or will meet:

2Classifying forms: indeterminate or determinate? ~3 min

What is this?

Now let both teams pull the same way. There is no contest: the rope goes that way. A tiny top over a huge bottom is like that: both make the fraction smaller, so it heads to 0 whatever the details. The question to ask about every form is always the same: do the two effects fight (indeterminate) or help each other (determinate)?

Consider \(\lim F(x)\), where "lim" may mean \(x\to a\), \(x\to a^\pm\) (one-sided) or \(x\to\pm\infty\), and \(F(x)\) is a quotient \(\frac{f}{g}\), a difference \(f-g\), a product \(fg\) or a power \(f^g\). Look at what \(f\) and \(g\) tend to separately:

FormTypePossible result
\(\frac00\), \(\frac\infty\infty\), \(\infty-\infty\), \(0\cdot\infty\), \(\infty^0\), \(1^\infty\), \(0^0\)indeterminatea real number, \(\pm\infty\), or DNE: more work needed
\(\frac{0}{\infty}\)determinate\(0\)
\(\frac{\infty}{0}\)determinate\(\pm\infty\)
\(0^\infty\)determinate\(0\)

Why the last three are determinate (the answer is known from the form alone):

  • \(\frac0\infty\): a tiny number divided by a huge number is tinier still, so the limit is 0. Both effects push the same way.
  • \(\frac\infty0\): a huge number divided by a tiny number is huger still, so the size grows without bound. The sign comes from the signs of top and bottom (as in Lecture 3). If the sign differs on the two sides, the two one-sided limits are \(+\infty\) and \(-\infty\) and the two-sided limit DNE.
  • \(0^\infty\) (base positive and tending to 0, exponent \(\to+\infty\)): a small positive number raised to a huge power is even smaller, e.g. \(\left(\frac12\right)^{100}\approx 8\times10^{-31}\). The limit is 0.

In words: a form is determinate when both effects push the same way, and indeterminate when they fight.

Going further: more determinate forms

These are also determinate (and often appear inside exam limits): \(\infty+\infty=\infty\); \(\infty\cdot\infty=\infty\); \((\text{non-zero number})\cdot\infty=\pm\infty\); \(\frac{\text{number}}{\infty}=0\); \(\frac{\text{non-zero number}}{0}=\pm\infty\) (check signs); \(0^{-\infty}=\infty\) for a positive base; \(\infty^{\infty}=\infty\). Only the seven indeterminate forms of section 1 need extra work.

3L'Hôpital's Rule ~8 min

What is this?

Think of the top and the bottom as two runners who leave the same start line at the same moment: at the point, both are at 0. A split second later, the one who has run twice as far is the one running twice as fast, so the ratio of distances is the ratio of speeds. Zoom in on the two graphs and you see the same thing: both look like straight lines through zero, and the ratio of two such lines is the ratio of their slopes. The picture needs a real race (both at 0, or both running off to infinity), a speed for each runner at every moment (differentiable), a bottom runner who never stands still near the point (\(g'\ne0\)), and a ratio of speeds that settles down.

Theorem: L'Hôpital's Rule

If \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable functions, \(g'(a)\ne0\), and \(\lim\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) is an indeterminate form \(\dfrac00\) or \(\dfrac\infty\infty\), then

\[ \lim\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\lim\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}, \]

provided this limit exists (or is \(\pm\infty\)). Here "lim" refers to \(x\to a\), \(x\to a^\pm\) or \(x\to\pm\infty\).

In words: limit of (top ÷ bottom) = limit of (rate of change of top ÷ rate of change of bottom), as long as you started with \(\frac00\) or \(\frac\infty\infty\) and the new limit exists or is \(\pm\infty\).

Reading the formula
\(f(x),\ g(x)\)
the two runners, top and bottom. Both are at 0 at the point (\(\frac00\)), or both run off to infinity (\(\frac\infty\infty\)): a genuine race.
\(\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\)
how far the top runner has gone for each unit the bottom runner has gone.
\(f'(x),\ g'(x)\)
their speeds at the moment \(x\). Both must exist (\(f\), \(g\) differentiable).
\(\dfrac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}\)
the ratio of speeds. It only makes sense if the bottom runner keeps moving: \(g'\ne0\) near the point.
provided this limit exists
the ratio of speeds must settle (or grow without bound). If it keeps swinging, the rule tells you nothing (section 9).

On the hypothesis: the theorem above states the condition as \(g'(a)\ne0\). The more common version asks instead that \(g'(x)\ne0\) for all \(x\) near \(a\) (except possibly at \(a\) itself); for \(x\to\pm\infty\) it means \(g'(x)\ne0\) for all large \(|x|\). This is the condition used in the proof in section 7. In practice: the rule needs \(g'\) to be non-zero close to the point you approach.

Three things to notice:

  • Differentiate top and bottom separately. \(\frac{f'}{g'}\) is NOT the derivative of \(\frac fg\); do not use the Quotient Rule (Lecture 6).
  • Only two forms qualify: \(\frac00\) and \(\frac\infty\infty\). Every other indeterminate form must first be rewritten as one of these two.
  • One-way street. If \(\lim\frac{f'}{g'}\) exists or is \(\pm\infty\), it equals \(\lim\frac fg\). If \(\lim\frac{f'}{g'}\) does not exist, the rule says nothing (section 9).

Why it is believable: zoom in

Suppose \(f(a)=g(a)=0\). Zoomed in close to \(a\), a differentiable graph looks like its tangent line (Lecture 5), so

\[ \begin{gathered}f(x)\approx f'(a)(x-a),\\ g(x)\approx g'(a)(x-a)\\ \Longrightarrow\quad \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\approx\frac{f'(a)(x-a)}{g'(a)(x-a)}=\frac{f'(a)}{g'(a)}.\end{gathered} \]

In words: near the point, both top and bottom are straight lines through zero, and the ratio of two such lines is the ratio of their slopes.

Analogy. Two runners leave the same start line at the same moment. After a split second, the ratio of the distances they have run is (almost exactly) the ratio of their speeds. The distances are \(f\) and \(g\); the speeds are \(f'\) and \(g'\).

Rewriting the other forms

What is this? (rewriting the other forms)

The rule can only judge a race written as one fraction: one top against one bottom. A difference, a product or a power is a tug-of-war in a different costume. The job is to change the costume without changing the value, so the rule can judge it.

Rewrite indeterminate forms \(\infty-\infty\), \(0\cdot\infty\), \(\infty^0\), \(1^\infty\), \(0^0\) as indeterminate quotients \(\frac00\) or \(\frac\infty\infty\) to apply L'Hôpital's Rule:

FormRewriteExample
\(\infty-\infty\)combine into one fraction (common denominator, or trig identities), or rationaliseExample 4
\(0\cdot\infty\)\(fg=\dfrac{f}{1/g}\) or \(\dfrac{g}{1/f}\) (move one factor to the bottom as a reciprocal)Example 3
\(1^\infty,\ 0^0,\ \infty^0\)take logarithms: \(\ln\big(f^g\big)=g\ln f\), a \(0\cdot\infty\) form; then as aboveExamples 5 to 7

The same table as steps:

  1. Do: Find the limit of each piece separately and name the form. The form tells you which costume the tug-of-war is wearing, and so which rewrite to use.
  2. Do: \(\infty-\infty\): combine into one fraction (common denominator, or trig identities), or rationalise. The gap between two runaway quantities becomes one top racing one bottom, usually \(\frac00\).
  3. Do: \(0\cdot\infty\): move one factor to the bottom as a reciprocal, \(fg=\dfrac{f}{1/g}\). Dividing by \(\frac1g\) is the same as multiplying by \(g\), so the value is unchanged; a factor that runs off to infinity becomes a bottom that shrinks to 0, giving \(\frac00\) (or \(\frac\infty\infty\)).
  4. Do: \(1^\infty,\ 0^0,\ \infty^0\): take logarithms, \(\ln\big(f^g\big)=g\ln f\), a \(0\cdot\infty\) form; then as above. The logarithm turns "raise to the power \(g\)" into "multiply by \(g\)", so the power becomes a product (this needs \(f\gt0\)).
  5. Do: Apply the rule, then undo any logarithm by exponentiating. After taking logs you have found the limit of \(\ln y\), not of \(y\).
How to write it up so it earns marks
  1. Do: Check the form by substituting. Write it next to the limit, e.g. \(\left[\frac00\right]\). If it is not \(\frac00\) or \(\frac\infty\infty\), do NOT use the rule. The rule only judges a genuine race; if there is no tug-of-war the answer is already known, and the rule can give a wrong one (Example 2's trap).
  2. Do: Say "by L'Hôpital's Rule" and differentiate the top and bottom separately. You are swapping each runner's distance for its speed. The Quotient Rule would give the rate of change of the ratio, a different thing.
  3. Do: Simplify the new fraction before going further. Common factors often cancel, which makes the next form check quick and the next derivatives shorter.
  4. Do: Substitute again. If the result is a number or a determinate form, stop. The race is decided: the speeds no longer tie, so their ratio is the answer.
  5. Do: If it is again \(\frac00\) or \(\frac\infty\infty\), say so and repeat. Check the form before every application. Most lost marks come from applying the rule once too often. If both speeds are also 0 at the point, the runners are still level, so compare the next level down; each round must be a genuine race again.

You can now: state L'Hôpital's Rule with its conditions (\(\frac00\) or \(\frac\infty\infty\), \(f\) and \(g\) differentiable, \(g'\ne0\) near the point, the new limit exists or is \(\pm\infty\)); explain it by zooming in on the tangent lines; choose the rewrite for each of the other five forms; write up a solution with a form check before every application.

4Indeterminate quotients ~3 min

What is this?

Here the race is already written as one fraction. First make sure both runners really are level (both at 0, or both running off to infinity). Then compare their speeds. If the speeds give a clear answer, that is the limit; if the speeds are level too, compare again one level down, but only after checking that it is still a race.

Example 1

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^x-1}{x}\).

Solution. Check the form: top \(e^0-1=0\), bottom \(0\). Form \(\frac00\), and both are differentiable. By L'Hôpital's Rule, Both runners are at 0 when \(x=0\), so this is a genuine race and the rule may judge it.

\[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^x-1}{x}\ \overset{\left[\frac00\right]}{=}\ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^x}{1}=e^0=1. \]

In words: near 0, \(e^x-1\) and \(x\) shrink at the same rate (slope 1 each), so their ratio tends to 1.

This limit is also the definition of the derivative of \(e^x\) at 0 (Lecture 5): \(\lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^{0+h}-e^0}{h}\). Remember the result \(\frac{e^x-1}{x}\to1\); it reappears in many past paper questions.

Example 2

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x^2}\).

Solution. Check the form: \(\sin0=0\), \(0^2=0\). Form \(\frac00\). By L'Hôpital's Rule,

\[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x^2}\ \overset{\left[\frac00\right]}{=}\ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\cos x}{2x}. \]

Check the form again: top \(\to\cos0=1\), bottom \(\to0\). This is \(\frac10\), which is not indeterminate. Stop using the rule and decide the sign on each side (Lecture 3): The top has arrived at 1 while the bottom is still shrinking to 0. That is no longer a race: a fixed amount divided by something tiny is huge.

\[ \begin{gathered}\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{\cos x}{2x}=\frac{1}{0^+}=+\infty,\\ \lim_{x\to0^-}\frac{\cos x}{2x}=\frac{1}{0^-}=-\infty.\end{gathered} \]

The one-sided limits disagree, so \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x^2}\) does not exist (it is \(+\infty\) from the right and \(-\infty\) from the left).

Trap

Applying the rule a second time to \(\frac{\cos x}{2x}\) would give \(\lim\frac{-\sin x}{2}=0\), which is wrong. The second application was illegal because \(\frac10\) is not \(\frac00\) or \(\frac\infty\infty\).

L'Hôpital's own example, solved

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}\frac{\sqrt{2a^3x-x^4}-a\sqrt[3]{a^2x}}{a-\sqrt[4]{ax^3}}\) for a constant \(a\gt0\).

Solution. The form is \(\frac00\) (shown in section 1). Write the top as \(N(x)=(2a^3x-x^4)^{1/2}-a\,(a^2x)^{1/3}\) and the bottom as \(D(x)=a-(ax^3)^{1/4}=a-a^{1/4}x^{3/4}\). Using the power rule with the chain rule (Lectures 6 and 7):

\[ \begin{aligned}N'(x)&=\frac{2a^3-4x^3}{2\sqrt{2a^3x-x^4}}-\frac{a\cdot a^{2/3}}{3}\,x^{-2/3},\\ D'(x)&=-\frac34\,a^{1/4}x^{-1/4}.\end{aligned} \]

At \(x=a\): \(\sqrt{2a^4-a^4}=a^2\), so

\[ \begin{aligned}N'(a)&=\frac{-2a^3}{2a^2}-\frac{a^{5/3}}{3a^{2/3}}\\&=-a-\frac a3=-\frac{4a}{3},\\ D'(a)&=-\frac34.\end{aligned} \]

\(N'\) and \(D'\) are continuous near \(a\) and \(D'(a)\ne0\), so by L'Hôpital's Rule Continuity lets us find the limit of \(\frac{N'}{D'}\) by substituting \(x=a\); and since \(D'\) is continuous with \(D'(a)\ne0\), \(D'\ne0\) near \(a\) too, so the bottom runner keeps moving.

\[ \lim_{x\to a}\frac{N(x)}{D(x)}=\frac{N'(a)}{D'(a)}=\frac{-4a/3}{-3/4}=\frac{16a}{9}. \]

In words: a problem that defeats every algebraic trick becomes one line of differentiation.

You can now: evaluate a \(\frac00\) or \(\frac\infty\infty\) limit with a form check before each application; stop as soon as the form is determinate and find the sign of \(\frac c0\) from each side; use the chain rule on roots and powers inside the rule.

5Indeterminate products and differences ~2 min

What is this?

A product such as "tiny × huge" is a tug-of-war that is not yet written as a race. Move one factor to the bottom as its reciprocal and it becomes one: tiny over tiny, or huge over huge. A difference of two runaway quantities is similar: put them over one denominator, and the question "how far apart are they?" becomes one top racing one bottom.

Example 3: indeterminate product

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}e^{-x}(x^2+9)\).

Solution. As \(x\to\infty\), \(e^{-x}\to0\) and \(x^2+9\to\infty\): form \(0\cdot\infty\). Rewrite as a quotient by moving \(e^{-x}\) to the bottom as \(e^{x}\): Multiplying by \(e^{-x}\) is the same as dividing by \(e^{x}\), so the value is unchanged; now top and bottom both run off to infinity.

\[ e^{-x}(x^2+9)=\frac{x^2+9}{e^x}\qquad\left[\frac\infty\infty\right]. \]

Apply L'Hôpital's Rule, checking the form each time:

\[ \begin{aligned}\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^2+9}{e^x}\ &\overset{\left[\frac\infty\infty\right]}{=}\ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x}{e^x}\\ &\overset{\left[\frac\infty\infty\right]}{=}\ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2}{e^x}=0.\end{aligned} \]

The last step is \(\frac{2}{\infty}\), determinate, so we stop. After two rounds the top's speed is the constant 2 while the bottom's is still growing, so the bottom wins and the fraction goes to 0. So \(y=0\) is a horizontal asymptote of \(y=e^{-x}(x^2+9)\) as \(x\to\infty\).

In words: the exponential \(e^x\) grows faster than any power of \(x\), so (power) ÷ (exponential) → 0.

Choosing the rewrite. The other choice, \(\dfrac{e^{-x}}{1/(x^2+9)}\), is also \(\frac00\), but differentiating it gives \(\dfrac{-e^{-x}}{-2x/(x^2+9)^2}\), which is worse than before. Choose the rewrite whose derivatives get simpler (see the summary, section 8).

Example 4: indeterminate difference

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to(\pi/2)^-}(\sec x-\tan x)\).

Solution. Recall \(\sec x=\frac1{\cos x}\) and \(\tan x=\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\). As \(x\to\frac\pi2\) from the left, \(\cos x\to0^+\) and \(\sin x\to1\), so both \(\sec x\to+\infty\) and \(\tan x\to+\infty\): form \(\infty-\infty\). Combine into one fraction (same denominator): Both terms already share the bottom \(\cos x\), so the gap between the two infinities becomes a single top over a single bottom.

\[ \begin{aligned}\sec x-\tan x&=\frac{1}{\cos x}-\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\\&=\frac{1-\sin x}{\cos x}\qquad\left[\frac00\right].\end{aligned} \]

By L'Hôpital's Rule:

\[ \begin{aligned}\lim_{x\to(\pi/2)^-}\frac{1-\sin x}{\cos x}\ &\overset{\left[\frac00\right]}{=}\ \lim_{x\to(\pi/2)^-}\frac{-\cos x}{-\sin x}\\&=\frac{0}{1}=0.\end{aligned} \]

In words: the two infinities are "the same size" near \(\frac\pi2\): their difference shrinks to 0.

You can now: turn \(0\cdot\infty\) into a quotient, choosing which factor to move so the derivatives get simpler; turn \(\infty-\infty\) into one fraction; find a horizontal asymptote such as \(y=0\) for \(e^{-x}(x^2+9)\).

6Indeterminate powers: the logarithmic transformation ~6 min

What is this?

In a power the tug-of-war is between the base and the exponent: a base close to 1 pulls the answer towards 1, while a huge exponent magnifies the small gap from 1. The logarithm acts as a translator: it turns "raise to a power" into "multiply", so the power becomes a product, and section 5 already handles products. At the end you translate back with \(e\). The translator only works on a positive base, because \(\ln\) needs a positive input.

For a power \(f(x)^{g(x)}\) with form \(1^\infty\), \(0^0\) or \(\infty^0\), the exponent is the problem. The natural logarithm \(\ln\) brings an exponent down, \(\ln\big(f^g\big)=g\ln f\) (a law of logarithm), turning the power into a product. This needs \(f(x)\gt0\).

Logarithmic transformation

Set \(y=\lim f(x)^{g(x)}\) and evaluate as follows.

  1. Do: Take the natural logarithm of both sides: \[ \ln y=\ln\Big(\lim f(x)^{g(x)}\Big). \]This brings in the translator that will let the exponent come down.
  2. Do: Apply continuity and the laws of logarithms to rewrite the limit (\(\ln\) is continuous, so it can move inside the limit; then bring the exponent down): \[ \begin{aligned}\ln y&=\lim\ln\big(f(x)^{g(x)}\big)\\&=\lim\big(g(x)\ln f(x)\big).\end{aligned} \]The power is now a product, a \(0\cdot\infty\) tug-of-war of the kind met in section 5 (this needs \(f(x)\gt0\)).
  3. Do: Evaluate \(\lim g(x)\ln f(x)\), using one of \[ \begin{gathered}\lim g(x)\ln f(x)\ \ (\text{Sandwich Theorem?}),\\ \lim\frac{\ln f(x)}{1/g(x)}\ \ (\text{L'Hôpital}),\\ \lim\frac{g(x)}{1/\ln f(x)}\ \ (\text{L'Hôpital}).\end{gathered} \]Each choice settles the product's tug-of-war: squeeze it, or dress it as a race with one factor moved to the bottom.
  4. Do: Exponentiate both sides: \[ \begin{gathered}e^{\ln y}=e^{\lim(g(x)\ln f(x))}\\ \iff y=\lim f(x)^{g(x)}.\end{gathered} \]You have found the limit of \(\ln y\), not of \(y\); \(e^{(\cdot)}\) translates back.

In words: limit of the power = \(e\) raised to (limit of exponent × ln of base).

Going further: the careful version for exam write-ups

Step 1 writes \(\ln\) of a limit before we know the limit exists. A tidier version: let \(y=f(x)^{g(x)}\) (a function, not a limit). Then \(\ln y=g(x)\ln f(x)\). Find \(L=\lim g(x)\ln f(x)\). Because \(e^x\) is continuous,

\[ \lim f(x)^{g(x)}=\lim e^{\ln y}=e^{\lim \ln y}=e^{L}. \]

If \(L=+\infty\) the limit is \(+\infty\); if \(L=-\infty\) the limit is \(e^{-\infty}=0\). Both versions get the same answer; the second is easier to justify.

Reading the formula
\(f(x)\)
the base. It must stay positive, so that \(\ln f(x)\) makes sense.
\(g(x)\)
the exponent, the side that magnifies.
\(g(x)\ln f(x)\)
the power translated into a product by the logarithm.
\(L\)
who wins that product's tug-of-war: a number, \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\).
\(e^{L}\)
translating back. \(L=+\infty\) gives \(+\infty\); \(L=-\infty\) gives 0.

Which of the three choices in step 3? Use the Sandwich Theorem when one factor wiggles (like \(\cos\frac1x\)) and the other shrinks to 0. Otherwise, put the factor that is easier to differentiate as a reciprocal in the bottom. Usually that means keeping \(\ln f(x)\) on top: \(\dfrac{\ln f(x)}{1/g(x)}\).

Example 5: indeterminate power

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}x^x\).

Solution. Base \(\to0^+\), exponent \(\to0\): form \(0^0\). Let \(y=x^x\), so \(\ln y=x\ln x\), form \(0\cdot(-\infty)\). Rewrite with \(\ln x\) on top: The derivative of \(\ln x\) is the simple \(\frac1x\); keeping \(x\) on top instead would put \(\frac1{\ln x}\) on the bottom and make each round worse (section 9).

\[ \begin{aligned}\lim_{x\to0^+}x\ln x&=\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{\ln x}{1/x}\\ &\overset{\left[\frac{-\infty}{\infty}\right]}{=}\ \lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{1/x}{-1/x^2}\\&=\lim_{x\to0^+}(-x)=0.\end{aligned} \]

Exponentiate: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}x^x=e^0=1\).

In words: as \(x\to0^+\), \(\ln x\) runs to \(-\infty\) but much more slowly than \(x\) shrinks, so \(x\ln x\to0\) and \(x^x\to1\). Keep the result \(x\ln x\to0\) as \(x\to0^+\): it is used again and again.

(\(\frac{-\infty}{\infty}\) counts as an \(\frac\infty\infty\) form: L'Hôpital's Rule allows either sign.)

Example 6: indeterminate power?

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}x^{x^x}\), that is, \(x^{(x^x)}\).

Here \(x^{x^x}\) means \(x^{(x^x)}\) (a power tower, evaluated from the top down), the usual meaning.

Solution. Let \(y=x^{x^x}\), so \(\ln y=x^x\ln x\). From Example 5, \(x^x\to1\), and \(\ln x\to-\infty\). So

\[ \begin{aligned}\lim_{x\to0^+}\ln y&=\lim_{x\to0^+}x^x\cdot\lim_{x\to0^+}\ln x\\&=1\cdot(-\infty)=-\infty,\end{aligned} \]

a determinate form. Exponentiate: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}x^{x^x}=e^{-\infty}=0\).

Lesson: the base \(\to0\) and the exponent \(\to1\), so the form is really \(0^1=0\), not indeterminate. Always identify the form first; here no L'Hôpital is needed. (Numerically, \(x=10^{-6}\) gives \(x^{x^x}\approx1.0002\times10^{-6}\).)

Example 7: e as a limit

Show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(1+\frac1x\right)^x=e\).

Solution. Base \(\to1\), exponent \(\to\infty\): form \(1^\infty\). Let \(y=\left(1+\frac1x\right)^x\), so

\[ \begin{aligned}\ln y&=x\ln\!\left(1+\frac1x\right)\\&=\frac{\ln\left(1+\frac1x\right)}{1/x}\qquad\left[\frac00\right].\end{aligned} \]

By L'Hôpital's Rule (chain rule on the top: \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln\big(1+\frac1x\big)=\frac{1}{1+1/x}\cdot\left(-\frac1{x^2}\right)\)):

\[ \begin{aligned}&\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln\left(1+\frac1x\right)}{1/x}\\&\quad=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\dfrac{1}{1+1/x}\cdot\left(-\dfrac1{x^2}\right)}{-\dfrac1{x^2}}\\&\quad=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{1+\frac1x}=1.\end{aligned} \]

Exponentiate: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(1+\frac1x\right)^x=e^1=e\). \(\blacksquare\)

In words: 1 plus a tiny bit, raised to a huge power, can land anywhere; here the balance is exact and gives \(e\approx2.718\).

Analogy. A bank pays 100% interest per year, credited \(n\) times a year at \(\frac{100\%}{n}\) each time. \$1 becomes \(\left(1+\frac1n\right)^n\) dollars. Crediting more and more often never gives more than \(e\approx\$2.718\).

7Why the rule works ~7 min

What is this?

Back to the two runners who start level at 0. At any moment, the ratio of the distances they have covered is the ratio of their average speeds since the start, because both have been running for the same time. Shrink that time towards zero and the average speeds become the speeds at the start. That is the whole proof of the special case below.

Theorem: L'Hôpital's Rule, special case

If \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable functions, \(f(a)=g(a)=0\), \(f'\) and \(g'\) are continuous, \(g'(a)\ne0\), and \(\lim\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) is an indeterminate form \(\frac00\), then

\[ \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}, \]

provided this limit exists (or is \(\pm\infty\)).

Proof. Since \(f(a)=g(a)=0\), we may subtract them for free, then divide top and bottom by \(x-a\): Subtracting 0 changes nothing, but it turns each runner's position into a distance covered since the start; dividing by \(x-a\) (the time taken) turns it into an average speed.

\[ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{g(x)-g(a)}=\frac{\dfrac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}}{\dfrac{g(x)-g(a)}{x-a}}. \]

As \(x\to a\), the top difference quotient tends to \(f'(a)\) and the bottom one to \(g'(a)\) (definition of the derivative, Lecture 5). Since \(g'(a)\ne0\), the Quotient Law for limits applies:

\[ \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{f'(a)}{g'(a)}=\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}, \]

where the last step uses that \(f'\) and \(g'\) are continuous at \(a\). \(\blacksquare\)

In words: the ratio of the functions = the ratio of their average slopes from \(a\), and those average slopes become the actual slopes at \(a\).

The general proof needs Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem

The special case needs \(f'\), \(g'\) continuous and \(g'(a)\ne0\). The general rule is proved with a two-function version of the Mean Value Theorem (MVT, Lecture 8).

What is this? (Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem)

Two cars drive during the same stretch of time. If car A covers 3 km while car B covers 2 km, then at some moment A was going exactly 1.5 times as fast as B, provided B never stops (and both drive smoothly, with no jumps). The ordinary MVT is the case where car B is a clock, moving at a steady speed of 1.

Theorem: Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem, CMVT

Suppose \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\), and \(g'(x)\ne0\) on \((a,b)\). Then there is a number \(c\) in \((a,b)\) at which

\[ \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}. \]

In words: at some moment \(c\), the ratio of the two instantaneous rates of change = the ratio of the two total changes over \([a,b]\).

Reading the formula
\(f(b)-f(a),\ g(b)-g(a)\)
the total distance each car covers between times \(a\) and \(b\).
\(\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}\)
the ratio of the total distances.
\(f'(c),\ g'(c)\)
the two speeds at one moment \(c\) inside the stretch.
\(g'(x)\ne0\) on \((a,b)\)
car B never stops, so the ratio of speeds always makes sense and \(g(b)\ne g(a)\).
continuous, differentiable
no jumps, and a speed at every moment inside the stretch.

With \(g(x)=x\) it is exactly the ordinary MVT: \(\frac{f'(c)}{1}=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\).

Picture. As \(t\) runs from \(a\) to \(b\), plot the point \(\big(g(t),f(t)\big)\). It traces a curve. The right side \(\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}\) is the slope of the chord joining its ends; the left side \(\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}\) is the slope of the curve at \(t=c\) (rise rate ÷ run rate). So the CMVT says: some tangent is parallel to the chord, which is the MVT for this curve.

Proof of the CMVT. Define

\[ h(x)=\big(f(b)-f(a)\big)g(x)-\big(g(b)-g(a)\big)f(x). \]
  1. \(h\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\), since \(f\) and \(g\) are, and the brackets are constants.
  2. Expanding, \(h(a)=f(b)g(a)-g(b)f(a)\) and \(h(b)=f(b)g(a)-g(b)f(a)\) (the \(f(b)g(b)\) terms cancel in \(h(b)\), and the \(f(a)g(a)\) terms cancel in \(h(a)\)). So \(h(a)=h(b)\).
  3. By Rolle's Theorem (Lecture 8), there is \(c\) in \((a,b)\) with \(h'(c)=0\): \[ \big(f(b)-f(a)\big)g'(c)-\big(g(b)-g(a)\big)f'(c)=0. \]
  4. \(g(b)\ne g(a)\): otherwise Rolle's Theorem applied to \(g\) would give a point with \(g'=0\), which is ruled out. Also \(g'(c)\ne0\). Divide by both: \[ \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}.\qquad\blacksquare \]

From CMVT to L'Hôpital's Rule (case \(\frac00\), \(x\to a^+\)). Suppose \(f(x)\to0\) and \(g(x)\to0\) as \(x\to a^+\); set \(f(a)=g(a)=0\) so both are continuous at \(a\). Take \(x\) slightly bigger than \(a\). The CMVT on \([a,x]\) gives a \(c_x\) between \(a\) and \(x\) with

\[ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{g(x)-g(a)}=\frac{f'(c_x)}{g'(c_x)}. \]

As \(x\to a^+\), \(c_x\) is squeezed between \(a\) and \(x\), so \(c_x\to a^+\) too. Hence if \(\lim_{x\to a^+}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}\) exists (or is \(\pm\infty\)), \(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) has the same limit. The case \(x\to a^-\) is the same on \([x,a]\), and together they give \(x\to a\). Notice this needs \(g'\ne0\) on the whole interval near \(a\), which is why the standard hypothesis is "\(g'(x)\ne0\) near \(a\)". \(c_x\) is a moment between the start \(a\) and \(x\). As the stretch shrinks to the start line, that moment is pushed to the start too, so the ratio of distances follows the ratio of speeds near \(a\).

Going further: the other cases

For \(x\to\infty\), substitute \(x=\frac1t\) and use the case \(t\to0^+\) (the chain rule factors \(-\frac1{t^2}\) cancel top and bottom). The \(\frac\infty\infty\) case is also proved from the CMVT, but the argument is more delicate; it is done in analysis courses.

You can now: prove the special case from the definition of the derivative; state Cauchy's MVT with its conditions and get the ordinary MVT from it with \(g(x)=x\); explain how it gives L'Hôpital's Rule as \(x\to a^+\), and why \(g'\ne0\) near \(a\) is needed.

8Summary

  • L'Hôpital's Rule is a powerful technique that uses differentiation to evaluate indeterminate forms.
  • Indeterminate quotients may be evaluated by comparing the rates of change of the two functions: \(\lim\frac fg=\lim\frac{f'}{g'}\) for \(\frac00\) or \(\frac\infty\infty\).
  • Rewrite indeterminate differences, products and powers \(\{\infty-\infty,\ 0\cdot\infty,\ 0^0,\ 1^\infty,\ \infty^0\}\) as indeterminate quotients \(\{\frac00,\ \frac\infty\infty\}\) before applying the rule; choose a rewrite that makes the computation simpler.
  • L'Hôpital's Rule does not apply to determinate forms such as \(\frac0\infty\), \(\frac\infty0\), \(0^\infty\).
  • The logarithmic transformation is used to evaluate indeterminate powers.

The two exercises below are built from the common mistakes in section 9.

A. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2+x}\).

A student's work:

\[ \begin{aligned}\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2+x}&=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{2x+1}\\&=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\cos x}{2}=\frac12.\end{aligned} \]
Hint

Check the form before each "=". What does \(\frac{\sin x}{2x+1}\) tend to?

Answer

The first step is fine: \(1-\cos0=0\) and \(0^2+0=0\), so the form is \(\frac00\) and L'Hôpital's Rule applies. But \(\frac{\sin x}{2x+1}\to\frac{0}{1}=0\): that is not indeterminate, so the second application is illegal. The student also wrote no form checks at all.

Correct: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2+x}\overset{\left[\frac00\right]}{=}\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{2x+1}=\frac01=0.\)

B. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}(\cos x)^{1/x^2}\).

A student's work:

\[ \begin{aligned}&\lim_{x\to0}(\cos x)^{1/x^2}=1^\infty=1?\\ &\text{No, use logs: }\\ &\ln y=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\ln(\cos x)}{x^2}\\ &\quad=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\dfrac{-\sin x}{\cos x}\cdot x^2-\ln(\cos x)\cdot2x}{x^4}\end{aligned} \]

"This is getting messy. Start again:"

\[ \begin{aligned}\ln y&=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\ln(\cos x)}{x^2}=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{-\tan x}{2x}\\&=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{-\sec^2x}{2}=-\frac12.\\ &\text{So the limit is } -\tfrac12.\end{aligned} \]
Hint 1

In the first attempt, which rule from Lecture 6 did the student use on the fraction?

Hint 2

Can a positive number like \((\cos x)^{1/x^2}\) (for small \(x\), \(\cos x\gt0\)) have a negative limit? What is the last step of the logarithmic transformation?

Answer

Mistake 1: the first attempt applied the Quotient Rule to \(\frac{\ln(\cos x)}{x^2}\) instead of differentiating top and bottom separately.

Mistake 2: the second attempt is correct up to \(\ln y\to-\frac12\) (both steps were \(\frac00\): check \(\ln\cos0=0\), and \(-\tan0=0\)), but the student forgot to exponentiate. The answer must be positive.

Correct: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}(\cos x)^{1/x^2}=e^{-1/2}=\frac1{\sqrt e}\approx0.607.\) (The opening "\(1^\infty\) is indeterminate, so use logs" was right.)

11Group work

Evaluate each limit. Name the form first.

1. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\ln x}\)

Hint

\(\sin\pi=0\) and \(\ln1=0\). Chain rule on the top.

Answer
\[ \overset{\left[\frac00\right]}{=}\lim_{x\to1}\frac{\pi\cos(\pi x)}{1/x}=\frac{\pi\cos\pi}{1}=-\pi. \]

2. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}(1+2x)^{3/x}\)

Hint

Form \(1^{\pm\infty}\). Logarithmic transformation: \(\ln y=\frac{3\ln(1+2x)}{x}\).

Answer

\(\ln y=\dfrac{3\ln(1+2x)}{x}\) is \(\frac00\); by L'Hôpital, \(\lim_{x\to0}\dfrac{3\cdot\frac{2}{1+2x}}{1}=6\). (Works for \(x\to0\) from both sides.) Exponentiate: \(e^6\).

3. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pi^-}\frac{\sin x}{1-\cos x}\)

Hint

Substitute first. What is \(1-\cos\pi\)?

Answer

Top \(\to\sin\pi=0\), bottom \(\to1-(-1)=2\). The form is \(\frac02\), not indeterminate: the limit is 0 by substitution. (L'Hôpital would give \(\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\to-\infty\) from the left, a wrong answer. This one is a test of checking the form.)

4. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}x\cot x\)

Hint

Form \(0\cdot\infty\). Write \(\cot x=\frac1{\tan x}\).

Answer
\[ \begin{gathered}x\cot x=\frac{x}{\tan x}\ \left[\tfrac00\right]\\ \Rightarrow\ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{1}{\sec^2x}=1.\end{gathered} \]

5. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{\ln x}{\cot x}\)

Hint

Form \(\frac{-\infty}{\infty}\). After one application, simplify before doing anything else; you may need \(\frac{\sin x}{x}\to1\).

Answer
\[ \begin{aligned}&\overset{\left[\frac{-\infty}{\infty}\right]}{=}\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{1/x}{-\csc^2x}\\&=\lim_{x\to0^+}\left(-\frac{\sin^2x}{x}\right)\\&=\lim_{x\to0^+}\left(-\frac{\sin x}{x}\cdot\sin x\right)\\&=-1\cdot0=0.\end{aligned} \]

6. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}\left(\frac1{x^2}-\frac1{\tan x}\right)\)

(A similar limit with \(\frac1x\) in place of \(\frac1{x^2}\) gives a finite answer; with \(\frac1{x^2}\), as written here, the answer is infinite.)

Hint

Form \(\infty-\infty\). Combine over the common denominator \(x^2\tan x\), then check the form of the new fraction.

Answer
\[ \begin{aligned}&\frac1{x^2}-\frac1{\tan x}=\frac{\tan x-x^2}{x^2\tan x}\ \left[\tfrac00\right]\\ &\overset{\text{L'H}}{\Rightarrow}\ \lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{\sec^2x-2x}{2x\tan x+x^2\sec^2x}\\ &\quad=\frac{1}{0^+}=+\infty.\end{aligned} \]

(The bottom \(2x\tan x+x^2\sec^2x\) is positive for small \(x\gt0\).) So the limit is \(+\infty\): the \(\frac1{x^2}\) term wins.

7. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}x^{\sin x}\)

Hint

Form \(0^0\). \(\ln y=\sin x\ln x=\frac{\ln x}{\csc x}\).

Answer
\[ \begin{aligned}\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{\ln x}{\csc x}&\overset{\left[\frac{-\infty}{\infty}\right]}{=}\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{1/x}{-\csc x\cot x}\\&=\lim_{x\to0^+}\left(-\frac{\sin x}{x}\cdot\tan x\right)\\&=-1\cdot0=0.\end{aligned} \]

Exponentiate: \(e^0=1\).

8. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt x}{e^{\sqrt x}}\)

Hint

Form \(\frac\infty\infty\). The chain rule gives both derivatives a factor \(\frac1{2\sqrt x}\). (Or substitute \(t=\sqrt x\).)

Answer
\[ \overset{\left[\frac\infty\infty\right]}{=}\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\frac1{2\sqrt x}}{e^{\sqrt x}\cdot\frac1{2\sqrt x}}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1{e^{\sqrt x}}=0. \]

9. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{x^2+x}-x\right)\)

Hint

Form \(\infty-\infty\). Rationalise (Lecture 3) rather than forcing L'Hôpital.

Answer
\[ \begin{aligned}\sqrt{x^2+x}-x&=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+x}+x}\\&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac1x}+1}\to\frac12.\end{aligned} \]

10. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{a^x-b^x}{x}\) \((a,b\gt0)\)

Hint

Form \(\frac00\). \(\frac{d}{dx}a^x=a^x\ln a\) (Lecture 7).

Answer
\[ \begin{aligned}&\overset{\left[\frac00\right]}{=}\lim_{x\to0}\frac{a^x\ln a-b^x\ln b}{1}\\&=\ln a-\ln b=\ln\frac ab.\end{aligned} \]

(If \(a=b\) the top is identically 0 and the answer \(\ln1=0\) still holds.)

C1. "\(1^\infty=1\), because 1 to any power is 1." What is wrong? Give limits of the form \(1^\infty\) with different answers.

Answer

The base is only close to 1, and a huge exponent magnifies the gap. As \(x\to\infty\): \(\left(1+\frac1x\right)^x\to e\), \(\left(1-\frac1x\right)^x\to\frac1e\), \(\left(1+\frac1{x^2}\right)^x\to1\), \(\left(1+\frac1x\right)^{x^2}\to\infty\). Only a base exactly equal to 1 gives 1.

C2. Why is \(0^\infty\) determinate while \(0^0\) is not? (Base positive in both.)

Answer

Take logs: \(\ln(f^g)=g\ln f\). For \(0^\infty\): \((+\infty)\cdot(-\infty)=-\infty\), determinate, so \(f^g\to e^{-\infty}=0\). For \(0^0\): \(0\cdot(-\infty)\), a fight between a factor shrinking to 0 and one growing in size, so anything can happen.

C3. A student "proves" \(\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1\) by L'Hôpital: \(\lim\frac{\cos x}{1}=1\). The calculation is right. Why is it not a valid proof in this course?

Answer

It is circular. To use the rule you need \(\frac{d}{dx}\sin x=\cos x\), and that derivative (Lecture 6) was proved using \(\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1\) (Lecture 2). You cannot use a result to prove itself. As a computation it is fine.

C4. For \(\lim_{x\to0^+}x^2\ln x\), which rewrite would you choose, \(\frac{\ln x}{x^{-2}}\) or \(\frac{x^2}{1/\ln x}\)? Why?

Answer

\(\frac{\ln x}{x^{-2}}\): the derivative of \(\ln x\) is the simple \(\frac1x\), giving \(\frac{1/x}{-2x^{-3}}=-\frac{x^2}2\to0\). The other choice makes \(\frac1{\ln x}\) appear on the bottom and gets worse with every round.

C5. How can you tell, before computing anything, that L'Hôpital is a poor choice for \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^x+\sin x}{e^x}\)?

Answer

Derivatives of \(\sin x\) cycle through \(\cos x,-\sin x,-\cos x,\sin x\), so the top will never simplify; meanwhile dividing by \(e^x\) gives \(1+\frac{\sin x}{e^x}\), which the Sandwich Theorem finishes at once (limit 1).

C6. Order \(\ln x\), \(x^{0.1}\), \(x^{100}\), \(e^x\) by how fast they grow as \(x\to\infty\). Justify one comparison with L'Hôpital.

Answer

\(\ln x\ll x^{0.1}\ll x^{100}\ll e^x\). For example \(\frac{\ln x}{x^{0.1}}\overset{\left[\frac\infty\infty\right]}{=}\frac{1/x}{0.1x^{-0.9}}=\frac{10}{x^{0.1}}\to0\). And \(\frac{x^{100}}{e^x}\to0\) after 100 applications (each lowers the power by 1, the bottom stays \(e^x\)), exactly like Example 3. Logs lose to powers; powers lose to exponentials.
