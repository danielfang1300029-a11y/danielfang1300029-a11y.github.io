Techniques of Integration I: Substitution and Integration by Parts

0Where this lecture sits

Integrating means running differentiation backwards: you are handed a derivative and asked what it came from. Each differentiation rule leaves its own fingerprint on the answer. This lecture learns to spot two of them: the chain rule's fingerprint (an inside function with its derivative beside it) and the product rule's (a product of two different kinds of function).

Chapter on one page

  2. §2 Substitution is the chain rule run backwards: spot an inside function and its derivative, then rename the inside \(u\).

  10. §10 Two methods can give different-looking answers that differ by a constant.
  11. §11–§16 Summary, common mistakes, practice and the exam taster.

Recall: the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Theorem (Part I) · Lecture 12

If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), then the function

\[ g(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt,\qquad a\le x\le b \]

is an antiderivative of \(f\): \(g'(x)=f(x)\) for all \(x\) in \((a,b)\).

In words: the rate at which the area under \(f\) (from \(a\) up to \(x\)) grows = the height of \(f\) at \(x\).

Theorem (Part II, Evaluation Theorem) · Lecture 12

If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), then

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a), \]

where \(F\) is any antiderivative of \(f\).

In words: net area under \(f\) from \(a\) to \(b\) = (antiderivative at the right end) − (antiderivative at the left end).

Part II is why this lecture matters: every definite integral becomes "find an antiderivative", and the two techniques below are how you find one.

Goals for this chapter: evaluate integrals by the Substitution Rule; use the properties of odd and even integrands over symmetric intervals; evaluate integrals by Integration by Parts; derive and apply reduction formulas.

1Definite vs indefinite, and why rules come in pairs ~3 min

What is this?

A times table read backwards is a division table: knowing \(6\times7=42\) tells you at once that \(42\div7=6\). In the same way, every derivative you know, read from right to left, is an integral you know. The definite integral uses such an answer to get one number (an area); the indefinite integral is the answer itself, a whole family of functions that differ only by a constant.

Terms: the integrand is the function being integrated (\(f(x)\) above). The limits of integration are \(a\) (lower) and \(b\) (upper). The \(dx\) says which letter is the variable of integration. Both columns need the same thing: an antiderivative \(F\). So the real question is: how do you find \(F(x)\)? The rest of this chapter answers it.

Every differentiation rule has a corresponding integration rule

By the Power Rule for differentiation,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}\right)=x^n . \]

Integrating both sides (an antiderivative of a derivative is the function itself, plus a constant) gives the Power Rule for integration:

\[ \begin{aligned}\int x^n\,dx&=\int\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}\right)dx\\&=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\qquad(n\ne-1).\end{aligned} \]

In words: to integrate a power of \(x\), raise the power by one and divide by the new power.

Reading the formula

the derivative you were handed.
\(\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}\)
the function it came from: the power rule run backwards (up by one, then divide).
\(+C\)
any constant would have vanished when differentiated, so the backwards answer is only known up to a constant.
\(n\ne-1\)
the one power where "divide by \(n+1\)" would mean dividing by 0.

The condition \(n\ne-1\) is needed because \(n+1\) would be 0. For \(n=-1\), \(\int\frac1x\,dx=\ln|x|+C\) (Lecture 11).

So the Power Rule (for derivatives) corresponds to the Power Rule (for integrals). The next question: what about the Chain Rule and the Product Rule? They correspond to the Substitution Rule (section 2) and Integration by Parts (section 7).

2The Substitution Rule ~3 min

What is this?

When the chain rule differentiates a function of an inside function, it always leaves the derivative of the inside behind as an extra factor. That factor is the chain rule's fingerprint. Substitution looks for it: if the integrand is something of an inside function, times the derivative of that inside function, then it came from the chain rule. Rename the inside \(u\), and the fingerprint becomes \(du\), leaving a plain integral of \(u\). The renaming only works if the whole fingerprint is there (up to a constant factor).

The Chain Rule corresponds to the Substitution Rule.

Theorem: Substitution Rule

If \(u=g(x)\) is a differentiable function whose range is an interval \(I\) and \(f\) is continuous on \(I\), then

\[ \int f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx=\int f(u)\,du . \]

In words: if the integrand is (a function of an inside expression) × (the derivative of that inside expression), rename the inside expression \(u\); the integral becomes a plain integral of \(f(u)\) with respect to \(u\).

Reading the formula
\(g(x)\)
the inside function, which gets the new name \(u\).
\(g'(x)\)
the chain rule's fingerprint: the derivative of the inside, sitting outside as a factor.
\(f(g(x))\)
the outer function, applied to the inside.
\(g'(x)\,dx\)
together these become \(du\), so the fingerprint is absorbed by the renaming.
\(\int f(u)\,du\)
what is left: a plain integral in one letter, usually one from your table.

Terms: the range of \(g\) is the set of output values \(g(x)\). The conditions make sure \(f(u)\) is continuous wherever \(u\) can land, so \(f\) has an antiderivative there (Lecture 12).

The \(du\) notation

If \(u=g(x)\), then \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=g'(x)\). We write this as

\[ du=g'(x)\,dx . \]
What is this? (the du notation)

Think of an exchange rate. If \(u\) changes three times as fast as \(x\) at some point, a small step in \(x\) there produces a step in \(u\) three times as big. The line \(du=g'(x)\,dx\) is that exchange rate written down: it converts steps in \(x\) into steps in \(u\).

In words: a small change in \(u\) = (rate of change of \(u\) with respect to \(x\)) × (small change in \(x\)).

\(du\) and \(dx\) are called differentials. Treating \(\frac{du}{dx}\) as a fraction is not a proof (the proof is below), but it is a reliable bookkeeping device: in \(\int f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx\), the piece \(g'(x)\,dx\) is exactly \(du\), and \(g(x)\) is exactly \(u\).

Proof

Let \(F\) be an antiderivative of \(f\), so \(F'=f\). The proof just runs the chain rule forwards and shows it produces exactly the integrand with the fingerprint \(g'(x)\). By the Chain Rule, \(\frac{d}{dx}F(g(x))=F'(g(x))\,g'(x)=f(g(x))\,g'(x)\). So

\[ \begin{aligned} \int f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx &= \int\frac{d}{dx}F(g(x))\,dx\\ &\qquad \text{(Chain Rule)}\\ &= F(g(x))+C\\ &\qquad \text{(an antiderivative of a}\\ &\qquad \text{derivative; FTC)}\\ &= F(u)+C\qquad (u=g(x))\\ &= \int F'(u)\,du\\ &= \int f(u)\,du . \end{aligned} \]

Analogy. In HKDSE you solve \(x^4-5x^2+4=0\) by letting \(u=x^2\): the equation becomes \(u^2-5u+4=0\), which you already know how to solve. Substitution in integrals is the same idea: rename the awkward inside part so the problem becomes one from your table. The difference is that \(dx\) must be renamed too, which is why the derivative \(g'(x)\) has to be present.

You can now: recognise an integrand of the form \(f(g(x))\,g'(x)\); state the Substitution Rule with its conditions; write \(du=g'(x)\,dx\); and explain the rule as the chain rule run backwards.

3Substitution: worked examples ~4 min

What is this?

Each example is a search for the chain rule's fingerprint. Sometimes it is sitting there in full (Example 1), sometimes it is off by a constant (Example 2), sometimes the integrand must be rewritten before it shows (Example 3), and sometimes a leftover \(x\) has to be rewritten in terms of \(u\) (Example 4).

Example 1: new and old technique

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int (x^2-3)^3\,2x\,dx\) by (a) the Substitution Rule, (b) expanding the integrand.

(a) The inside function is \(x^2-3\) and its derivative \(2x\) is sitting right there. Let \(u=x^2-3\), so \(du=2x\,dx\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\int (x^2-3)^3\,2x\,dx&=\int u^3\,du=\frac{u^4}{4}+C\\&=\frac{(x^2-3)^4}{4}+C .\end{aligned} \]

(b) Expand \((x^2-3)^3=x^6-9x^4+27x^2-27\), multiply by \(2x\) and use the Power Rule term by term:

\[ \begin{aligned}&\int\left(2x^7-18x^5+54x^3-54x\right)dx\\&\quad=\frac{x^8}{4}-3x^6+\frac{27x^4}{2}-27x^2+C .\end{aligned} \]

Are they the same? Expanding (a) gives \(\frac{x^8}4-3x^6+\frac{27x^4}2-27x^2+\frac{81}4\). The two answers differ only by the constant \(\frac{81}{4}\), which the arbitrary \(+C\) absorbs. Both are correct. (See the "answers that differ by a constant" graph in section 10.)

Lesson: substitution is much shorter, and would be the only practical way for \((x^2-3)^{50}\,2x\).

Example 2: constant multiple of \(du\)

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int \frac{e^{\frac{1}{2x}}}{x^2}\,dx\).

Solution. The inside function is \(\frac1{2x}=\frac12x^{-1}\). Let \(u=\frac{1}{2x}\). Then

\[ du=-\frac{1}{2x^2}\,dx\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dx}{x^2}=-2\,du . \]

The integrand has \(\frac{dx}{x^2}\), which is not exactly \(du\) but a constant multiple of it. A constant can be carried outside the integral, so the fingerprint only needs to be right up to a constant factor. Constants can be moved outside the integral, so this is fine:

\[ \begin{aligned}\int e^{\frac1{2x}}\cdot\frac{dx}{x^2}&=\int e^u\,(-2\,du)\\&=-2e^u+C=-2e^{\frac{1}{2x}}+C .\end{aligned} \]

Check: \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(-2e^{\frac1{2x}}\right)=-2e^{\frac1{2x}}\cdot\left(-\frac1{2x^2}\right)=\frac{e^{\frac1{2x}}}{x^2}\). ✓

Lesson: a missing constant factor is never a problem; a missing \(x\) factor is (see Common mistakes).

Example 3: choice of \(u\)

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int\tan x\,dx\) by substitution with (a) \(u=\cos x\), (b) \(u=\sin x\).

First rewrite the integrand: \(\tan x=\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\).Written as \(\tan x\) there is no inside function to see; as a fraction, \(\cos x\) and its derivative \(-\sin x\) appear together.

(a) \(u=\cos x\), \(du=-\sin x\,dx\), so \(\sin x\,dx=-du\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\int\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\,dx&=\int\frac{-du}{u}=-\ln|u|+C\\&=-\ln|\cos x|+C\\&=\ln|\sec x|+C .\end{aligned} \]

(b) \(u=\sin x\), \(du=\cos x\,dx\). We need \(\cos x\,dx\) in the integral, so multiply top and bottom by \(\cos x\) and use \(\cos^2x=1-\sin^2x\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\int\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\,dx&=\int\frac{\sin x\,\cos x\,dx}{\cos^2x}\\&=\int\frac{u\,du}{1-u^2}.\end{aligned} \]

This needs a second substitution \(w=1-u^2\), \(dw=-2u\,du\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\int\frac{u\,du}{1-u^2}&=-\frac12\int\frac{dw}{w}\\&=-\frac12\ln|1-u^2|+C\\&=-\frac12\ln(\cos^2x)+C\\&=-\ln|\cos x|+C .\end{aligned} \]

Lesson: both choices give the same answer, but (a) takes one line and (b) takes two substitutions. Choose \(u\) so that the new integral is as simple as possible.

Example 4: back substitution

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int 2x^3\sqrt{1+x^2}\,dx\).

Solution. Let \(u=1+x^2\), \(du=2x\,dx\). Split \(2x^3=x^2\cdot2x\). The leftover \(x^2\) is not yet in terms of \(u\), but we can solve the substitution for it: \(x^2=u-1\). This is back substitution: expressing leftover \(x\)'s using the substitution equation.An integral with respect to \(u\) must contain only \(u\); a leftover \(x\) is not a constant and cannot stay.

\[ \begin{aligned}&\int x^2\sqrt{1+x^2}\;2x\,dx\\&\quad=\int (u-1)\sqrt u\,du\\&\quad=\int\left(u^{3/2}-u^{1/2}\right)du\\&\quad=\frac25u^{5/2}-\frac23u^{3/2}+C,\end{aligned} \]

so

\[ \begin{aligned}\int 2x^3\sqrt{1+x^2}\,dx&=\frac25(1+x^2)^{5/2}\\&\quad-\frac23(1+x^2)^{3/2}+C .\end{aligned} \]

Lesson: after substituting, no \(x\) may remain. If one does, use \(u=g(x)\) to rewrite it in terms of \(u\).

You can now: carry out a substitution when the derivative of the inside is present exactly or up to a constant; rewrite the integrand first when the pair is hidden; use back substitution for leftover \(x\)'s; and check an answer by differentiating.

4Steps, and how to choose \(u\) ~3 min

What is this?

Substitution is a translation. You translate the integral into the language of \(u\), where it is one you can read off a table, do it there, and translate the answer back into \(x\). The hard part is choosing which piece to call \(u\): pick the inside piece whose derivative also appears in the integrand.

Steps to apply \(u\)-substitution

Given an indefinite integral of the form \(\displaystyle\int f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx\):

  1. Do: Choose a \(u\)/\(du\) pair: \(u=g(x)\), \(du=g'(x)\,dx\). This names the inside function and its fingerprint, the two pieces the chain rule left behind.
  2. Do: Solve for "\(dx\)" (or for the piece of the integrand that equals a constant multiple of \(du\)). This is the exchange rate between steps in \(x\) and steps in \(u\), so the \(dx\) can be traded for \(du\).
  3. Do: Rewrite the integral entirely in terms of \(u\) and \(du\). If any \(x\) is left over, the translation is unfinished and the integral is not yet a plain one in \(u\).
  4. Do: Evaluate the rewritten integral with respect to \(u\). A good choice of \(u\) has turned it into an integral from your table.
  5. Do: Return the answer in terms of \(x\) (replace \(u\) by \(g(x)\)). The question was asked in \(x\), so the answer must be translated back.

Choosing \(u\)/\(du\) pairs

  • The choice of \(u\) may not be unique (Example 3).
  • Choose \(u\) so that \(\int f(u)\,du\) is simpler than the integral you were given.
  • Look for a pair of subexpressions where one is (a constant multiple of) the derivative of the other. Common pairs:
\(u\)\(x^n\)\(\sin x\)\(\cos x\)\(\tan x\)\(e^x\)\(\ln x\)\(\sqrt x\)
\(c\cdot du\) contains\(x^{n-1}\)\(\cos x\)\(\sin x\)\(\sec^2x\)\(e^x\)\(\frac1x\)\(\frac1{\sqrt x}\)

In words: if you see \(\ln x\) somewhere and \(\frac1x\) somewhere else, try \(u=\ln x\); if you see \(x^3\) inside something and \(x^2\) outside, try \(u=\) the thing containing \(x^3\); and so on. The \(c\) means a constant factor is allowed (Example 2).

Indirect applications (when the pair is not visible at first):

  • Rewrite the integrand first, e.g. \(\tan x=\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\) (Example 3), or multiply top and bottom by something.
  • Back substitution: express leftover \(x\)'s in terms of \(u\) (Example 4).
  • Double substitution: substitute twice in a row (Example 3(b)), or once with a bigger \(u\). For instance, in \(\int 2x\cos(x^2)\,e^{\sin(x^2)}\,dx\) you can let \(u=x^2\) and then \(w=\sin u\), or directly \(w=\sin(x^2)\), \(dw=2x\cos(x^2)\,dx\), giving \(\int e^w\,dw=e^{\sin(x^2)}+C\).
What is this? (choosing u)

You are looking for two pieces of the integrand where one is the derivative of the other, up to a constant: \(\ln x\) with \(\frac1x\), \(\sin x\) with \(\cos x\), \(x^3\) inside something with \(x^2\) outside. The table lists the common pairs. If no pair shows, rewrite the integrand until one does.

Choosing \(u\) is a non-linear problem: there is no fixed recipe that always works; it needs practice, intuition and perseverance. If one choice fails, try another. You can always check an indefinite integral by differentiating your answer.

You can now: apply the five substitution steps in order; spot a \(u\)/\(du\) pair from the table of common pairs; and use the indirect moves (rewrite first, back substitution, double substitution) when no pair is visible at first.

5Substitution in definite integrals ~4 min

What is this?

Changing from \(x\) to \(u\) is like swapping one ruler for another with different markings. The start and the finish of the integral are the same places, but you must read them on the new ruler: the start becomes \(g(a)\) and the finish becomes \(g(b)\). Once the limits are read on the \(u\)-ruler, you never need to go back to \(x\).

Theorem: definite integral substitutions

If \(g'\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(f\) is continuous on the range of \(u=g(x)\), then

\[ \int_a^b f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx=\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)}f(u)\,du . \]

In words: when you change the variable from \(x\) to \(u\), change the limits too: the lower limit \(a\) becomes \(g(a)\) (the value of \(u\) when \(x=a\)) and the upper limit \(b\) becomes \(g(b)\). Then you never need to go back to \(x\).

Reading the formula
\(a,\ b\)
the start and finish, read on the \(x\)-ruler.
\(g(a),\ g(b)\)
the same start and finish, read on the \(u\)-ruler.
\(f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx\)
the integrand with the chain rule's fingerprint \(g'(x)\), which turns into \(du\).
\(\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)}f(u)\,du\)
the same area, worked out entirely in \(u\).

Why it is true. If \(F'=f\), then by the Evaluation Theorem both sides equal \(F(g(b))-F(g(a))\): the left side because \(F(g(x))\) is an antiderivative of \(f(g(x))g'(x)\) (Chain Rule), the right side directly.

Area is preserved. Compare two graphs. On the left, the area under \(y=f(g(x))\,g'(x)\) from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\); on the right, the area under \(y=f(u)\) from \(u=g(a)\) to \(u=g(b)\). The shapes look completely different, yet the two areas are equal. Substitution stretches and squashes the horizontal axis, and the factor \(g'(x)\) adjusts the heights so the area comes out the same. Example 5: adjust limits?

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2x\,\cos x\,dx\) (a) keeping the limits in \(x\), (b) changing the limits to \(u\).

(a) Limits in \(x\). First find an antiderivative as an indefinite integral: \(u=\sin x\), \(du=\cos x\,dx\), \(\int u^2\,du=\frac{u^3}{3}+C=\frac{\sin^3x}{3}+C\). Then go back to \(x\) and use the original limits:

\[ \begin{aligned}\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2x\cos x\,dx&=\left[\frac{\sin^3x}{3}\right]_0^{\pi/2}\\&=\frac{1^3}{3}-\frac{0^3}{3}=\frac13 .\end{aligned} \]

(b) Limits in \(u\). \(u=\sin x\): when \(x=0\), \(u=\sin0=0\); when \(x=\frac\pi2\), \(u=\sin\frac\pi2=1\). So

\[ \begin{aligned}\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2x\cos x\,dx&=\int_0^1u^2\,du\\&=\left[\frac{u^3}{3}\right]_0^1=\frac13 .\end{aligned} \]

Lesson: either (a) substitute back to \(x\) and use the \(x\)-limits, or (b) change the limits and stay in \(u\).Limits and integrand must be read on the same ruler; an \(x\)-limit put into a \(u\)-integral measures the wrong place. Never mix: \(u\)-integrand with \(x\)-limits is the classic error.

Notation: \(\big[F(x)\big]_a^b\) means \(F(b)-F(a)\).

You can now: change the limits when you substitute in a definite integral; evaluate it either by staying in \(u\) or by returning to \(x\) with the original limits; and explain why the two areas in the area-preserving picture are equal.

6Odd and even functions over symmetric intervals ~6 min

What is this?

Fold the graph along the \(y\)-axis. For an even function the left half lands exactly on the right half, like a mirror image, so the two halves have the same area and the total is twice the right half. For an odd function the left half is the right half turned upside down, so every positive bit of area on one side is matched by an equal negative bit on the other, and the signed areas cancel to 0. This needs the function to be continuous on the whole interval \([-a,a]\).

Definition
  • \(f\) is even \(\iff f(-x)=f(x)\) for all \(x\) in the domain. The graph is symmetric about the \(y\)-axis (mirror image). Examples: \(x^2\), \(x^4\), \(\cos x\), \(|x|\).
  • \(f\) is odd \(\iff f(-x)=-f(x)\) for all \(x\). The graph is symmetric about the origin (rotate it half a turn and it looks the same). Examples: \(x\), \(x^3\), \(\sin x\), \(\tan x\).

In words: even means "changing the sign of the input does nothing"; odd means "changing the sign of the input changes the sign of the output".

Theorem

Let \(f\) be continuous on \([-a,a]\).

  • If \(f\) is even, then \(\displaystyle\int_{-a}^{a}f(x)\,dx=2\int_0^af(x)\,dx\).
  • If \(f\) is odd, then \(\displaystyle\int_{-a}^{a}f(x)\,dx=0\).

In words: for an even function the left half has the same area as the right half, so the total is twice the right half. For an odd function the left half is the right half flipped upside down, so the signed areas cancel exactly.

Reading the formula
\([-a,a]\)
an interval centred at 0, so it has a left half and a matching right half.
\(2\int_0^af(x)\,dx\)
even: the mirror-image left half adds the same area again.
\(0\)
odd: the upside-down left half cancels the right half.

no breaks or blow-ups anywhere on the interval, so both halves are genuine areas.

Going further: why the theorem is true, and how to tell odd from even

Split \(\int_{-a}^a=\int_{-a}^0+\int_0^a\). In the first piece substitute \(x=-u\)The substitution \(x=-u\) is the fold along the \(y\)-axis: it carries the left half onto the right half., \(dx=-du\); \(x=-a\) gives \(u=a\) and \(x=0\) gives \(u=0\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\int_{-a}^0f(x)\,dx&=\int_a^0f(-u)(-du)\\&=\int_0^af(-u)\,du .\end{aligned} \]

If \(f\) is even this is \(\int_0^af(u)\,du\), so the total is twice the right half. If \(f\) is odd it is \(-\int_0^af(u)\,du\), so the total is 0.

Products and quotients follow sign rules like multiplying \(\pm1\): even × even = even, odd × odd = even, odd × even = odd (the same for ÷). A sum of an odd and an even function is usually neither.

Example 6: symmetric functions

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\tan x}{1+x^2+x^4}\,dx\).

Solution. No substitution helps here, and you do not need one. Let \(f(x)=\dfrac{\tan x}{1+x^2+x^4}\).

  • Continuity: \(\tan x\) is continuous on \([-1,1]\) because \(\cos x\ne0\) there (\(1\lt\frac\pi2\)), and \(1+x^2+x^4\ge1\gt0\). So \(f\) is continuous on \([-1,1]\).
  • Oddness: \(f(-x)=\dfrac{\tan(-x)}{1+(-x)^2+(-x)^4}\) \({}=\dfrac{-\tan x}{1+x^2+x^4}=-f(x)\). (Odd top, even bottom.)

The interval \([-1,1]\) is symmetric, so by the theorem \(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\tan x}{1+x^2+x^4}\,dx=0\).

Lesson: before integrating over \([-a,a]\), check for odd or even symmetry. In the marking scheme, stating "\(f\) is continuous and odd on a symmetric interval" is the working.

Going further (examined): the \(x\to a-x\) trick
What is this? (the reversal trick)

Walk across the interval from right to left instead of from left to right. You pass over exactly the same area, so the integral is unchanged. The trick is useful when reading the interval backwards turns the integrand into a partner that adds nicely to the original.

A related symmetry appears in exams (question type L13.4). For \(f\) continuous on \([0,a]\),

\[ \int_0^af(x)\,dx=\int_0^af(a-x)\,dx . \]

In words: reading the interval from right to left instead of left to right does not change the area.

Proof. Substitute \(u=a-x\), \(du=-dx\); \(x=0\to u=a\), \(x=a\to u=0\): \(\int_0^af(a-x)\,dx=\int_a^0f(u)(-du)\) \({}=\int_0^af(u)\,du\).

How it is used. Call the integral \(I\). After the substitution you get a second expression for \(I\); adding (or comparing) the two expressions makes something cancel. Example: \(I=\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\sin x}{\sin x+\cos x}\,dx\). With \(x\to\frac\pi2-x\), \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) swap, so \(I=\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos x}{\cos x+\sin x}\,dx\). Adding,

\[ \begin{aligned}2I&=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\sin x+\cos x}{\sin x+\cos x}\,dx\\&=\int_0^{\pi/2}1\,dx=\frac\pi2,\\I&=\frac\pi4 .\end{aligned} \]

You can now: test a function for odd or even symmetry using \(f(-x)\); evaluate an integral over \([-a,a]\) by symmetry, stating continuity; split a function that is neither into its odd and even parts; and use the \(x\to a-x\) trick.

7Integration by Parts (IBP) ~4 min

What is this?

Picture a rectangle with sides \(u\) and \(v\) that both grow. Its area \(uv\) grows in two strips: one from \(u\) getting longer, one from \(v\) getting longer. That is the product rule. Integration by parts reads it backwards: if you want one strip, take the whole rectangle and subtract the other strip. So it does not finish the integral; it trades the integral you have for a different one, and it is worth doing only when the new one is easier.

The Product Rule corresponds to Integration by Parts.

Theorem: Integration by Parts

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be differentiable functions. Then

\[ \begin{aligned}\int f(x)\,g'(x)\,dx&=f(x)\,g(x)\\&\quad-\int f'(x)\,g(x)\,dx .\end{aligned} \]

In words: integral of (first × derivative of second) = (first × second) − integral of (derivative of first × second).

Proof

By the Product Rule, \(\frac{d}{dx}\big[f(x)g(x)\big]=f(x)g'(x)+f'(x)g(x)\). These two terms are the two strips of the growing rectangle; integrating both gives back the whole rectangle \(fg\). Integrating both sides (FTC: an antiderivative of a derivative is the function itself),

\[ \begin{aligned}&\int\big[f(x)g'(x)+f'(x)g(x)\big]\,dx\\&\quad=\int\frac{d}{dx}\big[f(x)g(x)\big]\,dx\\&\quad=f(x)g(x).\end{aligned} \]

By the addition property of integrals (the integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals) and rearranging,

\[ \int f(x)g'(x)\,dx=f(x)g(x)-\int f'(x)g(x)\,dx . \]

The \(+C\) is hidden: both sides contain an indefinite integral, which already carries an arbitrary constant.

Differential form

To simplify the notation, let \(u=f(x)\) and \(v=g(x)\). Then \(du=f'(x)\,dx\) and \(dv=g'(x)\,dx\), and the theorem becomes

Theorem: IBP, differential form \[ \int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du . \]

In words: split the integrand into a part \(u\) that you will differentiate and a part \(dv\) that you will integrate. The integral of \(u\,dv\) = \(u\) times \(v\), minus the integral of \(v\,du\).

Reading the formula
\(\int u\,dv\)
the strip you want: the integral you were given.
\(uv\)
the whole rectangle, a finished piece with no integral left in it.
\(\int v\,du\)
the other strip: the new integral you trade for. It must be easier.
\(u\)
the factor you differentiate; choose one that gets simpler.
\(dv\)
the factor you integrate; choose one you can integrate without it getting worse.

IBP does not finish the integral. It trades \(\int u\,dv\) for \(\int v\,du\). It is worth doing only if the new integral is easier.

Analogy. IBP is like a currency exchange: you hand over one integral and get another, minus a fee (\(uv\)). The exchange is only worth it if the integral you receive is one you can actually spend.

Going further: a picture of IBP

Plot \(v\) against \(u\) as a curve from \((u_1,v_1)\) to \((u_2,v_2)\). The area under the curve (to the \(u\)-axis) is \(\int v\,du\); the area to the left of the curve (to the \(v\)-axis) is \(\int u\,dv\). Together they fill the big rectangle minus the small one:

\[ \begin{aligned}\int_{v_1}^{v_2}u\,dv+\int_{u_1}^{u_2}v\,du&=u_2v_2-u_1v_1\\&=\Big[uv\Big] .\end{aligned} \]

In words: left area + lower area = big rectangle − small rectangle. Rearranged, this is exactly the IBP formula.

∫ v du (under the curve)∫ u dv (left of the curve)

Curve used: \(v=\frac{u^2}{4}+\frac12\), from \(u_1=1\). The unshaded corner is the small rectangle \(u_1v_1\).

You can now: state integration by parts in both forms; prove it from the product rule; explain it as a trade of \(\int u\,dv\) for \(\int v\,du\); and read the area picture of IBP.

8Integration by parts: worked examples ~4 min

What is this?

Each example asks the same question: after the trade, is the new integral easier? A power of \(x\) gets easier when differentiated, \(e^x\) and \(\sin\), \(\cos\) stay the same size when integrated, and \(\ln x\) turns into \(\frac1x\). A good split differentiates the piece that shrinks and integrates the piece that does not grow.

Example 6′: choose \(u\) and \(dv\)

This example is called 6′ to keep it apart from Example 6 (symmetric functions) in section 6.

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int xe^x\,dx\) with (a) \(u=x,\ dv=e^x\,dx\), (b) \(u=e^x,\ dv=x\,dx\).

(a) \(u=x\Rightarrow du=dx\); \(dv=e^x\,dx\Rightarrow v=e^x\).

\[ \begin{aligned}\int xe^x\,dx&=xe^x-\int e^x\,dx\\&=xe^x-e^x+C\\&=(x-1)e^x+C .\end{aligned} \]

(b) \(u=e^x\Rightarrow du=e^x\,dx\); \(dv=x\,dx\Rightarrow v=\frac{x^2}{2}\).

\[ \int xe^x\,dx=\frac{x^2}{2}e^x-\int\frac{x^2}{2}e^x\,dx . \]

This is true, but the new integral has \(x^2\) instead of \(x\): it is harder than the original. Choice (b) is a dead end.

Lesson: pick \(u\) to be the factor that gets simpler when differentiated (\(x\to1\)), and \(dv\) to be a factor you can integrate without it getting worse (\(e^x\to e^x\)).

Example 7: double IBP

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int x^2e^x\,dx\).

Solution. \(u=x^2\Rightarrow du=2x\,dx\); \(dv=e^x\,dx\Rightarrow v=e^x\):

\[ \int x^2e^x\,dx=x^2e^x-2\int xe^x\,dx . \]

The power dropped from 2 to 1. Each trade lowers the power of \(x\) by one, so after two trades no power of \(x\) is left. Apply IBP again with the same type of choice (\(u\) = the power of \(x\)); the result is Example 6′(a):

\[ \begin{aligned}\int x^2e^x\,dx&=x^2e^x-2\big(xe^x-e^x\big)+C\\&=e^x\left(x^2-2x+2\right)+C .\end{aligned} \]
Example 8: reduction formula

Use IBP to prove the reduction formula

\[ I_n(x)=\int x^ne^x\,dx=x^ne^x-nI_{n-1}(x). \]

A reduction formula expresses an integral that depends on a whole number \(n\) in terms of the same integral with a smaller \(n\). Applying it repeatedly "reduces" \(n\) down to a case you can do directly.

Proof. \(u=x^n\Rightarrow du=nx^{n-1}\,dx\); \(dv=e^x\,dx\Rightarrow v=e^x\):

\[ \begin{aligned}I_n&=\int x^ne^x\,dx\\&=x^ne^x-\int e^x\cdot nx^{n-1}\,dx\\&=x^ne^x-n\int x^{n-1}e^x\,dx\\&=x^ne^x-nI_{n-1}.\end{aligned} \]

In words: the integral with power \(n\) = \(x^ne^x\) − \(n\) × (the integral with power \(n-1\)).

Using it. \(I_0=\int e^x\,dx=e^x+C\). Then \(I_1=xe^x-I_0\), \(I_2=x^2e^x-2I_1\) (Example 7), and

\[ \begin{aligned}I_3&=x^3e^x-3I_2\\&=e^x\left(x^3-3x^2+6x-6\right)+C .\end{aligned} \]

The formula is written without \(+C\). Since \(I_n\) and \(I_{n-1}\) are indefinite integrals, the formula is true up to an arbitrary constant, and a \(+C\) is added once at the end.

Example 9: set \(dv=dx\)

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int\ln x\,dx\).

Solution. There is only one factor, so think of the integrand as \(\ln x\cdot1\). You cannot integrate \(\ln x\) yet, but you can differentiate it, so it must be \(u\); the 1 is the easiest possible \(dv\). Let \(u=\ln x\Rightarrow du=\frac1x\,dx\); \(dv=dx\Rightarrow v=x\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\int\ln x\,dx&=x\ln x-\int x\cdot\frac1x\,dx\\&=x\ln x-\int1\,dx\\&=x\ln x-x+C .\end{aligned} \]

Lesson: when a function is easy to differentiate but you don't know its integral (\(\ln x\), \(\arctan x\), \(\sin^{-1}x\)), let it be \(u\) and let \(dv=dx\).

Example 10: substitution with IBP

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int\cos\sqrt x\,dx\).

Solution. First substitute to remove the square root: \(w=\sqrt x\), so \(x=w^2\) and \(dx=2w\,dw\).There is no \(\frac1{\sqrt x}\) to act as a fingerprint, so instead solve for \(x\); the exchange rate \(2w\,dw\) brings in the factor \(w\) that makes a product for IBP.

\[ \begin{aligned}\int\cos\sqrt x\,dx&=\int\cos w\cdot2w\,dw\\&=2\int w\cos w\,dw .\end{aligned} \]

Now IBP with \(u=w\Rightarrow du=dw\); \(dv=\cos w\,dw\Rightarrow v=\sin w\):

\[ \begin{aligned}&2\int w\cos w\,dw\\&\quad=2\left(w\sin w-\int\sin w\,dw\right)\\&\quad=2\left(w\sin w+\cos w\right)+C .\end{aligned} \]

Return to \(x\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\int\cos\sqrt x\,dx&=2\sqrt x\,\sin\sqrt x\\&\quad+2\cos\sqrt x+C .\end{aligned} \]

Lesson: a substitution can turn an integral that looks impossible into a standard IBP. (Here the letter \(w\) is used for the substitution so that \(u\) is free for IBP.)

You can now: choose \(u\) and \(dv\) so the new integral is easier; apply IBP twice in a row for \(x^2e^x\)-type integrals; prove and use a reduction formula such as \(I_n=x^ne^x-nI_{n-1}\); use \(dv=dx\) for \(\ln x\); and substitute first when a root is inside.

9Steps for IBP, and choosing \(u\) and \(dv\) ~5 min

What is this?

IBP is always the same three moves: split the integrand into the strip you differentiate and the strip you integrate, work out the other strip, and make the trade. Everything depends on the split, and LIATE is a ranking that tells you which piece to differentiate.

Steps to apply IBP

Given an indefinite integral of the form \(\displaystyle\int f(x)\,g'(x)\,dx\):

  1. Do: Choose a \(u\)/\(dv\) pair. The pair decides whether the trade gives an easier integral; LIATE (below) makes the choice for you.
  2. Do: Calculate \(du\) (differentiate \(u\)) and \(v\) (integrate \(dv\); no \(+C\) needed here). These give the other side of the rectangle and the other strip, the pieces of the new integral; one \(+C\) at the very end covers everything.
  3. Do: Apply the IBP formula \(\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du\). This makes the trade: the finished rectangle \(uv\), minus the new strip \(\int v\,du\), which you then evaluate.
Theorem: IBP for definite integrals

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be differentiable functions (with \(f'\), \(g'\) continuous on \([a,b]\), so that the integrals exist). Then

\[ \begin{aligned}\int_a^bf(x)\,g'(x)\,dx&=\Big[f(x)g(x)\Big]_a^b\\&\quad-\int_a^bg(x)\,f'(x)\,dx .\end{aligned} \]

In words: same formula as before, but the "\(uv\)" part is evaluated at the two limits, and the new integral keeps the same limits.

Reading the formula
\(\Big[f(x)g(x)\Big]_a^b\)
the big rectangle minus the small rectangle: the finished piece, evaluated at the two ends.
\(\int_a^bg(x)\,f'(x)\,dx\)
the new strip, over the same interval.
same limits
unlike substitution, the variable is still \(x\), so the limits do not change.

For example, with \(u=x\), \(dv=\cos x\,dx\) (so \(du=dx\), \(v=\sin x\)):

\[ \begin{aligned}\int_0^{\pi}x\cos x\,dx&=\Big[x\sin x\Big]_0^{\pi}-\int_0^{\pi}\sin x\,dx\\&=0-\Big[-\cos x\Big]_0^{\pi}\\&=0-(1+1)=-2 .\end{aligned} \]

Choosing \(u\)/\(dv\) pairs

  • Choose \(u\) and \(dv\) so that the integral \(\int v\,du\) is simpler than the one given.
  • Try \(u\) = the "part" of the integrand that is easier to differentiate (gets simpler when differentiated).
  • Set \(u\) by the LIATE priority ordering (roughly): Logarithmic, Inverse trigonometric, Algebraic (powers of \(x\), polynomials), Trigonometric, Exponential. Whichever type comes first in this list is \(u\); the rest is \(dv\). The types early in the list get simpler when differentiated, and the types late in the list stay the same size when integrated, so the trade gives an easier integral.
  • Indirect applications:
    • Multiple applications, useful for integrands like \(x^n\cos x\), \(x^ne^x\), \(e^x\cos x\), \(\sin mx\cos nx\). Keep the same function type as \(u\) (and as \(dv\)) each time; switching undoes the previous step. Switching integrates what you just differentiated, so the second trade hands back the integral you started with, as \(I=I\).
    • Set \(dv=dx\) (Example 9).
    • Combine the Substitution Rule with IBP (Example 10).
  • Like substitution, choosing the parts is a non-linear problem: it needs practice, intuition and perseverance.
What is this? (LIATE)

LIATE ranks function types by how much they gain from being differentiated. At the top, logs and inverse trig functions turn into simple fractions when differentiated, but you may not even know their integrals. At the bottom, exponentials and sines and cosines integrate without getting worse. So differentiate the type that comes first and integrate the rest.

Why LIATE works. Logs and inverse trig functions have simple derivatives (\(\frac1x\), \(\frac1{1+x^2}\)) but awkward integrals, so they should be differentiated. Exponentials and sines/cosines integrate without getting worse, so they make a good \(dv\). Powers of \(x\) sit in the middle: they shrink when differentiated.

LIATE chooser: try both choices of u

The first choice in each list is the LIATE choice.

Going further (examined): two exam patterns

1. The integral comes back. For \(I=\int e^x\sin x\,dx\), two rounds of IBP (keeping \(u\) = the trig function both times) give \(I=e^x\sin x-e^x\cos x-I\). Do not go round again: treat \(I\) as an unknown and solve, \(2I=e^x(\sin x-\cos x)\), then add \(+C\). (Group work 5, and Example 11(b) below.)

2. The function is not given. IBP works even if \(f\) is an unknown function, as long as you are told enough values. For example, with \(u=x\), \(dv=f'(x)\,dx\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\int_0^1x\,f'(x)\,dx&=\Big[x\,f(x)\Big]_0^1-\int_0^1f(x)\,dx\\&=f(1)-\int_0^1f(x)\,dx .\end{aligned} \]

In words: moving the derivative off \(f\) and onto \(x\) costs a boundary term. Question type L13.3 (and reduction formula proofs) use this.

You can now: apply the three IBP steps; use the definite form with the boundary term \(\big[uv\big]_a^b\); choose \(u\) by LIATE and explain why; keep the same type as \(u\) in repeated IBP; solve for \(I\) when the integral comes back; and use IBP when \(f\) is known only by its values.

10Choice of techniques ~2 min

What is this?

Two different keys can open the same lock. Substitution and IBP can both find an antiderivative of the same function, and the answers may look different. But two antiderivatives of the same function have the same slope everywhere, so on an interval their graphs are the same curve shifted up or down: they differ only by a constant, and the \(+C\) absorbs it.

Example 11

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int\sin x\cos x\,dx\) by (a) the Substitution Rule, (b) Integration by Parts.

(a) \(u=\sin x\), \(du=\cos x\,dx\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\int\sin x\cos x\,dx&=\int u\,du=\frac{u^2}{2}+C\\&=\frac{\sin^2x}{2}+C .\end{aligned} \]

(With \(u=\cos x\) instead, \(du=-\sin x\,dx\) and you get \(-\frac{\cos^2x}{2}+C\).)

(b) \(u=\sin x\Rightarrow du=\cos x\,dx\); \(dv=\cos x\,dx\Rightarrow v=\sin x\). Call the integral \(I\):

\[ \begin{aligned}I&=\sin x\cdot\sin x-\int\sin x\cos x\,dx\\&=\sin^2x-I .\end{aligned} \]

The original integral reappears. Treat \(I\) as an unknown number in an equation; going round again would only bring it back once more. Solve for it: \(2I=\sin^2x\), so \(I=\dfrac{\sin^2x}{2}+C\).

Three answers? Using \(\sin x\cos x=\frac12\sin2x\) gives a third answer, \(-\frac{\cos2x}{4}+C\). All three are correct: \(-\frac{\cos^2x}{2}=\frac{\sin^2x}{2}-\frac12\) and \(-\frac{\cos2x}4=\frac{\sin^2x}2-\frac14\). They differ by constants. Check with the graph below.

Lesson: two correct antiderivatives of the same function can look different but always differ by a constant (the graphs are vertical shifts of each other). To check an answer, differentiate it.

11Summary

  • Thanks to the FTC, every differentiation rule has a corresponding integration rule: if \(y'\) is known, then \(\int y'\,dx=y+C\).
    • \(y=x^n\): Power Rule \(\iff\) Power Rule
    • \(y=f(g(x))\): Chain Rule \(\iff\) Substitution Rule
    • \(y=f(x)g(x)\): Product Rule \(\iff\) Integration by Parts
  • The Substitution Rule will be your "go to" tool for integration. Look for an "inside function" and its derivative.
  • If substitution fails, especially for products of functions, reach for Integration by Parts with a smart choice for \(u\) (LIATE).
  • To check your answer to an indefinite integral, see if the integrand matches the derivative of your answer.
  • Over a symmetric interval \([-a,a]\): odd functions integrate to 0, even functions to twice the right half.
  • More techniques of integration come in Lecture 14 (trigonometric integrals, trigonometric substitution, partial fractions).

1. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2x\cos x\,dx\).

A student's work:

Let \(u=\sin x\), \(du=\cos x\,dx\).

\[ \begin{aligned}\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2x\cos x\,dx&=\int_0^{\pi/2}u^2\,du\\&=\left[\frac{u^3}{3}\right]_0^{\pi/2}=\frac{\pi^3}{24}.\end{aligned} \]
Hint

When \(x=\frac\pi2\), what is \(u\)?

Answer

The limits were not changed. \(x=0\to u=0\) and \(x=\frac\pi2\to u=\sin\frac\pi2=1\). Correct value: \(\int_0^1u^2\,du=\frac13\) (Example 5).

2. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int x\sin x\,dx\).

A student's work:

\(u=x\), \(dv=\sin x\,dx\), so \(du=dx\), \(v=\cos x\).

\[ \begin{aligned}\int x\sin x\,dx&=x\cos x-\int\cos x\,dx\\&=x\cos x-\sin x+C.\end{aligned} \]
Hint

Differentiate the final answer. Do you get \(x\sin x\)?

Answer

\(v\) is wrong: an antiderivative of \(\sin x\) is \(-\cos x\). Then \(\int x\sin x\,dx=-x\cos x-\int(-\cos x)\,dx\) \({}=-x\cos x+\sin x+C\). (Differentiating the student's answer gives \(\cos x-x\sin x-\cos x=-x\sin x\): off by a sign.)

3. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int\cos(x^2)\,dx\).

A student's work:

Let \(u=x^2\), \(du=2x\,dx\), so \(dx=\dfrac{du}{2x}\).

\[ \begin{aligned}\int\cos(x^2)\,dx&=\int\cos u\,\frac{du}{2x}\\&=\frac{1}{2x}\sin u+C\\&=\frac{\sin(x^2)}{2x}+C.\end{aligned} \]
Hint

Which step treats something that is not a constant as a constant?

Answer

\(\frac1{2x}\) is not a constant, so it cannot be taken outside \(\int\ldots du\) (the "leaving an \(x\)-term" mistake). Check: the derivative of \(\frac{\sin(x^2)}{2x}\) is \(\cos(x^2)-\frac{\sin(x^2)}{2x^2}\), not \(\cos(x^2)\). The substitution needs a factor \(x\) that is not there. In fact \(\int\cos(x^2)\,dx\) has no formula in terms of the usual functions Going further; compare \(\int x\cos(x^2)\,dx=\frac12\sin(x^2)+C\), which works.

\(du=\frac{dx}{1+x^2}\), \(v=x\): \(\int\arctan x\,dx=x\arctan x-\int\frac{x}{1+x^2}\,dx\). With \(w=1+x^2\), \(dw=2x\,dx\): \(\int\frac{x}{1+x^2}dx=\frac12\ln(1+x^2)\). So

\[ \begin{aligned}\int\arctan x\,dx&=x\arctan x\\&\quad-\tfrac12\ln\left(1+x^2\right)+C .\end{aligned} \]

5. \(\displaystyle\int e^x\sin(x)\,dx\)

Hint 1

IBP twice, keeping \(u\) = the trig function both times (section 9: keep the same type).

Hint 2

After two rounds the original integral appears again. Call it \(I\) and solve the equation for \(I\).

Answer

\(u=\sin x\), \(dv=e^x\,dx\): \(I=e^x\sin x-\int e^x\cos x\,dx\). Then \(u=\cos x\), \(dv=e^x\,dx\): \(\int e^x\cos x\,dx=e^x\cos x+\int e^x\sin x\,dx\) \({}=e^x\cos x+I\). So \(I=e^x\sin x-e^x\cos x-I\), and

\[ \int e^x\sin x\,dx=\frac{e^x(\sin x-\cos x)}{2}+C . \]

6. \(\displaystyle\int\frac{x}{x^4+1}\,dx\)

Hint

Write \(x^4=(x^2)^2\). Which substitution makes the bottom \(1+u^2\)? Recall \(\int\frac{du}{1+u^2}=\arctan u+C\).

Answer

\(u=x^2\), \(du=2x\,dx\): \(\displaystyle\int\frac{x\,dx}{1+(x^2)^2}=\frac12\int\frac{du}{1+u^2}\) \(\displaystyle{}=\frac12\arctan(x^2)+C\).

7. \(\displaystyle\int x\sqrt{x+2}\,dx\)

Hint

Back substitution (Common mistakes): \(u=x+2\) and \(x=u-2\).

Answer

\(\int(u-2)\sqrt u\,du=\int\left(u^{3/2}-2u^{1/2}\right)du\) \({}=\frac25u^{5/2}-\frac43u^{3/2}+C\), so

\[ \begin{aligned}\int x\sqrt{x+2}\,dx&=\tfrac25(x+2)^{5/2}\\&\quad-\tfrac43(x+2)^{3/2}+C .\end{aligned} \]

(IBP with \(u=x\), \(dv=\sqrt{x+2}\,dx\) also works and gives an equal answer.)

8. \(\displaystyle\int x^3\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx\)

Hint

Same idea as Example 4: \(u=1-x^2\), \(du=-2x\,dx\), and \(x^2=1-u\).

Answer

\(x^3\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx=x^2\sqrt{1-x^2}\cdot x\,dx\) \({}=(1-u)\sqrt u\cdot\left(-\tfrac12du\right)\):

\[ \begin{aligned}&-\frac12\int\left(u^{1/2}-u^{3/2}\right)du\\&\quad=-\frac13u^{3/2}+\frac15u^{5/2}+C\\&\quad=\frac15(1-x^2)^{5/2}\\&\qquad-\frac13(1-x^2)^{3/2}+C .\end{aligned} \]

9. \(\displaystyle\int\frac{e^{2x}}{1+e^x}\,dx\)

Hint

\(e^{2x}=e^x\cdot e^x\). Let \(u=1+e^x\); then \(e^x\,dx=du\) and the other \(e^x\) is \(u-1\).

Answer

\(\displaystyle\int\frac{e^x\cdot e^x\,dx}{1+e^x}=\int\frac{u-1}{u}\,du\) \(\displaystyle{}=\int\left(1-\frac1u\right)du=u-\ln|u|+C\). So the answer is \(1+e^x-\ln(1+e^x)+C\), which is the same as \(e^x-\ln\left(1+e^x\right)+C\) (the 1 joins the constant).

10. Use IBP to prove the reduction formula

\[ \begin{aligned}I_{m,n}(x)&=\int x^n(\ln x)^m\,dx\\&=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}(\ln x)^m\\&\quad-\frac{m}{n+1}I_{m-1,n}(x)+C .\end{aligned} \]
Hint

LIATE: \(u=(\ln x)^m\) (logarithmic), \(dv=x^n\,dx\). What must \(n\) not be?

Answer

For \(n\ne-1\): \(u=(\ln x)^m\Rightarrow du=m(\ln x)^{m-1}\cdot\frac1x\,dx\); \(dv=x^n\,dx\Rightarrow v=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned}I_{m,n}&=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}(\ln x)^m\\&\quad-\int\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}\cdot\frac{m(\ln x)^{m-1}}{x}\,dx\\&=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}(\ln x)^m\\&\quad-\frac{m}{n+1}\int x^n(\ln x)^{m-1}\,dx,\end{aligned} \]

and the last integral is \(I_{m-1,n}\). (For \(n=-1\), use the substitution \(u=\ln x\) instead, as in Group work 2.)

15Concept checks

Short questions to test understanding, not computation.

C1. Why does \(u=x^2\) work for \(\int x\,e^{x^2}\,dx\) but not for \(\int e^{x^2}\,dx\)?

Answer

\(du=2x\,dx\). In the first integral the factor \(x\,dx=\frac12du\) is present, so everything converts to \(u\). In the second there is no \(x\) to absorb, and \(dx=\frac{du}{2x}\) would leave an \(x\) behind. (\(\int e^{x^2}dx\) has no formula in terms of the usual functions at all.)

C2. Student A gets \(\frac{\sin^2x}{2}+C\) and student B gets \(-\frac{\cos^2x}{2}+C\) for \(\int\sin x\cos x\,dx\). Who is right?

Answer

Both. \(-\frac{\cos^2x}2=-\frac{1-\sin^2x}2=\frac{\sin^2x}2-\frac12\): the two differ by a constant, which \(C\) absorbs. Differentiating either gives \(\sin x\cos x\).

C3. In a definite integral done by substitution with changed limits, do you need to substitute back to \(x\) at the end?

Answer

No. Once the limits are \(g(a)\) and \(g(b)\), the answer is a number computed entirely in \(u\). Substituting back is only needed if you kept the \(x\)-limits (Example 5(a)).

C4. Is \(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}(x^3+1)\,dx=0\) because the interval is symmetric?

Answer

No. \(x^3+1\) is neither odd nor even. Split it: \(\int_{-1}^1x^3\,dx=0\) (odd), but \(\int_{-1}^11\,dx=2\) (even). The value is 2. Only the odd part cancels.

C5. For \(\int x\ln x\,dx\), which should be \(u\), and why?

Answer

\(u=\ln x\) (L before A). Then \(du=\frac{dx}x\), \(v=\frac{x^2}2\), and \(\int v\,du=\int\frac x2\,dx\) is easy: the answer is \(\frac{x^2\ln x}{2}-\frac{x^2}{4}+C\). With \(u=x\) you would need \(v=\int\ln x\,dx=x\ln x-x\) and the new integral would contain \(x\ln x\) again.

C6. IBP needs two factors. How can it help with \(\int\cos^{-1}x\,dx\), which has only one?

Answer

Use \(dv=dx\) (Example 9): \(u=\cos^{-1}x\), \(du=-\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\), \(v=x\). Then \(\int\cos^{-1}x\,dx=x\cos^{-1}x+\int\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}dx\) \({}=x\cos^{-1}x-\sqrt{1-x^2}+C\) (substitution \(w=1-x^2\) for the last integral).
