0Where this lecture sits

1Rates of change: why we need limits ~6 min

What is this?

You drive across a long bridge. Your average speed for the whole crossing is easy: distance ÷ time. Now shrink the time window: your average speed over the last minute, the last second, the last thousandth of a second. These averages settle down to one number, the reading on your speedometer. You can never use a window of zero length (that would be zero distance ÷ zero time), but you can see clearly where the averages are heading. That "where it is heading" is a limit.

Picture a long sea bridge and think of driving across it. Two different "speeds" make sense:

  • your average speed for the whole crossing: total distance ÷ total time;
  • your speed at one instant, the number on the speedometer right now.

The first is easy arithmetic. The second is what calculus is for. Now make the same two ideas precise for any function \(y=f(x)\). A rate of change means "how much \(y\) changes for each unit change in \(x\)".

Definition

The average rate of change of \(y=f(x)\) over the interval \([x_1,x_2]\) is

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=\frac{f(x_2)-f(x_1)}{x_2-x_1} \] \[ =\frac{f(x_1+h)-f(x_1)}{h},\qquad h\ne0. \]

In words: average rate of change = (change in output) ÷ (change in input) = (rise) ÷ (run).

Reading the formula
\(f(x_1+h)-f(x_1)\)
how much the output changed during the step: the distance covered in the time window.
\(h\)
the size of the step: the length of the time window.
\(\dfrac{f(x_1+h)-f(x_1)}{h}\)
the average rate over that window: the slope of the line through the two points.
\(h\ne0\)
a window of zero length would give 0 ÷ 0. Getting round this is exactly what a limit does.

Notation: \(\Delta\) (the Greek capital letter "delta") means "change in". So \(\Delta x=x_2-x_1\) is the change in \(x\) and \(\Delta y=f(x_2)-f(x_1)\) is the change in \(y\). The second form writes the step size as \(h=\Delta x\), so \(x_2=x_1+h\). We need \(h\ne0\), otherwise we would divide by zero.

What is this? (secant and tangent)

On a graph, an average rate is the slope of a straight line joining two points of the curve. Shrinking the time window slides the second point along the curve towards the first, and the line swings round until it just touches the curve at the first point. The slope it settles to is the rate at that one instant.

Geometrically, put \(P=(x_1,f(x_1))\) and \(Q=(x_2,f(x_2))\) on the graph. A secant line is a straight line through two points of a curve. Its slope is exactly rise ÷ run, so:

  • for \(\Delta x=h\gt0\), \(m_{PQ}\) (the slope of the line \(PQ\)) is the slope of the secant line between \(x_1\) and \(x_2\);
  • as \(\Delta x\to0\) (read: "as \(\Delta x\) approaches 0"), \(m_{PQ}\) becomes the slope of the tangent line at \(x_1\): the straight line that just touches the curve at \(P\) and points in the same direction as the curve there.

Secant lines turning into the tangent line

y = f(x) = x²/2 + 1/2secant PQtangent at PΔx and Δy

Here \(f(x)=\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac12\) and \(x_1=1\), so \(P=(1,1)\). Drag the slider towards 0: \(Q\) slides down the curve to \(P\), and the secant slope \(m_{PQ}=1+\frac h2\) gets closer and closer to 1, the slope of the tangent. From either side the answer is the same.

Definition

The instantaneous rate of change of \(y=f(x)\) at \(x_1\) is the slope of the tangent line at \(x_1\).

In words: rate of change at one point = slope of the line that just touches the graph there = what the secant slopes settle down to as the second point moves onto the first.

The problem: we cannot simply put \(h=0\) in \(\frac{f(x_1+h)-f(x_1)}{h}\), because that gives \(\frac00\). We need a precise way to say "what the expression gets close to as \(h\) gets close to 0". That is a limit. (The slope of the tangent, written as a limit, is the derivative, Lecture 5.)

Example (for the figure)

For \(f(x)=\frac{x^2}2+\frac12\) at \(x_1=1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} m_{PQ}&=\frac{f(1+h)-f(1)}{h}\\ &=\frac{\frac{(1+h)^2}{2}+\frac12-1}{h}\\ &=\frac{h+\frac{h^2}{2}}{h}=1+\frac h2\quad(h\ne0). \end{aligned} \]

As \(h\) gets closer to 0 (from either side), \(1+\frac h2\) gets closer to 1, so the tangent at \(P\) has slope 1. Notice that we cancelled \(h\) before letting \(h\) shrink; this "simplify first, then let it approach" pattern returns in section 7.

You can now: work out an average rate of change as rise ÷ run; describe the tangent slope as what the secant slopes settle down to; and explain why putting \(h=0\) fails, so a limit is needed.

2What a limit is ~6 min

What is this?

Walk along the graph towards one point on the x-axis, from both sides, and watch your height. The limit is the height you are clearly heading for as you get closer and closer. What happens exactly at the point does not count: there may be a dot at that height, a dot somewhere else, or no dot at all. A bridge with a missing last plank still clearly leads to the far bank.

The concept

  • A limit studies the behaviour of \(f(x)\) around a point \(x_0\): what the values do for \(x\) near \(x_0\).
  • The limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\to x_0\) exists if the values of \(f(x)\) get closer to a single number \(L\) as \(x\) gets closer to \(x_0\).
  • The value of \(f(x)\) at \(x_0\) itself is NOT taken into account. It may equal \(L\), be something else, or not exist at all; the limit does not care.

Analogy: the limit is where the road is heading, not what is (or isn't) at the end of the road. A bridge with a missing last plank still clearly leads to the far bank.

Definition, intuitive

Suppose \(f(x)\) is a function defined on an open interval about \(x_0\). We write

\[ \lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=L \]

and say "the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(x_0\) is \(L\)" if we can make the values of \(f(x)\) arbitrarily close to \(L\) for all \(x\) sufficiently close to \(x_0\) but not equal to \(x_0\).

In words: limit of f as x approaches x₀ = the single number that f(x) can be kept as close to as you like, just by taking x close enough to (but not at) x₀.

The terms, in plain language:

  • Open interval about \(x_0\): an interval of the form \((x_0-r,\,x_0+r)\) with \(r\gt0\), not including its endpoints. It means \(f\) is defined at points on both sides of \(x_0\), as close as we like.
  • Arbitrarily close to \(L\): however small a gap you name (0.1, 0.001, 0.000001, …), the distance between \(f(x)\) and \(L\) can be made smaller than it.
  • Sufficiently close to \(x_0\): close enough. How close is "enough" may depend on how small a gap you named.
  • but not equal to \(x_0\): \(x=x_0\) itself is never used.

Note on the wording: since \(x=x_0\) is never used, \(f\) need not be defined at \(x_0\) itself (the function \(g\) in section 3 is undefined at \(x_0\), yet its limit exists). So read "defined on an open interval about \(x_0\)" as "defined on an open interval about \(x_0\), except possibly at \(x_0\)", which is the standard form of the definition.

Note: the precise \(\epsilon,\delta\)-definition is out of scope for this course. You will not be examined on it.

Going further
What is this? (the precise definition)

Think of it as a challenge game. You name a tolerance: "get the height within 0.01 of L". I must answer with a distance: "keep x within this distance of the point, but not on it, and every height you see will be within your tolerance." The limit is L if I can win every round, however tiny your tolerance. A smaller tolerance usually forces me to answer with a smaller distance. If even one tolerance beats me, the limit is not L.

For the curious, the precise definition turns "arbitrarily close" and "sufficiently close" into numbers. \(\epsilon\) ("epsilon") is the allowed gap for \(f(x)\) and \(\delta\) ("delta") is how close \(x\) must be:

\[ \lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=L \]

means: for every \(\epsilon\gt0\) there is a \(\delta\gt0\) such that

\[ 0\lt|x-x_0|\lt\delta\ \Rightarrow\ |f(x)-L|\lt\epsilon. \]

In words: whatever accuracy ε you demand for f(x), there is a distance δ such that every x within δ of x₀ (other than x₀ itself) gives f(x) within ε of L. The "\(0\lt\)" part is the "not equal to \(x_0\)".

Reading the formula
\(\epsilon\)
your tolerance: how close to \(L\) the height must be.
\(\delta\)
my answer: how close to the point \(x\) must be.
\(0\lt|x-x_0|\lt\delta\)
x is within my distance of the point, but not the point itself.
\(|f(x)-L|\lt\epsilon\)
the height there is within your tolerance of \(L\).
for every \(\epsilon\gt0\) there is a \(\delta\gt0\)
whatever tolerance you name, I have a winning answer.

Here \(x_0=3\). The three options show the same curve, but with the point at \(x_0\) filled in, missing (open circle), or moved. Shrink the distance: the orange and green points close in on the height \(L\approx2.069\) in all three cases. The limit is the same each time because the value at \(x_0\) is not taken into account.

You can now: explain a limit as the height the graph heads for as \(x\) approaches a point; say why the value at the point itself is ignored; and read the terms "arbitrarily close" and "sufficiently close" in the definition.

3One-sided limits, and when a limit exists ~8 min

What is this?

Now walk towards the point from one side only. The left-hand limit is the height you head for walking in from the left; the right-hand limit is the height you head for walking in from the right. Picture two hikers coming along the path from opposite sides towards the same spot. If they head for the same height, the limit exists and is that height. If there is a step in the path, they head for different heights, and there is no single limit.

One-sided limits

Sometimes the function behaves differently on the two sides of \(x_0\) (think of a staircase: just before the step you are at one height, just after it at another). Then it helps to look at each side separately.

Definition, intuitive

We write

\[ \lim_{x\to x_0^-}f(x)=L \]

and say "the left-hand limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(x_0\) is \(L\)", or "the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(x_0\) from the left is \(L\)", if we can make the values of \(f(x)\) arbitrarily close to \(L\) for all \(x\) sufficiently close to \(x_0\) and less than \(x_0\).

In words: left-hand limit = the number f(x) settles towards when x creeps up to x₀ from below (from the left on the number line).

The right-hand limit of \(f(x)\) is defined similarly, with \(x\to x_0^+\): use only \(x\) greater than \(x_0\) (approaching from the right).

Notation: the small superscript is only a direction label. \(x_0^-\) is not a number "slightly less than \(x_0\)"; \(x\to x_0^-\) just means "\(x\) approaches \(x_0\) through values less than \(x_0\)". The ordinary limit \(\lim_{x\to x_0}\) is called the two-sided limit when we want to contrast it with these.

When the limit exists

Take three functions \(f\), \(g\), \(h\) with identical graphs except at \(x_0\) (choose them in the figure of section 2):

\(f(x_0)=L\)The point at \(x_0\) is on the curve, at height \(L\).
\(g(x_0)\) DNE"DNE" = "does not exist": \(g\) is not defined at \(x_0\) (an open circle, a "hole").
\(h(x_0)\ne L\)\(h\) is defined at \(x_0\), but the dot sits away from the curve.

In all three cases

\[ \lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=\lim_{x\to x_0}g(x)=\lim_{x\to x_0}h(x)=L\in\mathbb R \]

(\(\in\mathbb R\) means "is a real number", i.e. a finite ordinary number, not \(\infty\)), and for each of them the left-hand and right-hand limits agree:

\[ \lim_{x\to x_0^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to x_0^+}f(x),\qquad \lim_{x\to x_0^-}g(x)=\lim_{x\to x_0^+}g(x), \] \[ \lim_{x\to x_0^-}h(x)=\lim_{x\to x_0^+}h(x). \]

In words: the limit only looks at the curve near x₀, so filling in, removing or moving the single point at x₀ never changes it.

When the limit does not exist

What is this? (when the limit fails)

A walk can fail to head for one height in three ways. The two sides can lead to different heights (a step). The path can climb or fall forever (it blows up). Or it can zigzag up and down, faster and faster, and never settle.

Here are four functions near \(x=0\). Try them in the figure below.

Four ways a limit at 0 can fail

the functionx = −d (from the left)x = +d (from the right)

Move the slider towards 0 and watch the two readouts: they head to two different numbers (\(f\)), off to opposite infinities (\(g\)), keep jumping about (\(h\)), or both grow without bound (\(k\)).

  • \(f(x)=\dfrac{|x|}{x}\): for \(x\gt0\), \(|x|=x\) so \(f(x)=1\); for \(x\lt0\), \(|x|=-x\) so \(f(x)=-1\). Hence \(\lim_{x\to0^-}f(x)=-1\ne1=\lim_{x\to0^+}f(x)\): the one-sided limits disagree (a jump).
  • \(g(x)=\dfrac1x\): just left of 0 the values are huge and negative, just right of 0 huge and positive, so \(\lim_{x\to0^-}g(x)\ne\lim_{x\to0^+}g(x)\). (In fact neither side settles to a number at all.)
  • \(h(x)=\sin\left(\dfrac1x\right)\) oscillates infinitely: as \(x\to0\), \(\frac1x\) runs through infinitely many whole turns, so \(h\) swings between \(-1\) and \(1\) infinitely often and never settles on one value.
  • \(k(x)=\dfrac1{x^2}\): from both sides the values grow without bound. We write \(\lim_{x\to0^-}k(x)=\infty=\lim_{x\to0^+}k(x)\).

Conclusion:

\[ \lim_{x\to0}f(x),\ \lim_{x\to0}g(x),\ \lim_{x\to0}h(x)\ \text{DNE}; \] \[ \lim_{x\to0}k(x)=\infty. \]
What "= ∞" means

\(\infty\) ("infinity") is not a number. Writing \(\lim_{x\to0}k(x)=\infty\) is shorthand for "the values grow larger than any number you name". The limit still does not exist as a real number (it is not \(L\in\mathbb R\)); the notation just records how it fails. Infinite limits are the topic of Lecture 3.

The existence theorem

Theorem \[ \lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=L \]

if and only if

\[ \lim_{x\to x_0^-}f(x)=L=\lim_{x\to x_0^+}f(x). \]

In words: the two-sided limit is L exactly when the left-hand limit and the right-hand limit are both L.

Reading the formula
\(\lim_{x\to x_0^-}f(x)\)
the height the hiker from the left heads for.
\(\lim_{x\to x_0^+}f(x)\)
the height the hiker from the right heads for.
\(=L=\)
both hikers head for the same height \(L\).
if and only if
it works both ways: a limit means both hikers agree, and both hikers agreeing means a limit.

"If and only if" means the statement works in both directions: if the two-sided limit is \(L\) then both one-sided limits are \(L\); and if both one-sided limits are \(L\) then the two-sided limit is \(L\). In summary:

  • Limit exists: \(\lim_{x\to x_0^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to x_0^+}f(x)=L\in\mathbb R\).
  • Limit does not exist, for example when
    • \(\lim_{x\to x_0^-}f(x)=L_1\ne L_2=\lim_{x\to x_0^+}f(x)\) (the two sides disagree, like \(\frac{|x|}x\));
    • \(f(x)\) oscillates infinitely (like \(\sin\frac1x\));
    • \(f(x)\) approaches \(\pm\infty\) (like \(\frac1x\) or \(\frac1{x^2}\)).
How the theorem is used

Whenever a function has a different formula on each side of a point (absolute values, piecewise definitions):

  1. Do: work out the two one-sided limits separately, each with the formula for its own side. Each formula only describes the path on one side, so each hiker must be followed with the right one.
  2. Do: compare. Same number: that is the limit. Both hikers head for the same height, so by the theorem the two-sided limit is that height.
  3. Do: Different numbers: the limit does not exist. There is a step in the path at the point, so there is no single height to head for.

This is exactly how the group work questions and the exam taster's absolute-value questions are solved.

You can now: read one-sided limits off a graph or a formula; use the existence theorem to decide whether a two-sided limit exists; and name the ways a limit can fail (a jump, blowing up, endless oscillation).

4Three ways to find a limit ~3 min

What is this?

There are three ways to find out where the walk is heading. A table is like taking height readings at a few spots on the way in. A graph is like looking at the whole path from above. Algebra rewrites the formula so you can see exactly where it leads. The first two are evidence; only the third is proof.

Here is one limit found three ways:

\[ \lim_{x\to0}f(x),\qquad f(x)=\frac{\sqrt{x^2+1}-1}{x^2}. \]

Putting in \(x=0\) gives \(\frac{\sqrt1-1}{0}=\frac00\), which has no value, so we must work harder.

1. Numeric: make a table

Evaluate \(f(x)\) at values of \(x\) closer and closer to 0. Since \(f(-x)=f(x)\) (only \(x^2\) appears), the same value works for \(\pm x\).

\(x\)\(f(x)\)
\(\pm1\)0.414213562
\(\pm0.5\)0.472135955
\(\pm0.25\)0.492422502
\(\pm0.1\)0.498756211
\(\pm0.01\)0.499987501
\(\pm0.001\)0.500000000

The values creep up towards 0.5, which suggests the limit is \(\frac12\).

Note on the last row: the exact value is \(f(\pm0.001)=0.499999875\ldots\), not exactly 0.5; the table's figure is what a calculator shows after rounding. The pattern (approaching 0.5) is unaffected.

2. Graphic: draw the graph

The graph of f(x) = (√(x²+1) − 1)/x²

y = f(x)the points x = ±(table value)

The graph rises to a peak near height \(\frac12\) at \(x=0\), but there is a hole there because \(f(0)\) is \(\frac00\), undefined. The slider steps through the table's rows.

Numeric and graphic methods suggest the answer; they do not prove it. A table can be fooled (see concept check 5), and a graph only shows what you choose to draw. The algebraic method gives the exact answer.

3. Algebraic: simplify, then use the Limit Laws

Multiply the top and bottom by \(\sqrt{x^2+1}+1\), the conjugate of the numerator (same two terms, opposite sign in the middle). This uses \((a-b)(a+b)=a^2-b^2\) to clear the square root: Multiplying top and bottom by the same non-zero number does not change the function, but it turns the top into \(x^2\), which can cancel the \(x^2\) that makes the bottom 0.

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\to0}f(x)&=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sqrt{x^2+1}-1}{x^2}\\ &=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x^2}{x^2\left(\sqrt{x^2+1}+1\right)}\\ &=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+1}+1}\\ &=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(0)^2+1}+1}\\ &=\frac12. \end{aligned} \]

In words: the factor x² that made the bottom zero cancels; what is left can be evaluated by putting x = 0.

The numerator in line 2 is \(\left(\sqrt{x^2+1}\right)^2-1^2=x^2+1-1=x^2\). Cancelling \(x^2\) is allowed because the limit never uses \(x=0\), so \(x^2\ne0\). Why the last step (putting in \(x=0\)) is allowed is explained in sections 5 to 7.

You can now: estimate a limit from a table or a graph; say why these only suggest the answer; and find it exactly by multiplying by the conjugate, cancelling and substituting.

5The Limit Laws ~5 min

What is this?

If one walker's height is heading for 3 and another's is heading for 4, then the sum of their heights is heading for 7 and the product for 12. The Limit Laws say you can do ordinary arithmetic on where things are heading. The catch: each piece must really be heading for a number, and for a quotient the bottom must not be heading for 0.

The Limit Laws say that limits behave well with ordinary arithmetic: you can take the limit of each piece and then combine, provided the pieces' limits exist.

Theorem: Limit Laws

If \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}g(x)\) exist, then:

1. Sum Rule

\[ \lim_{x\to c}[f(x)+g(x)]=\lim_{x\to c}f(x)+\lim_{x\to c}g(x) \]

In words: limit of a sum = sum of the limits.

2. Difference Rule

\[ \lim_{x\to c}[f(x)-g(x)]=\lim_{x\to c}f(x)-\lim_{x\to c}g(x) \]

In words: limit of a difference = difference of the limits.

3. Constant Multiple Rule (\(k\in\mathbb R\), any fixed real number)

\[ \lim_{x\to c}[kf(x)]=k\lim_{x\to c}f(x) \]

In words: a constant factor can be taken outside the limit.

4. Product Rule

\[ \lim_{x\to c}[f(x)g(x)]=\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\cdot\lim_{x\to c}g(x) \]

In words: limit of a product = product of the limits.

5. Quotient Rule, if \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}g(x)\ne0\):

\[ \lim_{x\to c}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right]=\frac{\lim_{x\to c}f(x)}{\lim_{x\to c}g(x)} \]

In words: limit of a quotient = quotient of the limits, but only when the bottom's limit is not zero.

6. Power Rule (\(n\in\mathbb Z^+\), a positive whole number 1, 2, 3, …)

\[ \lim_{x\to c}[f(x)]^n=\left[\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\right]^n \]

In words: limit of a power = power of the limit.

7. Root Rule (\(n\in\mathbb Z^+\))

\[ \lim_{x\to c}\sqrt[n]{f(x)}=\sqrt[n]{\lim_{x\to c}f(x)} \]

In words: limit of an n-th root = n-th root of the limit.

(If \(n\) is even, we assume that \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\gt0\).)

Notes on the conditions:

  • The whole theorem starts with "if both limits exist". If either limit does not exist, the laws say nothing (section 9 and the Find the Mistake show what goes wrong).
  • The Quotient Rule needs the bottom's limit to be non-zero, because dividing by 0 is meaningless. When the bottom's limit is 0, you must simplify first (section 7).
  • The Root Rule's extra condition for even \(n\) (square roots, fourth roots, …) is there because even roots of negative numbers are not real numbers. Odd roots (cube roots, …) need no condition: \(\sqrt[3]{-8}=-2\).

Also note:

  • The laws are proved using the precise \(\epsilon,\delta\)-definition of limit (not required in this course).
  • They apply for one-sided limits also: replace every \(x\to c\) by \(x\to c^-\) (or every one by \(x\to c^+\)).
Going further

Two basic limits, which every calculation uses: for any constant \(k\),

\[ \lim_{x\to c}k=k,\qquad \lim_{x\to c}x=c. \]

In words: a constant stays put; and as x approaches c, x itself approaches c.

From these and the laws, any polynomial's limit can be found by substituting (section 6).

Example 1

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{z\to0}(2z-8)^{\frac13}\).

Here \((2z-8)^{\frac13}=\sqrt[3]{2z-8}\), the cube root. First the inside, using the Difference Rule, then the Constant Multiple Rule and the two basic limits:

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{z\to0}(2z-8)&=\lim_{z\to0}2z-\lim_{z\to0}8\\ &=2\lim_{z\to0}z-8\\ &=2(0)-8=-8. \end{aligned} \]

This limit exists, so the Root Rule with \(n=3\) applies (odd, so no sign condition): A Limit Law may only be used once each piece is known to have a limit; the first calculation shows that the inside does.

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{z\to0}(2z-8)^{\frac13}&=\left(\lim_{z\to0}(2z-8)\right)^{\frac13}\\ &=(-8)^{\frac13}=-2. \end{aligned} \]

In words: the inside heads to −8, so the cube root heads to the cube root of −8, which is −2.

You can now: state the seven Limit Laws; check their conditions (both limits exist, the bottom's limit is not 0, a positive limit inside an even root) before using one; and evaluate a limit step by step with them.

6Direct substitution ~3 min

What is this?

Polynomials, rational functions, roots and trig functions have graphs with no hole or jump at any point where they are defined. So when you walk towards such a point, you arrive at exactly the height the function has there: where the path is heading is where it is. That is why you can just plug in. At a point outside the domain (for example where the bottom is 0) there is no value to plug in, and the shortcut says nothing.

Using the laws step by step every time is slow. For the common types of function, the result is always the same: just put \(x=c\) in.

Theorem: Direct Substitution Property \[ \lim_{x\to c}f(x)=f(c) \]

if \(f\) is any of the following types of functions and \(c\) is in the domain of \(f\) (the set of inputs where \(f\) is defined):

  1. Polynomial functions: sums of terms \(a x^n\) with \(n\) a whole number, e.g. \(3x^4-x+7\).
  2. Rational functions: one polynomial divided by another, e.g. \(\dfrac{y^2+5y+6}{y+2}\).
  3. Root functions: \(\sqrt[n]{x}\), e.g. \(\sqrt x\), \(\sqrt[3]x\).
  4. Trigonometric functions: \(\sin x\), \(\cos x\), \(\tan x\), ….

In words: for these everyday functions, the limit as x approaches c is simply the value at c, as long as the function is defined at c.

A function with this property is called continuous at \(c\): its graph has no break, hole or jump there. Continuity gets its own lecture.

In this set of notes continuity is Lecture 4 (Lecture 3 is limits involving infinity).

Trap: "c is in the domain"

The condition matters. \(\dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1}\) is a rational function, but \(x=1\) is not in its domain (the bottom is 0), so direct substitution does not apply at 1. Putting \(x=1\) in gives \(\frac00\), which tells you nothing about the limit. You must simplify first (section 7). Also, functions built from these types by adding, multiplying, dividing and composing (e.g. \(\cos^4x\), \(\sqrt{x^2+8}\)) can be substituted into as well, as long as everything is defined at \(c\).

Example 2

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{y\to2}\frac{y^2+5y+6}{y+2}\).

This is a rational function, and at \(y=2\) the bottom is \(2+2=4\ne0\), so \(2\) is in the domain. By direct substitution:

\[ \lim_{y\to2}\frac{y^2+5y+6}{y+2}=\frac{2^2+5(2)+6}{2+2}=\frac{20}{4}=5. \]

Check by factorising: \(y^2+5y+6=(y+2)(y+3)\), so for \(y\ne-2\) the function is \(y+3\), and \(2+3=5\). ✓

You can now: decide when direct substitution is allowed (an everyday function, with \(c\) in its domain) and use it; and spot when it is not, such as a bottom that is 0 at \(c\).

7When substitution gives 0/0: factorisation and rationalisation ~4 min

What is this?

Getting 0/0 means the formula breaks down at that one point. Often the path on both sides is perfectly fine and the graph just has a hole there. Cancelling the factor that caused the 0/0 gives a new formula for the same path, which no longer breaks down at the point. Plug into the new formula and you read off the height of the hole, which is the limit. (If the bottom of the new formula is still 0 there, the path blows up instead: Lecture 3.)

If putting \(x=c\) in gives \(\frac00\), the limit might still exist; \(\frac00\) is called an indeterminate form: the form alone does not determine the answer (it could turn out to be 0, 5, \(-\frac13\), anything, or not exist). The trick is to replace the function by a simpler one that agrees with it everywhere except at \(c\). This is justified by the following theorem.

Theorem

If \(f(x)=g(x)\) when \(x\ne c\), then

\[ \lim_{x\to c}f(x)=\lim_{x\to c}g(x), \]

provided the limit exists.

In words: two functions that agree everywhere near c (except possibly at c itself) have the same limit at c.

This is the "value at \(x_0\) is not taken into account" idea from section 2: the two graphs differ by at most one point, which the limit ignores. So cancelling a factor such as \((x-1)\), which is zero only at \(x=1\), does not change the limit at 1.

Example 3: factorisation

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\frac{x^2-2x+1}{x^2+2x-3}\).

Step 1: try substitution. At \(x=1\): top \(1-2+1=0\), bottom \(1+2-3=0\). This is \(\frac00\), so simplify.

Step 2: factorise. Since both are 0 at \(x=1\), both have a factor \((x-1)\) (factor theorem): \((x-1)\) is the cause of the 0/0: it is the part of each formula that is 0 exactly at the hole.

\[ x^2-2x+1=(x-1)^2, \] \[ x^2+2x-3=(x-1)(x+3). \]

Step 3: cancel and substitute. For \(x\ne1\), Cancelling changes the formula only at \(x=1\), which the limit ignores (the theorem above).

\[ \frac{(x-1)^2}{(x-1)(x+3)}=\frac{x-1}{x+3}, \]

so by the theorem above, and then direct substitution (\(x+3=4\ne0\) at \(x=1\)):

\[ \lim_{x\to1}\frac{x^2-2x+1}{x^2+2x-3}=\lim_{x\to1}\frac{x-1}{x+3}=\frac{0}{4}=0. \]

In words: after cancelling the common factor that caused 0/0, the top still heads to 0 while the bottom heads to 4, so the fraction heads to 0.

Example 4: rationalisation

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-1}\frac{\sqrt{x^2+8}-3}{x+1}\).

Step 1. At \(x=-1\): top \(\sqrt{9}-3=0\), bottom \(0\). Again \(\frac00\).

Step 2: rationalise (remove the square root from the numerator) by multiplying top and bottom by the conjugate \(\sqrt{x^2+8}+3\): \((a-b)(a+b)=a^2-b^2\) clears the root, and the new top \(x^2-1\) shows the hidden factor \((x+1)\) that makes the bottom 0.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\sqrt{x^2+8}-3}{x+1}&=\frac{(x^2+8)-9}{(x+1)\left(\sqrt{x^2+8}+3\right)}\\ &=\frac{x^2-1}{(x+1)\left(\sqrt{x^2+8}+3\right)}\\ &=\frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{(x+1)\left(\sqrt{x^2+8}+3\right)}. \end{aligned} \]

Step 3: cancel \((x+1)\) (allowed for \(x\ne-1\)) and substitute:

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\to-1}\frac{\sqrt{x^2+8}-3}{x+1}&=\lim_{x\to-1}\frac{x-1}{\sqrt{x^2+8}+3}\\ &=\frac{-1-1}{\sqrt{9}+3}=\frac{-2}{6}=-\frac13. \end{aligned} \]

In words: multiplying by the conjugate turns "root minus 3" into "x² − 1", which contains the troublesome factor (x + 1); cancel it, then substitute.

original functionsimplified function (dashed)x = c ± d

The original function and the simplified one lie on top of each other everywhere except at \(c\), where the original has a hole (open circle). The limit is the height of the hole: \(0\) in Example 3, \(-\frac13\) in Example 4.

You can now: recognise \(\frac00\) as an indeterminate form; justify cancelling with the theorem above; and evaluate such limits by factorising or by multiplying by the conjugate.

8Summary

  • Limits are the foundation of calculus, the study of the rate of change.
  • Limits describe the behaviour of a function as it approaches a point from the left or right, or both.
  • Limits may or may not exist.
  • We apply the Limit Laws and algebraic techniques such as substitution, factorisation and rationalisation to evaluate limits.
  • Only if the conditions are satisfied does a Limit Law apply.
Method: evaluating a limit at a finite point
  1. Do: Plug in first. If the function is defined at \(c\) and is built from polynomials, roots, trig functions, … (section 6), the answer is \(f(c)\). These graphs have no hole or jump at \(c\), so where the path is heading is the height it has there.
  2. Do: If you get \(\frac{\text{non-zero}}{0}\), the limit is not a finite number (Lecture 3 handles these). The bottom shrinks towards 0 while the top does not, so the fraction grows without bound: the path blows up.
  3. Do: If you get \(\frac00\): factorise and cancel (polynomials), multiply by the conjugate (square roots), or combine fractions; then plug in again. \(\frac00\) only says the formula breaks down at \(c\); removing the cause shows the height of the hole.
  4. Do: If there is an absolute value or a piecewise formula, find the left-hand and right-hand limits separately and compare (section 3). The formula changes at \(c\), so each hiker must be followed with the formula for their own side.

9Common mistakes

Using Limit Laws when the conditions are not met

✗\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2}\frac{2f(x)}{x-2}=\frac{2\lim_{x\to2}f(x)}{\lim_{x\to2}(x-2)}\). ✓ The Quotient Rule needs the bottom's limit to be non-zero, but \(\lim_{x\to2}(x-2)=0\). (Also, nothing says \(\lim_{x\to2}f(x)\) exists.) The right-hand side would mean dividing by 0, which is meaningless. ✗\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\left(x\cdot\frac1x\right)=\lim_{x\to0}x\cdot\lim_{x\to0}\frac1x\). ✓ The Product Rule needs both limits to exist, but \(\lim_{x\to0}\frac1x\) does not (section 3). Simplify first: \(x\cdot\frac1x=1\) for \(x\ne0\), so the limit is \(1\).

Substitutions resulting in indeterminate forms

✗\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}\left(\frac1{x^2}-\frac1x\right)=\infty-\infty=0\). ✓ \(\infty\) is not a number, so you cannot subtract it from itself. \(\infty-\infty\) is an indeterminate form: the answer depends on which "infinity" grows faster. Combine the fractions instead. ✗\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\left(\frac1{x-1}-\frac1{\ln x}\right)=\infty-\infty=0\). ✓ Same error. (Here \(\ln x\) is the natural logarithm, \(\log_e x\) with \(e\approx2.718\), Lecture 7.)

The correct values:

\[ \frac1{x^2}-\frac1x=\frac{1-x}{x^2}, \]

and as \(x\to0^+\) the top heads to \(1\) while the bottom is a tiny positive number, so

\[ \lim_{x\to0^+}\left(\frac1{x^2}-\frac1x\right)=\infty. \]

For the second one the true value is

\[ \lim_{x\to1}\left(\frac1{x-1}-\frac1{\ln x}\right)=-\frac12, \]

found with L'Hôpital's Rule Beyond Lecture 1 (Lecture 10). Neither answer is 0. See them in the figure.

The functions in the common mistakes

the functionthe point being approached

\(x\cdot\frac1x\) is the line \(y=1\) with a hole at \(x=0\): limit 1. \(\frac1{x^2}-\frac1x\) shoots up to \(+\infty\) as \(x\to0^+\). \(\frac1{x-1}-\frac1{\ln x}\) has a hole at height \(-\frac12\) at \(x=1\). None of them has limit 0.

Notation errors

✗Extraneous (unnecessary) limit notation: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}x^2-3x+1=\lim_{x\to1}(1)^2-3(1)+1=-1\). ✓ Once you substitute \(x=1\), there is no \(x\) left to approach anything, so the "lim" must be dropped at that moment. Also put brackets round the whole expression: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}(x^2-3x+1)=(1)^2-3(1)+1=-1\). ✗Omitting limit notation: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\frac{4x^2-4}{2(x-1)}=\frac{4(x-1)(x+1)}{2(x-1)}=\frac{4(x+1)}{2}=4\). ✓ The middle expressions are not equal to 4 (they still contain \(x\)); each must keep "\(\lim_{x\to1}\)" in front until you substitute: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\frac{4(x-1)(x+1)}{2(x-1)}=\lim_{x\to1}\frac{4(x+1)}{2}=\frac{4(2)}{2}=4\). ✗Omitting the function: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}=5\). ✓ "lim" must always be followed by the expression whose limit is taken, e.g. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}f(x)=5\).

The final numbers in the first two were right; markers still deduct for wrong notation because the written statements are false.

More traps

✗"Substitution gives \(\frac00\), so the limit is 0 (or 1, or does not exist)." ✓ \(\frac00\) only says "more work needed"; Examples 3 and 4 give 0 and \(-\frac13\), the example of section 4 gives \(\frac12\). ✗"\(f(c)\) is undefined, so \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\) does not exist." ✓ The value at \(c\) is ignored (like \(g\) in section 3). ✗"\(f(c)=5\), so \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=5\)." ✓ Only true for functions that have the Direct Substitution Property at \(c\) (\(h\) in section 3 is a counter-example). ✗Replacing \(|x|\) by \(x\) near a negative point. ✓ For \(x\lt0\), \(|x|=-x\). Decide the sign of what is inside the absolute value on the side you approach from.

10Find the mistake

Here is a student's answer to this question:

If \(\lim_{x\to c}[f(x)+g(x)]=2\) and \(\lim_{x\to c}[f(x)-g(x)]=1\), find \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)g(x)\). Use Limit Laws to evaluate and provide a clear explanation of your reasoning.

The student's work, line by line:

Given:

\[ \lim_{x\to c}[f(x)+g(x)]=2\ \text{ and} \] \[ \lim_{x\to c}[f(x)-g(x)]=1 \]

By Limit Laws:

\[ \lim_{x\to c}[f(x)+g(x)]=\lim_{x\to c}f(x)+\lim_{x\to c}g(x)=2 \] \[ \lim_{x\to c}[f(x)-g(x)]=\lim_{x\to c}f(x)-\lim_{x\to c}g(x)=1 \]

Elimination to reduce variable (adding):

\[ 2\left[\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\right]=3\ \ \therefore\ \lim_{x\to c}f(x)=\frac32 \]

Plug into equation:

\[ \frac32+\lim_{x\to c}g(x)=2\ \ \therefore\ \lim_{x\to c}g(x)=\frac12 \]

Calculate the \(\lim f(x)g(x)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\lim_{x\to c}[f(x)g(x)]\\ &\overset{\text{Limit Law (Product)}}{=}\left[\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\right]\left[\lim_{x\to c}g(x)\right] \end{aligned} \] \[ =\frac32\cdot\frac12=\frac34 \]
Hint 1

Read the first line of the Limit Laws theorem again: "If \(\lim f(x)\) and \(\lim g(x)\) exist, then …". What does the question tell you about \(\lim f(x)\) and \(\lim g(x)\) on their own?

Hint 2

Write \(f\) and \(g\) using only \(f+g\) and \(f-g\), whose limits you do know: \(f=\frac12[(f+g)+(f-g)]\).

Answer

The mistake (lines 3 and 4): the student splits \(\lim[f+g]\) into \(\lim f+\lim g\) using the Sum and Difference Rules. Those rules may only be used if \(\lim f(x)\) and \(\lim g(x)\) are already known to exist, and the question does not say so. (A sum can have a limit when its parts do not, e.g. \(\frac{|x|}x+\left(-\frac{|x|}x\right)=0\) at \(c=0\).) The final number is right, but the reasoning, which the question explicitly asks for, is not valid.

Correct reasoning: apply the laws only to functions whose limits are given. For \(x\) near \(c\),

\[ f(x)=\tfrac12\big([f(x)+g(x)]+[f(x)-g(x)]\big). \]

Both brackets have limits (2 and 1), so by the Sum and Constant Multiple Rules

\[ \lim_{x\to c}f(x)=\tfrac12(2+1)=\tfrac32 \]

and in particular this limit exists. Similarly \(g(x)=\frac12\big([f+g]-[f-g]\big)\), so by the Difference and Constant Multiple Rules

\[ \lim_{x\to c}g(x)=\tfrac12(2-1)=\tfrac12. \]

Now both limits exist, so the Product Rule applies:

\[ \lim_{x\to c}f(x)g(x)=\frac32\cdot\frac12=\frac34. \]

A neat alternative: \(fg=\frac14\big[(f+g)^2-(f-g)^2\big]\), so by the Power, Difference and Constant Multiple Rules the limit is \(\frac14(2^2-1^2)=\frac34\) directly.

Hint

For a one-sided limit, run your finger along the curve towards the point from that side and read the height the curve heads to; ignore the separate dots. For the value \(f(c)\), look only for the filled dot above \(x=c\). For a two-sided limit, use the existence theorem.

Notice: at \(x=0\) the limit exists but differs from the value (like \(h\) in section 3); at \(x=1\) the limit fails because of a jump (like \(|x|/x\) in section 3).

2. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-1}\frac{x^2+2x+1}{x^4-1}\)

Hint

Substitution gives \(\frac00\). Factorise: the top is a perfect square, and the bottom is a difference of squares twice: \(x^4-1=(x^2-1)(x^2+1)\).

Answer
\[ \begin{aligned} &\lim_{x\to-1}\frac{(x+1)^2}{(x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1)}\\ &=\lim_{x\to-1}\frac{x+1}{(x-1)(x^2+1)}\\ &=\frac{0}{(-2)(2)}=0. \end{aligned} \]

3. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\cos^4x}{5+2x^3}\)

Hint

Plug in first. Is the bottom zero at \(x=0\)? (\(\cos^4x\) means \((\cos x)^4\).)

Answer

Top and bottom are built from trig and polynomial functions, and the bottom is \(5\ne0\) at \(x=0\). By direct substitution (or the Quotient and Power Rules):

\[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\cos^4x}{5+2x^3}=\frac{(\cos0)^4}{5+0}=\frac15. \]

4. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sqrt{3+x}-\sqrt3}{x}\)

Hint

\(\frac00\) with roots: multiply top and bottom by the conjugate \(\sqrt{3+x}+\sqrt3\).

Answer
\[ \begin{aligned} &\lim_{x\to0}\frac{(3+x)-3}{x\left(\sqrt{3+x}+\sqrt3\right)}\\ &=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{3+x}+\sqrt3}\\ &=\frac1{2\sqrt3}=\frac{\sqrt3}{6}. \end{aligned} \]

This is the slope of the tangent to \(y=\sqrt x\) at \(x=3\), an "instantaneous rate of change" as in section 1 (with \(h\) called \(x\)).

5. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-2}\frac{2-|x|}{2-x}\)

Hint

Plug in first: is the bottom zero at \(x=-2\)? (Watch \(|{-2}|=2\).)

Answer

At \(x=-2\) the bottom is \(2-(-2)=4\ne0\) and the top is \(2-|{-2}|=0\). Near \(-2\), \(|x|=-x\), so the function is \(\frac{2+x}{2-x}\), a rational function defined at \(-2\):

\[ \lim_{x\to-2}\frac{2-|x|}{2-x}=\frac{2-2}{2+2}=0. \]

A trap: it is \(\frac04\), not \(\frac00\). Do not try to cancel anything.

6. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^-}\left(\frac1x-\frac1{|x|}\right)\)

Hint

From the left, \(x\lt0\), so \(|x|=-x\). Rewrite \(\frac1{|x|}\) and combine.

Answer

For \(x\lt0\): \(\dfrac1{|x|}=\dfrac1{-x}\), so

\[ \frac1x-\frac1{|x|}=\frac1x+\frac1x=\frac2x. \]

As \(x\to0^-\), \(\frac2x\) is negative and grows without bound in size, so

\[ \lim_{x\to0^-}\left(\frac1x-\frac1{|x|}\right)=-\infty. \]

The limit does not exist as a real number; it tends to \(-\infty\) (Lecture 3). (From the right the expression is \(\frac1x-\frac1x=0\), so it would be 0: the side matters.)

7. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1^+}\frac{x-1}{\sqrt x-1}\)

Hint

\(\frac00\). Either rationalise the bottom, or notice \(x-1=(\sqrt x-1)(\sqrt x+1)\) (difference of squares, for \(x\ge0\)).

Answer
\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\to1^+}\frac{x-1}{\sqrt x-1}&=\lim_{x\to1^+}\frac{(\sqrt x-1)(\sqrt x+1)}{\sqrt x-1}\\ &=\lim_{x\to1^+}\left(\sqrt x+1\right)=2. \end{aligned} \]

(The two-sided limit is also 2; the one-sided version is asked so that \(x\) stays where \(\sqrt x\) is defined and the bottom is non-zero.)

12Concept checks

1. You are told \(f(2)=7\). What can you conclude about \(\lim_{x\to2}f(x)\)?

Answer

Nothing. The limit ignores the value at 2: it could be 7, another number, or not exist. Only if \(f\) has the Direct Substitution Property at 2 (e.g. it is a polynomial) would the limit be 7.

2. Suppose \(\lim_{x\to3^-}f(x)=4\), \(\lim_{x\to3^+}f(x)=4\) and \(f(3)=-1\). Does \(\lim_{x\to3}f(x)\) exist? If so, what is it?

Answer

Yes: by the existence theorem, both one-sided limits equal 4, so \(\lim_{x\to3}f(x)=4\). The value \(f(3)=-1\) is irrelevant (like \(h\) in section 3).

3. Can \(\lim_{x\to c}[f(x)+g(x)]\) exist even though neither \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\) nor \(\lim_{x\to c}g(x)\) exists?

Answer

Yes. Take \(c=0\), \(f(x)=\frac{|x|}x\) and \(g(x)=-\frac{|x|}x\). Neither has a limit at 0 (jump from \(-1\) to \(1\)), but \(f(x)+g(x)=0\) for all \(x\ne0\), so the sum's limit is 0. This is why the Sum Rule only works in the direction "parts exist ⇒ sum's limit = sum of limits", and why the student in section 10 needed more care.

4. Explain why the Quotient Rule cannot be used directly on \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}\), and find the limit.

Answer

The Quotient Rule needs \(\lim_{x\to1}(x-1)\ne0\), but this limit is 0. Factorise instead: for \(x\ne1\), \(\frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1}=x+1\), so the limit is \(1+1=2\).

5. A student makes a table of \(f(x)=\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{x}\right)\) at \(x=0.1,\ 0.01,\ 0.001\) and gets \(0,\ 0,\ 0\) (up to rounding). They conclude \(\lim_{x\to0}f(x)=0\). Are they right?

Hint

Work out \(\frac{\pi}{x}\) at those points, then try \(x=\frac{2}{5},\ \frac{2}{41},\ \frac{2}{401}\).

Answer

No. At \(x=\frac1n\), \(\sin(n\pi)=0\), so their chosen points all happen to give 0. But at \(x=\frac{2}{4k+1}\) (e.g. \(\frac25,\frac2{41},\frac2{401}\)) we get \(\sin\left(2k\pi+\frac\pi2\right)=1\). Like \(h(x)=\sin\frac1x\) in section 3, this function oscillates infinitely between \(-1\) and \(1\) near 0, so the limit does not exist. A numeric table only suggests an answer; it can be fooled by an unlucky choice of points.

6. Find the average rate of change of \(f(x)=x^2\) over \([1,1+h]\), and use it to find the instantaneous rate of change of \(f\) at \(x=1\).

Answer
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{f(1+h)-f(1)}{h}&=\frac{(1+h)^2-1}{h}\\ &=\frac{2h+h^2}{h}\\ &=2+h\quad(h\ne0). \end{aligned} \] \[ \lim_{h\to0}(2+h)=2. \]

So the tangent to \(y=x^2\) at \((1,1)\) has slope 2: the instantaneous rate of change is 2.

May 2026 Q1(a)Q1 total 10 ptsGuide · L1.1 Plug in first: the limit isn't actually indeterminate

Find the following limit. Your answer should be a number, \(\infty\), \(-\infty\), or "non-existence."

\[ \text{(a)}\quad \lim_{x\to\frac\pi2}\frac{\sin x}{x} \]
Hint

Plug in first. Is the bottom zero at \(x=\frac\pi2\)? (Do not confuse this with \(x\to0\), which is Lecture 2.)

Answer

\(\dfrac2\pi\)

May 2026 Q1(b)Q1 total 10 ptsGuide · L1.3 Absolute values and one-sided limits

Find the following limit. Your answer should be a number, \(\infty\), \(-\infty\), or "non-existence."

\[ \text{(b)}\quad \lim_{t\to-3^+}\frac{t+3}{|2t+6|} \]
Hint

From the right, \(t\gt-3\), so \(2t+6\gt0\) and \(|2t+6|=2t+6=2(t+3)\). Simplify, then cancel.

Answer

\(\dfrac12\)

May 2026 Q1(c)Q1 total 10 ptsGuide · L1.2 0/0 with polynomials or roots: factor, rationalise or combine fractions

Find the following limit. Your answer should be a number, \(\infty\), \(-\infty\), or "non-existence."

\[ \text{(c)}\quad \lim_{w\to-3}\frac{2-\sqrt{w^2-5}}{w+3} \]
Hint

\(\frac00\) with a root. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate \(2+\sqrt{w^2-5}\); the top becomes \(4-(w^2-5)=9-w^2\), which has a factor \((w+3)\).

Answer

\(\dfrac32\)

Dec 2021 Q1(a)Q1 total 10%Guide · L1.3 Absolute values and one-sided limits

In each of the following, either evaluate the limit or explain carefully why the limit does not exist. Indicate clearly the theorems you have used in your argument.

\[ \text{(a)}\quad \lim_{t\to a^-}\frac{a+|t|}{|a-t|}\quad\text{where } a\lt0. \]
Hint

Work out the signs first. For \(t\) just to the left of \(a\): \(t\lt a\lt0\), so \(|t|=-t\), and \(a-t\gt0\), so \(|a-t|=a-t\). Rewrite the fraction without absolute values.

Answer

\(1\)
