MATH1851 Exam Guide · Continuity and the IVT

The short version

What to expect

Continuity questions are in 6 of the 13 recent papers (May 2019, Dec 2019, Dec 2020, Dec 2021, May 2024, Dec 2025), usually as one or two parts of Part I. They come in four types: choose constants so a function joins up (L4.1); read limits off a graph or off given one-sided limits, often through a composite like \(u(u(x))\) (L4.2); name a kind of discontinuity or build an example (L4.3); and show an equation has a solution with the Intermediate Value Theorem (L4.4). The functions change every time, but each type has a standard procedure below. Follow its steps in order and you never have to invent an approach.

How to practise: use Start here to name the type from the wording. Then work the question with the type's procedure open, check with the Hint if stuck and with the Solution at the end. Newest papers come first; 2013–2018 papers are tagged older format. For the ideas behind each step, use the Chapter note links. All answers checked with sympy.

Exam format

  • Where: Part I, usually Q1 or Q2 (10–15%), often sharing a stem with a curve-sketching or derivative part. Graph questions (L4.2) were the favourite in 2019–2020; since 2022 continuity appears mostly as a single part (May 2024 Q3(c), Dec 2025 MC1). The IVT is also the last step of several questions in other guides (pointers in L4.4).
  • Speed: Dec 2025 moved part of Part I to multiple choice (Dec 2025 MC1 is a 2-point "which kind of discontinuity?" item), and May 2026 made Part I Q1–3 answers only. There only the letter or the value scores. On written papers the stems say "with explanation", "justify your answer" or "give a mathematical justification", and the reasons carry the marks.
  • Formula sheet: recent papers print trig identities and (on most) the derivatives of \(\sin^{-1}\), \(\cos^{-1}\), \(\tan^{-1}\); nothing else for this topic. No paper prints the definition of continuity, the limit laws, \(\frac{\sin x}{x}\to1\), or the statement of the Intermediate Value Theorem. Dec 2019 has no formula sheet in the file.
  • Older papers (2013–2018) often ask "State the Intermediate Value Theorem" (Dec 2015, Dec 2016) and "indicate clearly the theorems you have used". Graph questions mark a filled dot ● for the value of the function and an open circle ○ for a point the graph only approaches.

L4.1Choose constants so a piecewise function is continuous

Chapter note · continuity at a point · Chapter note · continuity of combinations

Frequency: 2 of 13 recent papers (Dec 2020, Dec 2021); 3 of 10 older papers (May 2015, May 2016, May 2017). May 2019 Q1(b) is also this kind of task; it is solved under L4.2 because it needs the graph.

Recognise it

A function given in pieces (or with one separate value at a point), unknown constants, and the words "continuous on \((-\infty,\infty)\)" or "for what value of … will it be continuous".

Standard procedure

\(f\) is continuous at \(x=c\) when \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to c^+}f(x)=f(c)\): left limit, right limit and value all agree.

  1. Away from the joins. Say why each piece is continuous on its own open interval: polynomials, \(\sin\), \(e^x\), \(\ln\) (for positive inputs) and their sums, products, quotients (bottom \(\ne0\)) and composites are continuous. For a composite with a graph-given \(u\), check that the inside stays where \(u\) has no break.
  2. Value at each join \(x=c\): take it from the piece that owns \(c\) (the one with \(\le\), \(\ge\) or "\(x=c\)").
  3. Left and right limits at \(c\).
    • 3a. The piece is continuous at \(c\) too (polynomial, \(\ln\), \(e^x\), …): just substitute. Asked: May 2017, May 2016.
    • 3b. Substituting gives \(\frac00\) with a sine: divide top and bottom by \(x\) and use \(\frac{\sin kx}{x}\to k\). Asked: Dec 2020, May 2016, May 2015.
    • 3c. The piece is \(u(g(x))\) with \(u\) given by a graph: find where \(g(x)\) tends and from which side, then read \(u\) there (as in L4.2). Asked: Dec 2020, May 2019.
    • 3d. Whether the limit exists depends on the constants (a \(\sin(\frac bx)\) that may swing): split into cases (constant \(=0\) or \(\ne0\)) and settle each one. Asked: Dec 2021.
  4. Set left limit = right limit = value and solve. Use any extra condition in the question (a slope, a second join) as another equation. Asked: May 2016.
  5. Check and conclude. Keep every root that satisfies the conditions (e.g. \(q\gt0\)); write one sentence: "with this value \(f\) is continuous at \(c\), and by step 1 everywhere else, so on \(\mathbb R\)."
Worked example · the procedure in action

Dec 2020 Q1(b)(I), Q1: 15%. Let \(u\) be the function whose graph is shown in Diagram I. Let \(q\gt0\) be a constant, and

\[t(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac1x\sin\dfrac{x}{q} & \text{for } x\gt0,\\[2mm] u\big(2-3e^x\big) & \text{for } x\le0.\end{cases}\]

(I) For what value of \(q\) will the function \(t(x)\) be continuous on \(\mathbb R\)?

Diagram I. \(y=u(x)\) (redrawn; ● = value of \(u\), ○ = point not on the graph):

Step 1 For \(x\gt0\), \(\frac1x\sin\frac xq\) is continuous (bottom \(x\ne0\)). For \(x\lt0\), \(0\lt e^x\lt1\), so the inside \(s=2-3e^x\) lies in \((-1,2)\). On \([-1,2)\) the graph of \(u\) is one unbroken piece, so \(u(s)\) is continuous there. Only \(x=0\) is left to check.

The jump of \(u\) at \(x=2\) never matters: \(2-3e^x\lt2\) for every \(x\). Saying so is what "continuous on \(\mathbb R\)" asks for.

Step 2 Value: \(t(0)=u(2-3)=u(-1)=0.5\) (filled dot at \((-1,0.5)\)).

Read a value only from a filled dot ●.

Step 3c Left limit. As \(x\to0^-\), \(e^x\to1^-\), so \(s=2-3e^x\to-1^+\) (just above \(-1\)). The graph starts at the filled dot and is unbroken to its right, so

\[\lim_{x\to0^-}t(x)=\lim_{s\to-1^+}u(s)=0.5.\]

The side matters: \(u\) is not defined to the left of \(-1\). Here \(s\) comes from the right, which is where the graph is.

Step 3b Right limit, form \(\frac00\):

\[\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac1x\sin\frac xq=\frac1q\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{\sin(x/q)}{x/q}=\frac1q.\]

Multiply and divide by \(q\) so the standard limit \(\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\to1\) appears with \(\theta=\frac xq\to0\).

Step 4 Left = right = value: \(\frac1q=0.5\), so \(q=2\).

Step 5 \(q=2\gt0\) is allowed. With \(q=2\), \(t\) is continuous at 0 and, by step 1, everywhere else.

\[\boxed{q=2}\]

Common slip: using \(u(2)\) or \(u(-1^-)\); the inside tends to \(-1\) from above.

Traps
  • Forgetting the side of the inside in a composite (Dec 2020 Q1(b), May 2019 Q1(b)): \(2-3e^x\to-1^+\), \(2-|x|\to2^-\). The wrong side gives a wrong value.
  • Treating every constant the same (Dec 2021 Q2(b)): \(\frac ax\sin\left(x^2+\frac bx\right)\) has a limit when \(a=0\) or \(b=0\), and none when both are non-zero. Settle each case.
  • Dropping a root (May 2015 Q1(b)): \(2-A=A^2\) has two solutions, \(A=1\) and \(A=-2\); both make \(g\) continuous.
Ready answer · the sine-quotient join

For constants \(p\), \(q\):

\[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin(px)-\sin(qx)}{x+x^3}=p-q.\]

Why: divide top and bottom by \(x\): \(\dfrac{\frac{\sin px}{x}-\frac{\sin qx}{x}}{1+x^2}\to\dfrac{p-q}{1}\). It is the left limit in May 2016 Q1(b) (\(A-B\)) and May 2015 Q1(b) (\(2-A\)). On a written paper, show the division.

Dec 2021 Q2(b)Q2: 10%; part marks not shown

Let \(a,b\) be two constants. Consider the function

\[f(x)=\begin{cases}\left(x^2+\frac ax\right)\sin\left(x^2+\frac bx\right),\\[1mm] \qquad\qquad\text{for } x\gt0,\\[1mm] -xe^{2x},\quad\text{for } x\le0.\end{cases}\]

(b) Determine with explanation all the possible values of \(a,b\) so that \(f\) is continuous on \((-\infty,\infty)\).

Part (a), a sketch of \(f\) for \(x\le0\), is in the L9 guide; part (c), differentiability, is in the L5 guide.

Hint

Type L4.1, the case-split version (step 3d). First step: write the right-hand piece as \(x^2\sin\varphi+\dfrac ax\sin\varphi\) with \(\varphi=x^2+\frac bx\), and ask what happens when \(a=0\), when \(b=0\), and when neither is 0.

Solution

Step 1 For \(x\lt0\), \(-xe^{2x}\) is continuous; for \(x\gt0\), the right piece is built from continuous functions with \(x\ne0\) in the bottom. Only \(x=0\) matters.

Step 2–3a \(f(0)=-0\cdot e^0=0\), and the left limit is also \(0\) by substitution.

Step 3d Right limit. Put \(\varphi(x)=x^2+\frac bx\). Then \(f(x)=x^2\sin\varphi+\frac ax\sin\varphi\), and \(|x^2\sin\varphi|\le x^2\to0\) (Sandwich Theorem).

Case \(a=0\): \(f(x)=x^2\sin\varphi\to0\) for every \(b\). Continuous.

Case \(a\ne0\), \(b=0\):

\[\frac ax\sin(x^2)=a\,x\cdot\frac{\sin(x^2)}{x^2}\to a\cdot0\cdot1=0.\]

Continuous.

Case \(a\ne0\), \(b\ne0\): suppose the right limit \(L\) existed. Then

\[\begin{aligned}\sin\varphi&=\frac{x}{a}\big(f(x)-x^2\sin\varphi\big)\\&\to0\cdot\frac{L-0}{a}=0.\end{aligned}\]

But \(|\varphi(x)|\to\infty\) as \(x\to0^+\) (the \(\frac bx\) term), and \(\varphi\) is continuous, so arbitrarily close to 0 it passes through values \(\frac\pi2+k\pi\), where \(|\sin\varphi|=1\). So \(\sin\varphi\not\to0\): a contradiction. The right limit does not exist and \(f\) is not continuous at 0.

Step 5 \(f\) is continuous on \(\mathbb R\) exactly when \(a=0\) or \(b=0\).

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&a=0\ (b\text{ any}),\ \text{or}\\&b=0\ (a\text{ any})\end{aligned}}\]

Marker: "with explanation" means each case needs its reason: Sandwich for \(a=0\), \(\frac{\sin\theta}\theta\to1\) for \(b=0\), and an argument for why there is no limit when \(ab\ne0\).

Check. With \(a=2\), \(b=3\) the values at \(x=10^{-2},10^{-3},10^{-4}\) are about \(-200\), \(438\), \(-16053\): no limit. With \(a=2\), \(b=0\) they are \(0.02\), \(0.002\), \(0.0002\).

Equivalent forms. \(ab=0\).

May 2016 Q1(b)Q1: 10%; part marks not shownolder format

Given that

\[g(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\sin(Ax)-\sin(Bx)}{x+x^3} & \text{for } x\lt0,\\[2mm] (x+A)^2 & \text{for } x\ge0,\end{cases}\]

L4.2Limits and continuity read off a graph (including composites like u(u(x)))

Chapter note · one-sided limits and continuity · Chapter note · limits of composite functions

Frequency: 3 of 13 recent papers (May 2019, Dec 2019, Dec 2020), 6 parts; 2 of 10 older papers (May 2017, May 2018).

Recognise it

A graph of \(u\) with filled dots ● and open circles ○, or one-sided limits given as data, and a limit of a composite such as \(u(u(x)+1)\), \(u(\sqrt{u(x)})\), \(u(\ln x)\); or "for what \(a\) does the limit exist / is \(u\) continuous".

Standard procedure

On a graph, a filled dot ● is the value of the function; an open circle ○ is a point the graph gets close to but does not include.

  1. Mark the breaks. List the \(x\)-values where the graph jumps or has a hole, with the left limit, right limit and value at each.
  2. Split sums. For \(\lim\big(u(x)+u(-x)\big)\) and similar, find each piece's limit separately (Sum Law: allowed once each piece has a limit). For a two-sided \(x\to a\), do \(x\to a^-\) and \(x\to a^+\) separately whenever the inside might behave differently on the two sides.
  3. Inside first. For \(u(g(x))\), find \(L=\lim g(x)\) and the side: does \(g(x)\) stay above \(L\) (write \(L^+\)), below \(L\) (\(L^-\)), or equal to \(L\)? Read the side off the graph (rising or falling into the point) or the formula (\(e^x\lt1\) for \(x\lt0\), \(|x|\gt0\), \(\ln x\lt0\) for \(x\lt1\)).
  4. Then the outside.
    • 4a. \(u\) has no break at \(L\): the answer is \(u(L)\).
    • 4b. \(u\) breaks at \(L\): use the one-sided limit of \(u\) on the side found in step 3: \(\lim u(g(x))=\lim_{s\to L^\pm}u(s)\). Never the dot. Asked: May 2019, Dec 2020, Dec 2019, May 2018, May 2017.
    • 4c. No limit at all, just a value such as \(U(U(2)+1)\), or the inside tends to \(\infty\): read the dot, or use the formula for large \(x\). Asked: May 2018.
    • 4d. The data do not say which side the inside comes from: write what each side would give. If they differ, the limit cannot be decided from the data alone; say so and state the reading you use. Asked: Dec 2019.
  5. "Does the limit exist?" / "Is it continuous?" The limit at \(a\) exists exactly when left limit = right limit; \(u\) is continuous at \(a\) when, in addition, that common value equals \(u(a)\). A function that is not continuous at \(a\) is not differentiable there. Asked: May 2019, Dec 2019 (b), Dec 2019 (c), May 2018.
Worked example · the procedure in action

May 2019 Q1(a), Q1: 10%. Let \(u\) be a function whose graph is (partially) shown below. Evaluate (i) \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2^-}\big(u(x)+u(-x)\big)\) and (ii) \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to6^-}u\big(\sqrt{u(x)}\big)\).

Graph of \(y=u(x)\) (redrawn; ● = value of \(u\), ○ = point not on the graph):

Step 1 Breaks: at \(x=-2\) (left limit 2, right limit 4, no value shown) and at \(x=2\) (left limit 3, right limit \(-1\), value \(u(2)=1\)). The graph has no break at \(x=6\) from the left: it rises into the open circle \((6,4)\).

Listing the breaks first tells you exactly where the side will matter.

(i) Step 2 Two pieces: \(u(x)\) and \(u(-x)\), each as \(x\to2^-\).

Step 4b \(\lim_{x\to2^-}u(x)=3\) (the middle piece falls into \((2,3)\)).

Step 3–4b For \(u(-x)\): put \(s=-x\). As \(x\to2^-\), \(s\to-2^+\) (e.g. \(x=1.9\) gives \(s=-1.9\gt-2\)). So \(u(-x)\to\lim_{s\to-2^+}u(s)=4\).

The minus sign flips the side: approaching 2 from the left means approaching \(-2\) from the right. This is the step most answers get wrong.

Sum Law (both limits exist): \(3+4\).

\[\boxed{\text{(i) } 7}\]

(ii) Step 3 As \(x\to6^-\), \(u(x)\to4\) from below (rising into \((6,4)\)), so \(\sqrt{u(x)}\to2^-\) (the square root is increasing, and \(\sqrt4=2\)).

Track the side through every layer: below 4 in, below 2 out.

Step 4b \(u\) breaks at 2, so use the left limit there: \(\lim_{s\to2^-}u(s)=3\).

\[\boxed{\text{(ii) } 3}\]

Common slips: using the dot \(u(2)=1\), or the right limit \(-1\).

Traps
  • Reading the dot instead of the limit. In Dec 2020 Q1(a) the inside tends to \(5^-\); \(u(5)=0\) is a dot, but the answer is the left limit \(2.5\).
  • Losing the side through a minus sign or a root. May 2019 Q1(a): \(x\to2^-\) makes \(-x\to-2^+\); \(\sqrt{u(x)}\to2^-\). May 2017 Q1(a)(ii): \(1-x\to1^-\), and the graph falls into \((1,1)\) from above, so \(f(1-x)\to1^+\).
  • Differentiating across a jump. May 2018 Q2(iv): \(U\) jumps at 4, so \(U'(4)\) does not exist, even though the slopes on both sides look fine.
Ready answer · the composite limit rule Beyond the handouts

If \(g(x)\to L\) and \(g(x)\gt L\) near the point, then

\[\lim u\big(g(x)\big)=\lim_{s\to L^+}u(s),\]

and the same with \(\lt\) and \(L^-\). If \(u\) is continuous at \(L\), both are just \(u(L)\).

The handouts state only the continuous case, \(\lim u(g(x))=u(\lim g(x))\). The one-sided version is what the composite limits on this page use (May 2019, Dec 2019, Dec 2020, May 2017, May 2018, and May 2019 Q1(b)). On a written paper, say "\(g(x)\to L\) from above/below" in words; that sentence is what the marker looks for.

L4.3Classify a discontinuity, extend continuously, or construct an example

Chapter note · types of discontinuity

Frequency: 1 of 13 recent papers (Dec 2025, multiple choice); 1 of 10 older papers (May 2015). A thin type: the procedure and the two cards are all you need.

Recognise it

"What kind of discontinuity …", "can \(f\) be defined at \(a\) so that it is continuous", or "construct functions such that …".

Standard procedure
  1. Value. Is \(f(a)\) defined? (A zero bottom means it is not.)
  2. One-sided limits at \(a\), each found separately (factorise, \(\frac{\sin\theta}\theta\to1\), sign of the bottom).
  3. Name it from the limits:
    • 3a. Both finite and equal, but \(f(a)\) is missing or different: removable (redefine \(f(a)\) as the limit to make it continuous). Asked: Dec 2025.
    • 3b. Both finite, different: jump.
    • 3c. At least one is \(\pm\infty\): infinite (a vertical asymptote).
    • 3d. At least one does not exist because the function keeps swinging, like \(\sin\frac1{x-a}\): oscillatory.
    • 3e. Both finite, equal, and equal to \(f(a)\): continuous, no discontinuity.
  4. Construct (when asked for an example): use the simplest pieces that do the job, usually a step function (two constants) and a constant or \(x^2\). Then check every condition with steps 1–3. Asked: May 2015.
Traps
  • "Undefined" is not "infinite". Dec 2025 MC1: \(g(2)\) is \(\frac00\), but the limit is the finite number \(\frac4\pi\), so the discontinuity is removable, not infinite.
  • An example must be checked. May 2015 Q5(c): say why \(f\) is discontinuous at 1 and why \(g\circ f\) is continuous there; the example alone is not the answer.

L4.4Show a solution exists (Intermediate Value Theorem)

Chapter note · the Intermediate Value Theorem

Frequency: 3 of 13 recent papers (May 2019, Dec 2019, May 2024); 3 of 10 older papers (Dec 2015, Dec 2016, Dec 2018), 4 parts, two of them "State the IVT". The IVT is also a step in six questions on other pages (listed at the end of this section).

Recognise it

"Show that … has at least one (two) solution(s)", "show there exists \(u\) with …", "must the equation have a solution?", "the graphs intersect at least twice", or "State the Intermediate Value Theorem". ("Exactly one" is L8.3: IVT plus monotonicity.)

Standard procedure
  1. One function. Move everything to one side: \(h(x)=\text{left side}-\text{right side}\). A solution of the equation is a zero of \(h\).
  2. A closed interval where \(h\) is continuous. Name \([p,r]\) and say why \(h\) is continuous on all of it (sums, products and composites of continuous functions). If \(h\) breaks at the ends (e.g. at integers), work on a slightly smaller closed interval inside.
  3. Opposite signs at the ends.
    • 3a. Values follow from given inequalities (\(\le\), \(\ge\)): get \(h(p)\le0\le h(r)\). If an end value is \(0\), that end is the solution; otherwise the signs are strict. Asked: Dec 2018, Dec 2015.
    • 3b. Only one-sided limits are known at the ends: a limit \(\gt0\) means \(h\gt0\) at points close enough; pick such points \(p\), \(r\) inside. Asked: Dec 2019.
    • 3c. One end is not obvious (a function given by its derivative): bound it. Use the Mean Value Theorem, \(F(b)-F(a)=(b-a)F'(c)\), with the smallest or largest value of \(F'\), to find an explicit point with the right sign. Asked: May 2024, May 2019.
  4. Apply the IVT with \(N=0\): there is \(c\) in \((p,r)\) with \(h(c)=0\), so the original equation holds at \(x=c\).
  5. "At least two": repeat on a second interval that does not overlap the first (or add a root you already know, like \(x=0\)), and say the two solutions are different. Asked: Dec 2019, May 2024.
Worked example · the procedure in action

Dec 2019 Q1(d), Q1: 10%. Let \(u\) be a function continuous everywhere except at integers. Moreover, for any integer \(a\), \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a^-}u(x)=2-3x\), \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a^+}u(x)=x^2-2\), \(u(a)=a\). (d) Show that the equation \(u(x)=x-1\) has at least two solutions of \(x\) in the interval \([0,4]\).

The one-sided limits are read as \(2-3a\) and \(a^2-2\) (see Dec 2019 Q1(a)).

Step 1 Let \(h(x)=u(x)-(x-1)\).

Step 2 \(h\) is continuous except at integers (\(u\) is, and \(x-1\) is a polynomial). So the IVT can only be used on closed intervals with no integer in them, i.e. inside \((0,1)\), \((1,2)\), \((2,3)\) or \((3,4)\).

\([2,3]\) itself will not do: \(h\) is not continuous at 2 or 3. The marker wants a closed interval on which \(h\) really is continuous.

Step 3b One-sided limits of \(h\) at the ends of each gap: left limit of \(h\) at \(a\) is \((2-3a)-(a-1)\); right limit is \((a^2-2)-(a-1)\).

Gap\(h\) near left end\(h\) near right end
\((0,1)\)\(-1\)\(-1\)
\((1,2)\)\(-1\)\(-5\)
\((2,3)\)\(1\gt0\)\(-9\lt0\)
\((3,4)\)\(5\gt0\)\(-13\lt0\)

On \((2,3)\): \(\lim_{x\to2^+}h(x)=1\gt0\), so \(h(p)\gt0\) for some \(p\) slightly above 2; \(\lim_{x\to3^-}h(x)=-9\lt0\), so \(h(r)\lt0\) for some \(r\) slightly below 3, with \(2\lt p\lt r\lt3\).

This is what "a limit is positive" means: the function is positive at all points close enough. It turns limits into actual values.

Step 4 \(h\) is continuous on \([p,r]\) and \(h(p)\gt0\gt h(r)\). By the IVT there is \(c_1\in(p,r)\subset(2,3)\) with \(h(c_1)=0\), i.e. \(u(c_1)=c_1-1\).

Step 5 Same on \((3,4)\): \(h\to5\gt0\) at \(3^+\) and \(h\to-13\lt0\) at \(4^-\), so there is \(c_2\in(3,4)\) with \(u(c_2)=c_2-1\). Since \(c_1\lt3\lt c_2\), these are two different solutions in \([0,4]\).

\[\boxed{\text{solutions } c_1\in(2,3),\ c_2\in(3,4)}\]

The table shows why the first two gaps are useless (no sign change). At the integers themselves \(u(a)=a\ne a-1\), so no solution sits there.

Traps
  • IVT across a break. Dec 2019 Q1(d): \(u\) is not continuous at integers, so "IVT on \([2,3]\)" is wrong as written; shrink to \([p,r]\) inside.
  • Signs you have not proved. May 2019 Q5(b) and May 2024 Q3(c): "for large \(x\), \(F\) is bigger" needs an explicit point (\(x=8\), \(x=14\)) with a reason.
  • Forgetting the "=" case (Dec 2018 Q5(b), Dec 2015 Q2(b)): with \(\le\) data, an end value can be 0. Then that end is the answer; say so before using the IVT.
Ready answer · IVT statement and the 3-line write-up

Intermediate Value Theorem. Suppose \(f\) is continuous on the closed interval \([a,b]\), and let \(N\) be any number between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\), where \(f(a)\ne f(b)\). Then there is a number \(c\) in \((a,b)\) such that \(f(c)=N\).

Write-up for "show that \(F(x)=G(x)\) has a solution in \([a,b]\)":

  1. Let \(h(x)=F(x)-G(x)\); \(h\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) because … .
  2. \(h(a)=\dots\lt0\) and \(h(b)=\dots\gt0\) (or the other way round).
  3. By the IVT with \(N=0\), there is \(c\in(a,b)\) with \(h(c)=0\), i.e. \(F(c)=G(c)\).

Bounded-function form (Dec 2018 Q5(b), Dec 2015 Q2(b)): if \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and the bounds on \(f\) give \(h(a)\le0\le h(b)\), there is \(u\in[a,b]\) with \(h(u)=0\): an end if \(h\) is 0 there, otherwise by the IVT.

Hint

Type L4.4, sign found by a bound (step 3c). First step: \(G(x)=F(x)-\sqrt{x+1}\) has \(G(0)=-1\); find an explicit \(x\) where \(G\gt0\), using the MVT on \([1,x]\) and \(F'(t)\ge\sqrt{\ln2}\) there.

Solution

Step 1 Let \(G(x)=F(x)-\sqrt{x+1}\). \(G(0)=0-1=-1\lt0\).

Step 3c \(F'\gt0\) for \(x\gt0\), so \(F\) is increasing and \(F(1)\gt F(0)=0\). By the MVT on \([1,8]\), \(F(8)-F(1)=7F'(c)\) for some \(c\in(1,8)\), and \(F'(c)=\sqrt{\ln(c+1)}\gt\sqrt{\ln2}\). Since \(e\lt4\), \(\ln2\gt\frac12\), so

Solution

Step 1–2 \(p(x)=g(x)-\cos2x\) is continuous on \([0,\frac\pi2]\) (difference of continuous functions).

Step 3a \(p(0)=g(0)-1\le0\) and \(p(\frac\pi2)=g(\frac\pi2)-\cos\pi=g(\frac\pi2)+1\ge0\).

\[\boxed{\exists\,u\in[0,\tfrac\pi2]:\ g(u)=\cos2u}\]

Dec 2016 Q5(b)Q5: 12%; part marks not shownolder format

Also asked: Dec 2015 Q2(a) (Q2: 12%; same wording) older format

State the Intermediate Value Theorem.

Dec 2016 Q5(a) is in the L10 guide and Q5(c) in the L8 guide; Dec 2015 Q2(b) is below.

Hint

Type L4.4, statement. First step: three ingredients: continuous on a closed interval, a number \(N\) between the end values, a point \(c\) where \(f(c)=N\).

Solution

Suppose \(f\) is continuous on the closed interval \([a,b]\), and let \(N\) be any number between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\), where \(f(a)\ne f(b)\). Then there exists a number \(c\) in \((a,b)\) such that \(f(c)=N\).

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&f\text{ continuous on }[a,b],\\&N\text{ between }f(a)\ne f(b)\\&\Rightarrow\ f(c)=N,\ \text{some }c\in(a,b)\end{aligned}}\]

Marker: "continuous on the closed interval \([a,b]\)" is the condition most often left out.

Equivalent forms. Allowing \(N\) to equal an end value, \(c\in[a,b]\). Special case: if \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\) have opposite signs, \(f(c)=0\) for some \(c\in(a,b)\).
