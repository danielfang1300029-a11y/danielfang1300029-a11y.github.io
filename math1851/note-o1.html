First-Order ODEs: Separable, Linear, Exact

0Where this part sits

A first-order ODE is a rule that tells you how steep the unknown curve must be at every point of the plane. Drawn as short marks, the rule looks like the current on a river, and a solution is the path a leaf takes when you drop it in. This part gives three ways to write that path as a formula.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 An ODE is a rule for the slope at every point; a solution is a curve that follows it. One initial condition picks one curve.
  2. §2 Order is the highest derivative in the equation, not the highest power.
  3. §3 Linear means \(y\) and its derivatives appear plainly, multiplied only by functions of \(x\). Sums of solutions are solutions only when the right side is 0.
  4. §4 Few ODEs have formula solutions; first-order linear ones always do.

  7. §7 Partial derivatives: the slope of a hill walking due east or due north. The one new tool, needed for §8.
  8. §8 Exact: the equation is the total change of a hidden function \(F\), so solutions are its contour lines \(F=C\). In most recent papers
  9. §9 Not exact? Multiply by a factor that makes it exact.
  10. §10 Summary and how to choose the method, then mistakes, practice, concept checks and the exam taster.

1What a differential equation is ~6 min

What is this?

A first-order ODE is a rule that tells you, at every point of the plane, how steep the unknown curve must be there. Draw that steepness as a short mark at many points and the plane looks like a river with its current drawn in. Drop a leaf anywhere and it drifts along, always lined up with the current: its path is a solution. Drop it somewhere else and you get a different path, so one ODE has a whole family of solutions.

Why we need them

In science and engineering we often know a rule for how fast something changes, rather than a formula for the thing itself. Three typical situations:

  • (a) Population growth. A population \(P\) grows faster when there are more individuals: rate of growth is proportional to size, \(\dfrac{dP}{dt}=kP\).
  • (b) Rate of change of temperature. A hot cup of tea cools faster when it is much hotter than the room (Newton's law of cooling): \(\dfrac{dT}{dt}=-k\,(T-T_a)\), where \(T_a\) is the room temperature.
  • (c) Acceleration and force that varies with displacement. A mass \(m\) on a spring: the spring pulls back with force \(-kx\) (Hooke's law), and force = mass × acceleration, so \(m\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-kx\).

Each one links a quantity with its own rate of change. That is exactly a differential equation.

Definition

A differential equation is an equation involving an unknown function and one or more of its derivatives.

The unknown function is the dependent variable (usually \(y\); above, \(P\), \(T\) or \(x\)). The variable it depends on is the independent variable (usually \(x\) or time \(t\)).

It is an ordinary differential equation (ODE) when there is only one independent variable, so only ordinary derivatives like \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) appear. (If the unknown depends on several variables, such as position and time, the equation involves partial derivatives and is called a partial differential equation, PDE. PDEs are not in this course.)

In words: an ODE is a rule linking an unknown function of one variable with its rates of change.

Notation. We write \(y'=\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), \(y''=\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\), and so on (the prime means \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\)).

What "solving" means

What is this? (general solution)

Solving means writing the leaf's path as a formula instead of tracing it by hand. Because the leaf could have been dropped anywhere, the formula carries one free number \(C\): that is the general solution. Saying where the leaf is dropped (an initial condition) fixes \(C\) and picks one path.

Definition

Solving a differential equation means finding an analytical expression (a formula) for the dependent variable(s) in terms of the independent variable(s). In applications the dependent variable is usually a displacement, concentration, temperature, current, voltage or other physical quantity, and the independent variables are space and time.

In more difficult situations a numerical approximation (a table of approximate values computed step by step, usually by computer) is acceptable instead of a formula.

A solution is a function that makes the equation true for every \(x\) in some interval. To check a proposed solution, substitute it in.

Worked example 1.1

Check that \(y=\sin x+C\) solves \(y'=\cos x\) for every constant \(C\), and find the solution with \(y(0)=2\).

Differentiate: \(y'=\cos x+0=\cos x\). ✓ The equation holds whatever \(C\) is.

Condition \(y(0)=2\): \(\sin 0+C=2\), so \(C=2\) and \(y=\sin x+2\). This is where the leaf is dropped: at \(x=0\) the curve must be at height 2, and only one member of the family passes there.

Definitions: general solution, initial condition

A first-order ODE usually has a whole family of solutions containing one arbitrary constant \(C\) (because solving it involves one integration). The formula with \(C\) left in is the general solution.

An extra fact such as \(y(0)=2\) is an initial condition. It picks out one member of the family, the particular solution. An ODE together with an initial condition is an initial value problem.

In words: general solution = every possible answer (one per value of \(C\)); initial condition = the extra clue that picks one.

Slope field of \(y'=\cos x\) and the family \(y=\sin x+C\)

slope markschosen solutionother members

Each short mark has the slope the ODE demands at that point (\(\cos x\)). A solution curve must follow the marks everywhere. Changing \(C\) slides the curve up or down; the dot shows the initial value \(y(0)=C\). One ODE, infinitely many solutions; one initial condition, one solution.

Going further

The picture of little slope marks is called a slope field (or direction field). It is a way to "see" an ODE \(y'=f(x,y)\) without solving it, and it is the idea behind numerical methods: start at the initial point and keep stepping in the direction the marks point.

You can now: say what an ODE, its dependent and independent variables and a solution are; check a proposed solution by substituting it in; and use an initial condition to pick the particular solution out of the general solution.

2Order of an ODE ~1 min

What is this?

The order says how deep the rule reaches. A first-order rule gives the slope directly, like the current telling a leaf which way to go. A second-order rule only says how the slope must change, like a steering rule for a car: to know its path you also need where the car starts and which way it is pointing. That is why a higher order usually means more arbitrary constants.

Definition

The order of an ODE is the order of the highest derivative that occurs in it. If the highest derivative is the second derivative, it is a second-order equation.

In words: order = how many times the unknown is differentiated, at most.

\(\dfrac{dP}{dt}=kP\)first order (only \(P'\))
\(m\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-kx\)second order
\(y'''+x^2y=e^x\)third order
\((y')^2+y=x\)first order: the power 2 is not the order

The order roughly tells you how many arbitrary constants the general solution has (one integration per order). This whole note is about first-order equations.

You can now: state the order of an ODE from its highest derivative, without confusing it with a power such as \((y')^2\).

3Linear versus nonlinear ODEs ~5 min

What is this?

Think of a sound system. Each term with \(y\) in it is the signal \(y\) (or one of its derivatives) passed through a volume knob whose setting may depend on \(x\). Turn the signal up by any factor and every such term goes up by the same factor: that is a linear equation. A term like \(y^2\) or \(\sin y\) distorts the signal instead of just scaling it, and the equation is nonlinear.

Definition (intuitive)

An ODE is linear if no nonlinear function of the dependent variable \(y=y(x)\) (or its derivatives) occurs: no \(y^2\), \(\sin y\), \(\cos y\), \(e^y\), no products like \(y\,y'\), and so on. Otherwise it is nonlinear.

Functions of the independent variable \(x\) are allowed anywhere (\(\sin x\), \(x^2\), \(e^x\) are fine).

Put precisely, a linear ODE of order \(n\) can be written as

\[ a_n(x)\,y^{(n)}+\dots+a_1(x)\,y'+a_0(x)\,y=g(x). \]

In words: each derivative of \(y\) appears on its own (power 1, not inside another function), multiplied only by a function of \(x\); the leftover term \(g(x)\) contains no \(y\) at all.

Reading the formula
\(y,\ y',\ \dots,\ y^{(n)}\)
the signal and its derivatives, each to power 1 and never inside another function.
\(a_k(x)\)
the volume knob on each one; it may be turned by \(x\), but never by \(y\).
\(g(x)\)
an outside input that contains no \(y\). If it is 0, the equation is homogeneous.
\(n\)
the order: the highest derivative present.

The multipliers \(a_k(x)\) are the coefficients. If they are all constants, the equation has constant coefficients; if some depend on \(x\), it has variable coefficients.

Four examples

ODEOrderTypeWhy
(a) \(y'+y=\sin x\)1linear\(\sin x\) is a nonlinear function of the independent variable: that is OK
(b) \(y''+y'+y=0\)2linearconstant coefficients
(c) \(y'+\sin y=0\)1nonlinear\(\sin y\) is a nonlinear function of \(y\)
(d) \(y''+x\,y'+y=0\)2linearvariable coefficient \(x\) in front of \(y'\)

Superposition principle

What is this? (superposition)

In the sound-system picture, when there is no outside input (right side 0), two solutions can be played together and their sum still balances to 0. When there is an outside input \(g(x)\), each solution already accounts for one copy of it, so the sum produces two copies and no longer fits the equation. The box below works this out.

Superposition

For a linear ODE, if \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) are solutions, then their sum \(y_1+y_2\) is still a solution. This gives a test: if the sum of two solutions is still a solution, the ODE is linear.

In words: add two answers and you get another answer.

Read this carefully

The statement is exactly true only when the right-hand side \(g(x)\) is \(0\) (a homogeneous linear equation, such as (b) and (d) in the table above).

Example (b): if \(y_1''+y_1'+y_1=0\) and \(y_2''+y_2'+y_2=0\), adding gives \((y_1+y_2)''+(y_1+y_2)'+(y_1+y_2)=0+0=0\). ✓

Example (a) is linear but has \(g(x)=\sin x\ne0\). In section 5 we find that \(y_1=\tfrac12(\sin x-\cos x)\) and \(y_2=\tfrac12(\sin x-\cos x)+e^{-x}\) both solve it. Their sum \(\sin x-\cos x+e^{-x}\) gives \(y'+y=2\sin x\), not \(\sin x\). So for (a) the sum of two solutions is not a solution. What stays true for any linear equation is: the left-hand side of the sum is the sum of the left-hand sides. Superposition in the full sense (sum of two solutions is a solution) is for homogeneous equations; this is developed in parts O3 and O4.

For a nonlinear equation such as (c), even this fails: \(\sin(y_1+y_2)\ne\sin y_1+\sin y_2\).

You can now: tell linear from nonlinear by looking only at how \(y\) and its derivatives appear; say whether the coefficients are constant or variable; and say when the sum of two solutions is again a solution (homogeneous linear equations only).

4Which ODEs can we actually solve? ~2 min

What is this?

You can always trace a leaf through a slope field step by step, but only a few kinds of rule give a path you can write as a formula. The first-order linear kind always does, because it can be rearranged so the left side is the derivative of one single quantity. Undoing one derivative is one integration, just as you get distance travelled from a speed that depends only on time.

As you would expect, the higher the order, the harder it is to find an exact solution (a formula, as opposed to a numerical approximation). Nonlinear equations are usually difficult or impossible to solve exactly. Only certain classes of first-order ODEs, and some very special families of second- and higher-order ODEs, can be solved analytically. This course studies those classes.

Key fact

A first-order linear ODE can always be solved, by means of an integrating factor.

The idea: rewrite the ODE in the form

\[ \begin{aligned}&\frac{d}{dx}\big(\text{expression in }x, y\big)\\&\quad=\text{function of }x\text{ alone},\end{aligned} \]

and then integrate both sides.

In words: make the left side a single derivative, so that one integration undoes it.

"Always solvable" means a formula for the solution always exists, written with integrals. Whether you can evaluate those integrals in closed form depends on the functions involved.

You can now: explain why a first-order linear ODE is always solvable (one derivative on the left, integrate once), and why "solvable" can still leave an integral with no closed form.

5First-order linear ODEs: the integrating factor ~6 min

What is this?

The left side \(y'+p\,y\) is almost the derivative of a product, but not quite. The product rule says \((\mu y)'=\mu y'+\mu' y\). Multiply the whole equation by a well-chosen \(\mu\) and the left side becomes \(\mu y'+\mu p\,y\), which is exactly \((\mu y)'\) as long as \(\mu'=p\,\mu\). So the integrating factor is the multiplier that turns the left side into one tidy product-rule derivative, and one integration then finishes the job.

Standard form \[ \frac{dy}{dx}+p(x)\,y=q(x) \]

In words: rate of change of \(y\) + (function of \(x\)) × \(y\) = (function of \(x\)).

Important, before you do anything else:

  1. Do: the coefficient of the \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) term must be \(1\) (divide through if it is not); in \((\mu y)'=\mu y'+\mu'y\) the \(y'\) carries exactly the multiplier \(\mu\), so you must start from a plain \(y'\).
  2. Do: the \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) and \(y\) terms must be on the same side of the equation. \(p\) is the partner of \(y\) in the missing product-rule piece; moving \(y\) across the equals sign flips its sign, and a wrong sign gives the wrong \(\mu\).

Only then can you read off \(p(x)\), including its sign.

Integrating factor \[ \mu(x)=\exp\!\left[\int p(x)\,dx\right]=e^{\int p(x)\,dx} \]

In words: integrating factor = \(e\) to the power (integral of the coefficient of \(y\)).

Multiply the standard form by \(\mu\). The left side collapses into one derivative:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\big(\mu y\big)=\mu\, q \] \[ y=\frac{1}{\mu(x)}\left[\int \mu(x)\,q(x)\,dx+C\right] \]

In words: (integrating factor × \(y\))′ = integrating factor × right-hand side; integrate, then divide by the integrating factor.

Reading the formula
\(\mu=e^{\int p\,dx}\)
the multiplier that completes the product rule: differentiating it brings down \(p\), so \(\mu'=p\,\mu\).
\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\mu y)=\mu q\)
the whole left side is now one derivative, and the right side is a function of \(x\) alone.
\(\int\mu q\,dx\)
one integration undoes that one derivative.
\(+C\)
which curve of the family you are on; it goes in before dividing, so it ends up as \(C/\mu\).
\(\dfrac{1}{\mu(x)}\)
unwraps \(\mu y\) to give \(y\) itself.

Why it works

By the chain rule (Lecture 6), \(\mu'=\dfrac{d}{dx}e^{\int p\,dx}=p\,e^{\int p\,dx}=p\,\mu\). By the product rule,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}(\mu y)=\mu y'+\mu' y=\mu y'+p\mu y=\mu\,(y'+py). \]

So \(\mu\times(\text{left side})\) is exactly \((\mu y)'\). This is the form promised in section 4: \((\mu y)'\) is the derivative of a single expression in \(x\) and \(y\), and \(\mu q\) is a function of \(x\) alone. The name: \(\mu\) is a factor that makes the equation directly integrable.

No \(+C\) inside \(\mu\). Using \(\int p\,dx+K\) instead would multiply \(\mu\) by the constant \(e^K\), which cancels from both sides. So take the simplest antiderivative. Likewise, if \(\int p\,dx=\ln|x|\) you may use \(\mu=x\) on an interval where \(x\gt0\) (or \(\mu=-x\), equally good, where \(x\lt0\)).

Recipe:

  1. Do: (1) standard form; the trick only works from a plain \(y'+p\,y\), with \(p\) read off with its sign.
  2. Do: (2) \(\mu=e^{\int p\,dx}\); this is the multiplier with \(\mu'=p\,\mu\), the missing piece of the product rule (no \(+C\) needed).
  3. Do: (3) write \((\mu y)'=\mu q\); multiplying both sides by \(\mu\) collapses the left side into one derivative.
  4. Do: (4) integrate both sides, with \(+C\); one integration undoes the one derivative, and \(C\) records where the leaf was dropped.
  5. Do: (5) divide by \(\mu\); you have found \(\mu y\), but the question asks for \(y\).
  6. Do: (6) use any initial condition. It fixes \(C\) and so picks one curve of the family.
Worked example 5.1: example (a) of section 3

Solve \(y'+y=\sin x\), then find the solution with \(y(0)=0\).

Already in standard form with \(p=1\), \(q=\sin x\). Integrating factor \(\mu=e^{\int1\,dx}=e^{x}\).

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\big(e^{x}y\big)=e^{x}\sin x \]

Integrate by parts twice (Lecture 13): \(\displaystyle\int e^x\sin x\,dx=\tfrac12e^x(\sin x-\cos x)\). So

\[ e^{x}y=\tfrac12e^{x}(\sin x-\cos x)+C \] \[ y=\tfrac12(\sin x-\cos x)+Ce^{-x}. \]

With \(y(0)=0\): \(\tfrac12(0-1)+C=0\), so \(C=\tfrac12\) and \(y=\tfrac12(\sin x-\cos x)+\tfrac12e^{-x}\).

Worked example 5.2

Solve \(y'+2y=4x\).

\(p=2\), \(\mu=e^{2x}\). Then \((e^{2x}y)'=4xe^{2x}\). By parts with \(u=4x\), \(dv=e^{2x}dx\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\int4xe^{2x}\,dx&=2xe^{2x}-\int2e^{2x}\,dx\\&=2xe^{2x}-e^{2x}+C.\end{aligned} \]

Divide by \(e^{2x}\): \(y=2x-1+Ce^{-2x}\).

Worked example 5.3: using rule (1)

Solve \(x\,y'-y=x^2\sin x\) for \(x\gt0\).

The coefficient of \(y'\) is \(x\), not 1. Divide by \(x\) first:

\[ y'-\frac1x\,y=x\sin x. \]

Now \(p=-\dfrac1x\) (note the minus sign). \(\int p\,dx=-\ln x\), so \(\mu=e^{-\ln x}=\dfrac1x\). Check: \(\left(\dfrac yx\right)'=\dfrac{y'}{x}-\dfrac{y}{x^2}=\dfrac1x\left(y'-\dfrac1x\,y\right)\), exactly \(\mu\) times the left side.

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)=\frac1x\cdot x\sin x=\sin x \] \[ \frac{y}{x}=-\cos x+C\quad\Rightarrow\quad y=x\,(C-\cos x). \]
Worked example 5.4: using rule (2)

Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=x-y\).

Move the \(y\) term to the same side: \(y'+y=x\). So \(p=+1\) (reading \(p=-1\) from the original would be wrong). \(p\) is defined by the form \(y'+p\,y\), with both terms on the left; in the original, \(y\) sits on the other side with the opposite sign. \(\mu=e^x\), \((e^xy)'=xe^x\), and \(\int xe^x\,dx=xe^x-e^x+C\). Hence \(y=x-1+Ce^{-x}\).

What the integrating factor does: \(y'+y=\sin x\)

chosen solutionother CC = 0

In the \(y\) view the curves for different \(C\) are not simple shifts of each other (the term \(Ce^{-x}\) has a different size at each \(x\)), and all of them settle onto the orange curve \(\tfrac12(\sin x-\cos x)\) as \(x\) grows. Switch to the \(\mu y\) view: now every curve has the same slope \(e^x\sin x\) at each \(x\), so they are vertical shifts of one another, just like the \(y=\sin x+C\) family in section 1. That is the point of the integrating factor: it turns the ODE into "derivative = function of \(x\)", which one integration solves.

You can now: put a linear ODE in standard form and read off \(p\) with its sign; find \(\mu=e^{\int p\,dx}\) without a \(+C\); and solve \((\mu y)'=\mu q\), adding \(C\) before dividing by \(\mu\), then fix \(C\) from an initial condition.

6Separable equations ~6 min

What is this?

In a separable equation the slope is (something about \(x\)) times (something about \(y\)). That lets you sort the equation like laundry: every \(y\)-thing, including \(dy\), goes to one side, and every \(x\)-thing, including \(dx\), goes to the other. Each side then involves only one letter, so each can be integrated on its own. One warning from the river picture: wherever the \(y\)-part is zero the current is flat, so a leaf dropped at that height drifts straight along it for ever. Sorting divides by that zero, so these flat-line solutions must be checked separately.

Definition

A first-order ODE is separable if it has the format

\[ \frac{dy}{dx}=X(x)\,Y(y), \]

where \(X(x)\) is a function of \(x\) only and \(Y(y)\) is a function of \(y\) only.

In words: the rate of change is (a function of \(x\)) times (a function of \(y\)).

Method
  1. Do: By observation, move everything with \(x\) to one side and everything with \(y\) to the other: \[ \frac{1}{Y(y)}\,dy=X(x)\,dx, \] after sorting, each side mentions only one letter, so neither integral needs the other.
  2. Do: then integrate both sides (simple integration): \[ \int\frac{1}{Y(y)}\,dy=\int X(x)\,dx+C. \] Each side is a one-letter integral; one \(+C\) is enough because the constants from the two sides combine into one.
  3. Do: check where \(Y(y)=0\) (see the trap below). At such a height the current is flat, so that horizontal line is a solution, and the division in the first step threw it away.

In words: all the \(y\) with \(dy\), all the \(x\) with \(dx\), integrate each side, one \(+C\).

Reading the formula
\(\int\frac{1}{Y(y)}\,dy\)
the \(y\)-pile: integrated as if \(x\) did not exist.
\(\int X(x)\,dx\)
the \(x\)-pile: an ordinary integral in \(x\).
\(+C\)
one constant for the whole family of paths; different \(C\), different leaf.
\(=\)
an equation linking \(x\) and \(y\); solve it for \(y\) if you can, otherwise leave it implicit (example 6.5).

Why moving "dy" and "dx" around is allowed

\(\frac{dy}{dx}\) is not really a fraction, but the step is shorthand for something legitimate. Divide the ODE by \(Y(y)\): \(\dfrac{1}{Y(y)}\dfrac{dy}{dx}=X(x)\). Let \(G\) be an antiderivative of \(\frac1Y\). By the chain rule, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}G(y)=G'(y)\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{1}{Y(y)}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). So the ODE says \(\dfrac{d}{dx}G(y)=X(x)\), and integrating with respect to \(x\) gives \(G(y)=\int X\,dx+C\). This is the same idea as substitution in Lecture 13.

Trap: dividing by Y(y)

Dividing by \(Y(y)\) assumes \(Y(y)\ne0\). If \(Y(y_0)=0\) for some number \(y_0\), then the constant function \(y=y_0\) is a solution (both sides are 0) which the division may lose. Always check for such constant solutions separately.

Worked example 6.1: population growth (situation (a) of section 1)

Solve \(\dfrac{dP}{dt}=kP\) with \(P(0)=P_0\).

Separate (for \(P\ne0\)): \(\dfrac{dP}{P}=k\,dt\). Integrate: \(\ln|P|=kt+C\), so \(|P|=e^{C}e^{kt}\) and \(P=Ae^{kt}\) with \(A=\pm e^{C}\). \(e^{C}\) is always positive; the \(\pm\) covers negative \(P\), and \(A=0\) below brings back the flat solution, so one letter \(A\) covers every case. The constant solution \(P=0\) is the case \(A=0\), so \(A\) can be any real number. With \(P(0)=P_0\): \(A=P_0\).

\[ P=P_0\,e^{kt} \]

In words: population = starting population × \(e^{(\text{growth rate})\times\text{time}}\), i.e. exponential growth.

Worked example 6.2: Newton's law of cooling (situation (b) of section 1)

Solve \(\dfrac{dT}{dt}=-k(T-T_a)\), \(T(0)=T_0\).

Separate: \(\dfrac{dT}{T-T_a}=-k\,dt\), so \(\ln|T-T_a|=-kt+C\), and as before \(T-T_a=Ae^{-kt}\). At \(t=0\): \(A=T_0-T_a\).

\[ T=T_a+(T_0-T_a)\,e^{-kt} \]

In words: temperature = room temperature + (starting gap) × a decaying exponential; the gap shrinks to 0.

(Situation (c), the spring, is second order: it is solved in part O3.)

Worked example 6.3

Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=xy\).

\(X(x)=x\), \(Y(y)=y\). For \(y\ne0\): \(\dfrac{dy}{y}=x\,dx\), \(\ln|y|=\dfrac{x^2}{2}+C\), so \(y=Ae^{x^2/2}\). The constant solution \(y=0\) (where \(Y=0\)) is included by allowing \(A=0\).

\[ y=Ae^{x^2/2},\quad A\in\mathbb R. \]

Slope field of \(y'=xy\) and the family \(y=Ae^{x^2/2}\)

slope markschosen solutiony = 0 (constant solution)

Here the slope depends on both \(x\) and \(y\), so the curves are no longer vertical shifts. Set \(A=0\): the solution is the red line \(y=0\), the one that dividing by \(y\) would have lost. No other solution curve ever crosses it.

Worked example 6.4: with an initial condition

Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=2x(1+y^2)\), \(y(0)=0\).

\(\dfrac{dy}{1+y^2}=2x\,dx\Rightarrow\tan^{-1}y=x^2+C\) (Lecture 7: \(\int\frac{dy}{1+y^2}=\tan^{-1}y\)). \(1+y^2\) is never 0, so no solution is lost. \(y(0)=0\) gives \(C=0\), so \(y=\tan(x^2)\) (valid while \(x^2\lt\frac\pi2\)). \(\tan\) blows up at \(\frac\pi2\): the path shoots off to infinity there, so this solution only exists on that interval.

Worked example 6.5: implicit answer

Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{x^2}{1+y^2}\).

\((1+y^2)\,dy=x^2\,dx\Rightarrow y+\dfrac{y^3}{3}=\dfrac{x^3}{3}+C\).

This cannot be rearranged nicely into "\(y=\dots\)". Leaving it as an equation linking \(x\) and \(y\) is acceptable: it defines \(y\) implicitly (as in implicit differentiation, Lecture 6). Such an answer is called an implicit solution.

You can now: recognise \(y'=X(x)\,Y(y)\); separate, integrate with one \(+C\) and tidy \(\ln|y|\) into \(y=Ae^{\dots}\); recover constant solutions lost by dividing by \(Y(y)\); and leave an implicit answer when \(y\) cannot be isolated.

7Partial derivatives ~7 min

What is this?

Picture a hill drawn on a map: you give your position (how far east, how far north) and the function gives the height of the ground there. A partial derivative is the steepness you feel walking due east only, or due north only, with the other direction frozen. This section is the toolkit for §8, where the unknown is exactly such a hill and the solution curves are its contour lines.

Exact equations (section 8) need one new tool. Everything so far used functions of one variable, like \(f(x)=x^2\). Now we need functions of two variables.

Function of two variables

A function \(F(x,y)\) takes two input numbers and gives one output. Example: \(F(x,y)=x^2y\) gives \(F(3,2)=9\times2=18\).

Think of a hilly landscape: \(x\) = distance east, \(y\) = distance north, \(F(x,y)\) = height of the ground there. The variables \(x\) and \(y\) are independent: you can choose each freely.

Definition: partial derivative

For a function \(F\) of two or more independent variables \((x,y,\dots)\),

\[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\quad(\text{read "partial }F\text{ partial }x\text{"}) \]

means: differentiate with respect to \(x\), treating (pretending) \(y\) and all other variables as constants. Similarly \(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y}\) treats \(x\) as a constant.

In words: \(\partial F/\partial x\) = how fast \(F\) changes when you move in the \(x\) direction only, with \(y\) frozen. On the landscape: the steepness of the ground if you walk due east.

The curly \(\partial\) (instead of \(d\)) signals that other variables are being held fixed.

Example

\(F=x^2y\).

\(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial x}=2xy\): treat \(y\) like a number, say 5; then \(5x^2\) differentiates to \(10x\), i.e. \(2x\cdot y\).

\(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y}=x^2\): treat \(x^2\) like a number; \(x^2\cdot y\) is that number times \(y\), whose derivative in \(y\) is \(x^2\).

More examples

\(F=x^3+xy^2+\sin y\): \(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial x}=3x^2+y^2\) (the \(\sin y\) term is a "constant", derivative 0); \(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y}=2xy+\cos y\) (the \(x^3\) term is a constant).

\(F=e^{xy}\): by the chain rule, \(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial x}=y\,e^{xy}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y}=x\,e^{xy}\).

A partial derivative is an ordinary derivative along a slice: \(F=x^2y\)

F along the slicetangent at the dot

Freezing one variable turns \(F\) into an ordinary one-variable function, and the partial derivative is just its slope. With \(y\) frozen at \(y_0\), the slice is the parabola \(y_0x^2\), slope \(2xy_0\) at the dot (\(x=1\)). With \(x\) frozen at \(x_0\), the slice is the straight line \(x_0^2\,y\), slope \(x_0^2\) everywhere.

Total differential

What is this? (total differential)

Take one small step that is partly east and partly north. Your height changes for two reasons: the east part of the step times the east steepness, plus the north part times the north steepness. Over a tiny step the hill looks like a flat tilted board, so the two parts simply add.

Total differential \[ dF=\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\,dx+\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\,dy \]

In words: small change in \(F\) ≈ (rate in \(x\) direction × small step in \(x\)) + (rate in \(y\) direction × small step in \(y\)).

Here \(dx\), \(dy\) stand for small changes in \(x\) and \(y\), and \(dF\) for the resulting change in \(F\). On the landscape: if you step a little east and a little north, the change in height is (east-steepness × east step) + (north-steepness × north step).

Reading the formula
\(dF\)

\(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\,dx\)
east steepness × the east part of the step.
\(\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\,dy\)

\(+\)
over a tiny step the hill is a flat tilted board, so the two effects add.
Going further: chain rule for F(x, y(x))

If \(y\) is itself a function of \(x\), then \(F(x,y(x))\) is a function of \(x\) alone, and "dividing the total differential by \(dx\)" gives the two-variable chain rule:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}F\big(x,y(x)\big)=\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\,\frac{dy}{dx}. \]

In words: total rate of change of \(F\) along the curve = direct effect of \(x\) + (effect of \(y\)) × (how fast \(y\) moves).

This is what makes exact equations work (section 8). It is the same rule you used in implicit differentiation (Lecture 6): differentiating \(x^2y\) with \(y=y(x)\) gave \(2xy+x^2y'\).

Mixed partial derivatives

What is this? (mixed partial derivatives)

The east steepness is not the same everywhere on the hill. Ask how it changes as you step north; or ask how the north steepness changes as you step east. On a smooth hill (every function in this course is smooth enough) both questions measure the same twist of the ground, so they give the same answer. This is the fact behind the exactness test in §8.

Partial derivatives are functions of \(x\) and \(y\) too, so they can be differentiated again. Differentiating \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\) with respect to \(y\) gives a mixed partial derivative, written

\[ \frac{\partial^2F}{\partial y\,\partial x}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\right). \]
Mixed partials are equal \[ \frac{\partial^2F}{\partial y\,\partial x}=\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x\,\partial y} \]

In words: differentiating \(F\) with respect to \(x\) first and then \(y\) gives the same as \(y\) first and then \(x\). The order does not matter.

Check

\(F=x^2y\): \(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2xy)=2x\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2)=2x\). Equal.

\(F=x^3+xy^2+\sin y\): \(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(3x^2+y^2)=2y\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(2xy+\cos y)=2y\). Equal.

\(F=e^{xy}\): \(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(ye^{xy})=e^{xy}+xye^{xy}\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(xe^{xy})=e^{xy}+xye^{xy}\). Equal.

Going further

This fact is called Clairaut's (or Schwarz's) theorem. It needs the second partial derivatives to be continuous, which is true for every function in this course.

You can now: compute \(\partial F/\partial x\) and \(\partial F/\partial y\) by freezing the other variable; write the total differential \(dF=F_x\,dx+F_y\,dy\); and check that the two mixed partial derivatives agree.

8Exact equations ~8 min

What is this?

Imagine the hill from §7 is hidden: you cannot see it, but the equation hands you its east steepness \(M\) and its north steepness \(N\). Then the left side \(M\,dx+N\,dy\) is the change in height for a small step, and the equation says that change is zero. A walker who never goes up or down is walking along a contour line. So, when such a hill really exists (the equation is exact), the solutions are its contour lines, and the job is to rebuild the hidden hill from its two steepnesses.

The "differential" form of an ODE

Exact equations are written as

\[ M(x,y)\,dx+N(x,y)\,dy=0. \]

This is shorthand for \(M+N\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0\) (divide by \(dx\)), i.e. \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{M}{N}\). Any first-order ODE can be written this way, e.g. \(y'=x-y\) becomes \((x-y)\,dx-dy=0\).

Definition

The word exact means the left-hand side is exactly the total differential \(dF\) of some function \(F(x,y)\). That is, a function \(F\) (a "scalar function": its output is a single number) exists with

\[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}=M,\qquad\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}=N. \]

Then the ODE says \(dF=0\), and the solution is

\[ F(x,y)=C. \]

In words: if the left side is the total change in some \(F\), the equation says "\(F\) does not change", so \(F\) stays at a constant value.

Why \(F=C\) solves the ODE (using the chain rule above): if \(y(x)\) satisfies \(F(x,y(x))=C\), differentiating gives \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}y'=0\), i.e. \(M+Ny'=0\). That is the ODE.

The test for exactness

What is this? (the exactness test)

Not every pair \(M\), \(N\) comes from a real hill. On a real smooth hill the two mixed slopes agree (§7): the rate at which the east steepness changes going north equals the rate at which the north steepness changes going east. So if \(M\) and \(N\) fail this check, no hill has them as its steepnesses, and the equation is not exact.

Test

\(M\,dx+N\,dy=0\) is exact if

\[ \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}. \]

In words: (\(y\)-derivative of the \(dx\)-coefficient) = (\(x\)-derivative of the \(dy\)-coefficient).

Reading the formula
\(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\)
how the east steepness changes as you step north.
\(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\)
how the north steepness changes as you step east.
\(=\)
both are the same twist of the hidden hill, \(\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial y\,\partial x}=\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x\,\partial y}\), so on a real hill they must agree.
the crossed pairing
\(M\) belongs to \(dx\) but is differentiated in \(y\); \(N\) belongs to \(dy\) but is differentiated in \(x\).

Why: if \(M=\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\) and \(N=\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\), then \(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial y\,\partial x}\) and \(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x\,\partial y}\), and these are equal because mixed partials agree (§7). So an exact equation must pass the test. Watch the pairing: \(M\) goes with \(y\), \(N\) goes with \(x\) (cross over).

Going further

The converse is also true on any rectangle (or any region without holes) where \(M,N\) and their partial derivatives are continuous: if the test passes, such an \(F\) exists. So the test is the standard way to decide exactness.

A confusing point

Independent or dependent?

When testing for exactness with partial derivatives, we treat \(x\) and \(y\) as independent variables (e.g. in \(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\), \(x\) is held fixed). But what we are really looking for is \(y\) as an implicit function of \(x\): a function \(y(x)\) defined by the equation \(F(x,y)=C\) rather than by a formula \(y=\dots\).

How to think about it: first treat \(x,y\) as two free variables to find the landscape \(F(x,y)\). The solutions of the ODE are then the contour lines (level curves) \(F=C\) of that landscape, and along a contour line \(y\) does depend on \(x\). Both views are correct; they are just two stages of the method.

Finding F

By inspection (for simple cases, inspection is usually enough). Learn to recognise these differentials (each follows from the total differential):

Expressionis the differential of
\(y\,dx+x\,dy\)\(d(xy)\)
\(x\,dx+y\,dy\)\(d\!\left(\tfrac12(x^2+y^2)\right)\)
\(\dfrac{y\,dx-x\,dy}{y^2}\)\(d\!\left(\dfrac xy\right)\)
\(\dfrac{x\,dy-y\,dx}{x^2}\)\(d\!\left(\dfrac yx\right)\)

For example, \(y\,dx+x\,dy=0\) is \(d(xy)=0\), so \(xy=C\); and \(x\,dx+y\,dy=0\) gives \(x^2+y^2=C\) (circles).

Method
  1. Do: From \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}=M\): \(F=\displaystyle\int M\,dx+g(y)\). (Integrate with respect to \(x\), treating \(y\) as a constant. The "constant" of integration can be any function of \(y\) only, because its \(x\)-derivative is 0.) Integrating the east steepness rebuilds the hill along each east–west line, but not how high each line starts; that unknown starting height can differ from line to line, so it is a function of \(y\).
  2. Do: From \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}=N\): \(F=\displaystyle\int N\,dy+h(x)\). (Integrate with respect to \(y\); the "constant" is any function of \(x\) only.) The same hill rebuilt along north–south lines, with the unknown now depending on \(x\).
  3. Do: Compare the two expressions and choose \(g(y)\) and \(h(x)\) so that they agree. Write the solution \(F(x,y)=C\). Both are the same hidden hill; each version shows the terms the other one's unknown function was hiding. The solutions are its contour lines.

In words: integrate \(M\) in \(x\), integrate \(N\) in \(y\), and combine: every term that appears in either answer appears once in \(F\).

Worked example 8.1

Solve \((2xy+1)\,dx+(x^2+2y)\,dy=0\).

Test. \(M=2xy+1\), \(N=x^2+2y\). \(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}=2x\), \(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}=2x\). Equal, so exact.

Integrate.

\[ \begin{aligned}\int M\,dx&=x^2y+x+g(y)\\ \int N\,dy&=x^2y+y^2+h(x)\end{aligned} \]

Compare. Both must be the same \(F\). Choose \(g(y)=y^2\) and \(h(x)=x\): \(y^2\) appears in the \(N\) result but not in the \(M\) one, so it is what \(g(y)\) was hiding; likewise \(x\) is \(h(x)\). The shared term \(x^2y\) is kept once.

\[ F=x^2y+x+y^2. \]

Check: \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}=2xy+1=M\) ✓, \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}=x^2+2y=N\) ✓.

Solution: \(x^2y+x+y^2=C\) (implicit).

Going further: "differentiate and match"

When comparing by eye is unclear, a safer route: from step 1, \(F=x^2y+x+g(y)\). Differentiate in \(y\) and set equal to \(N\): \(x^2+g'(y)=x^2+2y\), so \(g'(y)=2y\), \(g=y^2\). Same \(F\). If \(g'(y)\) still contained an \(x\), the equation would not be exact (recheck the test).

Solutions of an exact equation are level curves: \(x^2y+x+y^2=C\)

slope marks −M/Nlevel curves C = −3,…,3chosen level curve

The faint curves are contour lines of the landscape \(F(x,y)=x^2y+x+y^2\), like height contours on a map. Each one is a solution of \((2xy+1)\,dx+(x^2+2y)\,dy=0\): it follows the slope marks \(\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{M}{N}\) everywhere. The readout checks this at one point. Some level curves have two separate pieces; \(y\) is only an implicit function of \(x\), and different pieces can be different solutions.

You can now: write an ODE as \(M\,dx+N\,dy=0\); test exactness with \(M_y=N_x\) (crossed pairing); rebuild \(F\) by integrating and comparing, or by differentiate-and-match; and write the solution as \(F(x,y)=C\), using an initial condition to find \(C\).

9Making an equation exact ~3 min

What is this?

Sometimes the slope marks are right but no hill fits: \(M\) and \(N\) give the correct slope \(-M/N\), yet fail the test. Multiplying both by the same factor \(\sigma\) (defined and non-zero where you work) leaves the ratio \(-M/N\) unchanged, so the slope field and every solution curve stay exactly the same. What changes is that, for a good choice of \(\sigma\), the rescaled pair are now the steepnesses of a real hill, and §8 applies. The integrating factor of §5 is one such multiplier.

Integrating factor for M dx + N dy = 0

Sometimes \(M\,dx+N\,dy=0\) is not exact, but there is a multiplying factor \(\sigma(x,y)\) such that

\[ \sigma M\,dx+\sigma N\,dy=0 \]

is exact. Then solve that equation as in section 8. \(\sigma\) is also called an integrating factor. (Multiplying the whole equation by a nonzero \(\sigma\) does not change its solutions.)

In words: if the equation is not quite a total differential, multiply it by something that makes it one.

Worked example 9.1

Solve \(y\,dx-x\,dy=0\).

\(M=y\), \(N=-x\): \(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}=1\ne-1=\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\). Not exact.

Multiply by \(\sigma=\dfrac1{y^2}\): \(\dfrac{y\,dx-x\,dy}{y^2}=0\). This \(\sigma\) turns \(y\,dx-x\,dy\) into the quotient-rule pattern \(d(x/y)\) from the table in section 8. From the table in section 8 this is \(d\!\left(\dfrac xy\right)=0\). Check: \(M=\frac1y\), \(N=-\frac{x}{y^2}\), \(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}=-\frac1{y^2}=\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\) ✓. So \(\dfrac xy=C\), i.e. straight lines through the origin.

(Multiplying by \(-\frac1{x^2}\) instead gives \(d\!\left(\frac yx\right)=0\), the same lines. Integrating factors are not unique.)

Link with section 5

The linear equation \(y'+p(x)y=q(x)\) in differential form is \(\big(p(x)\,y-q(x)\big)\,dx+dy=0\). Here \(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}=p\) but \(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}=0\), so it is usually not exact. Multiplying by \(\sigma=e^{\int p\,dx}\) gives \(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(\sigma(py-q))=\sigma p\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\sigma=\sigma p\): exact. So the integrating factor of section 5 is a special case of this \(\sigma\).

Similarly, every separable equation \(X(x)\,dx-\frac1{Y(y)}\,dy=0\) is exact (\(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}=0=\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\)).

Going further: finding σ that depends on one variable

Requiring \(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(\sigma M)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\sigma N)\) with \(\sigma=\sigma(x)\) gives \(\dfrac{\sigma'}{\sigma}=\dfrac{M_y-N_x}{N}\) (writing \(M_y=\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\), \(N_x=\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\)). So:

If \(\dfrac{M_y-N_x}{N}\) depends on \(x\) only:

\[ \sigma=e^{\int\frac{M_y-N_x}{N}\,dx}. \]

If \(\dfrac{N_x-M_y}{M}\) depends on \(y\) only:

\[ \sigma=e^{\int\frac{N_x-M_y}{M}\,dy}. \]

Example 9.1: \(\frac{N_x-M_y}{M}=\frac{-2}{y}\) depends on \(y\) only, so \(\sigma=e^{-2\ln|y|}=\frac1{y^2}\). Exam questions usually tell you the form of \(\sigma\) if one is needed.

You can now: show that an equation is not exact, multiply by a given factor \(\sigma\), test again and solve; see the linear integrating factor as a special \(\sigma\); and, going further, find a \(\sigma\) that depends on \(x\) or \(y\) alone.

10Summary

  • An ODE links an unknown function of one variable with its derivatives. Its order is the highest derivative. It is linear if \(y\) and its derivatives appear only to the first power, multiplied by functions of \(x\).
  • Superposition (sum of solutions is a solution) holds for homogeneous linear ODEs.
  • A first-order ODE's general solution has one constant \(C\); an initial condition fixes it.

Which method? Go through these in order:

  1. Do: write the equation as \(y'=\dots\). If the right side is (function of \(x\)) × (function of \(y\)), separate (§6). When the \(x\)-stuff and \(y\)-stuff are multiplied (not added), sorting them to opposite sides is the quickest route.
  2. Do: otherwise, if \(y\) and \(y'\) appear only to the first power, put it in standard form \(y'+p\,y=q\) and use \(\mu=e^{\int p\,dx}\) (§5). A linear rule can always be turned into one product-rule derivative, so this never fails; only the last integral can be hard.
  3. Do: if it is given as \(M\,dx+N\,dy=0\), or fits neither of the above, test \(M_y=N_x\); if they are equal, find \(F\) and write \(F(x,y)=C\) (§8). Passing the test means a hidden hill exists, and the solutions are its contour lines.
  4. Do: if the test fails, multiply by the factor \(\sigma\) the question gives (or one found as in §9) and test again; also check whether it separates. Rescaling keeps the slope field the same but can make a hill fit; and an equation that fails the test may still factor into \(X(x)\,Y(y)\).
  5. Do: if nothing fits, try a substitution from part O2. Those substitutions turn harder equations into one of these shapes.

11Common mistakes

✗Reading \(p(x)\) before putting the equation in standard form. From \(xy'-y=x^2\sin x\), taking \(p=-1\). ✓ Divide by \(x\) first: \(p=-\frac1x\). ✗From \(y'=x-y\), taking \(p=-1\). ✓ Move \(y\) to the left first: \(y'+y=x\), so \(p=+1\). ✗Forgetting \(+C\), or adding it after dividing by \(\mu\): writing \(y=\frac1\mu\int\mu q\,dx+C\). ✓ \(C\) goes in before the division: \(y=\frac1\mu\left(\int\mu q\,dx+C\right)\); the constant term becomes \(\frac{C}{\mu}\), e.g. \(Ce^{-x}\). ✗Multiplying only the left side by \(\mu\). ✓ Both sides: \((\mu y)'=\mu q\). ✗Separating \(y'=x+y\) as if it were separable. ✓ \(x+y\) is not a product \(X(x)Y(y)\); it is linear: \(y'-y=x\). ✗Losing constant solutions by dividing by \(Y(y)\) (e.g. \(y=0\) for \(y'=xy\)). ✓ Check where \(Y(y)=0\). ✗Writing \(\ln|y|=x^2/2+C\Rightarrow y=e^{x^2/2}+C\). ✓ \(y=e^{x^2/2+C}=Ae^{x^2/2}\): the constant multiplies. ✗Exactness test with the wrong pairing, e.g. comparing \(\frac{\partial M}{\partial x}\) with \(\frac{\partial N}{\partial y}\). ✓ \(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\) vs \(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\). ✗Adding \(\int M\,dx\) and \(\int N\,dy\), which counts shared terms twice (e.g. \(2x^2y\) in example 8.1). ✓ Each term appears once in \(F\). ✗Writing the answer of an exact equation as \(F(x,y)=0\) or just \(F\). ✓ \(F(x,y)=C\).

1. State the order of each ODE and whether it is linear or nonlinear.

(a) \(y'''+x^2y=e^x\)   (b) \((y')^2+y=x\)   (c) \(y\,y''=1\)   (d) \(x^2y''-3xy'+y=\ln x\)   (e) \(y'=y\sin x\)

Hint

Order: highest derivative. Linear: look for powers, products or functions of \(y\), \(y'\), \(y''\). Functions of \(x\) do not matter.

Answer

(a) 3rd order, linear. (b) 1st order, nonlinear (\((y')^2\)). (c) 2nd order, nonlinear (product \(y\,y''\)). (d) 2nd order, linear with variable coefficients. (e) 1st order, linear (\(y'-(\sin x)\,y=0\)); it is also separable.

Hint

Separable: \(\frac{dy}{y^2}=x\,dx\). \(\int y^{-2}\,dy=-\frac1y\).

Answer

\(-\frac1y=\frac{x^2}2+C\); \(y(0)=1\) gives \(C=-1\).

\[ y=\frac{2}{2-x^2}\quad\left(-\sqrt2\lt x\lt\sqrt2\right) \]

3. Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{1+y^2}{1+x^2}\).

Hint

Separable; both sides integrate to \(\tan^{-1}\) (Lecture 7).

Answer

\(\tan^{-1}y=\tan^{-1}x+C\). Equivalently, taking \(\tan\) of both sides and writing \(k=\tan C\):

\[ y=\frac{x+k}{1-kx}. \]

4. Solve \(y'+y\tan x=\cos x\) for \(-\frac\pi2\lt x\lt\frac\pi2\).

Hint

Linear with \(p=\tan x\). \(\int\tan x\,dx=-\ln(\cos x)\) on this interval, so \(\mu=\frac{1}{\cos x}=\sec x\).

Answer

\((y\sec x)'=\cos x\sec x=1\), so \(y\sec x=x+C\):

\[ y=(x+C)\cos x. \]

5. Solve \(y'-2xy=x\), \(y(0)=0\), (a) as a linear equation and (b) as a separable equation.

Hint

(a) \(\mu=e^{-x^2}\); \(\int xe^{-x^2}\,dx=-\frac12e^{-x^2}\) (substitution). (b) \(y'=x(1+2y)\).

Answer

(a) \(e^{-x^2}y=-\frac12e^{-x^2}+C\), so \(y=-\frac12+Ce^{x^2}\). (b) \(\frac{dy}{1+2y}=x\,dx\), \(\frac12\ln|1+2y|=\frac{x^2}2+c\), the same family. With \(y(0)=0\): \(C=\frac12\).

\[ y=\frac{e^{x^2}-1}{2} \]

Hint

\(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\) and \(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\) are both \(4xy\). Then \(\int M\,dx=x^3+x^2y^2+g(y)\), \(\int N\,dy=x^2y^2+y^4+h(x)\).

Answer
\[ x^3+x^2y^2+y^4=C \]

7. Solve \((2x+y\cos x)\,dx+(\sin x+2y)\,dy=0\), \(y(0)=1\).

Hint

Exact: \(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}=\cos x=\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\). Find \(F\), then put \(x=0\), \(y=1\) into \(F=C\).

Answer

\(F=x^2+y\sin x+y^2\); \(F(0,1)=1\).

\[ x^2+y\sin x+y^2=1 \]

8. Show that \((3xy+y^2)\,dx+(x^2+xy)\,dy=0\) is not exact, but becomes exact after multiplying by \(\sigma=x\). Hence solve it.

Hint

Before: \(M_y=3x+2y\), \(N_x=2x+y\). After multiplying by \(x\): \(M=3x^2y+xy^2\), \(N=x^3+x^2y\); test again.

Answer

After: \(M_y=3x^2+2xy=N_x\), exact. \(F=x^3y+\frac12x^2y^2\).

\[ x^3y+\tfrac12x^2y^2=C \]

13Concept checks

1. Is \(y=x^2\) a solution of \(x\,y'=2y\)? Is it the only one?

Answer

Yes: \(x\cdot2x=2x^2=2y\). Not the only one: the general solution is \(y=Cx^2\) (separable), and \(y=x^2\) is the case \(C=1\).

2. Why is no \(+C\) needed when computing the integrating factor \(e^{\int p\,dx}\)?

Answer

A constant \(K\) in the exponent multiplies \(\mu\) by the constant \(e^K\). Since we multiply both sides of the equation by \(\mu\), that constant cancels; any one integrating factor is enough.

3. Is \(y'+y^2=0\) linear? Separable? Solve it.

Answer

Not linear (\(y^2\)). Separable: \(-\frac{dy}{y^2}=dx\), so \(\frac1y=x+C\), \(y=\dfrac{1}{x+C}\). Also the constant solution \(y=0\), lost when dividing by \(y^2\).

4. \(y_1=1\) and \(y_2=1+e^{-x}\) both solve \(y'+y=1\). Is \(y_1+y_2\) a solution? What does this say about superposition?

Answer

\(y_1+y_2=2+e^{-x}\) gives \(y'+y=-e^{-x}+2+e^{-x}=2\ne1\). Not a solution. The equation is linear but not homogeneous (right side not 0), so "sum of solutions is a solution" does not apply. (The difference \(y_2-y_1=e^{-x}\) does solve the homogeneous version \(y'+y=0\).)

5. Why must an exact equation satisfy \(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\)?

Answer

Exact means \(M=F_x\) and \(N=F_y\) for some \(F\). Then \(M_y\) and \(N_x\) are the two mixed partial derivatives of \(F\), which are equal.

6. Which method(s) apply to \(y'=x+y\)? Solve it.

Answer

Not separable (a sum, not a product). Linear: \(y'-y=x\), \(\mu=e^{-x}\), \((e^{-x}y)'=xe^{-x}\), \(e^{-x}y=-xe^{-x}-e^{-x}+C\). So \(y=Ce^{x}-x-1\).
