Curve Sketching

0Where this lecture sits

Think of a graph as a road you drive along from left to right. The first derivative \(f'\) says whether the road goes uphill or downhill. The second derivative \(f''\) says whether the road bends like a cup (curving up) or like a cap (curving down). This lecture turns those two readings into a hand sketch of the whole road, and into the best design in optimisation problems.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 \(f'\gt0\) means uphill, \(f'\lt0\) means downhill. One test point per piece of road is enough.
  2. §2 A hilltop is where uphill turns into downhill; a valley is the reverse. The First Derivative Test, with a sign chart.
  3. §3 Optimisation: turn the story into a road of one variable, then find its highest or lowest point.
  4. §4 \(f''\gt0\) means the road bends like a cup; \(f''\lt0\) like a cap. Bending is not the same as going up.
  5. §5 An inflection point is where cup switches to cap, on a smooth stretch of road (no corner).
  6. §6 At a flat spot, a cap is a hilltop and a cup is a valley. If \(f''=0\) there, the test says nothing.
  7. §7 The concavity sign chart is the §2 method one derivative higher.
  8. §8 The sketching checklist, step by step. Most common L9 exam type
  9. §9 Given only the gradient readings \(f'\), rebuild the shape of the road \(f\).

Recall the MVT (Lecture 8): if \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\), then there is at least one \(c\) in \((a,b)\) with

\[ f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. \]

In words: slope of the tangent at some point \(c\) = slope of the straight line joining the two ends.

Reading the formula
\(f(b)-f(a)\)
the height you gained between the two ends of the road.
\(b-a\)
how far you drove to the right.
\(\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\)
the average gradient over the whole trip.
\(f'(c)\)
the gradient of the road at one particular point \(c\).
at least one \(c\)
somewhere on the trip the road is exactly as steep as the average, provided the road is unbroken on \([a,b]\) (continuous) and has no sharp corners in between (differentiable).

1Monotonicity: increasing and decreasing ~5 min

What is this?

Drive along the graph from left to right. Increasing on a stretch means the road only ever climbs there; decreasing means it only ever descends. Monotonic means the stretch goes one way only, with no turning back. The derivative \(f'\) is the gradient of the road: positive where you climb, negative where you descend.

Definition (standard)

Let \(I\) be an interval. A function \(f\) is

  • increasing on \(I\) if \(\;x_1\lt x_2\;\Rightarrow\; f(x_1)\lt f(x_2)\;\) for all \(x_1,x_2\) in \(I\);
  • decreasing on \(I\) if \(\;x_1\lt x_2\;\Rightarrow\; f(x_1)\gt f(x_2)\;\) for all \(x_1,x_2\) in \(I\).

A function that is increasing on all of \(I\), or decreasing on all of \(I\), is monotonic (one-directional) on \(I\). "Monotonicity" means the question of where a function increases and where it decreases.

In words: increasing means "move right, go up"; decreasing means "move right, go down". Monotonic means it never changes direction.

What is this? (the Increasing/Decreasing Test)

If the road is uphill at every point of a stretch, then the end of the stretch is higher than the start. That sounds obvious, but it needs the road to be unbroken: a road with a gap could drop you to a lower level in between. The Mean Value Theorem is what turns many "uphill right here" readings into "higher overall".

Corollary: Increasing/Decreasing Test

Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\).

  • If \(f'(x)\gt0\) for all \(x\in(a,b)\), then \(f\) is increasing on \((a,b)\).
  • If \(f'(x)\lt0\) for all \(x\in(a,b)\), then \(f\) is decreasing on \((a,b)\).

In words: positive slope everywhere on an interval → the graph goes up across that interval; negative slope everywhere → it goes down.

A corollary is a result that follows quickly from a bigger theorem. Here the bigger theorem is the MVT, which is why it was proved first.

Going further: why the test is true

Take any two points \(x_1\lt x_2\) in \((a,b)\). \(f\) is continuous on \([x_1,x_2]\) and differentiable on \((x_1,x_2)\), so the MVT gives a \(c\) between them with

\[ f(x_2)-f(x_1)=f'(c)\,(x_2-x_1). \]

In words: change in height = (slope somewhere in between) × (distance moved to the right).

If \(f'\gt0\) everywhere, then \(f'(c)\gt0\), and \(x_2-x_1\gt0\), so the product is positive: \(f(x_2)\gt f(x_1)\). That is exactly the definition of increasing. If \(f'\lt0\), the product is negative and \(f\) is decreasing. Because \(f\) is continuous at the ends, the same argument works with \(x_1=a\) or \(x_2=b\), so \(f\) is in fact increasing on the closed interval \([a,b]\).

Notice why a single slope reading is not enough: \(f'(1)\gt0\) tells you about one instant. The MVT turns "\(f'\gt0\) at every point" into "\(f\) goes up between any two points".

Trap: intervals separated by a gap in the domain

\(f(x)=\dfrac1x\) has \(f'(x)=-\dfrac1{x^2}\lt0\) everywhere it is defined. It is decreasing on \((-\infty,0)\) and decreasing on \((0,\infty)\). But it is not decreasing on \((-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)\): \(-1\lt1\) yet \(f(-1)=-1\lt f(1)=1\). The test works on one interval at a time. Write the answer as "decreasing on \((-\infty,0)\) and on \((0,\infty)\)".

Going further: isolated zeros of f′ are harmless

If \(f'\gt0\) except at a few isolated points where \(f'=0\), \(f\) is still increasing. Example: \(f(x)=x^3\) has \(f'(0)=0\) but \(f'\gt0\) elsewhere; it is increasing on \((-\infty,0]\) and on \([0,\infty)\), so on the whole real line. This is why, in sign charts, you can often merge two neighbouring "\(+\)" intervals across a critical point.

You can now: say what increasing, decreasing and monotonic mean; use the sign of \(f'\) on an interval to decide whether \(f\) increases or decreases there; and keep intervals separated by a gap in the domain apart.

2Local extrema: the First Derivative Test ~11 min

What is this?

A hilltop on the road is where uphill turns into downhill; a valley bottom is where downhill turns into uphill. At such a place the road is either flat for an instant or has a sharp crest or dip, which is why we only look at critical points. But a flat spot alone proves nothing: the road can level off for a moment and then keep climbing. The First Derivative Test checks what the road does on each side.

Recall (Lecture 8) a critical point of \(f\) is an interior point \(c\) of the domain where \(f'(c)=0\) or \(f'(c)\) does not exist (DNE). Every local maximum or minimum at an interior point is a critical point, but not every critical point is an extremum (think of \(x^3\) at 0). The First Derivative Test decides which critical points are extrema.

Theorem: First Derivative Test

Let \(f\) be a continuous function with a critical point at \(c\).

  • If \(f'\) changes from positive to negative at \(c\), then \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\).
  • If \(f'\) changes from negative to positive at \(c\), then \(f\) has a local minimum at \(c\).
  • If \(f'\) does not change sign at \(c\), then \(f\) has no local maximum or minimum at \(c\).

In words: going up then down = a hilltop; going down then up = a valley; up then up (or down then down) = just a pause, no hill and no valley.

"\(f'\) changes from positive to negative at \(c\)" means: \(f'\gt0\) on some small interval just to the left of \(c\) and \(f'\lt0\) on some small interval just to the right. Why it works: on the left \(f\) is increasing (section 1), so values there are below \(f(c)\); on the right \(f\) is decreasing, so values there are also below \(f(c)\). Continuity at \(c\) is needed so that \(f(c)\) joins the two pieces (a jump at \(c\) could spoil it).

Technique to find local extrema

  1. Do: Find the critical points \(c_i\) of \(f\). These are the only points of the road where it can switch between uphill and downhill, by going flat or at a sharp corner.
  2. Do: Partition (cut up) the domain \(D_f\) of \(f\) into subintervals whose endpoints are the critical points. Also cut at any point not in the domain (such as a vertical asymptote). Each piece then has no flat spot, corner or gap inside, so the road cannot change direction within it.
  3. Do: Apply the Increasing/Decreasing Test on each subinterval: choose a test point \(t\) inside it, decide whether \(f'(t)\) is positive or negative, and conclude that \(f\) is increasing or decreasing on the whole subinterval. Repeat for every subinterval. Since the direction cannot change inside a piece, one gradient reading tells you the direction of the whole piece.
  4. Do: Apply the First Derivative Test at each critical point \(c_i\): does the sign of \(f'\) change there, and which way? Uphill then downhill is a hilltop, downhill then uphill a valley, and no switch is only a pause.

Why one test point is enough: on a subinterval with no critical points and no gaps, \(f'\) exists and is never 0. If \(f'\) is continuous there, it cannot change sign without passing through 0 (Intermediate Value Theorem, Lecture 4), so its sign at one point is its sign everywhere on that piece. Example 2: local extrema

Find the local extrema of \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^3}{x^2-1}\).

Domain. \(x^2-1\ne0\), so \(D_f=\mathbb R\setminus\{-1,1\}\) (all real numbers except \(\pm1\)).

Derivative (quotient rule, Lecture 6):

\[\begin{aligned} f'(x)&=\frac{3x^2(x^2-1)-x^3\cdot2x}{(x^2-1)^2}\\&=\frac{x^4-3x^2}{(x^2-1)^2}\\&=\frac{x^2(x^2-3)}{(x^2-1)^2}.\end{aligned}\]

Critical points. \(f'(x)=0\) when \(x^2=0\) or \(x^2=3\): \(x=0,\ \pm\sqrt3\). \(f'\) fails to exist only at \(x=\pm1\), which are not in the domain, so they are not critical points (but we still cut the number line there). \(x=\pm1\) are gaps in the road, and the direction may change across a gap.

3Optimisation problems ~6 min

What is this?

In an optimisation problem you turn one dial (the radius of a can, say) and watch one meter (the metal used). The constraint ties every other measurement to that dial, so the meter reading becomes a function of one variable: a road again. The best design is the lowest (or highest) point on that road. If the road has ends, check them too; if it runs on forever, you need to show that it falls all the way to your point and rises all the way after it.

An optimisation problem asks for the best value of some quantity: the largest area, the least cost, the shortest time. Calculus does the "best value" part (Lecture 8's Closed Interval Method, or the First Derivative Test); the real skill is turning words into a function of one variable.

Steps to solve optimisation problems
  1. Do: Read and understand the problem. What is given? What is unknown? You need to know which quantity is the meter (to be made best) and which are the dials.
  2. Do: Draw a picture, assign variables and restrict the domain (for example a length must be positive). The domain fixes where the road starts and ends: a negative length is not part of the road.
  3. Do: Write an equation for the quantity to be maximised or minimised. It must end up as a function of a single variable. Usually you first get it in two variables, and the problem gives a second equation (a constraint, such as a fixed volume) linking them: two equations, two unknowns. Use the constraint to eliminate one variable. The derivative tests work for one variable only, so the constraint must remove the extra dial.
  4. Do: Find the absolute maximum or minimum value: apply the Closed Interval Method (Lecture 8) if the domain is a closed interval, or the First Derivative Test otherwise. A closed interval has ends to compare with; an open one needs the "falls all the way, then rises all the way" argument below.
Going further: when the domain is open

In step 4, the First Derivative Test gives local extrema, but the question wants the absolute best. The standard bridge (sometimes called the First Derivative Test for Absolute Extreme Values): if \(c\) is the only critical point in an interval \(I\), and \(f'\lt0\) for all \(x\lt c\) in \(I\) and \(f'\gt0\) for all \(x\gt c\) in \(I\), then \(f(c)\) is the absolute minimum on \(I\). (Similarly \(+\) then \(-\) gives the absolute maximum.) The reason: \(f\) decreases all the way to \(c\) and increases all the way after it. Saying this sentence in an exam earns the "justify" mark.

Example 3: the soup can

A soup can (a closed cylinder) is made to hold 1 L of soup. What dimensions will use the least material?

Step 1. Given: volume \(1\ \text{L}=1000\ \text{cm}^3\). Unknown: the radius and height that make the surface area (the metal used) as small as possible. We ignore the thickness of the metal and the overlaps at the seams.

Step 2. Let the radius be \(r\) cm and the height \(h\) cm, with \(r\gt0\), \(h\gt0\).

Step 3. Surface area = top + bottom + side. The side unrolls into a rectangle of width \(2\pi r\) (the circumference) and height \(h\):

\[ S=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh. \]

In words: material = two circular ends + (circumference × height).

Reading the formula
\(2\pi r^2\)
the top and the bottom, two circles of area \(\pi r^2\) each.
\(2\pi r\)
the circumference: the width of the side once it is unrolled.
\(2\pi rh\)
the side, a rectangle of that width and height \(h\).
\(S\)
the total metal. A wider can spends more on the ends; a taller can spends more on the side. The fixed volume stops you shrinking both at once.

The constraint is the volume: \(\pi r^2h=1000\), so \(h=\dfrac{1000}{\pi r^2}\). Substitute:

\[\begin{aligned} S(r)&=2\pi r^2+2\pi r\cdot\frac{1000}{\pi r^2}\\&=2\pi r^2+\frac{2000}{r},\qquad r\gt0.\end{aligned}\]

Step 4. The domain \((0,\infty)\) is open, so use the First Derivative Test.

\[\begin{aligned} S'(r)&=4\pi r-\frac{2000}{r^2}\\&=\frac{4\pi r^3-2000}{r^2}=0\\ &\iff r^3=\frac{500}{\pi}\\ &\iff r=\sqrt[3]{\frac{500}{\pi}}\approx5.42.\end{aligned}\]

For \(0\lt r\lt\sqrt[3]{500/\pi}\), \(4\pi r^3\lt2000\), so \(S'\lt0\); for larger \(r\), \(S'\gt0\). This is the only critical point, and \(S\) decreases before it and increases after it, so it gives the absolute minimum.

Then The critical-point equation says \(\pi r^3=500\). Multiplying top and bottom by \(r\) uses it directly, so no decimals are needed and the neat result \(h=2r\) appears.

\[\begin{aligned} h&=\frac{1000}{\pi r^2}=\frac{1000}{\pi r^3}\cdot r\\&=\frac{1000}{500}\,r=2r\approx10.84.\end{aligned}\]

Answer: radius \(r=\sqrt[3]{500/\pi}\approx5.42\) cm and height \(h=2r=2\sqrt[3]{500/\pi}\approx10.84\) cm. The height equals the diameter. Least material \(\approx554\ \text{cm}^2\).

Lesson: a thin tall can wastes metal on the side; a wide flat can wastes it on the ends. The best can balances the two. (Real cans are taller than this because of costs like seams and labels.)

You can now: turn an optimisation story into a function of one variable using the constraint; state its domain; and justify an absolute max or min with the Closed Interval Method or the only-critical-point argument.

4Concavity ~5 min

What is this?

Drive along the road and watch the gradient. If it keeps increasing (an uphill that gets steeper, or a downhill that eases off), the road curves upwards like the inside of a cup: concave up. If the gradient keeps decreasing, the road curves downwards like a cap, an upside-down cup: concave down. A cup lies above every tangent line you lay against it; a cap lies below. The bend says nothing about whether you are going up or down.

Two graphs can both be increasing yet look different: one bends upwards like a bowl, the other bends downwards like a dome. This bending is concavity. A handy way to remember it is with faces: concave up ☺ (a smile), concave down ☹ (a frown).

Definition

The graph of a differentiable function \(y=f(x)\) is

  • concave up on an open interval \(I\) if \(f'\) is increasing on \(I\);
  • concave down on an open interval \(I\) if \(f'\) is decreasing on \(I\).

Equivalently:

  • the graph of \(f\) lies above all of its tangents on \(I\): concave up;
  • the graph of \(f\) lies below all of its tangents on \(I\): concave down.

In words: concave up = the slope keeps getting bigger (the curve turns left as you walk along it, and holds water); concave down = the slope keeps getting smaller (the curve turns right, and spills water).

Take \(y=x^3\): for \(x\lt0\) the slope \(f'\) decreases (from very steep down to 0) and the curve sits below its tangent lines; for \(x\gt0\) the slope increases and the curve sits above them. What is this? (the Concavity Test)

The second derivative is the rate at which the gradient changes as you drive right. Positive means the gradient is going up, so the road is curving into a cup; negative means the gradient is going down, so the road is curving into a cap. It is the uphill/downhill test of section 1, applied to the gradient instead of the height.

Theorem: Concavity Test

Let \(y=f(x)\) be twice-differentiable on an interval \(I\).

  • If \(f''(x)\gt0\) for all \(x\) in \(I\), then \(f\) is concave up on \(I\).
  • If \(f''(x)\lt0\) for all \(x\) in \(I\), then \(f\) is concave down on \(I\).

In words: second derivative positive → the slope is increasing → concave up (☺). Second derivative negative → the slope is decreasing → concave down (☹).

Twice-differentiable means \(f''\) exists at every point of \(I\). The test is just the Increasing/Decreasing Test of section 1 applied to the function \(f'\) instead of \(f\): \(f''\) is the derivative of \(f'\), so \(f''\gt0\) means \(f'\) is increasing, which is the definition of concave up.

Do not confuse concavity with increasing. Concavity is about the slope changing, not about the height. \(e^{-x}\) is decreasing but concave up (it falls ever more gently); \(\sqrt x\) is increasing but concave down (it rises ever more gently).

Analogy. If \(f(t)\) is the position of a car, \(f'\) is its velocity and \(f''\) its acceleration. Concave up means accelerating (pressing the pedal), concave down means braking, whatever direction you are moving.

You can now: define concave up and concave down through \(f'\) or through tangent lines; use the sign of \(f''\) to decide the concavity on an interval; and tell concavity apart from increasing or decreasing.

5Inflection points ~3 min

What is this?

An inflection point is where the road changes from a cup bend to a cap bend, or back, like the middle of an S-bend. The road must be smooth there, with a definite direction (a tangent line); a sharp corner does not count. A point where the second derivative is zero is only a suspect: the bend has to actually switch.

Definition

An inflection point (IP) \((c,f(c))\) on the graph of \(f\) occurs when

  1. \(f''(c)=0\) or \(f''(c)\) does not exist,
  2. \(f\) has a tangent line at \(c\), and
  3. \(f\) has a change in concavity at \(c\).

In words: an inflection point is a point on the curve where the bending switches from smile to frown (or frown to smile), and the curve is smooth enough there to have a tangent line.

Condition (i) tells you where to look (the candidates). Condition (iii) is what actually makes it an inflection point; check it with a sign chart for \(f''\). Condition (ii) rules out sharp corners. A tangent line exists if \(f'(c)\) exists, and also when the graph has a vertical tangent (the slope blows up to \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\) on both sides, like \(\sqrt[3]x\) at 0).

6The Second Derivative Test ~3 min

What is this?

At a flat spot the road is level for an instant. If the road is bent like a cap there, every step away from it goes down: you are on a hilltop. If it is bent like a cup, every step away goes up: you are in a valley. If the bend reads zero, the road may be a very flat cup, a very flat cap, or an S-bend, so this test cannot tell, and you go back to the uphill/downhill check of section 2.

Theorem: Second Derivative Test

Suppose that \(f''\) is continuous near \(c\).

  • If \(f'(c)=0\) and \(f''(c)\lt0\), then \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\).
  • If \(f'(c)=0\) and \(f''(c)\gt0\), then \(f\) has a local minimum at \(c\).
  • If \(f'(c)=0\) and \(f''(c)=0\), then the test fails: \(f\) may have a local maximum, a local minimum, or neither.

In words: flat tangent + frowning (☹) = top of a hill; flat tangent + smiling (☺) = bottom of a valley; flat tangent + no clear bend = no conclusion, use the First Derivative Test instead.

Here "\(f''\) is continuous near \(c\)" means the function \(f''\) is continuous on an interval around \(c\).

Why it works: if \(f''(c)\lt0\) and \(f''\) is continuous, then \(f''\lt0\) on a small interval around \(c\), so \(f'\) is decreasing there. Since \(f'(c)=0\), \(f'\) is positive just before \(c\) and negative just after: by the First Derivative Test, a local maximum.

7Finding concavity and inflection points ~5 min

What is this?

This is the sign chart of section 2 again, but reading the bend instead of the direction. Cut the road wherever the bend could switch, take one reading of the second derivative on each piece, and mark the piece cup (∪) or cap (∩). The inflection points are the cuts where the mark changes and the road is smooth.

Technique

  1. Do: Find the critical points \(c_i\) of \(f'\): the interior points where \(f''(c_i)=0\) or \(f''(c_i)\) does not exist. These are the only points where the bend can switch between cup and cap on an unbroken road.
  2. Do: Partition the domain \(D_f\) into subintervals with these points (and any gaps in the domain) as endpoints. The bend can also switch across a gap in the road, as at the asymptotes of Example 5, so gaps are cuts too.
  3. Do: Apply the Concavity Test on each subinterval: choose a test point \(t\), decide whether \(f''(t)\) is positive or negative, and conclude concave up or concave down. Repeat for all subintervals. The bend cannot switch inside a piece, so one reading gives the bend of the whole piece.
  4. Do: Decide whether \(f\) has an inflection point at each \(x=c_i\): does the concavity change there, and does \(f\) have a tangent line there? A real switch on a smooth part of the road is an inflection point; no switch, or a corner, is not.
  5. Do: Repeat for each critical point of \(f'\). Each candidate is checked on its own: one being an inflection point says nothing about the others.

It is the section 2 technique one derivative higher. The sign chart now has rows \(x\), \(f''(x)\) and \(f(x)\), with ∪ for concave up and ∩ for concave down.

8Guidelines for curve sketching ~6 min

What is this?

Before drawing a road, collect the facts that pin it down: where it exists, where it crosses the axes, whether one half mirrors the other, and where it runs off towards a line or shoots up a cliff. Then add the two readings of this lecture: uphill or downhill, and cup or cap. Each fact fixes part of the picture; together they leave only one sensible shape.

Here is everything collected into one checklist. Work through it in order and the sketch almost draws itself.

  1. Do: Domain. All values of \(x\) such that \(f(x)\) is defined. This says where the road exists and where it has gaps; every later sign chart is cut at the gaps.
  2. Do: Intercepts. \(x\)-intercepts: solve \(f(x)=0\). \(y\)-intercept: evaluate \(f(0)\). These are fixed points the road must pass through, so they anchor the sketch.
  3. Do: Symmetry.
    Even: \(f(-x)=f(x)\) for all \(x\) in \(D\) (mirror image in the \(y\)-axis).
    Odd: \(f(-x)=-f(x)\) for all \(x\) in \(D\) (unchanged by a half-turn about the origin).
    Periodic: \(f(x+p)=f(x)\) for all \(x\) in \(D\) (repeats every \(p\)). A symmetry saves work: sketch one part, then mirror, rotate or repeat it.
  4. Do: Asymptotes (Lecture 3).
    Horizontal \(y=c\): \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)=c\).
    Vertical \(x=a\): \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a^\pm}f(x)=\pm\infty\).
    Oblique \(y=mx+b\): \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{f(x)}{x}=m\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\big(f(x)-mx\big)=b\). These show where the road runs off: the lines it hugs far out, and where it shoots up or down beside a gap.
  5. Do: Intervals of increase/decrease (I/D). Find the critical points of \(f\); apply the I/D Test. This is the uphill or downhill reading for each piece of road.
  6. Do: Local max/min values. Apply the First Derivative Test (or the Second Derivative Test). Hilltops and valley bottoms are the turning points the sketch must show, with coordinates.
  7. Do: Intervals of concave up/down (CU/CD). Find the critical points of \(f'\); apply the Concavity Test. This is the cup or cap reading, which fixes how each piece curves between the plotted points.
  8. Do: Inflection points. Find the points where \(f\) changes concavity and has a tangent line. The bend switches there, so the sketch must change from cup to cap at exactly those points.

For the vertical asymptote, \(\lim_{x\to a^\pm}\) means the one-sided limits as \(x\) approaches \(a\) from the right (\(a^+\)) and from the left (\(a^-\)); it is enough for one of them to be \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\). An oblique (slant) asymptote is a non-horizontal straight line that the graph approaches far out; \(m\) is its slope and \(b\) its \(y\)-intercept.

In words (oblique): slope of the asymptote = what \(f(x)/x\) settles down to; intercept = what is left of \(f(x)\) after removing \(mx\).

Reading the formula
\(\dfrac{f(x)}{x}\to m\)
far out, the height grows like \(m\) times the distance: \(m\) is the long-run gradient of the road.
\(f(x)-mx\)
the gap between the road and the line of gradient \(m\) through the origin.
\(f(x)-mx\to b\)
that gap settles to a fixed amount \(b\), so the road hugs the line \(y=mx+b\) ever more closely.
Example 6: curve sketching

Sketch the graph of \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^3}{x^2-1}\).

Domain: \(\mathbb R\setminus\{-1,1\}\).

Intercepts: \(f(x)=0\iff x^3=0\iff x=0\); \(f(0)=0\). The only intercept is the origin \((0,0)\).

Symmetry: \(f(-x)=\dfrac{(-x)^3}{(-x)^2-1}=-\dfrac{x^3}{x^2-1}=-f(x)\): odd, symmetric under a half-turn about the origin. Not periodic. So we could work out \(x\gt0\) and rotate.

Vertical asymptotes at \(x=\pm1\) (the denominator is 0, the top is not). Signs near \(x=1\): the top is near 1; \(x^2-1\) is a small positive number for \(x\) just above 1 and a small negative number just below, so

\[\begin{gathered} \lim_{x\to1^+}f(x)=+\infty,\quad \lim_{x\to1^-}f(x)=-\infty,\\ \lim_{x\to-1^+}f(x)=+\infty,\quad \lim_{x\to-1^-}f(x)=-\infty.\end{gathered}\]

Horizontal asymptotes: none, since \(f(x)\to\pm\infty\) as \(x\to\pm\infty\).

Oblique asymptote:

\[\begin{aligned} m&=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{f(x)}{x}=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{x^2}{x^2-1}=1,\\ b&=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\Big(\frac{x^3}{x^2-1}-x\Big)\\&=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{x}{x^2-1}=0.\end{aligned}\]

So \(y=x\) is an oblique asymptote at both ends. Since \(f(x)-x=\dfrac{x}{x^2-1}\) is positive for large \(x\), the graph approaches the line from above on the right (and from below on the left).

I/D and local extrema (Example 2): increasing on \((-\infty,-\sqrt3)\) and \((\sqrt3,\infty)\); decreasing on \((-\sqrt3,-1)\), \((-1,1)\) and \((1,\sqrt3)\); local max \(\big(-\sqrt3,-\tfrac{3\sqrt3}2\big)\approx(-1.73,-2.60)\); local min \(\big(\sqrt3,\tfrac{3\sqrt3}2\big)\approx(1.73,2.60)\).

Concavity and IP (Example 5): concave up on \((-1,0)\), \((1,\infty)\); concave down on \((-\infty,-1)\), \((0,1)\); inflection point \((0,0)\), with a horizontal tangent there.

10Summary

  • Use a combination of old and new techniques to sketch the graph of a function.
  • \(f'(x)\) tells you where the function is increasing or decreasing, and where its local extrema are (First Derivative Test).
  • \(f''(x)\) tells you the concavity of the function and its inflection points (Concavity Test; the Second Derivative Test also classifies extrema).
  • Together with the domain, intercepts, symmetry and asymptotes, these let you sketch the graph.
  • Use a sign chart for \(f'\) and for \(f''\).
  • Check endpoints if the domain is restricted.
  • Start with simpler functions before tackling more complicated ones.
  • Optimisation: write the quantity as a function of one variable using the constraint, restrict the domain, then find its absolute max or min.

Answer

Top corner \((x,\sqrt{r^2-x^2})\), so width \(2x\), height \(\sqrt{r^2-x^2}\):

\[ A(x)=2x\sqrt{r^2-x^2},\qquad 0\le x\le r. \] \[\begin{aligned} A'(x)&=2\sqrt{r^2-x^2}-\frac{2x^2}{\sqrt{r^2-x^2}}\\&=\frac{2(r^2-2x^2)}{\sqrt{r^2-x^2}}=0\\ &\iff x=\frac{r}{\sqrt2}.\end{aligned}\]

Closed Interval Method: \(A(0)=0\), \(A(r)=0\), \(A\big(\tfrac{r}{\sqrt2}\big)=2\cdot\tfrac{r}{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac{r}{\sqrt2}=r^2\). Largest area \(r^2\), for a rectangle of width \(\sqrt2\,r\) and height \(\dfrac{r}{\sqrt2}\).

(a) \(y=x^3-6x^2+9x+1\)   (b) \(f(x)=\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\)   (c) \(g(x)=\dfrac{\ln x}{x}\)   (d) \(f(\theta)=\cos\theta+\sin\theta\)

Hints

(a) A cubic: no asymptotes. Factor \(y'=3(x^2-4x+3)\).

(b) Domain: \(x^2-1\gt0\). Is \(f\) odd or even? Find \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\) and \(\lim_{x\to-\infty}\) separately (careful: \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\)).

(c) Domain \(x\gt0\). Quotient rule twice; the critical points involve \(\ln x=1\) and \(\ln x=\frac32\). For the horizontal asymptote, \(\ln x\) grows more slowly than \(x\) (L'Hôpital, Lecture 10, makes this precise).

(d) Periodic with period \(2\pi\). Note \(f''=-f\), so the inflection points are where \(f=0\). You can also write \(f(\theta)=\sqrt2\sin\big(\theta+\frac\pi4\big)\).

Answers

(a) Domain \(\mathbb R\); \(y\)-intercept \(1\); one \(x\)-intercept near \(x\approx-0.10\); no symmetry, no asymptotes. \(y'=3(x-1)(x-3)\): increasing on \((-\infty,1)\) and \((3,\infty)\), decreasing on \((1,3)\); local max \((1,5)\), local min \((3,1)\). \(y''=6x-12\): concave down on \((-\infty,2)\), concave up on \((2,\infty)\); IP \((2,3)\).

(b) Domain \((-\infty,-1)\cup(1,\infty)\); no intercepts; odd. Vertical asymptotes \(x=1\) (\(f\to+\infty\) as \(x\to1^+\)) and \(x=-1\) (\(f\to-\infty\) as \(x\to-1^-\)). Horizontal asymptotes \(y=1\) as \(x\to\infty\) and \(y=-1\) as \(x\to-\infty\). \(f'(x)=-\dfrac{1}{(x^2-1)^{3/2}}\lt0\): decreasing on each piece, no extrema. \(f''(x)=\dfrac{3x}{(x^2-1)^{5/2}}\): concave down on \((-\infty,-1)\), concave up on \((1,\infty)\); no inflection point (0 is not in the domain).

(c) Domain \((0,\infty)\); \(x\)-intercept \((1,0)\); vertical asymptote \(x=0\) (\(g\to-\infty\) as \(x\to0^+\)); horizontal asymptote \(y=0\) as \(x\to\infty\). \(g'(x)=\dfrac{1-\ln x}{x^2}\): increasing on \((0,e)\), decreasing on \((e,\infty)\); local (and absolute) max \(\big(e,\frac1e\big)\approx(2.72,0.37)\). \(g''(x)=\dfrac{2\ln x-3}{x^3}\): concave down on \((0,e^{3/2})\), concave up on \((e^{3/2},\infty)\); IP \(\big(e^{3/2},\frac{3}{2e^{3/2}}\big)\approx(4.48,0.33)\).

(d) Domain \(\mathbb R\); periodic, period \(2\pi\); \(y\)-intercept 1; \(\theta\)-intercepts \(\frac{3\pi}4+k\pi\). \(f'=\cos\theta-\sin\theta\): max \(\sqrt2\) at \(\theta=\frac\pi4+2k\pi\), min \(-\sqrt2\) at \(\theta=\frac{5\pi}4+2k\pi\). \(f''=-(\cos\theta+\sin\theta)\): concave down where \(f\gt0\), concave up where \(f\lt0\); IPs at \(\big(\frac{3\pi}4+k\pi,\,0\big)\). (Here \(k\) is any integer.)

3. Find the value(s) of \(k\) such that \(f(x)=x^3+kx^2+3x\) has no local extrema.

Hint

\(f'\) is a quadratic. A local extremum needs \(f'\) to change sign. When does a quadratic with positive leading coefficient never change sign? (Discriminant.)

Answer

\(f'(x)=3x^2+2kx+3\), discriminant \(\Delta=(2k)^2-4\cdot3\cdot3=4k^2-36\). If \(\Delta\gt0\), \(f'\) has two roots and changes sign at each: two extrema. If \(\Delta\le0\), \(f'(x)\ge0\) everywhere (at most one double root, where \(f'\) touches 0 without changing sign), so by the First Derivative Test there are no extrema. \(4k^2-36\le0\iff k^2\le9\). \(-3\le k\le3\). (At \(k=\pm3\), \(f'(x)=3(x\pm1)^2\ge0\): a flat point but no extremum, like \(x^3\).)

4. Find the intervals on which \(f(x)=\ln(x^2+1)\) is concave up or down, and state any inflection points.

Hint

\(f'(x)=\dfrac{2x}{x^2+1}\). Quotient rule for \(f''\); its sign is the sign of the numerator.

Answer
\[\begin{aligned} f''(x)&=\frac{2(x^2+1)-2x\cdot2x}{(x^2+1)^2}\\&=\frac{2(1-x^2)}{(x^2+1)^2}.\end{aligned}\]

Zero at \(x=\pm1\), defined everywhere. Concave up on \((-1,1)\) (\(1-x^2\gt0\)); concave down on \((-\infty,-1)\) and \((1,\infty)\). Inflection points \((-1,\ln2)\) and \((1,\ln2)\).

5. If \(f\) is increasing and concave down on \((a,b)\), what can be inferred about \(f^{-1}\)?

Hint

The graph of \(f^{-1}\) is the reflection of the graph of \(f\) in the line \(y=x\) (Lecture 7). Try it with \(f(x)=\sqrt x\) on \((1,4)\). For a proof, use \((f^{-1})'(y)=\dfrac{1}{f'(f^{-1}(y))}\).

Answer

\(f^{-1}\) is increasing and concave up (on the interval of values of \(f\), from \(f(a^+)\) to \(f(b^-)\)).

Picture: reflecting in \(y=x\) swaps the roles of "across" and "up". A curve that rises ever more gently (concave down) becomes one that rises ever more steeply (concave up). Example: \(\sqrt x\) (increasing, concave down) has inverse \(x^2\) on \(x\gt0\) (increasing, concave up).

Argument, assuming \(f\) is differentiable with \(f'\gt0\): \((f^{-1})'(y)=\dfrac1{f'(f^{-1}(y))}\gt0\), so \(f^{-1}\) is increasing. As \(y\) increases, \(x=f^{-1}(y)\) increases, so \(f'(x)\) decreases (concave down) but stays positive, so \(\dfrac1{f'(x)}\) increases. Hence \((f^{-1})'\) is increasing: \(f^{-1}\) is concave up.

14Concept checks

Short questions to test understanding, not computation.

C1. You find \(f'(c)=0\) and \(f''(c)=0\). What can you conclude about \(c\)?

Answer

Nothing yet. \(x^4\) (minimum), \(-x^4\) (maximum) and \(x^3\) (neither, but an inflection point) all have \(f'(0)=f''(0)=0\). Use the First Derivative Test (sign of \(f'\) on each side) instead.

C2. Can a function be decreasing and concave up at the same time? Give an example.

Answer

Yes. \(e^{-x}\): \(f'=-e^{-x}\lt0\) (decreasing) and \(f''=e^{-x}\gt0\) (concave up). It falls, but more and more gently. Monotonicity is about \(f'\)'s sign; concavity is about \(f'\)'s direction of change.

C3. True or false: if \(f'\) is increasing on \(I\), then \(f\) is increasing on \(I\).

Answer

False. \(f'\) increasing means \(f\) is concave up. \(f(x)=x^2\) on \((-1,1)\): \(f'(x)=2x\) is increasing, but \(f\) decreases on \((-1,0)\).

C4. \(f(x)=\dfrac1{x^2}\) is increasing on \((-\infty,0)\) and decreasing on \((0,\infty)\). Does it have a local maximum at 0?

Answer

No. \(0\) is not in the domain, so there is no point \((0,f(0))\) to be a maximum (the graph shoots up to \(+\infty\)). The First Derivative Test needs \(f\) continuous at a critical point \(c\) in the domain.

C5. In the soup can problem the domain is \(r\gt0\). Why can't you just use the Closed Interval Method, and what replaces it?

Answer

\((0,\infty)\) is not a closed interval, so the EVT does not guarantee a minimum and there are no endpoints to check. Instead: there is only one critical point, \(S'\lt0\) before it and \(S'\gt0\) after it, so \(S\) decreases all the way to it and increases all the way after it, making it the absolute minimum.

C6. Two functions \(f\) and \(f+5\) have the same graph of \(f'\). What does that tell you about questions of the type "given the graph of \(f'\), sketch \(f\)"?

Answer

The graph of \(f'\) fixes the shape of \(f\) (where it rises, falls and bends) but not its height: any vertical shift has the same derivative. That is why such questions add a condition like "\(f(0)=0\)" or "\(f(-2)=0\)" to pin down one particular graph.

C7. At an inflection point, must the tangent line be horizontal?

Answer

No. At the inflection point of \(y=x^3-6x^2+9x+1\) (Group work 2(a)) at \((2,3)\), the slope is \(y'(2)=-3\). Horizontal tangents at inflection points (like \(x^3\) at 0) are a special case. The tangent can even be vertical (\(x^{1/3}\) at 0).
