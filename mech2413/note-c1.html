Sign convention and units for this chapter
  • Error \(e(t)=r(t)-y_{\rm m}(t)\): target minus measured output. \(e\gt0\) means the output is below the target.
  • Water-tank case study: level \(h\) and target \(r\) in cm, time in s. The pump command \(u\) is a fraction of full pump effort (0 to 1; \(u=0.23\) means 23%). The pump cannot run backwards, so \(u\) never goes below 0.
  • Gains then carry units: \(K_{\rm p}\) in cm⁻¹, \(K_{\rm i}\) in (cm·s)⁻¹, \(K_{\rm d}\) in s·cm⁻¹, so that every term of \(u\) is dimensionless.
  • Response curves: output on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal; rise time is measured 10% → 90% and settling uses a ±2% band (slide 15).

1Control in everyday life and engineering (slides 2–4) ~3 min

What is this?

You are driving and want to hold a steady speed. You glance at the speedometer, see you are a little slow, and press the pedal a bit more; a moment later you look again. That is control: a value you want, a reading of what you actually have, and an action that closes the gap. A thermostat, a welding robot and an autopilot all do the same thing with machines instead of a driver.

The handout opens with three applications. Each has the same three ingredients: a desired condition, a measured result, and an action that changes the result (slide 4).

ApplicationDesired conditionActionWhat upsets it
Room temperature (slide 2, a smart thermostat)a setpoint of 24 °C; a sensor reports the present temperatureheating or cooling, which changes the temperature gradually: the output does not jump to the targetdoors opening, sunlight, people in the room change the load
Robot motion (slide 3, welding robots)reach a position or follow a path at a required speedmotors and transmissions; position sensors report what the robot actually doesa heavier payload, friction, or contact with a part
Flight conditions (slide 4, a cockpit)hold an altitude (stay near a setpoint) or move to a new onecontrol surfaces (flaps, elevators) or thrustwind gusts; the action must also respect speed and load limits
What is this? (setpoint, regulation, tracking)

Holding the same speed mile after mile on a motorway, while hills and wind try to change it, is regulation. Speeding up smoothly when the speed limit rises, so that your speed follows a changing target, is tracking. The speed you are aiming for at any moment is the setpoint.

Definitions · setpoint, regulation, tracking
  • Setpoint: the desired value of the controlled quantity (24 °C, 40 cm).
  • Regulation: keeping the output near a fixed setpoint despite disturbances (holding altitude).
  • Tracking: making the output follow a changing target (a new altitude command, a robot path).

Key idea (slide 2): the controlled result is the room temperature, not whether the heater is switched on. A command being sent is not the same as the goal being reached (slide 3).

You can now: name the desired condition, the measured result and the action in an everyday control system; and tell regulation (hold a setpoint) from tracking (follow a changing target).

2The control task: target, input and output (slide 5) ~2 min

What is this?

In the car, the target is the speed you have in mind, the output is the speed the car actually has, and the input is the pedal: the only thing you can directly change. You cannot set the speed itself; you can only press the pedal and let the car respond. The pedal also has limits: it only goes down so far, and you cannot slam it instantly.

Control means selecting and applying actions so that a system behaves in a desired way. Four words describe every control task:

Definitions · the control task
  • Target \(r\) (reference): the desired quantity and value, e.g. a speed of 80 km/h, or a prescribed motion trajectory.
  • Output \(y\): the actual quantity being controlled (speed, temperature, position). It may differ from the target.
  • Input \(u\): the adjustable action we can apply (torque, cooling rate, voltage). Input and output often have different units: a voltage changes a speed.
  • Constraints: physical limits on the input's size, its rate of change, and the operating range. The controller must respect them.

In words: the input is the knob we can turn; the output is the result we want to change or hold; the target is where we want the output to be.

Example · written for these notes · car cruise control

Target \(r=80\u{km/h}\); output \(y\) = actual road speed (km/h); input \(u\) = throttle (engine torque demand, a fraction of full throttle); constraint: \(0\le u\le1\), and throttle cannot change instantly; disturbances: hills and headwind (Section 3).

You can now: identify the target \(r\), input \(u\), output \(y\) and constraints of a control task; and explain why input and output can have different units.

3Four roles in a control system; disturbances (slides 6–7) ~3 min

What is this?

Split the driving job into four jobs. Your brain decides how hard to press (controller). The engine and throttle act on that decision (actuator). The car itself responds by speeding up or slowing down (plant). The speedometer reports what happened (sensor), and your eyes carry that back to your brain.

A closed loop carries physical actions forward and measurement information back (slide 6):

The controller decides, the actuator acts, the plant responds, and the sensor reports the result (slide 6).

Definitions · the four roles
  • Controller: calculates the action from the information it receives (a computer program, a circuit, or a person).
  • Actuator: the device that applies the controller's command to the plant (a motor, a pump, a heater, a valve).
  • Plant: the system being controlled (the room, the robot arm, the aircraft, the tank of water).
  • Sensor: measures the output and returns the measurement (thermometer, encoder, level gauge).

Action path: controller → actuator → plant. Feedback: sensor → back to the controller, to guide the next action.

What is this? (disturbances)

Keep the pedal exactly where it is and drive onto an uphill stretch: the car slows down, although you changed nothing. The hill is a disturbance. A full car of passengers behaves differently from an empty one (uncertainty), and a speedometer that reads a little high misleads you (measurement problems).

Slide 7: the same input can give different results when conditions or system properties change.

DisturbanceAn outside effect that pushes the output away: an uphill road or headwind increases resistance, so the car slows if the drive force stays the same.
UncertaintyThe real plant differs from the model: the load, friction or efficiency are not what we assumed.
Measurement problemsNoise (random jitter) or bias (a constant offset) make the measured output differ from the true output, and so mislead the controller.
Two tasksA new speed target tests tracking. A changed load at the same target tests disturbance rejection.

In words: a changed target and a changed environment are two different tests of a controller (slide 7); Section 18 runs both on the water tank.

You can now: name the controller, actuator, plant and sensor of a loop and trace the action and feedback paths; and classify an upset as a disturbance, an uncertainty or a measurement problem.

4Open-loop control: following a preset plan (slides 8–9) ~2 min

What is this?

Open loop is driving with your eyes off the speedometer: you put the pedal at the position that "usually" gives the right speed and leave it there. On a flat, calm road that works. On a hill, or with a headwind, the speed drifts and nothing in your plan notices.

Definition · open-loop control

The input is decided by a preset time, program or model, and the output is never used to correct it. Slide 8's example: run a fan for a fixed time, then stop, without looking at air-quality measurements.

Open loop (slide 8): the chain runs forward only.

It suits tasks where conditions are predictable. Slide 9 weighs it up:

Potential benefitsImportant limits
A simple structure, easy to implement and inspect, especially for a fixed sequence of actions.An unexpected disturbance can make the planned input unsuitable for the desired result.
No continuous output measurement is needed, which can reduce cost.It relies on its assumptions: output error does not correct the action while it runs.
Repeatable results when the initial conditions, environment and load are consistent.Knowing that a command was sent does not prove that the desired result was achieved.

In words: open loop is "do the plan and hope". Whether that is acceptable depends on how predictable the conditions are and how much error the task can tolerate (slide 9). A washing machine's timer is fine; landing an aircraft is not.

You can now: define open-loop control; and give its benefits (simple, cheap, repeatable) and its limits (blind to disturbances and wrong assumptions).

5Feedback and the closed loop (slides 10–11) ~3 min

What is this?

Feedback is looking at the speedometer and letting what you see change your foot. Seeing that you are slow and pressing harder is feedback. Merely noticing the number, or a dashcam recording it, is not: the reading has to change the action.

Definition · feedback control

Feedback means the measured output is used to decide the next action (slide 10). It is a repeated cycle:

  1. Do: Measure the current output (e.g. vehicle speed) and check the reading is credible. A bad reading would steer the whole loop wrong, so the controller is only as good as its sensor.
  2. Do: Compare it with the target to find the direction and size of the error. The sign says whether to press or ease off; the size says how much.
  3. Do: Adjust the action, observe the new result, and repeat. The car takes time to respond, so one correction is rarely exactly right; repeating lets each new reading fix what the last one missed.

Displaying or storing a reading is only monitoring. It becomes feedback control only when the reading changes the input.

Slide 11 draws the standard closed-loop block diagram. The circle with Σ is a summing junction: it adds its inputs with the signs shown, so here it outputs \(r-y_{\rm m}\), the error.

Closed loop (slide 11). Forward path: the actuator applies the command and the plant responds over time. Feedback path: the returned measurement influences the next action.

\[e(t)=r(t)-y_{\rm m}(t)\]
Reading the formula
\(r(t)\)
the speed you want right now.
\(y_{\rm m}(t)\)
the speed the speedometer shows: the measured output, which can differ from the true speed if the sensor is noisy or biased.
\(e(t)\)
how far short you are. Positive means too slow, so press harder; negative means too fast, so ease off.
the minus sign
this is the "−" at the summing junction: the measurement is subtracted from the target.

In words: error = target − measured output. The controller sees only this error (in the basic structure), and turns it into the input \(u\).

The loop defines the connections; it does not say how to compute \(u\) from \(e\). That rule is the control law, and PID is one possible control law (slide 11).

Beyond the handouts · block diagrams

A block diagram shows signals as arrows and processes as boxes; each box turns its input signal into its output signal. Engineers write the rule inside each box as a transfer function (using the Laplace transform), so the whole loop can be analysed algebraically. MECH2413 does not use transfer functions; you only need to read and draw the diagram.

You can now: explain the difference between monitoring and feedback; draw and read the closed-loop block diagram; and compute the error \(e=r-y_{\rm m}\) with the right sign.

6Negative feedback: acting to reduce error (slide 12) ~2 min

What is this?

Negative feedback means pushing against the error: too slow, press harder; too fast, ease off. A shower is the classic warning. The water takes a few seconds to reach you, so if you turn the tap hard every time it feels wrong, you overshoot to too hot, then too cold, and keep swinging. The direction of each correction was right; its size and timing were not.

Definition · negative feedback

The measured output is subtracted from the target (the "−" at the summing junction), and the action is chosen to reduce the error.

Below targetTarget 80 km/h, reading 75 km/h: \(e=80-75=+5\u{km/h}\). Increase the action that raises speed.
Above targetReading 85 km/h: \(e=-5\u{km/h}\). Reduce drive effort, or brake if the actuator permits it.
Direction and timeThe action should reduce error, but too much gain, delay, or bad measurements can still spoil the response.
Watch

Negative feedback fixes the correcting direction, but it does not guarantee stability (slide 12). Correct too hard or too late and the output overshoots, then over-corrects the other way, and can oscillate with growing size. Think of an over-eager shower user swinging the tap between too hot and too cold.

Beyond the handouts · stability

A closed loop is stable if, after a change of target or a disturbance, the output settles to a steady value instead of oscillating forever or running away. Whether a loop is stable depends on the plant and on the gains together.

You can now: state what makes feedback negative; work out the sign of the error and the direction of the correction; and explain why negative feedback does not guarantee stability.

7Open loop versus closed loop (slides 13–14) ~2 min

What is this?

Two separate questions about the car. Who works the pedal: you, or a cruise-control computer? And does whoever works it look at the speed? A careful human driver watching the speedometer is a closed loop. A computer replaying a fixed pedal plan is an open loop, however automatic it is.

Ready table · open vs closed loop (slide 13)
FeatureOpen loopClosed loop
Decision basispreset program or modeltarget and feedback information
Output feedbackdoes not correct the inputinfluences later input decisions
Changing conditionscan shift the result from the targetcan trigger a corrective response
Main requirementassumptions match the taskreliable sensing and suitable dynamics
Typical costlimited adaptation to unknown changesmore sensing, computation and tuning

The test: is a measurement of the controlled output used to correct the input? Yes → closed loop. No → open loop.

8Reading a response curve (slide 15) ~3 min

What is this?

The speed limit goes up and you set a new target speed. Afterwards, grade how you did: how quickly you got most of the way there (rise time); how far past the new limit you went before coming back (overshoot); how long until you stayed close to it for good (settling time); and how far short you still were at the end (steady-state error). A response curve is just the speed plotted against time, so you can read off all four.

To compare controllers we change the target in one step and plot the output against time: the step response. A useful response reaches the target promptly, stays near it, and avoids large excursions.

A typical step response (written for these notes; final value 1)

Dashed: the target (here equal to the final value) and the ±2% band. Green ticks: the 10% and 90% crossing times. The curve overshoots (red dot), swings below the target (orange dot), then settles.

Definitions · what to measure (slide 15)
  • Rise time: the time to go from 10% to 90% of the final change. (Other conventions exist, e.g. 0–100%, so state yours.)
  • Overshoot: the peak excursion above the final value, usually given as a percentage of the change: \[\text{overshoot}=\frac{y_{\rm peak}-y_\infty}{y_\infty-y_0}\times100\%.\]
    Reading the formula
    \(y_{\rm peak}-y_\infty\)
    how far past the speed you finally settled at you went.
    \(y_\infty-y_0\)
    how much the speed had to change in total, from start to finish.
    \(\times100\%\)
    a percentage, so a big change and a small change can be compared fairly.
    \(y_\infty\), not \(r\)
    measured from the final value. If the output settles short of the target, the overshoot is still measured past where it settled.

    In words: how far past the end value the output went, compared with how far it had to travel.

  • Settling time: the time after which the output enters a chosen band around the final value (e.g. ±2%) and stays there.
  • Steady-state error: what is left at the end, \[e_{\rm ss}=r-y_\infty.\]

    In words: target minus final output, once everything has stopped changing.

Watch

State the rise-time convention and the settling band before comparing controllers (slide 15). "Settles at 150 s" means nothing without "±2%".

You can now: define and read rise time (10–90%), overshoot, settling time (±2% band) and steady-state error from a step response; and compute the overshoot as a percentage of the change.

9Case study: holding the water level at 40 cm (slides 16–18) ~6 min

What is this?

The rest of the handout swaps the car for a tank of water, which is easier to picture. Think of a bath with the plug pulled out: the tap is the pump (the action), the plug hole is the outlet (always draining), and your eye on the water line is the level sensor. The job is to work the tap so the water stays at one height.

The rest of the handout uses one apparatus (slide 16): a tank of water, a variable-speed pump that fills it, an outlet valve that drains it, and a level sensor. The task: adjust the inlet flow so that the water level approaches 40 cm and recovers after outlet changes.

The apparatus (slide 16). The controller drives the pump; the level sensor reports the level; opening the outlet valve wider is a disturbance.

  • The level sensor reports the result; the controller adjusts the pump through its drive.
  • The start switch only enables operation. Opening the outlet valve creates a disturbance.
  • The pump only supplies water: it cannot pump water out. After it stops, the outlet can still lower the level.

Key idea (slide 16): the program changes the pump command; the level changes only through the balance of inflow and outflow.

Water level depends on both flows (slide 17)

What is this? (flow balance)

The water in the tank is like money in a bank account. Inflow is deposits, outflow is spending. The balance rises when you deposit more than you spend, falls when you spend more, and stays put only when the two are equal. The level is the balance spread over the floor area of the tank, so a wide tank shows the same change as a smaller rise.

Result · flow balance (slide 17) \[A\frac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}=q_{\rm in}-q_{\rm out}\]

\(A\) = tank cross-sectional area (cm²), \(h\) = level (cm), \(q_{\rm in},q_{\rm out}\) = volume flow rates in and out (cm³/s).

Reading the formula
\(A\dfrac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}\)
how fast the stored volume grows: level speed times floor area.
\(q_{\rm in}\)
the tap: what the pump puts in per second.
\(q_{\rm out}\)
the plug hole: what drains out per second. In this model it grows with the level.
\(q_{\rm in}-q_{\rm out}\)
the net flow. Zero net flow means a steady level, even with the pump running.

In words: the rate at which the stored volume \(Ah\) grows equals what comes in minus what goes out. Dimension check: cm² × cm/s = cm³/s ✓.

  • Flow balance: the level rises when inflow exceeds outflow; equal flows hold the level constant.
  • Accumulation: a larger cross-section \(A\) gives a slower level change for the same net flow.
  • Continuing outflow: in this teaching model, outflow increases with the level. So holding a level still needs inflow.

Analogy: a bath with the plug pulled out. To keep the water at one height you must leave the tap running exactly as fast as the water drains. A constant level does not mean a stopped pump (slide 17).

Beyond the handouts · the model used for the simulations on this page

The handout's curves (slides 24–31) come from a simulation whose details are not given. Slide 25 does give its steady state: \(h_\infty=(80\u{cm})\,u_\infty\), so with the outlet as set, a steady command \(u\) holds a level of \(80u\) cm (outflow proportional to level). The curves on this page use:

\[\begin{aligned}\tau_{\rm t}\frac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}&=(80\u{cm})\,q-h,\\ \tau_{\rm p}\frac{\mathrm dq}{\mathrm dt}&=u-q,\quad 0\le u\le1,\end{aligned}\]

with tank time constant \(\tau_{\rm t}=80\u{s}\), pump lag \(\tau_{\rm p}=30\u{s}\) (\(q\) = pump flow as a fraction of full flow), starting from \(h=20\u{cm}\), \(q=0\).

In words: the first line is slide 17's flow balance divided through by the outflow constant; the second says the pump takes about 30 s to respond to a new command. A tank alone (no pump lag) could never overshoot under P control, but slide 24 shows a small overshoot, so the plant must have a second lag. These two time constants were chosen to reproduce the handout's curves (P settles at 30.48 cm; PI peaks near 44 cm at about 110 s); they are a reconstruction, not the lecturer's model.

Closing the water-level loop (slide 18)

The tank loop (slide 18): \(e(t)=r(t)-h_{\rm m}(t)\). Compare the measured level with the target to find the error; adjust the inflow; use the next measurement to decide again.

The loop is closed only when the level measurement actually influences the pump command (slide 18).

You can now: describe the tank apparatus and its loop; use \(A\,\mathrm dh/\mathrm dt=q_{\rm in}-q_{\rm out}\) to say when the level rises, falls or holds; and explain why holding a level needs a running pump.

10One cycle of feedback control; designing the controller (slides 19–20) ~2 min

What is this?

Each glance at the speedometer is one cycle: read the speed, see how far off you are, decide how much to move your foot, then wait a moment and look again. The tank does exactly the same with a level sensor and a pump. The only open question is the "decide" step: what rule turns "8 cm low" into a pump setting?

Target 40 cm, sensor reads 32 cm. At each update (slide 19):

  1. Do: Measure: read 32 cm and check the reading is credible. Everything after this uses the reading, so a faulty gauge would make every later step wrong.
  2. Do: Compare: \(e=40-32=+8\u{cm}\). The level is 8 cm below target: a positive error. This gives the direction, but not yet the pump command. The comparison tells you the tank needs more water, but not how hard to run the pump to get it.
  3. Do: Calculate: apply a control law to decide how much water the pump should supply. This is the step that turns "how wrong" into "what to do"; PID is the rule used from Section 11 on.
  4. Do: Observe again: let the process evolve, read the new level, update the action. The level responds gradually, so the right command changes as it rises; only a fresh reading shows what is needed now.

In words: feedback says how information returns; the control law says what to do with it (slide 19).

Slide 20 poses the design question: the controller receives "level error 8 cm" and must output "pump command ?%". Should it use only the current error, or also how the error changes over time? The answer is the control law, and the rest of the handout uses PID.

You can now: walk through one measure–compare–calculate–observe cycle with numbers; and explain what a control law adds to the loop.

11The PID control law (slides 21–23) ~4 min

What is this?

A good driver asks three questions at once. How far below the target speed am I now? (P, the present error.) Have I been below it for a long time, for example all the way up a long hill? (I, the accumulated past error.) Is the speed already climbing fast towards the target, so I should ease off soon? (D, where the error is heading.) PID adds up the three answers, each with its own weight, to decide the pedal position.

PID stands for Proportional–Integral–Derivative. Each letter uses the error information in a different way (slide 21). The handout explains it as a ship's helmsman, following Nicolas Minorsky's 1922 mathematical study of automatic ship steering, an early milestone in PID control:

P · current errorHow far are we off course now?
I · persistent errorWhat correction must be maintained (e.g. to hold against a steady side-current)?
D · rate of changeHow quickly are we already turning towards the target?
Result · the basic PID control law (slide 22) \[u(t)=K_{\rm p}\,e(t)+K_{\rm i}\int_0^t e(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau+K_{\rm d}\,\frac{\mathrm de(t)}{\mathrm dt}\] \[\begin{aligned}u(t)&=K_{\rm p}\,e(t)+K_{\rm i}\int_0^t e(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau\\ &\quad+K_{\rm d}\,\frac{\mathrm de(t)}{\mathrm dt}\end{aligned}\]
  • \(e\): error, target minus measured level.
  • \(u\): control command sent to the pump.
  • \(K_{\rm p}\): proportional gain, scales the current error.
  • \(K_{\rm i}\): integral gain, scales the accumulated error. (\(\tau\) is just the integration variable, running over past times from 0 to now.)
  • \(K_{\rm d}\): derivative gain, scales the error rate.

In words: command = (gain × how wrong we are now) + (gain × how wrong we have been, added up over time) + (gain × how fast the error is changing). Each gain sets the strength of one action; setting a gain to zero removes that term.

Reading the formula
\(K_{\rm p}\,e(t)\)
the present: push in proportion to how far below target you are right now.
\(K_{\rm i}\int_0^t e\,\mathrm d\tau\)
the past: a running total of the error over time. A small error that lasts a long time builds up a big total.
\(K_{\rm d}\,\dfrac{\mathrm de}{\mathrm dt}\)
the trend: how fast the error is changing. Closing in quickly makes this negative, so it eases off early.
\(K_{\rm p},K_{\rm i},K_{\rm d}\)
how strongly the driver listens to each of the three questions.
\(u(t)\)
the pedal (here the pump command): the three contributions simply add.

Inside the basic PID controller (slide 23): all three branches use the same error; their outputs add to give the pump command.

P, PI, PD and PID are the same loop with different branches switched on: PI means \(K_{\rm d}=0\), PD means \(K_{\rm i}=0\), and so on (slide 23).

Beyond the handouts · discrete updates

A computer updates the command every \(\Delta t\) seconds, so the integral becomes a running sum and the derivative a difference:

\[\begin{aligned}I_k&=I_{k-1}+K_{\rm i}\,e_k\,\Delta t,\\ u_k&=K_{\rm p}e_k+I_k+K_{\rm d}\,\frac{e_k-e_{k-1}}{\Delta t}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: add this step's error × time step to the stored total; estimate the slope from the last two errors. This is exactly how the simulations on this page work (\(\Delta t=0.05\u{s}\)).

You can now: state the PID law and what each term and gain means; explain P, I and D with the helmsman (or driver) picture; and say which gains are zero in P, PI and PD.

12P: act on the current error (slides 24–25) ~3 min

What is this?

A P-only driver presses the pedal in proportion to how far below the target speed the car is: twice as slow, twice the extra pedal. Far below, the push is strong; as the speed comes up, the push fades. It reacts only to the present error, with no memory of the past and no look ahead.

Result · proportional action (slide 24) \[u_{\rm P}(t)=K_{\rm p}\,e(t),\qquad e(t)=r(t)-h_{\rm m}(t)\]

In words: the command is proportional to how far below the target the level is now. Twice the error, twice the pumping.

Slide 24's numbers: \(K_{\rm p}=0.04\u{cm^{-1}}\), \(e=8\u{cm}\Rightarrow u_{\rm P}=0.04\times8=0.32\) (32% pump effort). Units: cm⁻¹ × cm = dimensionless ✓.

P control, \(K_{\rm p}=0.04\u{cm^{-1}}\), from 20 cm (re-simulated, cf. slide 24)

Ptarget

Fast reaction, then a final offset: the level settles near 30.48 cm, below the 40 cm target.

Why can pure P stop below the target? (slide 25)

What is this? (the P offset)

Even on a flat road a car needs some steady pedal to hold its speed, because drag and friction keep slowing it. A P-only driver presses only when the car is below the target. So at exactly the target speed this driver would lift off completely, and the car would slow. The car settles a little below the target, at the speed where the shortfall makes the driver press just hard enough to beat the drag. The tank is the same: the outlet is the drag, and the level settles where the error keeps the pump running just fast enough.

Holding a level needs inflow

The open outlet continuously removes water. At 40 cm the pump must replace the outgoing water. But with no bias, zero error gives zero proportional command: at 40 cm a P controller would switch the pump off.

So it settles lower

A positive error is needed to keep the pump running. At equilibrium, inflow equals outflow at a command of about 38.1%. A larger \(K_{\rm p}\) reduces the offset but can worsen the transient.

Worked example · slide 25 · the P-only equilibrium

At equilibrium nothing changes, so the plant gives \(h_\infty=(80\u{cm})\,u_\infty\), and the controller gives \(u_\infty=K_{\rm p}(r-h_\infty)\): The settled level must satisfy two rules at once: the tank's balance (inflow = outflow) and the controller's rule for the command. Solving them together finds the one level that fits both.

\[\begin{aligned}h_\infty&=(80\u{cm})(0.04\u{cm^{-1}})\\ &\quad\times(40\u{cm}-h_\infty)\\ &=3.2\,(40\u{cm}-h_\infty)\\ \Rightarrow\ 4.2\,h_\infty&=128\u{cm}\\ h_\infty&=\boxed{30.48\u{cm}},\\ u_\infty&=\frac{30.48}{80}=\boxed{0.381\ (38.1\%)}.\end{aligned}\]

Check: \(K_{\rm p}e_\infty=0.04\times9.52=0.381\) ✓. Steady-state error \(e_{\rm ss}=9.52\u{cm}\).

Beyond the handouts · the general offset formula

Write \(G=80\u{cm}\) for the plant's steady-state gain (level per unit command) and \(K=GK_{\rm p}\) for the loop gain. The same algebra gives

\[h_\infty=\frac{K}{1+K}\,r,\qquad e_{\rm ss}=\frac{r}{1+K}.\]

In words: the offset shrinks as the loop gain grows but never reaches zero for finite \(K_{\rm p}\). A stronger driver needs a smaller shortfall to press hard enough, but still needs some shortfall to press at all. With \(K_{\rm p}=0.08\u{cm^{-1}}\): \(K=6.4\), \(h_\infty=34.59\u{cm}\), \(e_{\rm ss}=5.41\u{cm}\).

Key idea: this result depends on a continuing outlet flow and a P controller with no bias (slide 25). A tank with no outlet would reach 40 cm under P alone, because zero inflow would then be exactly right.

You can now: compute a P command \(u_{\rm P}=K_{\rm p}e\) with units; explain why P alone leaves an offset when the outlet keeps draining; and find the P-only steady level and command by solving the plant and controller relations together.

13I: accumulate a persistent error (slide 26) ~3 min

What is this?

Driving up a long hill, you notice you have been a little slow for a while, so you press a bit further, and a bit further, and you keep the pedal there. That memory of past shortfall is the I term. It is what lets you hold the target exactly: once you are at the speed, the extra pedal you built up stays, even though the error is now zero. The catch: if you build up too much on the way, you sail past the target before the extra pedal is taken back.

Result · integral action (slide 26) \[I(t)=I(0)+K_{\rm i}\int_0^t e(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau\]
Reading the formula
\(I(0)\)
what was already stored when you started.
\(\int_0^t e\,\mathrm d\tau\)
the area under the error curve: error × time, added up. 2 cm low for 50 s adds as much as 1 cm low for 100 s.
\(K_{\rm i}\)
how fast the store fills for a given error.
\(e\lt0\) periods
add negative area, so the store shrinks: this is the "unwinding" after an overshoot.

In words: the integral term is a running total. While the error stays positive the total keeps growing, so the pump is pushed harder and harder until the error is gone. When \(e=0\) the total stops growing but keeps its value.

P versus PI: \(K_{\rm p}=0.04\u{cm^{-1}}\), \(K_{\rm i}=0.0006\u{(cm\,s)^{-1}}\) (re-simulated, cf. slide 26)

PPItarget

First the final error: PI reaches 40 cm while P stays at 30.48 cm. Then the transient: these PI gains overshoot before settling.

  • PI removes the offset. At 40 cm the P term is zero, but the stored integral can still hold the command at the 50% needed (\(u=40/80=0.5\)).
  • Zero error stops further accumulation; it does not erase the stored integral (slide 26). That stored value is what keeps the pump running at zero error.
  • The price: the integral is still growing as the level passes 40 cm, so the level overshoots, and the integral must "unwind" (shrink during negative error) before the level settles.

Key idea (slide 26): for a stable loop with a reachable target, I can remove a constant steady-state error.

Beyond the handouts · integral windup

If the actuator is at its limit (pump at 100%) and the error stays large, the integral keeps growing even though the pump cannot do more. When the error finally changes sign, this oversized total takes a long time to unwind, causing a big overshoot. This is integral windup; practical controllers stop integrating while the actuator is saturated ("anti-windup"). It is also why a target that is not reachable (slide 33, test B) is a problem for I.

You can now: explain how the integral term builds up and why it keeps its value at zero error; say why PI removes the offset and why it tends to overshoot; and describe integral windup.

14D: respond to how fast the error changes (slide 27) ~2 min

What is this?

D looks at where the error is heading. If the speed is climbing fast towards the target, a good driver lifts off a little before reaching it, because the car will keep gaining speed for a moment. If the speed is dropping fast, the driver presses early. D does nothing once the speed is steady, because then nothing is changing.

Result · derivative action (slide 27) \[\begin{aligned}u_{\rm D}(t)&=K_{\rm d}\frac{\mathrm de}{\mathrm dt}\\ &=-K_{\rm d}\frac{\mathrm dh_{\rm m}}{\mathrm dt}\quad(r\ \text{const.})\end{aligned}\]
Reading the formula
\(\dfrac{\mathrm de}{\mathrm dt}\)
how fast the error is changing: negative when you are closing in on the target.
\(K_{\rm d}\)
how strongly you react to that trend.
\(-\dfrac{\mathrm dh_{\rm m}}{\mathrm dt}\)
with a fixed target the error shrinks exactly as fast as the level rises, hence the minus sign.
\(u_{\rm D}\lt0\)
level rising: D takes some command away, easing off before the target.

In words: with a fixed target, \(e=r-h_{\rm m}\), so the error falls exactly as fast as the level rises. A rising level gives a negative D term that eases off the pump before the target is reached, like braking as you approach a stop line.

P versus PD: same \(K_{\rm p}\), \(K_{\rm d}=0.50\u{s\,cm^{-1}}\) (re-simulated, cf. slide 27)

PPDtarget

PD reduces the swing around the final level. Both settle near 30.48 cm: at constant level the derivative term is zero.

Key idea (slide 27): D changes the transient (the path to the end); it does not remove this tank's steady-state offset, because at steady state \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt=0\) and D contributes nothing.

Beyond the handouts · derivative kick and noise

If the target jumps suddenly, \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt=\mathrm dr/\mathrm dt\) is huge for an instant and the D term "kicks" the actuator. Many controllers therefore differentiate only the measurement, \(-K_{\rm d}\,\mathrm dh_{\rm m}/\mathrm dt\), which is the form slide 27 uses. Differentiating a noisy measurement amplifies the noise, so D is usually combined with a filter.

You can now: compute a D term with the correct sign from \(\mathrm dh_{\rm m}/\mathrm dt\); explain why D damps the transient; and explain why D cannot remove a steady-state offset.

What is this?

Put the three habits together in one driver. I makes sure you end up exactly at the target speed; D stops you overshooting on the way; P gives the main push. Whether the combination is better than PI alone depends on the car and on how strongly each habit is used. To compare drivers fairly, give them the same car, the same road and the same starting speed.

16What happens when a gain increases? (slide 29) ~2 min

What is this?

Back in the shower. Turn the tap hard for every small error (big \(K_{\rm p}\)) and you reach a good temperature fast, but swing past it. Remember every past error too eagerly (big \(K_{\rm i}\)) and you keep turning long after you should stop. React strongly to how fast it is changing (big \(K_{\rm d}\)) and you calm the swings, but any flicker in the water makes you jerk the tap. Each habit helps in moderation and hurts in excess.

Change one gain at a time and compare the complete response, including the control effort \(u\) (slide 29).

Ready table · effect of increasing one gain (slide 29)

In words: P = strength, I = persistence, D = anticipation. Each helps up to a point and then hurts.

Watch

These are useful tendencies, not universal laws: the plant dynamics and the other gains also matter (slide 29). In an exam, say "typically" or "tends to", and give the trade-off.

17Worked example: calculate the pump command (slide 30) ~2 min

What is this?

The level is close to the target and rising. Three voices give advice. P says "still a little low: push a bit". I says "keep the push we have built up". D says "we are rising: ease off so we do not overshoot". The pump command is the three pieces of advice added together.

Worked example · slide 30

Given the current state and gains: \(r=40\u{cm}\), \(h_{\rm m}=38\u{cm}\), integral contribution \(I=0.25\), \(K_{\rm p}=0.04\u{cm^{-1}}\), \(K_{\rm d}=0.50\u{s\,cm^{-1}}\). The target is fixed and the level is rising at \(\mathrm dh_{\rm m}/\mathrm dt=+0.20\u{cm\,s^{-1}}\). Find the pump command.

The signal path is the PID diagram of Section 11: one error feeds three branches whose outputs add.

  1. Do: Error: \(e=r-h_{\rm m}=40-38=2\u{cm}\). Target minus measurement, so a positive value means the level is still below target.
  2. Do: P term: \(u_{\rm P}=K_{\rm p}e=0.04\u{cm^{-1}}\times2\u{cm}=0.08\). A small push, because the level is only a little low.
  3. Do: D term: the target is fixed, so \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt=-\mathrm dh_{\rm m}/\mathrm dt=-0.20\u{cm\,s^{-1}}\), and \[\begin{aligned}u_{\rm D}&=0.50\u{s\,cm^{-1}}\\ &\quad\times(-0.20\u{cm\,s^{-1}})\\ &=-0.10.\end{aligned}\] The level is rising, so the error is shrinking: D is negative and eases off before the target.
  4. Do: Add the given I contribution: \[u=0.08+0.25-0.10=0.23.\] The three branches act on the same error and their outputs add (Section 11); I is given as a ready-made contribution, so it is added as it is.
  5. Do: Units: every term is dimensionless (cm⁻¹·cm; s·cm⁻¹·cm·s⁻¹), as a fraction of full pump effort must be ✓. If a term had units left over, a gain or a sign would have been used wrongly.

\(\boxed{u=0.23\ \text{(23% pump effort)}}\)

In words: a negative D term reduces inflow; it does not reverse the pump (slide 30). The level is close and rising, so D eases off to avoid overshooting.

You can now: compute a PID command from given readings, with the correct sign of the D term and a units check; and explain what each term is doing.

18Two simple tests of the same controller (slide 33) ~2 min

What is this?

Two ways to test a driver. Raise the speed limit and see how well they follow the new target (tracking). Or keep the limit and send them up a hill, and see how well they hold the speed (disturbance rejection). Change only one thing at a time, or you cannot tell which change caused what.

The method (slide 33):

  1. Do: Keep the plant and the gains unchanged. Then any difference in the response comes from the condition you changed, not from the controller.
  2. Do: change one condition at a time. Two changes at once would mix their effects, and you could not say which caused what.
  3. Do: record both the water level and the pump command (slide 33). The command shows what the controller is doing and whether the pump has hit its 100% limit, which the level alone cannot show.
Test A · change the target (tracking)

Raise the target gradually from 40 cm to 45 cm. Predict how the error and the pump command change before drawing the level response; then record rise time, overshoot, final error and the command.

Test B · open the outlet valve (disturbance rejection)

Keep the target at 40 cm and increase the outflow. Predict the response of each PID term. Check whether the pump can supply enough water to restore the target.

Predictions, term by term

Test A. As the target rises, \(e\) becomes positive: P raises the command at once; the ramp also makes \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt\gt0\) so D adds a boost; I slowly builds up to the new steady command, \(45/80=0.5625\) (it was 0.5). Final error zero.

Test B. The level starts to fall, so \(e\gt0\) and \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt\gt0\): P and D both raise the pump command immediately; I keeps growing until the level is back at 40 cm, where the extra outflow needs \(u=1.25\times0.5=0.625\). Since that is below 1, the pump can restore the target. If the outlet opened so wide that \(u\) would have to exceed 1, the target would be unreachable: the level settles lower and the integral winds up.

19Check your understanding (slide 34) ~1 min

What is this?

Before answering, run the picture in your head. Is anyone looking at the speedometer? At the end, when the speed is steady, which of the driver's three habits is still pressing the pedal? Each question below is answered by tracing the loop and asking what balances what, not by memorising.

Slide 34 asks four questions. Explain the signal path and the physical balance before choosing a gain to change.

1 · A timer runs the pump without reading the level. Does automatic operation make this a closed loop?

Answer

No. It is open loop: the level (the controlled output) never influences the pump command. Automatic only says that a device, not a person, makes the decision (slide 14).

2 · P and PD both settle below the target. Why does D fail to remove the final offset?

Answer

At steady state the level is constant, so \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt=0\) and the D term is zero. The command is then just \(K_{\rm p}e\), which must be about 0.381 to balance the outflow, so \(e\) must stay at about 9.52 cm, exactly as with P alone.

3 · The level is 40 cm while the pump keeps running. Which term can keep a nonzero output at zero error?

Answer

The I term. At \(e=0\) the P term is zero and (at constant level) the D term is zero, but the stored integral keeps its value, here 0.5, which runs the pump at 50% to replace the outflow.

4 · PID has less overshoot in this comparison. Is that guaranteed for every plant and every gain?

Answer

No. The benefit of D depends on the plant and on the gains; too much \(K_{\rm d}\) can slow the response or amplify noise (slides 28–29). Try a large \(K_{\rm d}\) in the simulator in Section 15.

You can now: answer the four slide-34 questions: open loop despite automation, why D leaves the offset, why I holds the command at zero error, and why D's benefit is not guaranteed.

22Common mistakes

✗Calling any automatic system "closed loop". ✓Closed loop means the measured output changes the input; automatic only says who decides. ✗Treating logging or displaying a reading as feedback. ✓That is monitoring; feedback requires the reading to influence the action (slide 10). ✗Writing \(e=y-r\). ✓\(e=r-y_{\rm m}\): positive when the output is below target. ✗Taking \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt=+\mathrm dh/\mathrm dt\) in the D term. ✓With a fixed target \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt=-\mathrm dh_{\rm m}/\mathrm dt\): a rising level gives a negative D term (slide 30). ✗Saying D (or a larger \(K_{\rm p}\)) removes the steady-state error. ✓D is zero at steady state; larger \(K_{\rm p}\) only shrinks the offset. Only I removes it (for a stable loop and a reachable target). ✗Thinking that when \(e=0\) the integral term is zero. ✓Zero error stops accumulation; the stored value stays (slide 26). ✗Comparing controllers from different starting levels, targets or axes. ✓Same plant, target, initial state and axes (slide 31). ✗Giving gain effects as certain ("increasing \(K_{\rm d}\) always reduces overshoot"). ✓They are tendencies, with trade-offs (slide 29).

24Concept checks

Written for these notes.

1 · Name the parts

A drone holds a height of 2 m using a downward-facing range sensor that controls the rotor speed. Identify \(r\), \(u\), \(y\), the actuator, the plant, the sensor and one disturbance.

Answer

\(r\) = 2 m height; \(u\) = rotor speed (or motor voltage/throttle) command; \(y\) = actual height; actuator = motors and propellers; plant = the drone's vertical motion; sensor = range sensor; disturbance = a gust, or a heavier payload.

2 · Integral update

The level stays 2 cm below target for 50 s. With \(K_{\rm i}=0.0006\u{(cm\,s)^{-1}}\), by how much does the integral contribution grow?

Answer

\(\Delta I=K_{\rm i}\int e\,\mathrm d\tau=0.0006\times2\times50=0.06\) (6% pump effort). Units: (cm·s)⁻¹ × cm × s = 1 ✓.

3 · Bigger tank

The same pump and outlet are fitted to a tank with twice the cross-section \(A\). What changes in the response, and what stays the same?

Answer

For the same net flow the level changes half as fast (slide 17), so the response is slower. The steady state (\(h_\infty=80u_\infty\)) depends on the flows, not on \(A\), so the P offset (30.48 cm) and PI's final 40 cm do not change (though the gains may need retuning for a good transient).

4 · Which term?

Which PID term responds when the level is at the target but falling fast? Which when it has sat 1 cm low for a long time?

Answer

Falling fast at the target: \(e=0\) but \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt\gt0\), so D responds (raises the command). Steadily 1 cm low: I keeps growing (P gives a small constant push, D gives nothing).

5 · Open loop can be fine

Give a task where open loop is a good choice, and say why.

Answer

A toaster or a washing-machine timer: conditions are predictable, the error tolerance is wide, and a sensor would add cost (slide 9).

MECH2004 Aug 2010 (special exam) Q4(e)4 marksRelated courseBeyond the slidesC1.4 · changing a gain

Consider a control system for the elevation of a satellite-tracking antenna. The antenna and drive parts have a moment of inertia \(J\) and a damping \(B\); these arise to some extent from bearing and aerodynamic friction, but mostly from the back emf of the DC drive motor. The equations of motion are \(J\ddot\theta+B\dot\theta=T_c\), where \(T_c\) is the torque from the drive motor. Assume that \(J=600{,}000\u{kg\,m^2}\) and \(B=20{,}000\u{N\,m\,s}\). The torque follows the feedback law \(T_c=K(\theta_r-\theta)\), where \(K\) is the feedback gain. Parts (a)–(d), on guide C2a, found the transfer function \(\Theta/\Theta_r=K/(Js^2+Bs+K)\), the largest \(K\) for an overshoot \(M_p\lt10\%\) (477 N·m/rad) and the \(K\) for a rise time under 100 s (above 194 N·m/rad). (e) Comment on the relation between rise time and overshoot.

Hint

Match \(Js^2+Bs+K\) to \(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2\) and see which way \(\omega_n\) and \(\zeta\) move as \(K\) rises (§16 for the gain table; note C2 §9 for the formulas).

Answer

\(\omega_n=\sqrt{K/J}\) and \(\zeta=B/(2\sqrt{KJ})\) are both set by \(K\). Raising \(K\) raises \(\omega_n\), so the rise time (\(\approx1.8/\omega_n\)) falls, but lowers \(\zeta\), so the overshoot rises: they trade off. Only \(194\lt K\lt477\u{N\,m/rad}\) meets both specs (\(t_r\ge64\u{s}\) at the 10% limit). Since \(\zeta\omega_n=B/2J\) is fixed, P alone cannot improve both; derivative action adds damping so \(K\) can rise without extra overshoot.

3. (a) A cruise control system for a car is shown in Figure Q3(a), where \(G(s)\) and \(D(s)\) are, respectively, the transfer function of the car and the controller, \(R(s)\) is the desired speed and \(Y(s)\) is the actual speed of the car. [Figure Q3(a): unity negative feedback, \(R(s)\) → Σ → \(D(s)\) → \(G(s)\) → \(Y(s)\), with \(Y\) fed back to the − input; redrawn on the guide.] Let \(G(s)=\dfrac{b}{s+a}\) and \(D(s)=K_P+\dfrac{K_I}{s}\). (iv) Explain the advantages and disadvantages of PI controllers.

Hint

What does the integral store (§13), and what does an extra integrator do to the swing and to a saturated throttle?

Answer

Advantages: integral action removes the steady-state error to a step in desired speed or a constant load such as a hill (the stored integral holds the throttle at zero error); only two gains. Disadvantages: the integrator adds a pole at \(s=0\), raising the order and the lag, so the response tends to overshoot more and settle more slowly; a large \(K_I\) can destabilise the loop; integrator wind-up when the throttle saturates gives a large overshoot.

MECH2004 Dec 2011 Q4(v)4 marksRelated courseC1.6 · explain why

4. The position control of a DC motor is modeled as a second-order system as below: \(G_p(s)=\dfrac{\omega_o^2}{s(s+2\zeta_o\omega_o)}\). We assume negative unity feedback for the closed loop. (v) Comment on the advantages and disadvantages of the proportional-plus-derivative control.

Hint

D acts on the rate of change of error (§14): what does that do during the swing, at steady state, and to a noisy measurement?

Answer

Advantages: D adds damping: \(\omega_n=\omega_o\sqrt{K_P}\) is unchanged while \(\zeta\) rises by \(K_D\omega_o/(2\sqrt{K_P})\), so overshoot and settling fall and stability improves; \(K_P\) can then be raised for speed. Disadvantages: D is zero at steady state, so PD does not reduce the steady-state error; it amplifies high-frequency measurement noise (needs a filter); a sudden reference change gives a large derivative kick that can saturate the actuator.
