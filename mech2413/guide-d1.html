D1 exam guide · Dynamics of particles

0How this chapter is examined

Verdict: predictable. Since Dec 2016, Question B1 has been a particle-dynamics question in every sitting (Dec 2021 also gave the whole of B3 and Dec 2022 part of B2). The same four shapes come back: integrate a given \(a(v)\) or \(v(s)\); a car or cyclist on a curve (banking, leaning, curvature of \(y(x)\)); a vehicle on a slope with friction, rolling resistance and drag; and "state Newton's laws". Dec 2014 and Dec 2015 had no D1 part. Over 2014–2024: 34 parts on 9 papers, 147 marks, by far the most reliable 20 marks in Section B.

Exam format

3 h · attempt any five of six 20-mark questions · at least two from each section (A statics, B dynamics) · about 36 min per question, so a 4-mark part is worth about 7 minutes. Formula sheet (Dec 2021–Dec 2024): printed at the start of Section B, it gives relative velocity of a rigid body, \(I=\int r^2\,\mathrm dm\), orbit formulas and \(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\), and nothing for this chapter: no \(a_n=v^2/\rho\), no radius-of-curvature formula, no drag law, no banking result. Memorise them (they are in the Ready answers below). Dec 2017 and Dec 2018 printed \(a=a_t+a_n\), \(a_t=\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt\), \(a_n=v^2/\rho\) and the \(\rho\) formula beside the question; do not count on that now. May 2013 used \(g=9.8\u{m/s^2}\). 2026/27: orbital dynamics and re-entry were removed from the dynamics syllabus (D1 handout slide 7) and an introduction to PID control (C1) was added; how the 2026 paper divides its questions has not been announced, so expect particle dynamics to keep its usual place in Section B.

Sign convention (same as the notes)
  • Straight-line motion: one positive direction along the line, usually the direction of motion, shown with an arrow; \(s\), \(v\), \(a\) positive that way. \(a\lt0\) while \(v\gt0\) means slowing down.
  • Falling with drag: positive downward; gravity \(+g\), drag negative.
  • Curved motion: \(\e{t}\) along the velocity, \(\e{n}\) towards the centre of curvature.
  • Diagram colours: red = weight and driving forces; teal = forces from the surroundings (normal, friction, tension, drag, rolling resistance); green = acceleration or velocity (not a force); orange = fictitious (inertial) force, only in an accelerating frame.

D1.1Rectilinear kinematics: integrate \(a(v)\), \(v(s)\), \(a(t)\)

D1.1Straight-line motion with a given \(a(v)\), \(v(s)\) or \(a(t)\)5 sittings · 26 marks (2014–2024)
Looks like
"the acceleration can be expressed as \(a=-0.05v^2\)", "speed is defined as \(v=-4s^2\)", "deceleration is \(kv^2\)", or a drag force \(kv\) on a coasting car; asks for \(v\) at a time, a time, a distance, or \(v(t)\) and \(a(t)\). 2–6 marks, usually one part of a B1 story.
Do
(1) pick the pair of variables the question links (time → \(a=\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt\); distance → \(a=v\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm ds\); \(v(s)\) given → \(v=\mathrm ds/\mathrm dt\)); (2) separate variables; (3) integrate with the numbers as limits (no constant to find); (4) solve for the unknown and add units; (5) if functions of \(t\) are asked, differentiate the result and check it at \(t=0\).
Watch
\(a=-kv^2\) with time asked needs \(\int v^{-2}\,\mathrm dv\), with distance asked needs \(\int v^{-1}\,\mathrm dv\) (a log); convert km/h to m/s first; if a force is given (\(kv\), \(kv^2\)) divide by the mass before integrating.

Concept: Notes §3 · constant acceleration, §4 · varying acceleration, §5 · \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\).

Ready answers · recurring forms

Choose the form by what is asked:

\[\mathrm dt=\frac{\mathrm dv}{a(v)},\qquad \mathrm ds=\frac{v\,\mathrm dv}{a(v)}\]

Quadratic resistance \(a=-kv^2\), from \(v_0\):

\[\begin{aligned}v&=\frac{v_0}{1+kv_0t},\\ s&=\frac1k\ln\frac{v_0}{v}\end{aligned}\]

Linear resistance \(a=-(k/m)v\), from \(v_0\):

\[\begin{aligned}v&=v_0e^{-kt/m},\\ t&=\frac mk\ln\frac{v_0}{v}\end{aligned}\]

In words: under quadratic drag speed falls like 1/time and distance grows like a logarithm; under linear drag speed decays exponentially in time.

Dec 2024 B1(e)4 marksstem and figure: D1.2

(Race car, \(m=1000\u{kg}\).) (d) The car exits the curve and enters a straight, flat track. The net drag force that is opposing the race car's motion is \(kv\), where \(k=0.8\u{N\,s/m}\), and the rolling resistance of the wheels is \(0.01N\) (\(N\) = normal force exerted by the ground). …

(e) Assuming that the propulsion force just cancels out the rolling resistance, determine the time it takes for the car's speed to drop from 50 m/s to 5 m/s.

Hint

Type D1.1. Only the drag is left in the net force; write \(m\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt=-kv\) and separate.

Solution
  1. Along the track (positive forward): propulsion and rolling resistance cancel, so \(m\dfrac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=-kv\).
  2. Separate and integrate between the given speeds: \[\int_{50}^{5}\frac{\mathrm dv}{v}=-\frac km\int_0^t\mathrm dt\]
  3. \(t=\dfrac mk\ln\dfrac{50}{5}=\dfrac{1000\u{kg}}{0.8\u{N\,s/m}}\ln10\). Units: kg/(N s/m) = s.
Marker note

The drag here is tiny (40 N at 50 m/s), so the time is long; do not "fix" the answer. Quoting \(t\) in minutes as well shows you checked it.

\(\boxed{t=2.88\times10^{3}\u{s}\approx48\ \text{min}}\)

Dec 2023 B1(c)5 marksstem and figure: D1.3

(Cyclist, total mass \(m\).) The cyclist has now reached the top of the slope and the road becomes flat (\(\varphi=0\)). At this point (\(t=0\)), the cyclist stops pedalling and is travelling at an initial speed of 5 m/s. If we ignore friction, the acceleration can be expressed as \(a=-0.05v^2\u{m/s^2}\). Determine the cyclist's velocity 5 seconds later (\(t=5\u{s}\)).

Hint

Type D1.1. Time is asked, so use \(a=\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt\) and separate \(v\) from \(t\).

Solution
  1. Only air drag acts along the road (FBD), \(a=-0.05v^2\) with \(v\) in m/s.
  2. \[\begin{aligned}&\int_5^v\frac{\mathrm dv}{v^2}=-0.05\int_0^5\mathrm dt\\ &\Rightarrow\ \frac15-\frac1v=-0.25\\ &\Rightarrow\ \frac1v=0.45\u{s/m}\end{aligned}\]
  3. Same as the ready form \(v=v_0/(1+kv_0t)=5/(1+1.25)\).

\(\boxed{v=2.22\u{m/s}}\) (forward)

Dec 2022 B1(b), (e)2 + 5 marksstem and figure: D1.3

A truck weighs 2000 kg and reaches a speed of 40 km/hr from rest in a distance of 50 metres up the 10-percent incline with constant acceleration. …

(b) Determine the acceleration of the truck in m/s².

(e) When the truck reaches a speed of 60 km/hr, a gust of wind causes a drag force that opposes the truck's motion and the truck starts to decelerate. The total deceleration is \(kv^2\), where \(v\) is the truck's velocity and \(k=0.3\u{N\,s^2/m^2}\). Determine how far the truck has travelled up the slope when its speed has dropped to 10 km/hr.

(a), (c), (d) → D1.3

(e) as printed is inconsistent: "total deceleration is \(kv^2\)" but \(k\) has the units of a force coefficient (N s²/m²). Reading used (RESOLVED.md): the drive stops, and the truck is slowed by the drag force \(kv^2\) plus the weight component down the 10% slope. If instead the deceleration itself is \(0.3v^2\) (k in 1/m), \(s=\ln(6)/0.3=5.97\u{m}\).

Hint

(b) Type D1.1, constant acceleration: \(v^2=v_0^2+2as\). (e) Type D1.1 with distance asked: \(m v\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm ds=-(mg\sin\theta+kv^2)\).

Solution
  1. (b) \(v=40/3.6=11.11\u{m/s}\); \(a=\dfrac{v^2}{2s}=\dfrac{11.11^2}{2(50)}\).

\(\boxed{a=1.23\u{m/s^2}\ \text{up the slope}}\)

  1. (e) Slope: \(\tan\theta=0.1\), \(\theta=5.71^\circ\), \(\sin\theta=0.0995\). \(mg\sin\theta=2000(9.81)(0.0995)=1952\u{N}\).
  2. Up-slope positive: \(mv\dfrac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm ds}=-(mg\sin\theta+kv^2)\).
  3. \[\begin{aligned}s&=\int_{v_2}^{v_1}\frac{mv\,\mathrm dv}{mg\sin\theta+kv^2}\\ &=\frac{m}{2k}\ln\frac{mg\sin\theta+kv_1^2}{mg\sin\theta+kv_2^2}\end{aligned}\]
  4. \(v_1=16.67\u{m/s}\) (\(kv_1^2=83.3\u{N}\)), \(v_2=2.78\u{m/s}\) (\(kv_2^2=2.31\u{N}\)): \[s=\frac{2000}{0.6}\ln\frac{2035.3}{1954.3}\]
  5. Check: with no drag, \(s=(v_1^2-v_2^2)/(2g\sin\theta)=138\u{m}\); drag shortens it slightly.

\(\boxed{s=135\u{m}}\) up the slope (reading above)

Dec 2021 B3(c)4 marksstem and figure: D1.3

(Automobile of mass 1500 kg rolling from rest down a slope \(\alpha=\tan^{-1}0.1\); air resistance \(kv_P^2\), \(k=2.5\u{N\,s^2/m^2}\); rolling resistance \(0.01N\).) (b) gave \(\dot v_P=\dfrac km(c^2-v_P^2)\), \(c=\sqrt{\dfrac{mg}{k}(\sin\alpha-0.01\cos\alpha)}\).

(c) Show that the velocity of the automobile can be written as \(v_P=c\big[1-\exp(-2ks/m)\big]^{1/2}\).

Hint

Type D1.1. Distance \(s\) appears, so write \(\dot v_P=v_P\,\mathrm dv_P/\mathrm ds\) and separate; start from rest at \(s=0\).

Solution

FBD: see (a) in D1.3.

  1. \(v_P\dfrac{\mathrm dv_P}{\mathrm ds}=\dfrac km(c^2-v_P^2)\).
  2. \[\int_0^{v_P}\frac{v\,\mathrm dv}{c^2-v^2}=\frac km\int_0^s\mathrm ds\]
  3. Left side: \(-\tfrac12\ln\dfrac{c^2-v_P^2}{c^2}\), so \(\ln\Big(1-\dfrac{v_P^2}{c^2}\Big)=-\dfrac{2ks}{m}\).
  4. \(v_P^2=c^2\big(1-e^{-2ks/m}\big)\); take the positive root (moving down the slope). Check: \(s=0\Rightarrow v_P=0\); \(s\to\infty\Rightarrow v_P\to c\).

\(\boxed{v_P=c\big[1-\exp(-2ks/m)\big]^{1/2}}\)

Dec 2018 B1(a)6 marks

A particle is moving along a straight line such that its speed is defined as \(v=(-4s^2)\u{m/s}\), where \(s\) is in meters. If \(s=2\u{m}\) when \(t=0\), determine the velocity and acceleration as functions of time.

(b) → D2.1 · (c) → D1.2

Hint

Type D1.1, \(v(s)\) given. Find \(s(t)\) first from \(\mathrm ds/\mathrm dt=-4s^2\).

Solution
  1. \[\begin{aligned}&\int_2^s\frac{\mathrm ds}{s^2}=-4\int_0^t\mathrm dt\\ &\Rightarrow\ \frac12-\frac1s=-4t\\ &\Rightarrow\ s=\frac{2}{1+8t}\u{m}\end{aligned}\]
  2. \(v=\mathrm ds/\mathrm dt\), \(a=\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt\).
  3. Check at \(t=0\): \(v=-16=-4(2)^2\) ✓, and \(a=v\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm ds=(-4s^2)(-8s)=32s^3=256\) ✓.

\(\boxed{v=-\dfrac{16}{(1+8t)^2}\u{m/s},\quad a=\dfrac{256}{(1+8t)^3}\u{m/s^2}}\)

D1.2Curvilinear motion (n–t): banking, leaning, curvature

Decision procedure
  1. If you see a banked track or a leaning cyclist and "without friction" / "leaning angle": do a front-view FBD (centre of the curve to one side), \(\Sigma F_{\text{vertical}}=0\) and \(\Sigma F_{\text{to centre}}=mv^2/R\); divide the two equations → \(\tan(\text{angle})=v^2/(gR)\).
  2. If friction is also present (banked car going faster or slower than the design speed): add \(f\) along the slope (down-slope when the car tends to slide up and out), keep the same two directions, and use \(f\le\mu N\).
  3. If you see a path \(y(x)\) and a speed: do \(y'\), \(y''\) at the point → road angle \(\theta=\tan^{-1}|y'|\) and \(\rho\) from the curvature formula; then FBD with \(n\) towards the centre of curvature (concave side) and \(t\) along the velocity: \(\Sigma F_n=mv^2/\rho\), \(\Sigma F_t=ma_t\).
  4. If accelerations are given ("gaining speed at 0.4 g", "acceleration 3 g upward"): \(a_t\) = rate of change of speed, \(a_n=v^2/\rho\), \(|\vb a|=\sqrt{a_t^2+a_n^2}\); or project a given \(\vb a\) onto \(\e{t}\) and \(\e{n}\) to get \(a_t\) and then \(\rho=v^2/a_n\).
  5. If \(a_t(t)\) is given along a path made of straight pieces and arcs: integrate to \(v(t)\) and \(s(t)\), find \(t\) when \(s\) equals the path length to the point, then \(a_n=v^2/\rho\) there (zero on straight pieces).

Cue: "radius of curvature", "banked", "leaning", "over the hill", \(y=f(x)\), "total acceleration". Traps: \(\e{n}\) points up in a dip and down on a crest, so \(N\gt mg\cos\theta\) in a dip and \(N\lt mg\cos\theta\) on a crest; constant speed means \(a_t=0\), not \(a=0\); convert km/h; the inertial-frame FBD has no centrifugal force (draw it, in orange, only when the question asks for the car's frame); mass cancels in banking and leaning angles.

Concept: Notes §9 · n–t coordinates and \(\rho\), §10 · off-ramp, maglev, banking and leaning, §7 · the FBD recipe, inertial and car frames.

Ready answers · recurring forms

Banking with no friction needed, and lean angle from the vertical on a flat road:

\[\tan\theta_{\text{bank}}=\tan\beta_{\text{lean}}=\frac{v^2}{gR}\]

In words: the bank (or lean) angle is set by the ratio of the needed centripetal acceleration to \(g\); mass does not enter.

Radius of curvature of \(y(x)\) and the acceleration parts:

\[\begin{aligned}\rho&=\frac{\big[1+(\mathrm dy/\mathrm dx)^2\big]^{3/2}}{|\mathrm d^2y/\mathrm dx^2|},\\ a_n&=\frac{v^2}{\rho},\quad a_t=\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt},\\ |\vb a|&=\sqrt{a_t^2+a_n^2}\end{aligned}\]

Banked curve with friction \(\mu\) acting down the slope (fastest case), bank angle needed at speed \(v\):

\[\tan\theta=\frac{v^2/(gR)-\mu}{1+\mu v^2/(gR)}\]
Dec 2024 B1(a)–(c)4 + 4 + 4 marks

As shown in Figure B1, a curved section of a race track has a fixed banking angle of \(\theta\) and a fixed curve of radius of \(R\). All of the racing cars are travelling in this curved section without slipping up or down the banked track. You may assume that the racing cars have negligible size and a mass of \(m=1000\u{kg}\).

The paper shows a photo of racing cars on a banked track with \(\theta\) marked; redrawn as a front view, centre of the curve to the left.

(a) Assuming that friction between the wheels and the track is acting down the slope, draw a free body diagram of a racing car to describe this curvilinear motion: (i) in the inertial (Earth's) frame of reference, and (ii) in the non-inertial (the car's) frame of reference.

(b) Derive an expression for the banking angle \(\theta\) for the race track so that the cars will not have to depend on friction to prevent them from sliding up or down the track, and determine \(\theta\) if the racing cars travel at a fixed speed of \(v=50\u{m/s}\) and \(R=0.5\u{km}\).

(c) Describe how the banking angle \(\theta\) determined in part (b) is affected when friction is indeed acting between the wheels and the track to prevent the cars from sliding up the track.

(d) → D1.3 · (e) → D1.1

Hint

Type D1.2. (a) Forces: \(mg\), \(N\) perpendicular to the track, \(f\) down the slope; the acceleration \(v^2/R\) is horizontal, towards the centre. (b) Drop \(f\); resolve vertically and horizontally. (c) Put \(f=\mu N\) back in and solve for \(\tan\theta\).

Solution
  1. (a)(i) Inertial frame: \(mg\) down, \(N\) normal to the track, \(f\) down the slope; the car accelerates at \(a_n=v^2/R\) horizontally towards the centre (green, not a force): \[\begin{aligned}\leftarrow:&\ N\sin\theta+f\cos\theta=\frac{mv^2}{R}\\ \uparrow:&\ N\cos\theta-f\sin\theta-mg=0\end{aligned}\]
  2. (a)(ii) Car frame: the same three forces plus the fictitious force \(mv^2/R\) pointing away from the centre; the car is at rest in this frame, so all four forces balance.
  3. (b) Set \(f=0\): \(N\cos\theta=mg\), \(N\sin\theta=mv^2/R\). Divide: \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{v^2}{gR}=\dfrac{50^2}{9.81(500)}=0.510\).

\(\boxed{\tan\theta=\dfrac{v^2}{gR},\quad \theta=27.0^\circ}\) (independent of \(m\); \(N=mg/\cos\theta=11.0\u{kN}\))

  1. (c) With \(f=\mu N\) down the slope, the two equations of (a)(i) give \[\tan\theta=\frac{v^2/(gR)-\mu}{1+\mu v^2/(gR)}\] which is smaller than \(v^2/(gR)\): friction supplies part of the centripetal force, so a shallower bank is enough at 50 m/s (or, at the 27° bank, the cars could go faster before sliding up). Example (written for these notes): \(\mu=0.2\Rightarrow\theta=15.7^\circ\); if \(\mu\ge v^2/(gR)=0.51\) even a flat track would do.
Marker note

In (a) the two diagrams must differ only by the orange \(mv^2/R\) arrow (car frame) versus the green \(a_n\) arrow (Earth frame); drawing both on one diagram loses the marks.

\(\boxed{\text{(c) required }\theta\text{ decreases as }\mu\text{ increases}}\)

Dec 2023 B1(d), (e)5 + 2 marksstem and figure: D1.3

(Cyclist and bicycle, total mass \(m\).) (d) The cyclist starts pedalling again until he reaches a constant speed of 8 m/s. He then enters a turn with a radius of curvature of 10 m. During the turn the cyclist will be leaning towards the centre of rotation. Explain why this is the case and determine the leaning angle with the help of a free body diagram.

(e) Explain the effect of the cyclist's weight on the leaning angle during the turn.

Hint

Type D1.2. Only \(N\) and friction \(f\) act at the tyre; friction provides the centripetal force. For the bike not to rotate, the resultant tyre force must pass through the centre of mass \(G\).

Solution
  1. Why lean: the only horizontal force is the friction \(f\) at the tyre, pointing to the centre; it acts below \(G\), so on its own it would turn the bike outward (topple). Leaning in moves \(G\) inward until the weight's moment about the contact point balances it, i.e. the resultant of \(N\) and \(f\) passes through \(G\).
  2. \(\uparrow\): \(N=mg\). To centre: \(f=mv^2/R\).
  3. Lean angle \(\beta\) from the vertical: \(\tan\beta=\dfrac fN=\dfrac{v^2}{gR}=\dfrac{8^2}{9.81(10)}=0.652\).
  4. (e) \(m\) cancels: a heavier rider needs more friction but also has more normal force, in the same ratio. For the same speed and radius the lean angle is unchanged, and so is the friction coefficient needed, \(\mu\ge0.652\).

\(\boxed{\beta=33.1^\circ\ \text{from the vertical; independent of weight}}\)

Dec 2022 B2(a)2 + 3 marks

At a particular instant an airplane is diving downwards at a 30 degrees angle with respect to the horizontal. Its engine is off and it is travelling at a speed of 800 km/hr. Due to aerodynamic lift, its instantaneous acceleration is 3.0 g in the upward direction, where g is gravitational acceleration on Earth surface.

(i) Determine the rate of speed change of the airplane. (ii) Determine the radius of curvature of the path.

(b) → D2.2 · (c) → D2.1

Hint

Type D1.2. The total acceleration is given; split it into a part along the velocity (\(a_t\)) and a part perpendicular to it (\(a_n\)).

Solution
  1. \(\e{t}\) points along the velocity, 30° below horizontal; the vertical makes 120° with \(\e{t}\) and 30° with \(\e{n}\) (perpendicular, on the upper side).
  2. (i) \(a_t=3g\cos120^\circ=-1.5g=-1.5(9.81)\).
  3. (ii) \(a_n=3g\cos30^\circ=25.5\u{m/s^2}\); \(v=800/3.6=222.2\u{m/s}\); \(\rho=\dfrac{v^2}{a_n}=\dfrac{222.2^2}{25.5}\). Check: \(\sqrt{a_t^2+a_n^2}=3g\) ✓.

\(\boxed{a_t=-14.7\u{m/s^2}\ (\text{slowing}),\quad \rho=1.94\u{km}}\)

The boxes in Figure B1(b) travel along the industrial conveyor. If a box as in Figure B1(c) starts from rest at \(A\) and increases its speed such that \(a_t=(0.2t)\u{m/s^2}\), where \(t\) is in seconds, determine the magnitude of its acceleration when it arrives at point \(B\).

Dec 2018 B1(c)10 marks

The 0.8 Mg car travels over the hill having the shape of a parabola. If the driver maintains a constant speed of 9 m/s, determine both the resultant normal force and the resultant frictional force that all the wheels of the car exert on the road at the instant it reaches point A. Neglect the size of the car. (Printed beside the question: \(a=a_t+a_n\), \(a_t=\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt\), \(a_n=v^2/\rho\), \(\rho=[1+(\mathrm dy/\mathrm dx)^2]^{3/2}/|\mathrm d^2y/\mathrm dx^2|\).)

D1.3Newton's 2nd law with friction, incline, drag; terminal speed

Decision procedure
  1. Always first: isolate each body and draw its FBD: weight (red) at \(G\), normal force(s) and friction/rolling resistance/drag (teal) opposing the motion, any driving or propulsion force (red), and the acceleration as a separate green arrow.
  2. Axes: along and perpendicular to the motion (along the slope on an incline). Perpendicular: \(\Sigma F=0\) gives \(N\). Along: \(\Sigma F=ma\).
  3. Which friction? Skidding or sliding: \(F=\mu_kN\). Driving wheels that do not slip: \(F\) is unknown, then \(\mu_{\min}=F/N\). Rolling resistance: \(C_rN\) (e.g. \(0.01N\)). "Is it about to move?": impending, \(F=\mu_sN\), \(a=0\).
  4. Drag given (\(kv\), \(kv^2\), \(C_D\rho AV^2\), \(\tfrac12C_D\rho AV^2\)): use exactly the printed form. Initial acceleration: set \(v=0\). Terminal speed: set \(a=0\). Constant speed: \(a=0\), so driving force = total resistance.
  5. If the equation gives \(a(v)\) and a time or distance is asked: go to D1.1 (separate and integrate).
  6. Two connected bodies (truck and trailer, crates on a belt): one FBD each; the connecting force appears equal and opposite; the whole system gives the external friction in one line.
  7. Normal force under each axle: add a third equation, moments about \(G\) equal zero (the body translates without rotating).

Cue: "free body diagram to describe the motion", incline, coefficient of friction, drag, terminal velocity, "efficiency". Traps: \(N\neq mg\) on a slope (\(N=mg\cos\theta\)); a "10-percent incline" means \(\tan\theta=0.1\); km/h to m/s; drag always opposes the velocity, friction opposes the (tendency of) sliding; on an axle-load question the rear axle carries more when accelerating forward uphill.

Answer skeleton · "explain each term / compare" parts
  1. One line per term: what physical effect it is, what causes it, which way it acts (1 mark each).
  2. Name every symbol with its unit (\(m\) kg, \(g\) m/s², \(\varphi\), \(C_r\) dimensionless, \(C_d\) dimensionless, \(\rho\) kg/m³, \(A\) m², \(v\) m/s).
  3. Comparisons: say which parameter changes, which way, and the result for the term (bigger/smaller).
  4. Qualitative "what if the drag law changes": same start, new terminal speed, how fast it is approached; sketch on the same axes.

Concept: Notes §6 · Newton's second law, frames, §7 · recipe, §8 · parachute, §11 · drag and terminal velocity, §13 · turbulent drag, §14 · drag exercises.

Ready answers · recurring forms

Body sliding down a slope \(\theta\), kinetic friction \(\mu\), drag \(cV^2\):

\[\begin{aligned}a_0&=g(\sin\theta-\mu\cos\theta),\\ V_T&=\sqrt{ma_0/c}\end{aligned}\]

In words: the start-up acceleration has no drag in it; terminal speed is where the drag has grown to equal the net driving force \(ma_0\).

Falling with drag \(C_D\rho AV^2\) (form used in May 2013 and Dec 2016):

\[V_T=\sqrt{\frac{mg}{C_D\rho A}}\]

Distance form for quadratic drag from rest, with \(c\) the terminal speed:

\[v^2=c^2\big(1-e^{-2ks/m}\big)\]

Dec 2023 B1(a), (b)4 + 4 marks

A cyclist on a road bicycle is travelling up a slope (angle \(\varphi\)) at a speed of \(v\). You may assume that the total mass of the cyclist and the bicycle equals to \(m\) and assume that the load is evenly distributed between the two wheels. The resisting force (\(F\)) that is opposing the bicycle's motion is given by the following mathematical expression:

\[F=mg\sin\varphi+C_rmg\cos\varphi+\tfrac12C_d\rho Av^2\]

(a) There are three terms in the above expression. Explain the physical meaning of each term and explain every parameter.

(b) Explain how the magnitude of these terms will differ between a racing road bike (thin tyres) and a mountain bike (thick tyres).

(c) → D1.1 · (d), (e) → D1.2

Hint

Type D1.3, written. Use the answer skeleton above: gravity along the slope, rolling resistance, aerodynamic drag.

Solution
  1. (a) \(mg\sin\varphi\): component of the weight along the slope (gradient resistance); \(m\) total mass of rider and bike (kg), \(g\) gravitational acceleration (9.81 m/s²), \(\varphi\) slope angle. Zero on flat road.
  2. \(C_rmg\cos\varphi\): rolling resistance of the tyres; \(mg\cos\varphi\) is the normal force from the road and \(C_r\) the (dimensionless) rolling-resistance coefficient, from tyre deformation and road roughness.
  3. \(\tfrac12C_d\rho Av^2\): aerodynamic drag; \(C_d\) drag coefficient (shape and posture), \(\rho\) air density (kg/m³), \(A\) frontal area (m²), \(v\) speed relative to the air (m/s). Grows with \(v^2\), so it dominates at high speed.
  4. (b) Gravity term: proportional to \(m\); a mountain bike is heavier, so larger. Rolling: thin, smooth, high-pressure road tyres deform less, so \(C_r\) is smaller; thick, knobbly, low-pressure mountain-bike tyres have a larger \(C_r\). Drag: the crouched road position and slim bike give smaller \(C_dA\); the upright mountain-bike position and wide tyres give larger \(C_dA\). So all three terms are smaller for the road bike at the same speed.

\(\boxed{\text{gradient}+\text{rolling}+\text{drag: all three smaller for the road bike}}\)

D1.4Newton's laws stated (written)

Answer skeleton
  1. First law (1 mark): inertia, rest or constant velocity, unless a net external force acts.
  2. Second law (1 mark): net force = rate of change of momentum; \(\vb F=m\vb a\) for constant mass; force and acceleration in the same direction.
  3. Third law (1 mark): forces between two bodies are equal in size, opposite in direction, along the same line, and act on different bodies.

Model answer (exam length): (1) A body remains at rest, or moves in a straight line at constant speed, unless acted on by a net external force. (2) The net force on a body equals the rate of change of its momentum, \(\vb F=\mathrm d(m\vb v)/\mathrm dt\), which for constant mass is \(\vb F=m\vb a\), with \(\vb a\) in the direction of \(\vb F\). (3) When body A exerts a force on body B, B exerts on A a force of equal magnitude in the opposite direction along the same line of action.

Concept: Notes §0 · Newton's three laws, §6 · second law and inertial frames.

Hint

Type D1.4. Inertia; \(F=ma\); action and reaction. One sentence each.

Solution
