Dynamics of Rigid Bodies

0Where this chapter sits

In D1 every object was a particle: a point with mass but no size, so it could move but not turn. Real machine parts have size, and they rotate: a door swings on its hinge, a wheel rolls, a skater spins. This chapter adds rotation: first how rigid bodies move (kinematics, Sections 1–9), then why (kinetics, Sections 10–14).

1Rigid bodies and the types of planar motion ~4 min

What is this?

Think of a door. Lift it off its hinges and carry it across the room, upright and facing the same way: every point moves in the same way, which is translation. Hang it back up and swing it: every point goes round the hinge line, which is rotation about a fixed axis. Carry it while turning it to fit through a doorway and you get both at once: general plane motion. The door never bends, so it is a rigid body.

Definition · rigid body

A rigid body is a system of particles for which the distances between the particles never change. We must consider its size (unlike a particle) but we assume no deformation. The model suits any object of considerable size that rotates, such as a barbell plate.

A body in planar motion moves so that every point stays in one plane (or parallel planes). There are three types, plus their combination:

(1) Rectilinear translationEvery line in the body keeps its direction and every point moves along a straight line. Example: a rocket test sled.
(2) Curvilinear translationEvery line keeps its direction, but the points move along curves (identical curves, shifted). Example: the plate of a parallel-link swinging mechanism, which stays level while swinging.
(3) Rotation about a fixed axisEvery point moves in a circle about one fixed axis. Example: a compound pendulum.
(1)+(2)+(3) General plane motionTranslation and rotation together. Example: the connecting rod of a reciprocating engine.
Fact · translation

In a translation (types 1 and 2) all points of the body move with the same velocity and the same acceleration. So a translating rigid body can be treated as a single particle.

Mozzi–Chasles theorem

Any general plane motion can be split into a translation that carries a chosen reference point \(A\) along its path, plus a rotation about \(A\). Picture a plate moving from \(ABCD\) to \(A'B'C'D'\): first slide it so \(A\) lands on \(A'\), then turn it about \(A'\).

In words: total motion = translation + rotation. The translation part is governed by \(\sum F=ma_G\) (forces → acceleration of the centre of mass \(G\)); the rotation part by \(\sum M_G=I_G\alpha\) (moments about \(G\) → angular acceleration).

Analogy: carry a tray across a room while turning to face the door. Your feet do the translation; your turn is the rotation. The tray's final position does not care in which order you did them.

You can now: name the four types of planar motion and give an example of each; say why a translating body can be treated as a particle; and split any plane motion into a translation of one point plus a rotation about it.

2Translation: Newton's laws for a system of particles ~3 min

What is this?

A skater glides across the ice. However she waves her arms and legs, her centre of mass follows the path set by the outside forces alone, as if all her mass sat at that one point. Her limbs push and pull on each other, but those inside forces cancel in pairs. Later in the section the same skater spins: with nothing outside twisting her, pulling her arms in makes her spin faster.

As in ENGG1000, treat a body as many particles \(m_i\) at positions \(\vb r_i\). The centre of mass \(G\) is the mass-weighted average position:

\[\vb r_G=\frac{\sum m_i\vb r_i}{\sum m_i}=\frac{\sum m_i\vb r_i}{m}.\]

Each particle obeys Newton's second law, \(\vb F_i+\vb f_i=m_i\vb a_i\), where \(\vb F_i\) is the resultant external force on it and \(\vb f_i\) the resultant internal force (from the other particles). Add over all particles: internal forces come in action–reaction pairs and cancel, \(\sum\vb f_i=\vb 0\). Differentiating \(\sum m_i\vb r_i=m\vb r_G\) twice gives \(\sum m_i\vb a_i=m\vb a_G\). Hence

Result · motion of the centre of mass \[\textstyle\sum\vb F=m\,\vb a_G\]

In words: the sum of the external forces equals the total mass times the acceleration of the centre of mass. The centre of mass moves as if all the mass were there and all the forces acted there, so the D1 methods (rectilinear and curvilinear motion of a particle) apply to it.

Reading the formula
\(\sum\vb F\)
the outside forces only: gravity and the push of the ice on the skater. Her muscles pulling her own limbs are inside forces and drop out.
\(m\)
her whole mass.
\(\vb a_G\)
the acceleration of her centre of mass, not of her hands or feet, which can do anything.
What is this? (angular momentum)

Angular momentum is the amount of spin a body carries, counting both how fast it turns and how far its mass is from the axis. Like ordinary momentum, only something from outside can change it: for spin, an outside twist (a moment). When the skater pulls her arms in, that is an inside change, so her amount of spin stays fixed and she turns faster to make up for the mass now being closer in.

Conservation of angular momentum

The angular momentum of a particle about \(G\) is \(\vb H=\vb r\times m\vb v\) ("moment of momentum"). For the whole system, \(\vb H_G=\sum\vb r_i\times m_i\vb v_i\) with \(\vb r_i\) measured from \(G\). If there is no external angular impulse,

\[\begin{aligned}&\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\textstyle\sum\vb M_G\,\mathrm dt=0\\ &\Rightarrow\ \textstyle\sum\vb H_G=\text{constant}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: with no net twist from outside, the spin about the centre of mass cannot change. This holds even though \(G\) itself may be accelerating (a non-inertial point), because the moments of the "inertial forces" about \(G\) always add to zero. (A skater pulling in her arms spins faster for this reason.)

So: find the centre of mass, use \(\sum\vb F=m\vb a_G\), and treat the body as a particle for its translation. The rest of the chapter is about rotation. Rotation matters in a V8 crankshaft engine, a slider-crank, a robotic arm and many more machines.

You can now: find the centre of mass and use \(\sum\vb F=m\vb a_G\) for a body's translation; explain why internal forces drop out; and use conservation of angular momentum to explain a spinning skater.

3Rotation about a fixed axis ~4 min

What is this?

Swing a door open. Every point of the door goes round a circle about the hinge line, and every point turns through the same angle in the same time. So one angle \(\theta\), one turning rate \(\omega\) and one rate of speeding up \(\alpha\) describe the whole door. The handle, far from the hinge, travels further and faster than a point near the hinge, even though both turn at the same \(\omega\).

When a body rotates about a fixed axis, every point \(P\) travels on a circle centred on the axis. Its angular position \(\theta\) is the angle of the line from the axis to \(P\), measured from a fixed reference line.

Definitions · angular displacement, velocity, acceleration
  • Angular displacement \(\mathrm d\theta\): a change in angular position, in radians or revolutions; \(1\ \text{rev}=2\pi\ \text{rad}\).
  • Angular velocity \(\omega=\dfrac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dt}\) (rad/s), positive anticlockwise.
  • Angular acceleration \(\alpha=\dfrac{\mathrm d^2\theta}{\mathrm dt^2}=\dfrac{\mathrm d\omega}{\mathrm dt}=\omega\dfrac{\mathrm d\omega}{\mathrm d\theta}\) (rad/s²).

In words: these are exactly the D1 definitions \(v=\mathrm ds/\mathrm dt\), \(a=\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt=v\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm ds\), with the distance \(s\) replaced by the angle \(\theta\).

The direction of \(\vb\omega\) follows the right-hand rule: curl your fingers the way the body turns, and your thumb points along the axis in the direction of \(\vb\omega\).

Constant angular acceleration

What is this? (constant angular acceleration)

Push the door steadily at right angles and its swing speeds up at a steady rate. That is constant angular acceleration, the turning twin of a car speeding up evenly, so the same equations apply with angles in place of distances.

Result · constant \(\alpha=\alpha_c\) \[\begin{aligned}\omega&=\omega_0+\alpha_ct,\\ \theta&=\theta_0+\omega_0t+\tfrac12\alpha_ct^2,\\ \omega^2&=\omega_0^2+2\alpha_c(\theta-\theta_0).\end{aligned}\]

\(\theta_0\) and \(\omega_0\) are the initial angular position and angular velocity.

In words: the "suvat" equations of rectilinear motion with \(s\to\theta\), \(u\to\omega_0\), \(v\to\omega\), \(a\to\alpha_c\). Use them only when \(\alpha\) is constant.

Example

A grinding wheel turning at \(\omega_0=2\u{rad/s}\) speeds up at a constant \(3\u{rad/s^2}\) for 4 s. Find its final angular velocity and the angle turned.

\[\begin{aligned}\omega&=2+3(4)=14\u{rad/s},\\ \theta-\theta_0&=2(4)+\tfrac12(3)(4)^2\\ &=32\u{rad}=5.09\ \text{rev}.\end{aligned}\]

Check with the third equation: \(2^2+2(3)(32)=196=14^2\) ✓.The third equation does not use \(t\), so it is an independent check on both answers. \(\boxed{\omega=14\u{rad/s},\ \ \Delta\theta=32\u{rad}}\)

Velocity and acceleration of a point \(P\)

What is this? (a point on the door)

Hold the door handle while someone swings the door. Your hand is carried along the circle (the velocity). If the swing speeds up, you feel the handle pull ahead along the circle (tangential acceleration). Even at a steady swing, the handle has to pull your hand inwards, towards the hinge, to keep it on the circle (normal acceleration).

\(P\) moves on a circle of radius \(r\) about centre \(O\), and its position is the vector \(\vb r_P\) from the axis.

Result · point on a rotating body \[\begin{aligned}v&=r\omega\qquad\text{(speed)},\\ \vb v&=\vb\omega\times\vb r_P\qquad\text{(direction)},\\ \vb a&=\vb a_t+\vb a_n=\vb\alpha\times\vb r_P+\vb\omega\times(\vb\omega\times\vb r_P)\\ &=\vb\alpha\times\vb r-\omega^2\vb r.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the velocity is tangent to the circle, size "radius × angular speed". The acceleration has two parts: a tangential part \(a_t=r\alpha\) (from speeding up the spin) and a normal (centripetal) part \(a_n=r\omega^2\) pointing towards the centre (the minus sign in \(-\omega^2\vb r\)). These are the D1 \(n\)–\(t\) components with \(v=r\omega\), \(a_n=v^2/r\).

Reading the formula
\(v=r\omega\)
handle speed = distance from the hinge × turning rate: twice as far out, twice as fast.
\(\vb\omega\times\vb r_P\)
the direction: along the circle, at right angles to the line from the hinge.
\(\vb\alpha\times\vb r_P\) (\(a_t\))
the extra speed gained along the circle when the swing speeds up; zero for a steady swing.
\(-\omega^2\vb r\) (\(a_n\))
the inward pull towards the hinge that bends the path into a circle; there even at a steady swing.
Example

A point 0.3 m from the axis of a wheel with \(\omega=4\u{rad/s}\) and \(\alpha=2\u{rad/s^2}\): \(v=0.3(4)=1.2\u{m/s}\); \(a_t=0.3(2)=0.6\u{m/s^2}\); \(a_n=0.3(4)^2=4.8\u{m/s^2}\) towards the centre; \(|\vb a|=\sqrt{0.6^2+4.8^2}=\boxed{4.84\u{m/s^2}}\).The two parts are at right angles (one along the circle, one towards the centre), so they combine by Pythagoras.

You can now: convert between revolutions and radians; use the constant-\(\alpha\) equations; and find the speed and the tangential and normal accelerations of any point on a body turning about a fixed axis.

4Relative velocity in a rigid body ~4 min

What is this?

Carry a long plank across a room while turning it. A friend walks beside end \(A\), keeping step with it but not turning. To the friend, end \(B\) only swings round in a circle, because the plank cannot stretch or shrink. So \(B\)'s velocity is \(A\)'s velocity plus that swing. The swing is at right angles to the plank, and faster for a longer plank or a quicker turn.

Take two points \(A\) and \(B\) on one rigid body, and a fixed origin \(O\).

Definitions · relative position and relative velocity
  • \(\vb r_{B/A}=\overrightarrow{AB}\): the vector drawn from \(A\) to \(B\), the position of \(B\) relative to \(A\).
  • Positions add head to tail: \(\vb r_{B/O}=\vb r_{A/O}+\vb r_{B/A}\) (often written simply \(\vb r_B=\vb r_A+\vb r_{B/A}\)).
  • Differentiating: \(\vb v_B=\vb v_A+\vb v_{B/A}\), where \(\vb v_{B/A}=\mathrm d\vb r_{B/A}/\mathrm dt\) is the velocity of \(B\) relative to \(A\): how \(B\) appears to move to an observer riding along with \(A\) (without turning).

Because the body is rigid, the distance \(AB\) never changes. So, seen from \(A\), the point \(B\) can only move on a circle around \(A\). In other words, for a rigid body that both translates and rotates, the only possible relative motion of two of its points is rotation about each other. The relative velocity is perpendicular to \(AB\) with size

\[v_{B/A}=\frac{\mathrm dr_{B/A}}{\mathrm dt}\Big|_\perp=r_{B/A}\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dt}=r_{B/A}\,\omega.\]
Result · the relative velocity equation \[\begin{aligned}\vb v_B&=\vb v_A+\vb v_{B/A},\\ \vb v_{B/A}&=\vb\omega\times\vb r_{B/A},\\ \Rightarrow\ \vb v_B&=\vb v_A+\vb\omega\times\vb r_{B/A}.\end{aligned}\]

and equally \(\vb v_A=\vb v_B+\vb\omega\times\vb r_{A/B}\).

In words: velocity of \(B\) = velocity of \(A\) + the extra velocity \(B\) gets by swinging around \(A\). The swing velocity is perpendicular to \(AB\), of size \(AB\times\omega\). Every point of a rigid body shares the same \(\omega\), so one \(\omega\) serves for any pair of points.

Reading the formula
\(\vb v_B\)
what we want: the velocity of the far end of the plank.
\(\vb v_A\)
the velocity of the walking friend at end \(A\): what \(B\) would have if the plank did not turn.
\(\vb r_{B/A}\)
the plank itself, as an arrow from \(A\) to \(B\).
\(\vb\omega\times\vb r_{B/A}\)
\(B\) swinging round \(A\): at right angles to the plank, size \(AB\times\omega\).
one \(\omega\)
the whole plank turns at one rate, so every pair of points uses the same \(\omega\).

The cross product

What is this? (cross product)

The cross product turns "a rotation and an arm" into a velocity. Picture tightening a screw: curl the fingers of your right hand the way you turn it, and your thumb shows which way the screw travels along its axis. Here it gives the direction a point of the plank moves: at right angles to both the turning axis and the arm.

For the right-handed unit vectors: \(\hat\imath\times\hat\jmath=\hat k\), \(\hat\jmath\times\hat k=\hat\imath\), \(\hat k\times\hat\imath=\hat\jmath\) (and reversing the order flips the sign, \(\hat\jmath\times\hat\imath=-\hat k\); any vector crossed with itself is zero). For \(\vb u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)\), \(\vb v=(v_1,v_2,v_3)\):

\[\begin{aligned}\vb u\times\vb v&=\begin{vmatrix}\hat\imath&\hat\jmath&\hat k\\u_1&u_2&u_3\\v_1&v_2&v_3\end{vmatrix}\\ &=(u_2v_3-u_3v_2)\,\hat\imath+(u_3v_1-u_1v_3)\,\hat\jmath\\ &\quad+(u_1v_2-u_2v_1)\,\hat k.\end{aligned}\]

In words: \(\vb u\times\vb v\) is perpendicular to both, with size \(|\vb u||\vb v|\sin\theta\) (\(\theta\) the angle between them), direction by the right-hand rule (index finger along \(\vb u\), middle finger along \(\vb v\), thumb gives \(\vb u\times\vb v\)). To evaluate the determinant, use Sarrus's rule: copy the first two columns to the right of the determinant and add the "down-right" diagonal products minus the "up-right" ones.

Ready form · plane motion (used everywhere below) \[\omega\hat k\times(r_x\hat\imath+r_y\hat\jmath)=-\omega r_y\,\hat\imath+\omega r_x\,\hat\jmath.\]

In words: to get \(\vb\omega\times\vb r\) in 2D, swap the components of \(\vb r\), put a minus on the new \(x\), and multiply by \(\omega\). It is \(\vb r\) turned 90° anticlockwise and scaled by \(\omega\).

You can now: write \(\vb r_{B/A}\) pointing the right way; use \(\vb v_B=\vb v_A+\vb\omega\times\vb r_{B/A}\) for any two points on one body; and work out \(\omega\hat k\times\vb r\) in 2D in one line.

5Instantaneous centre of zero velocity; rolling ~4 min

What is this?

Watch a bicycle wheel rolling along the road without skidding. The bit of tyre touching the road is not sliding over it, so at that instant it is at rest, like the sole of your shoe while it is planted during a walk. For that instant the whole wheel pivots about the contact point. The top of the wheel, furthest from the pivot, moves fastest: twice the speed of the hub.

If point \(B\) of the body is stationary, \(\vb v_B=\vb 0\), and the relative velocity equation reduces to \(\vb v_A=\vb\omega\times\vb r_{A/B}\): at that instant the body is just rotating about \(B\).

Definition · instantaneous centre (IC) of zero velocity

For a rigid body in plane motion (with \(\omega\ne0\)) there is always one point, possibly off the body, whose velocity is zero at that instant: the instantaneous centre of zero velocity (IC). At that instant every point moves as if the body were rotating about the IC:

\[\vb v=\vb\omega\times\vb r,\qquad v=\omega\,r,\]

where \(\vb r\) is measured from the IC. The \(\omega\) about the IC is the same as about the centre of mass: it is a property of the body, not of the reference point.

In words: freeze the motion for an instant; it is a pure rotation about one point. Each point's velocity is perpendicular to the line from the IC, and grows with the distance from it.

Picture a wheel rolling on the ground: the contact point is the IC, the velocity arrows at points on the rim are perpendicular to the lines joining them to the contact point, and the top of the wheel moves fastest (twice the centre's speed).

Watch

The IC has zero velocity but generally not zero acceleration. Use the IC only for velocities.

What is this? (rolling without slipping)

Roll a paint roller along a wall: each turn lays down exactly one circumference of paint. That is rolling without slipping, and it is why the hub's speed equals \(\omega r\). If the wheel skids (a locked brake) or spins (a wheel on ice), the distance covered no longer matches the turning, and \(v_G\ne\omega r\).

Going further · rolling without slipping

A wheel of radius \(r\) rolls without slipping if its contact point has the same velocity as the ground (zero on fixed ground). Then the contact point is the IC, and with \(\omega\) clockwise for rolling to the right,

\[v_G=\omega r,\qquad a_G=\alpha r.\]

In words: each turn of the wheel lays down one circumference of track. If the wheel skids or spins (bowling ball, taster), the contact point is not at rest and \(v_G\ne\omega r\); use \(\vb v_A=\vb v_G+\vb\omega\times\vb r_{A/G}\) instead.

Exercise: use the relative velocity equation to find the velocities of points on a wheel of radius \(r\) rolling to the right without slipping, \(\vb\omega=-\omega\hat k\):

  • Contact point \(C\): \(\vb v_C=\vb v_G+(-\omega\hat k)\times(-r\hat\jmath)=v_G\hat\imath-\omega r\hat\imath=\vb 0\).At the bottom the swing about the hub points backwards and exactly cancels the hub's forward motion: that is what "no slip" means.
  • Top \(T\): \(\vb v_T=v_G\hat\imath+(-\omega\hat k)\times(r\hat\jmath)=(v_G+\omega r)\hat\imath=2v_G\hat\imath\).At the top the swing points forwards, so it adds to the hub's motion.
  • Front \(F\) (level with \(G\)): \(\vb v_F=v_G\hat\imath+(-\omega\hat k)\times(r\hat\imath)=v_G\hat\imath-v_G\hat\jmath\), size \(\sqrt2\,v_G\) at 45° down, perpendicular to \(CF\) ✓.

You can now: locate the instantaneous centre of a body and use \(v=\omega r\) from it for velocities (not accelerations); use \(v_G=\omega r\) only for rolling without slipping; and find the velocity of any rim point of a rolling or skidding wheel.

6Rotation of a rod in the 2D plane ~3 min

What is this?

Look down on a door from above as it swings. The hinge stays put and the handle moves on a circle round it, always at right angles to the door. This section turns that picture into components with the cross product, so the signs come out right without guessing.

The formal way: write \(\vb\omega\) and \(\vb r\) as 3-component vectors and expand the determinant. If all moving parts lie in the \(x\)–\(y\) plane, the rotation vector points along \(z\): \(\vb\omega=(0,0,\omega)\), \(\vb r=(r_x,r_y,0)\), and

\[\vb\omega\times\vb r=\begin{vmatrix}\e{x}&\e{y}&\e{z}\\0&0&\omega\\r_x&r_y&0\end{vmatrix}=-\omega r_y\,\e{x}+\omega r_x\,\e{y}.\]
Reading the formula
\(\omega\)
the door\'s turning rate with its sign: positive anticlockwise, negative clockwise.
\(-\omega r_y\,\e{x}\)
a handle above the hinge (\(r_y\gt0\)) moves left when the door turns anticlockwise.
\(\omega r_x\,\e{y}\)
a handle to the right of the hinge (\(r_x\gt0\)) moves up when the door turns anticlockwise.
together
the arm \(\vb r\) turned 90° anticlockwise and scaled by \(\omega\): at right angles to the door, as the picture says.

In words: the \(x\)-velocity involves \(r_y\), not \(r_x\), because the velocity is perpendicular to \(\vb r\); it is negative because a point above the hinge moves to the left when the rod turns anticlockwise. Positive \(\omega\) is anticlockwise; stick to these signs in the formula and give negative values to variables when needed (a clockwise 10 rad/s is \(\omega=-10\u{rad/s}\)).

A rod on a hinge. A rod is hinged at \(B\) (fixed, \(\vb v_B=\vb 0\)) and turns about it; its tip is \(A\) with \(\vb r_{A/B}=(r_x,r_y)\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}\vb v_A&=\vb v_B+\vb\omega\times\vb r_{A/B}=\vb\omega\times\vb r_{A/B}\\ &=-\omega r_y\,\hat\imath+\omega r_x\,\hat\jmath.\end{aligned}\]
Exercise

The rod is 0.5 m long and rotates anticlockwise about the hinge at a constant 5 rad/s. Find the velocity of point \(A\) when \(\theta=30^\circ\).

  3. Check: \(|\vb v_A|=\sqrt{1.25^2+2.165^2}=2.5\u{m/s}=r\omega\) ✓, and \(\vb v_A\cdot\vb r_{A/B}=-0.541+0.541=0\) (perpendicular) ✓.A point swinging about a fixed hinge must move at right angles to the rod with speed \(r\omega\); these two checks catch a swapped component or a wrong sign.

You can now: write \(\vb\omega\) and \(\vb r\) as 3-component vectors and expand the determinant; give \(\omega\) its sign from the direction of turn; and check that a hinged rod's tip velocity is perpendicular to the rod with size \(r\omega\).

7Two connected links ~4 min

What is this?

Your arm is a two-link chain: the upper arm turns about the shoulder, and the forearm turns about the elbow. Your hand moves because the elbow is carried along by the upper arm, and on top of that the forearm swings about the elbow. Add the two and you have the hand's velocity. That is the whole method for linked bars.

Machines are chains of rigid links joined by pins. Consider a two-link (2R) robot arm: link 1 (\(AB\)) turns about the fixed base \(A\) with \(\omega_{AB}\); link 2 (\(BC\)) is pinned to it at \(B\) and turns with its own \(\omega_{BC}\). Apply the relative velocity equation link by link, walking from the fixed point outwards:

\[\begin{aligned}\vb v_B&=\vb\omega_{AB}\times\vb r_{B/A}=-\omega_{AB}r_y\,\hat\imath+\omega_{AB}r_x\,\hat\jmath,\\ \vb v_C&=\vb v_B+\vb\omega_{BC}\times\vb r_{C/B}\\ &=-\left(\omega_{AB}r_y+\omega_{BC}r'_y\right)\hat\imath\\ &\quad+\left(\omega_{AB}r_x+\omega_{BC}r'_x\right)\hat\jmath.\end{aligned}\]

Here \((r_x,r_y)\) are the components of \(\vb r_{B/A}\) and \((r'_x,r'_y)\) those of \(\vb r_{C/B}\).

In words: the tip's velocity is the sum of what each joint's rotation contributes, each term perpendicular to its own link.

Watch

\(\omega_{BC}\) here is the absolute angular velocity of link \(BC\) (relative to the ground), which is what the relative velocity equation needs. A robot's joint motor sets the angle between the links, whose rate is \(\omega_{BC}-\omega_{AB}\).

Exercise · two-link robot arm

Points \(B\) and \(C\) are in the \(x\)–\(y\) plane. The angular velocity vectors of arms \(AB\) and \(BC\) are \(\vb\omega_{AB}=(0,0,-0.2)\u{rad/s}\) and \(\vb\omega_{BC}=(0,0,0.4)\u{rad/s}\). What is the velocity of point \(C\)?

The arm in its plane: \(AB=760\u{mm}\) at 40°, \(BC=920\u{mm}\) at 30° below horizontal

8The slider-crank mechanism ~5 min

What is this?

Think of a steam locomotive: the piston slides back and forth in its cylinder, and a rod turns that into the turning of the driving wheel. The piston has to stop and reverse at each end of its stroke, like a child on a swing at the top of each swing. So while the crank turns steadily, the piston's speed rises and falls twice in every turn.

Definition · slider-crank mechanism (SCM)

A crank (a short arm turning about a fixed axle \(O\)) is joined by a pin to a connecting rod, whose other end is pinned to a slider (a piston) that can only move along a straight guide (a cylinder). It converts rotation into straight-line back-and-forth motion (reciprocation), or the reverse. Uses: robotics, pumps, internal-combustion and steam engines, compressors. A variant is the pendulum (oscillating-cylinder) engine, where the piston rod is fixed and the cylinder swings.

Geometry: crank \(r\), rod \(l\), crank angle \(\alpha\), piston distance \(x\)

Velocity diagram at \(\alpha=60^\circ\) (example below)

Displacement. Let \(x\) be the distance from the crank axle to the piston pin, \(l\) the length of the connecting rod, \(r\) the crank length and \(\alpha\) the crank angle. The crank pin is at height \(r\sin\alpha\); the rod spans that height, so its horizontal reach is \(\sqrt{l^2-r^2\sin^2\alpha}\) (Pythagoras):

\[x=r\cos\alpha+\sqrt{l^2-r^2\sin^2\alpha}.\]

In words: piston distance = horizontal reach of the crank + horizontal reach of the rod.

Velocity. Differentiate with the chain rule, \(\dfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}=\dfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\alpha}\dfrac{\mathrm d\alpha}{\mathrm dt}\), where \(\omega=\mathrm d\alpha/\mathrm dt\) is the crank's angular velocity:

Result · piston velocity \[\begin{aligned}\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}&=\left(-r\sin\alpha-\frac{r^2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha}{\sqrt{l^2-r^2\sin^2\alpha}}\right)\omega.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the piston's velocity is the crank speed \(r\omega\) times a factor that depends on the crank angle. It is zero at \(\alpha=0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\) (the dead centres, where the piston reverses), and largest a little before or after 90° (because the rod is tilted).

Reading the formula
\(-r\sin\alpha\)
the crank pin\'s own sideways motion: all you would get with a very long rod (a pure sine).
\(\dfrac{r^2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha}{\sqrt{l^2-r^2\sin^2\alpha}}\)
the extra caused by the rod tilting; it makes the curve lopsided and shrinks as the rod gets longer.
\(\omega\)
the crank\'s turning rate: double it and the piston is twice as fast at every angle.
\(\sin\alpha=0\)
the dead centres: both terms vanish and the piston is at rest, like the swing at the top.

Try it · slider-crank: drag the crank angle

Crank \(r=0.1\u{m}\). The curve is \(\mathrm dx/\mathrm dt\) over one revolution; the dot is the current angle. Make \(l\) long and the curve approaches a pure sine, \(-r\omega\sin\alpha\); make it short and the curve becomes lopsided. The piston is momentarily at rest at both dead centres.

Worked example · two methods

A slider-crank has \(r=0.1\u{m}\), \(l=0.3\u{m}\). The crank turns anticlockwise at \(\omega=20\u{rad/s}\). At \(\alpha=60^\circ\), find the piston velocity and the angular velocity of the rod.

Method 1 · formula. \(\sqrt{l^2-r^2\sin^2\alpha}=\sqrt{0.09-0.0075}=0.2872\u{m}\), so \(x=0.05+0.2872=0.337\u{m}\) and

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}&=\left(-0.0866-\frac{0.01(0.866)(0.5)}{0.2872}\right)\\ &\quad\times(20)\\ &=(-0.0866-0.0151)(20)\\ &=-2.03\u{m/s}.\end{aligned}\]

Method 2 · relative velocity. Origin at \(O\); \(B=(0.05,\ 0.0866)\), \(C=(0.337,\ 0)\), so \(\vb r_{C/B}=(0.287,\ -0.0866)\).

  1. \(\vb v_B=20\hat k\times(0.05\hat\imath+0.0866\hat\jmath)=(-1.732,\ 1.0)\u{m/s}\).\(B\) is on the crank, which turns about the fixed axle \(O\).
  2. \(\vb v_C=\vb v_B+\omega_{BC}\hat k\times\vb r_{C/B}=(-1.732+0.0866\,\omega_{BC},\ \ 1.0+0.287\,\omega_{BC})\).
  3. The piston moves only along \(x\): \(y\)-component \(=0\Rightarrow\omega_{BC}=-3.48\u{rad/s}\) (clockwise).The cylinder only lets the piston slide along it; that one condition fixes the rod's unknown turning rate.
  4. \(x\)-component: \(v_C=-1.732+0.0866(-3.48)=-2.03\u{m/s}\) ✓ (same as method 1).

\(\boxed{v_C=2.03\u{m/s}\text{ towards the crank},\ \ \omega_{BC}=3.48\u{rad/s}\text{ clockwise}}\)

You can now: write the piston position and velocity of a slider-crank; find the piston velocity and the rod's angular velocity by the formula or by relative velocity with the condition "slider moves along its guide"; and say where the dead centres are.

9Other mechanisms ~2 min

What is this?

Turn a car's steering wheel and a small gear at the bottom of the column rolls along a toothed bar, pushing it sideways to steer the front wheels. That is a rack and pinion: rotation becomes straight-line motion. A four-bar linkage does a similar job with pinned bars instead of teeth.

Rack and pinionA small gear (pinion) meshes with a toothed bar (rack). Turning the pinion moves the rack in a straight line: \(v_{\text{rack}}=r\omega\) where they mesh. Uses: car steering (the steering wheel's rotation becomes the side-to-side motion that turns the front wheels), industrial machines (CNC cutting machines, sliding gates, lifting devices), rack railways that climb steep hills by gripping a central rack.

10Rotational kinetics: torque and \(M=I\alpha\) ~5 min

What is this?

Push a heavy door. Push near the handle, at right angles to the door, and it swings easily; push equally hard near the hinge and it hardly moves. The turning effect is force × distance from the hinge: the torque. A heavier or wider door picks up its swing more slowly under the same torque, because it has a larger moment of inertia. Torque, moment of inertia and angular acceleration play the parts of force, mass and acceleration.

Kinematics described rotation; kinetics explains it. Set Newton's second law beside its rotational version:

Newton's second law, linear and rotational \[F=ma\qquad\qquad \vb r\times\vb F=\vb T=I\vb\alpha\]

where \(\vb r\times\vb F\) is the torque \(T\) (the moment of the force about the axis, S1 Section 5), \(I=\int r^2\,\mathrm dm\) is the moment of inertia (Section 11), and \(\alpha=\mathrm d\omega/\mathrm dt\) the angular acceleration.

In words: net force = mass × linear acceleration; net torque = moment of inertia × angular acceleration. The torque plays the part of force, \(I\) the part of mass, \(\alpha\) the part of \(a\).

Reading the formula
\(\vb r\times\vb F=\vb T\)
how hard the push turns the door: larger for a longer lever arm, and only the part of the push at right angles to the door counts.
\(I\)
the door\'s resistance to being spun up: more mass, or mass further from the hinge, means more resistance.
\(\vb\alpha\)
how quickly the swing speeds up.
Definition · torque

Torque is a moment that turns a body about an axis: force × perpendicular distance from the axis to the force's line of action, unit N·m. "Torque" and "moment" mean the same thing here.

Where it comes from. A small mass \(m\) slides on a frictionless tabletop, tied by a string to a pivot, moving on a circle of radius \(r\). A force \(F\) pushes it tangentially (\(\vb F\perp\vb r\)). Newton's law along the tangent: \(F=ma_t=m(r\alpha)\). Multiply by \(r\):

\[T=rF=(mr^2)\,\alpha.\]

For one particle \(I=mr^2\). A rigid body is many particles at different radii all sharing the same \(\alpha\); adding them gives \(\sum T=\left(\sum m_ir_i^2\right)\alpha=I\alpha\). More generally, the torque changes the angular momentum: in 2D, \(\sum M=\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(I\omega)=I\alpha\).

Watch

Use \(\sum M=I\alpha\) about a fixed axis or about the centre of mass \(G\) (with \(I_G\)). For a body that both translates and rotates (a rolling disk), write both \(\sum\vb F=m\vb a_G\) and \(\sum M_G=I_G\alpha\), plus a kinematic link such as \(a_G=\alpha r\).

Worked example · drum and hanging mass

A uniform disk pulley (mass \(M=8\u{kg}\), radius \(R=0.2\u{m}\)) turns freely on a fixed horizontal axle. A light cord wound round it carries a block of \(m=3\u{kg}\). The block is released from rest. Find its acceleration, the cord tension and the pulley's angular acceleration.

System

FBDs: pulley and block (taking down / clockwise positive)

  1. Pulley (moments about the axle; the axle reaction and the pulley's weight pass through it): \(TR=I\alpha\), with \(I=\tfrac12MR^2=\tfrac12(8)(0.2)^2=0.16\u{kg\,m^2}\) (Section 12).Forces through the axle have no lever arm, so they cannot turn the pulley; only the cord tension does.
  2. Block (down +): \(mg-T=ma\).
  3. Kinematics: the cord does not slip, so \(a=\alpha R\).The cord moves with the rim, so the block's acceleration equals the rim's tangential acceleration \(R\alpha\).
  4. Combine: \(T=I\alpha/R=Ia/R^2=\tfrac12Ma\), so \(mg=\left(m+\tfrac12M\right)a\): \[a=\frac{3(9.81)}{3+4}=4.20\u{m/s^2}.\]
  5. \(T=m(g-a)=3(9.81-4.20)=16.8\u{N}\); \(\alpha=a/R=21.0\u{rad/s^2}\).
  6. Checks: dimension \([I\alpha/R]=\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}/m}=\mathrm N\) ✓; if \(M\to0\), \(a\to g\) (free fall) ✓; \(T\lt mg\) ✓.

\(\boxed{a=4.20\u{m/s^2},\ \ T=16.8\u{N},\ \ \alpha=21.0\u{rad/s^2}}\)

You can now: write \(\sum M=I\alpha\) about a fixed axis or about \(G\); combine it with \(\sum F=ma\) and a no-slip link such as \(a=\alpha R\); and solve a pulley-and-block problem with a heavy pulley.

11Moment of inertia ~5 min

What is this?

A figure skater spinning with her arms out turns slowly; when she pulls her arms in, she spins much faster. Her mass has not changed, but more of it is now close to the spin axis. The moment of inertia measures exactly this: how much mass there is and how far it sits from the axis, with distance counting twice over (squared). Small \(I\): easy to spin up. Large \(I\): hard to spin up and hard to stop.

Definition · (mass) moment of inertia

Split the body into tiny pieces of mass \(\mathrm dm\), each at perpendicular distance \(r\) from the rotation axis. Each contributes \(r^2\,\mathrm dm\):

\[I=\sum m_ir_i^2\qquad\text{or}\qquad I=\int r^2\,\mathrm dm.\]

Unit: kg·m². Picture a blob split into pieces \(dm_1,dm_2,\dots\) at distances \(r_1,r_2,\dots\) from \(O\).

In words: the moment of inertia measures how hard it is to change the body's spin. Mass far from the axis counts much more than mass near it (distance squared).

Reading the formula
\(\mathrm dm\)
a small lump of the body: a bit of the skater\'s hand, say.
\(r\)
that lump\'s perpendicular distance from the spin axis.
\(r^2\)
far lumps count much more: twice the distance, four times the effect. This is why her outstretched arms matter so much.
\(\int\) (or \(\sum\))
add up every lump of the body.
  • Small \(I\): easier to rotate (less torque needed for the same \(\alpha\)).
  • For the same object, \(I\) depends on where the axis is: how it is hinged.

Everyday examples: a door is easy to swing about its hinges but would be easier still about a vertical axis through its middle (\(\tfrac1{12}mL^2\) vs \(\tfrac13mL^2\), four times smaller); a pencil spins easily about its long axis but is harder to twirl end over end; a figure skater spins faster when she pulls her arms in (smaller \(I\), same angular momentum \(I\omega\)); a baseball bat is easier to swing when gripped further up ("choked").

In words: hollow beats solid (a hoop has twice the \(I\) of a disk of the same mass and radius, a shell more than a solid sphere) because its mass sits further out. The exam formula sheet gives only \(I=\int r^2\,\mathrm dm\), not this table: you are usually asked to derive \(I\) by integration (Section 12).

Moment of inertia versus mass

What is this? (moment of inertia versus mass)

When you push a shopping trolley, only its mass decides how quickly it speeds up. When you spin the skater, it also matters where her mass is: the same skater has a small \(I\) with her arms in and a large \(I\) with her arms out.

Newton's second law\(F=ma\)\(\tau=I\alpha\)
momentum\(p=mv\)\(L=I\omega\)
kinetic energy\(KE=\tfrac12mv^2\)\(KE=\tfrac12I\omega^2\)
work–energy\(F_{\rm net}d=\Delta\left[\tfrac12mv^2\right]\)\(\tau_{\rm net}\theta=\Delta\left[\tfrac12I\omega^2\right]\)

(\(\tau\) is another symbol for torque.) Both \(m\) and \(I\) resist changes in motion: mass resists linear acceleration, moment of inertia resists angular acceleration. The main differences:

  • Dependence on shape: mass is one number for an object; \(I\) changes with the position of the axis.
  • Mass distribution: \(I=\sum mr^2\) depends on how far the mass is from the axis; mass depends only on how much matter there is.

You can now: explain \(I\) as mass weighted by the square of its distance from the axis; quote the standard values for a disk, hoop, sphere and rod; and explain why the axis position and a hollow shape change \(I\).

12Finding \(I\) by integration ~4 min

What is this?

Cut through a log and look at the tree rings. Every bit of wood in one thin ring is the same distance from the centre, so that ring adds just (its mass) × (radius)² to \(I\). Add up all the rings from the centre outwards and you have \(I\) for the whole disk. The skill is choosing slices like these, each all at one distance from the axis.

Recipe · finding \(I\) in three steps
  1. Do: Express the tiny slice of mass \(\mathrm dm\) in terms of density and a length, area or volume (\(\mathrm dm=\rho_L\,\mathrm dx\), \(\rho_A\,\mathrm dA\) or \(\rho\,\mathrm dV\)), choosing the slice so that every point in it is the same distance \(r\) from the axis.Like one tree ring, such a slice adds exactly \(r^2\,\mathrm dm\), with no averaging needed.
  2. Do: Set up the integral \(I=\int r^2\,\mathrm dm\).Adding every slice's mass × distance² is what \(I\) means.
  3. Do: Put in the limits (the boundary of the body) and integrate; replace the density by mass ÷ size.The limits count every ring from the inside edge to the outside edge once; the density swap gives the answer in terms of the total mass, as the exam expects.
Derivation · uniform disk about its axis

Thin ring of radius \(r\), width \(\mathrm dr\)

Rimmed disk: mass between \(r_1\) and \(r_2\)

Disk of radius \(R\) and mass \(m\). Area density (mass per area) \(\rho_a=m/(\pi R^2)\).

  1. Slice into thin rings of radius \(r\) and width \(\mathrm dr\). Unrolled, a ring is a strip of length \(2\pi r\) and width \(\mathrm dr\): \(\mathrm dA=2\pi r\,\mathrm dr\), so \[\mathrm dm=\rho_a\,\mathrm dA=\frac{m}{\pi R^2}\,2\pi r\,\mathrm dr.\]
  2. \(\displaystyle I=\int r^2\,\mathrm dm=\frac{2m}{R^2}\int_0^Rr^2\cdot r\,\mathrm dr\)\(\frac{m}{\pi R^2}\cdot2\pi=\frac{2m}{R^2}\) is the same for every ring, so it comes outside the integral; the rings run from the centre (\(r=0\)) to the rim (\(r=R\)).
  3. \(\displaystyle\phantom{I}=\frac{2m}{R^2}\left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^R=\boxed{\tfrac12mR^2}\)

In words: a disk has half the moment of inertia of a ring of the same mass and radius, because much of its mass is near the centre.

Derivation · rimmed disk (annulus)

Mass spread uniformly between radii \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) (a disk with a hole, or a rimmed flywheel): \(m=\rho_a\pi(r_2^2-r_1^2)\).

\[\begin{aligned}I&=\int_{r_1}^{r_2}r^2\,\mathrm dm=\int_{r_1}^{r_2}r^2(\rho_a2\pi r\,\mathrm dr)\\ &=\rho_a\pi\frac{r_2^4-r_1^4}{2}=\boxed{\tfrac12m\left(r_2^2+r_1^2\right)}.\end{aligned}\]

(using \(r_2^4-r_1^4=(r_2^2-r_1^2)(r_2^2+r_1^2)\).) Checks: \(r_1=0\) gives the disk, \(\tfrac12mr_2^2\) ✓. If all the mass is at one radius \(r\) (\(r_1=r_2=r\)), \(I=mr^2\) (a thin hoop) ✓.

Two design questions.

  • To design a disk with maximum \(I\) for a given mass: put the mass as far from the axis as possible: a thin, heavy rim joined to the hub by light spokes or a thin web. In the limit \(I\to mR^2\), twice the solid disk's \(\tfrac12mR^2\).
  • Why a flywheel needs large \(I\): it stores kinetic energy \(\tfrac12I\omega^2\) and resists changes of speed (Section 14).
Going further · uniform bar (asked in Dec 2022, Dec 2021, Dec 2014)

A thin uniform bar, mass \(m\), length \(L\): line density \(\rho_L=m/L\), slice \(\mathrm dm=(m/L)\,\mathrm dx\) at distance \(x\) from the axis.

\[\begin{aligned}I_{\rm centre}&=\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}x^2\frac mL\,\mathrm dx\\ &=\frac mL\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-L/2}^{L/2}=\frac{mL^2}{12},\\ I_{\rm end}&=\int_0^Lx^2\frac mL\,\mathrm dx=\frac{mL^2}{3}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: moving the axis from the middle to the end makes \(I\) four times larger, because half the bar is now twice as far away on average. (The parallel axis theorem, next, gives the same.)

You can now: derive \(I\) for a disk, an annulus and a bar from \(I=\int r^2\,\mathrm dm\) by choosing slices all at one distance from the axis; and say how to design a disk with the largest \(I\) for its mass.

13Parallel axis theorem ~3 min

What is this?

A door turns about its hinge, at one edge. Compared with turning about a line through its middle, the door's mass is now further from the axis on average, so \(I\) is larger. The parallel axis theorem gives the exact extra: it is as if the whole mass sat at the centre of mass and swung round the hinge.

Theorem · parallel axis \[I=I_{\rm cm}+md^2\]
  • \(I\): moment of inertia about the parallel axis;
  • \(I_{\rm cm}\): moment of inertia about the axis through the centre of mass;
  • \(m\): total mass;
  • \(d\): perpendicular distance between the two parallel axes.

In words: about any axis, \(I\) equals the value about a parallel axis through the centre of mass, plus "the whole mass at distance \(d\)". So \(I\) is smallest about the centre of mass.

Reading the formula
\(I_{\rm cm}\)
the door turning about a line through its middle.
\(d\)
how far the hinge line is from that middle line (half the door\'s width).
\(md^2\)
the extra: the whole mass, placed at the centre of mass, swinging round the hinge.
\(+\)
the extra is never negative, so any axis away from the centre of mass gives a larger \(I\).

Example: a rod about its centre has \(I=\tfrac1{12}ML^2\). Move the axis to the end, \(d=L/2\):

\[\begin{aligned}I_{\rm end}&=\tfrac1{12}ML^2+M\left(\tfrac L2\right)^2\\ &=\tfrac1{12}ML^2+\tfrac3{12}ML^2=\tfrac13ML^2\ ✓.\end{aligned}\]

The theorem lets you find \(I\) for complex shapes (add the parts, each shifted to the common axis) and for bodies pivoted off-centre.

Going further · why it works

Measure \(\vb r\) from the centre of mass and let the new axis be at \(\vb d\). Then \(I=\int|\vb r-\vb d|^2\,\mathrm dm=\int r^2\,\mathrm dm-2\vb d\cdot\int\vb r\,\mathrm dm+d^2\int\mathrm dm\). The middle integral is zero by the definition of the centre of mass, leaving \(I_{\rm cm}+md^2\). The theorem therefore only works when one of the two axes passes through the centre of mass.

You can now: shift \(I\) from the centre of mass to any parallel axis with \(I=I_{\rm cm}+md^2\); get \(\tfrac13ML^2\) for a rod about its end from \(\tfrac1{12}ML^2\); and remember that one of the two axes must pass through the centre of mass.

14Flywheels and rotational kinetic energy ~4 min

What is this?

A potter kicks a heavy stone wheel now and then, yet it keeps turning smoothly between kicks. Each kick stores energy in the spinning wheel, and the wheel gives it back bit by bit as the potter shapes the clay. A flywheel does the same for an engine whose piston pushes only part of the time: it is an energy store that evens out the speed.

Definition · flywheel

A flywheel is a wheel with a large \(I\), achieved by a large mass and radius. It stores kinetic energy of rotation, so as to stabilise the rotational speed when either the load or the power source varies (e.g. a reciprocating engine, whose piston pushes only during part of each turn).

Result · rotational kinetic energy \[KE=\tfrac12I\omega^2\]

In words: a spinning body stores energy "half × moment of inertia × angular speed squared", the rotational twin of \(\tfrac12mv^2\). It follows from adding \(\tfrac12(\mathrm dm)v^2\) with \(v=r\omega\): \(\tfrac12\omega^2\int r^2\,\mathrm dm\).

Reading the formula
\(I\)
how much mass is spinning and how far out it sits: a heavy rim stores more than the same mass near the hub.
\(\omega^2\)
double the speed and the energy goes up four times.
\(\tfrac12I\omega^2\)
every lump\'s ordinary \(\tfrac12(\mathrm dm)v^2\), added up, with each lump\'s speed \(v=r\omega\).

In a multi-cylinder engine the flywheel sits on the crankshaft, with geared teeth on its rim for the starter motor. In a single-cylinder steam or gas engine, gas pressure pushes the piston, the connecting rod turns the crank, and the flywheel smooths out the fluctuations in speed.

Why it works: an energy change \(\Delta E\) changes the speed by \(\Delta\omega\approx\Delta E/(I\omega)\) (from \(\mathrm d(\tfrac12I\omega^2)=I\omega\,\mathrm d\omega\)). Large \(I\) and large \(\omega\) make the speed ripple small: the flywheel soaks up energy during the power stroke and gives it back during the others.

Try it · same mass, different flywheel

Inner radius 0 = uniform disk (the flywheel of the exercise below, at the start values). Push the inner radius towards \(R\) to make a rimmed flywheel: with the mass unchanged, \(I\) and the stored energy rise up to twice the disk's.

Exercise · industrial flywheel

An industrial flywheel shaped like a uniform solid disk has a diameter of 1.5 m and a mass of 250 kg. It spins up from rest to a maximum angular velocity of 1200 rpm. (1) Find its moment of inertia. (2) Calculate the rotational kinetic energy stored at maximum speed. (3) If a constant braking torque of 50 N·m is applied, how many revolutions does it take to come to a stop? Extension: if the disk radius is reduced by 1.5 times, how much faster does the flywheel need to rotate to keep the same rotational kinetic energy?

  1. \(R=0.75\u{m}\): \(I=\tfrac12mR^2=\tfrac12(250)(0.75)^2=70.3\u{kg\,m^2}\).
  2. \(\omega=1200\times\dfrac{2\pi}{60}=125.7\u{rad/s}\); \(E_k=\tfrac12(70.3)(125.7)^2=5.55\times10^5\u{J}=555\u{kJ}\).\(\tfrac12I\omega^2\) needs \(\omega\) in rad/s; each revolution is \(2\pi\) rad and each minute 60 s.
  3. Work–energy (\(\tau_{\rm net}\theta=\Delta[\tfrac12I\omega^2]\)): \(50\,\theta=555\,165\Rightarrow\theta=11\,103\u{rad}=\dfrac{11\,103}{2\pi}=1767\ \text{rev}\).The brake takes energy out at 50 J per radian turned, so the wheel stops when all the stored energy is used up.
  4. Extension: \(R'=R/1.5\Rightarrow I'=I/1.5^2=I/2.25\). Same energy: \(I'\omega'^2=I\omega^2\Rightarrow\omega'=1.5\,\omega=1800\ \text{rpm}\).

\(\boxed{I=70.3\u{kg\,m^2},\ E_k=555\u{kJ},\ 1767\ \text{rev},\ 1.5\times\text{ faster }(1800\ \text{rpm})}\)

Sense check: \(I\propto R^2\) and \(E\propto I\omega^2\), so \(\omega\propto1/R\) for fixed mass and energy. Units: kg·m²·(rad/s)² = J ✓.

You can now: find \(I\), the stored energy \(\tfrac12I\omega^2\) and the turns needed to stop under a braking torque; convert rpm to rad/s; and explain why a flywheel with its mass in the rim stores more energy and smooths the speed better.

15Summary

16Common mistakes

✗Putting a clockwise angular velocity into \(\vb\omega\times\vb r\) as a positive number. ✓Anticlockwise is \(+\hat k\): a clockwise 10 rad/s is \(\omega=-10\u{rad/s}\). ✗Writing \(v_x=\omega r_x\). ✓\(\vb\omega\times\vb r=(-\omega r_y,\ \omega r_x)\): the \(x\)-velocity comes from the \(y\)-distance. ✗Using a different \(\omega\) for different pairs of points on one link. ✓One rigid link has one \(\omega\); different links have different \(\omega\)s. ✗Assuming the slider or piston velocity is \(r\omega\). ✓Only the crank pin moves at \(r\omega\); the slider's speed comes from the relative velocity equation with the constraint "slider moves along its guide". ✗Using \(v_G=\omega r\) for a ball that skids or spins (bowling ball). ✓\(v_G=\omega r\) only when rolling without slipping. Otherwise use \(\vb v_A=\vb v_G+\vb\omega\times\vb r_{A/G}\). ✗Treating the tension on both sides of a heavy pulley as equal. ✓If the pulley has inertia, \((T_1-T_2)R=I\alpha\), so the tensions differ. ✗Taking \(I_{\rm end}=I_{\rm cm}-md^2\), or forgetting to square \(d\). ✓\(I=I_{\rm cm}+md^2\): moving the axis away from the centre of mass always increases \(I\). ✗Putting rpm straight into \(\tfrac12I\omega^2\). ✓Convert: \(\omega=n\cdot2\pi/60\) rad/s.

"The block hangs from the cord, so \(T=mg=3(9.81)=29.4\u{N}\), and \(\alpha=TR/I=29.4(0.2)/0.16=36.8\u{rad/s^2}\)."

Is the block in equilibrium?

The block accelerates, so \(T\ne mg\): \(mg-T=ma\) with \(a=\alpha R\). Solving both equations together gives \(a=4.20\u{m/s^2}\), \(T=16.8\u{N}\), \(\alpha=21.0\u{rad/s^2}\).

18Concept checks

1 · Translation or rotation?

The seat of a Ferris wheel stays level while the wheel turns. What type of motion does the seat have?

Answer

Curvilinear translation: every line in the seat keeps its direction, but each point moves on a circle. All points of the seat have the same velocity.

2 · Dead centres

At which crank angles is the piston of a slider-crank momentarily at rest, and why?

Answer

At \(\alpha=0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\), when crank and rod are in line. There the crank pin moves perpendicular to the guide, so the piston has no velocity along it; the formula gives \(\sin\alpha=0\Rightarrow\dot x=0\).

3 · Same mass, different \(I\)

A solid disk and a ring have the same mass and radius and are given the same torque. Which spins up faster?

Answer

The disk: \(I_{\rm disk}=\tfrac12mR^2\lt I_{\rm ring}=mR^2\), and \(\alpha=M/I\). The disk reaches twice the ring's angular acceleration.

4 · Where is the IC?

A ladder slides with its top on a vertical wall and its foot on the floor. How would you locate its instantaneous centre?

Answer

Draw a line through the top perpendicular to its velocity (i.e. horizontal) and a line through the foot perpendicular to its velocity (vertical). The IC is where they cross: the corner of the rectangle opposite the wall–floor corner.

5 · Doubling the speed

A flywheel's speed is doubled. What happens to its stored energy?

Answer

\(E_k=\tfrac12I\omega^2\) grows four times.
