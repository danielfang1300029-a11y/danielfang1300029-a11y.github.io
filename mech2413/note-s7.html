MECH2413 · Engineering Mechanics · Statics chapter 7

Thin-Walled Pressure Vessels

0Where this chapter sits

A pressure vessel is a sausage skin holding something that pushes outwards. Every formula in this chapter comes from one move: cut the vessel in half and balance the push of the pressure on the cut against the pull of the wall.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 A thin wall is a skin: it holds pressure by stretching, not bending. Thin means \(r/t\ge10\).
  2. §2 Cut it in half and balance forces: the hoop stress \(pr/t\) is twice the longitudinal stress \(pr/2t\). The derivation itself is asked.
  3. §3 The hoop stress is the bigger one, so a cylinder splits lengthwise, like a boiled sausage. Which radius to use.
  4. §4 A sphere has no long way: \(pr/2t\) in every direction, half a cylinder's hoop stress.
  5. §5 Maximum shear comes from the most unequal pair of stresses. Give both in-plane and absolute.
  6. §6 The skin stretches, so the vessel gets fatter and slightly longer. Most common question
  7. §7 A slanted weld feels a blend of the two stresses, plus a shear along it. Mohr's circle.
  8. §8 Cap and cylinder swell by different amounts, so the joint bends.
  9. §9 Pressure × area pushes the end off; the bolts or rivets must hold it.
  10. §10 Two tubes stretch together, and the stiffer one takes more stress.

You need: free-body diagrams and equilibrium (S1); stress, the 2D Hooke's law and Mohr's circle (S3). In the exam: one of the most heavily examined chapters in Section A (24 question parts since 2013), and very predictable: stresses, then strains and the change in diameter; "derive the formulas"; a weld on Mohr's circle; the number of bolts or rivets. See the S7 exam guide.

Sign convention for this chapter
  • Tension is positive, compression negative (as in S3). Internal pressure makes every wall stress in this chapter a tension.
  • Directions in the wall: hoop (also called circumferential, \(\theta\)) goes round the vessel; longitudinal (also called axial, \(x\)) runs along the vessel's axis; radial (\(r\)) points through the wall thickness.
  • \(p\) is the gauge pressure: inside pressure minus outside (atmospheric) pressure.
  • Angles of an inclined plane (a weld) are measured from the transverse plane (a plane cutting straight across the vessel), as the past papers do. On Mohr's circle a rotation of \(\beta\) on the element is \(2\beta\) on the circle, in the same sense.

1What a thin-walled pressure vessel is ~3 min

What is this?

Think of a sausage skin, or a long balloon. The filling pushes outwards on every bit of the skin. The skin is far too thin to resist by bending, so it does the only thing it can: it stretches tight, like a drum skin, and that tension holds the pressure in. This chapter is about how hard the skin is pulled.

Definition · pressure vessel

A pressure vessel is a closed container that holds a gas or liquid at a pressure different from the pressure outside: a compressed-air tank, a boiler, a gas cylinder, a water main, a submarine hull (pressure outside), an aerosol can, a balloon.

What is this? (thin-walled)

A sausage skin is very thin next to the width of the sausage. Because of that, the inner and outer faces of the skin are pulled almost equally hard, so one number describes the pull through the whole thickness. A thick rubber hose is different: its inner layers are pulled harder than its outer layers.

Definition · thin-walled

A vessel is thin-walled when its wall thickness \(t\) is small compared with its radius \(r\). The usual rule (Hibbeler, Gere) is

\[\frac rt\ge10\qquad\left(\text{equivalently } t\le\tfrac{r}{10}\right).\]

In words: the radius is at least ten times the wall thickness. Then the stress hardly changes across the thickness (it stays within about 5% of its average value), so we may treat it as uniform through the wall.

Example: a gas cylinder of inner diameter 300 mm with a 6 mm wall has \(r/t=150/6=25\ge10\): thin-walled. A hydraulic cylinder of bore 50 mm with a 10 mm wall has \(r/t=2.5\): thick-walled (needs Lamé's equations, not in this course).

What is this? (membrane stresses)

Look at a small patch of the sausage skin. It is pulled round the sausage and pulled along it, both in the plane of the skin. Those two pulls are the membrane stresses. The sausage is the same all the way round, so nothing tries to slide the patch sideways along these two directions.

Definition · membrane stresses

Because the wall is thin it cannot resist bending well; like a balloon skin or a soap film, it carries the pressure by stretching in its own plane. The resulting in-plane stresses are called membrane stresses. In a cylinder there are two: the hoop stress \(\sigma_h\) (also written \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_\theta\), \(\sigma_\varphi\)) and the longitudinal stress \(\sigma_l\) (also \(\sigma_2\), \(\sigma_x\), \(\sigma_a\)). Because of the symmetry, there is no shear stress on planes along and across the vessel, so these two are principal stresses (S3).

Analogy: blow up a long sausage-shaped balloon. It always splits along its length, never round its middle: the stress round the balloon (hoop) is twice the stress along it. Section 2 shows why.

What is this? (radial stress)

The filling presses on the inner face of the skin, and almost nothing presses on the outer face. That squeeze through the thickness is the radial stress. A thin skin has to be pulled very hard round the sausage to hold the pressure in, so next to that pull the squeeze is tiny, and we ignore it.

Definition · radial stress

The third normal stress, through the wall, is the radial stress \(\sigma_r\). It equals \(-p\) on the inside face (the gas pushes on it) and 0 on the outside face. Since \(p\) is small compared with \(\sigma_h=pr/t\) (it is \(t/r\) times smaller, under 10%), thin-wall theory sets \(\sigma_r\approx0\): the wall is in plane stress.

You can now: check that a vessel is thin-walled with \(r/t\ge10\); name the hoop, longitudinal and radial stresses and their directions; and explain why the radial stress is neglected, leaving plane stress.

2Cylinder: hoop and longitudinal stress ~8 min

What is this?

Imagine slicing a sausage in half lengthwise. The filling pushes the two halves apart over the whole long cut face, and only two thin strips of skin (the cut edges, one on each side) hold them together, so the skin is pulled hard. Now slice it straight across instead: the filling pushes on just a small circle, and a whole ring of skin holds it. Per bit of skin, the lengthwise cut gets twice the push. That is the whole section in one picture.

Take a closed cylinder of inner radius \(r\), wall thickness \(t\) and length \(L\), with internal gauge pressure \(p\). Both formulas come from cutting the vessel and balancing forces on one piece (method of sections, S1 Section 2). This derivation is asked directly (May 2019 A2(b), Dec 2015 A3(a), 5 marks each).

Hoop stress: cut along the length

Cut the cylinder by a plane through its axis and keep the top half, together with the gas inside it, over a length \(L\) (away from the ends).

Cross-section of the top half: pressure pushes outward on the inner surface; the cut walls pull down with \(\sigma_h\).

Easier free body: half-cylinder plus the gas in it. The gas on the cut plane pushes up on area \(2r\times L\).

  1. Do: Forces up. The pressure acts on every bit of the curved inner surface, normal to it. Easiest: include the gas in the free body. Then the only pressure force is on the flat cut through the gas, a rectangle \(2r\) wide and \(L\) long: \[F_p=p\,(2rL).\] With the gas inside the free body, its push on the curved skin becomes one push on a flat rectangle: the long cut face of the sausage.
  2. Do: Forces down. The wall is cut in two places (left and right), each a strip of area \(tL\), each pulled by the hoop stress: \[F_w=2\,\sigma_h\,(tL).\] Only the two cut edges of skin hold the two halves together, and each is just \(t\) thick.
  3. Do: Equilibrium \(\sum F_y=0\): \(2\sigma_htL=2prL\), so The half is not moving, so the push of the gas apart equals the pull of the skin holding the halves together.
Result · hoop (circumferential) stress \[\sigma_h=\frac{pr}{t}\]

In words: hoop stress = pressure × radius ÷ wall thickness. Bigger vessel or higher pressure → more stress; thicker wall → less.

Reading the formula
\(p\)
how hard the filling pushes on each square millimetre of the cut face.
\(r\)
the size of the vessel. A wider sausage has a wider cut face for the gas to push on, but no more skin at the cut edges to hold it.
\(t\)
the skin thickness: a thicker skin shares the same push over more material.
\(\dfrac{pr}{t}\)
the push on the long cut face, \(p\,(2rL)\), shared by the two cut edges of skin, \(2tL\). The length \(L\) cancels, so a long cylinder is no worse than a short one.
Same result by integration · for the derivation question

Without the gas trick: a small strip of the inner surface at angle \(\theta\) (measured from the horizontal) has area \(r\,\mathrm d\theta\,L\) and carries force \(p\,r\,\mathrm d\theta\,L\) normal to the wall. Its vertical part is \(p\,rL\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\). Adding over the half circle:

\[\begin{aligned}F_p&=\int_0^{\pi}p\,rL\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\\ &=prL\big[-\cos\theta\big]_0^{\pi}=2prL.\end{aligned}\]

The horizontal parts cancel in pairs (left and right). Same \(F_p\), same \(\sigma_h\). General rule: the net force of a pressure on a curved surface, in any direction, equals the pressure times the surface's projected area on a plane perpendicular to that direction.

Longitudinal stress: cut across

What is this? (longitudinal stress)

Now slice the sausage straight across. The filling pushes the end off, but only over the small round cross-section. Holding the end on is the whole ring of skin round the cut, much more skin than the two thin edges of the lengthwise cut. So the skin along the sausage is pulled less hard.

Now cut the cylinder by a transverse plane (straight across) and keep the left end, with its cap and the gas.

  1. Do: Pressure force on the gas cross-section (a circle of radius \(r\)), pushing the cap to the left: \[F_p=p\,(\pi r^2).\] This is the filling pushing the end off; whatever the cap's shape, only the round cross-section counts (projected area).
  2. Do: Wall force: the cut wall is a thin ring of circumference \(2\pi r\) and thickness \(t\), area \(\approx2\pi rt\), pulled to the right by \(\sigma_l\): \[F_w=\sigma_l\,(2\pi rt).\] The whole ring of skin round the cut holds the end on.
  3. Do: Equilibrium \(\sum F_x=0\): \(\sigma_l\,2\pi rt=p\,\pi r^2\), so The end is not moving, so the push on the circle equals the pull of the ring.
Result · longitudinal (axial) stress \[\sigma_l=\frac{pr}{2t}=\tfrac12\sigma_h\]

In words: the stress along a closed cylinder is half the stress round it. The pressure has a circle (\(\pi r^2\)) to push on along the axis, but a whole diametral rectangle (\(2rL\)) to push on sideways, while the wall area resisting is \(2\pi rt\) one way and only \(2tL\) the other.

Reading the formula
\(p\,\pi r^2\)
the push of the filling on the round end.
\(\sigma_l\,2\pi rt\)
the pull of the whole ring of skin holding the end on.
\(\tfrac12\)
push per unit of skin: \(\pi r^2/(2\pi rt)=r/2t\) for the cross-cut, against \(2rL/(2tL)=r/t\) for the lengthwise cut. Half as much, which is why the sausage splits lengthwise.
Beyond the handouts · where the approximation comes in

The ring's exact area is \(\pi[(r+t)^2-r^2]=2\pi rt+\pi t^2\). Using it gives \(\sigma_l=\dfrac{pr^2}{(r+t)^2-r^2}\), which tends to \(pr/2t\) as \(t/r\to0\). For \(r/t=10\) the difference is under 5%.

Watch

\(\sigma_l=pr/2t\) needs the ends to be closed and the pressure on the end caps to be carried by the cylinder wall. If the ends are free to slide on seals (Dec 2017 A3(b), a pipe with O-ring end plates, or a long pipeline anchored externally), the wall carries no end thrust and \(\sigma_l=0\). Read the question.

Worked example · written for these notes · gas cylinder

A gas cylinder has inner diameter 300 mm, wall 6 mm, and gauge pressure 2 MPa. Find the wall stresses.

  1. Thin? \(r/t=150/6=25\ge10\) ✓. The formulas assume the stress is the same through the wall (Section 1), so check this first.
  2. \(\sigma_h=\dfrac{pr}{t}=\dfrac{2\u{MPa}\times150\u{mm}}{6\u{mm}}=50\u{MPa}\).
  3. \(\sigma_l=\dfrac{pr}{2t}=25\u{MPa}\).
  4. Units: MPa × mm ÷ mm = MPa ✓. (1 MPa = 1 N/mm², a convenient unit when lengths are in mm.)

\(\boxed{\sigma_h=50\u{MPa},\quad\sigma_l=25\u{MPa}\ \ (\text{both tension})}\)

Ready answer · design for thickness

Given an allowable stress \(\sigma_{\rm allow}\), the hoop stress governs (it is the larger), so \(t\ge\dfrac{pr}{\sigma_{\rm allow}}\). Example: \(p=1.5\u{MPa}\), \(r=1000\u{mm}\), \(\sigma_{\rm allow}=120\u{MPa}\) gives \(t\ge12.5\u{mm}\).

You can now: derive \(\sigma_h=pr/t\) and \(\sigma_l=pr/2t\) from a free-body diagram of half the vessel; use the projected-area rule for pressure on a curved surface; size a wall from an allowable stress; and say when \(\sigma_l=0\) (ends free to slide).

3Which radius? How does it burst? ~3 min

What is this?

The sausage skin has an inner face and an outer face, and their circles are slightly different sizes. For a thin skin the difference is only a few per cent, so it hardly matters which you use: follow the paper, and say which you chose. The second question is which way the skin tears when the pressure gets too high.

Inner, mean or outer radius?

The derivation used the inner radius for the pressure area, and treated the wall area as \(2\pi rt\). For a thin wall the difference between radii is small, and papers say which to use: "mean radius 400 mm" (May 2014), "average diameter 400 mm" (Dec 2017), "outside diameter 500 mm, wall 10 mm" (Dec 2014: inner radius \(=250-10=240\u{mm}\), mean 245 mm). Rules:

  • If the paper gives one radius and says "thin wall", use it as given.
  • If it gives an outside diameter \(D_o\), the inner radius is \(r_i=D_o/2-t\) and the mean radius \(r_m=D_o/2-t/2\). Gere uses \(r_i\) (conservative for stress, slightly larger); Beer and Hibbeler use \(r_i\) in the formula derivation. State which you use.
  • Example: with inner \(r=150\u{mm}\) the gas cylinder above had \(\sigma_h=50\u{MPa}\); with mean \(r=153\u{mm}\) it is 51 MPa, only 2% more.

How a cylinder bursts

What is this? (how it bursts)

Boil a sausage too long and the skin splits along its length, never round its middle. The pull round the skin is twice the pull along it, so it reaches the skin's strength first, and the skin tears at right angles to that pull: along the sausage.

Since \(\sigma_h=2\sigma_l\), a cylinder overloaded by internal pressure fails first in the hoop direction. The crack opens perpendicular to the hoop stress, so it runs along the length (a longitudinal split), often with a "fish-mouth" opening. This is asked in Dec 2014 A2(c).

Ready answer · "sketch the fracture" (Dec 2014 A2(c))

Draw a cylinder with a crack running parallel to the axis. Reason: the hoop stress \(pr/t\) is twice the longitudinal stress, so it reaches the material's strength first, and a crack opens at right angles to the largest tensile stress. (Same reason a sausage splits lengthwise in the pan.)

You can now: find the inner and mean radius from an outside diameter and wall thickness; and sketch and explain the lengthwise burst of an over-pressured cylinder.

4Spheres and end caps ~3 min

What is this?

A ball has no long way. Every cut through its centre looks like the sausage's cross-cut: a circle of filling pushing, a whole ring of skin pulling. So the skin is pulled equally hard in every direction, at the smaller of the sausage's two stresses.

For a sphere of inner radius \(r\) and wall \(t\), every plane through the centre cuts it the same way. Cut it in half and keep one hemisphere with its gas (asked as a derivation in Dec 2021 A3(a)).

  1. Do: Pressure force on the flat gas face: \(F_p=p\,\pi r^2\). Cutting through the centre exposes a circle of gas, exactly like the sausage's cross-cut.
  2. Do: Wall force on the cut ring: \(F_w=\sigma\,2\pi rt\). The whole ring of skin round the cut holds the two halves together.
  3. Do: Equilibrium: \(\sigma\,2\pi rt=p\pi r^2\). Every plane through the centre gives this same balance, so the wall stress is the same in every direction.
Result · sphere \[\sigma_1=\sigma_2=\frac{pr}{2t}\]

In words: a sphere carries pressure equally in every direction in its wall, at half the hoop stress of a cylinder of the same radius and thickness. (The figure in Dec 2021 calls these \(\sigma_\varphi\) and \(\sigma_t\); they are equal.)

Worked example · written for these notes · sphere versus cylinder

A tank of radius 5 m must hold 0.5 MPa with allowable stress 100 MPa. Compare the wall needed for a sphere and a cylinder.

\[\begin{aligned}\text{sphere: } t&=\frac{pr}{2\sigma_{\rm allow}}=\frac{0.5(5000)}{2(100)}\\ &=12.5\u{mm},\\ \text{cylinder: } t&=\frac{pr}{\sigma_{\rm allow}}=25\u{mm}.\end{aligned}\]

\(\boxed{\text{the sphere needs half the thickness}}\). That is why large gas-storage tanks are spheres, and why cylinders get rounded (hemispherical or dished) ends. Spheres are harder to make, need more floor space per volume, and are awkward to transport, so cylinders are still more common.

What is this? (end caps)

A sausage must be closed at its ends. A flat end can only hold the push by bending, which needs a thick plate. A rounded end holds it by stretching, like the rest of the skin, so it can be thin.

Definition · end caps

A cylinder's ends are closed by end caps (heads): flat plates (which must be thick, because a flat plate carries pressure by bending, not stretching), dished heads, or hemispherical caps. A hemispherical cap is half a sphere, so its membrane stress is \(pr/(2t_{\rm cap})\) in every direction.

You can now: derive \(\sigma=pr/2t\) for a sphere by cutting it through the centre; compare the wall needed for a sphere and a cylinder; and explain why rounded end caps are used.

5Maximum shear stress: in-plane and absolute ~4 min

What is this?

Pull a sheet of skin harder one way than the other, and any slanted line drawn on it tries to slide: one side wants to move past the other. That sliding is shear, and it is largest on lines at \(45^\circ\) between the two pulls. The more unequal the pulls, the bigger the shear. In the sausage skin the most unequal pair is not round against along, but round against through the thickness, where there is almost no stress at all.

Ductile metals yield in shear (Tresca criterion, Beer ch. 7), so exams ask for \(\tau_{\max}\) (Dec 2015, Dec 2021, May 2019, Dec 2022). The wall element has three principal stresses: \(\sigma_h\), \(\sigma_l\), and \(\sigma_r\approx0\). Recall from S3: on Mohr's circle, the maximum shear between two principal stresses \(\sigma_a\), \(\sigma_b\) is the circle's radius \(\tfrac12|\sigma_a-\sigma_b|\), on planes at \(45^\circ\) to both.

Definitions · in-plane and out-of-plane shear
  • In-plane maximum shear: the largest shear on planes you get by rotating the element within the wall surface (about the radial axis). Uses only \(\sigma_h\) and \(\sigma_l\).
  • Out-of-plane (absolute) maximum shear: the largest shear on any plane, including planes tilted through the thickness (rotating about the hoop or longitudinal axis). Uses the largest and smallest of all three principal stresses.

The three Mohr's circles for a cylinder wall (\(\sigma_r=0\)), in units of \(pr/t\)

Each pair of principal stresses gives a circle. The biggest circle (from 0 to \(\sigma_h\)) gives the absolute maximum shear; the small one between \(\sigma_l\) and \(\sigma_h\) gives the in-plane maximum. For a sphere the in-plane circle shrinks to a point.

Results · maximum shear (thin wall, \(\sigma_r=0\))
in-plane \(\tau_{\max}\)absolute \(\tau_{\max}\)
cylinder\(\dfrac{\sigma_h-\sigma_l}{2}=\dfrac{pr}{4t}\)\(\dfrac{\sigma_h-0}{2}=\dfrac{pr}{2t}\)
sphere\(0\)\(\dfrac{\sigma-0}{2}=\dfrac{pr}{4t}\)

In words: in a cylinder, the biggest shear within the wall is a quarter of \(pr/t\), on planes at \(45^\circ\) between the hoop and longitudinal directions; but the biggest shear of all is half of \(pr/t\), on planes at \(45^\circ\) through the thickness. In a sphere every in-plane direction is the same, so there is no in-plane shear at all.

Watch

When a question says just "maximum shear stress", give both and say which is which (marks are often split this way, e.g. Dec 2021 A3(a)(ii)–(iii)). If it says "neglecting the radial stress", use \(\sigma_r=0\).

Beyond the handouts · including the radial stress

At the inner surface \(\sigma_r=-p\). Then the absolute maximum shear in a cylinder is \(\tfrac12(\sigma_h+p)=\tfrac p2\left(\tfrac rt+1\right)\), slightly larger (Gere §8.3). Only use this if asked.

You can now: find the in-plane and absolute maximum shear stress of a cylinder and a sphere from the right pair of principal stresses; say on which planes each acts; and give both when a question just says "maximum shear".

6Strains and the change in size ~5 min

What is this?

Pressurise the sausage and its skin stretches. The pull round it is big, so the sausage gets fatter. Along the sausage the pull is smaller, and stretching a material one way makes it narrower the other way (Poisson's effect), so the strong pull round partly cancels the stretch along. A closed cylinder gets fatter, and only a little longer.

The most common exam question (Dec 2023, Dec 2022, Dec 2018, Dec 2014, May 2014): find the stresses, then the strains, then how much the diameter grows. The wall is in plane stress with principal stresses \(\sigma_h\) and \(\sigma_l\), so the 2D Hooke's law of S3 applies.

Reminder · Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio

Young's modulus \(E\) is the stiffness of the material: stress divided by strain in a simple pull. Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) says how much a material gets thinner sideways when stretched: lateral strain \(=-\nu\times\) axial strain. Steel: \(E\approx200\u{GPa}\), \(\nu\approx0.3\) (papers often use 0.25).

Result · hoop and longitudinal strain \[\begin{aligned}\varepsilon_h&=\frac{1}{E}\left(\sigma_h-\nu\sigma_l\right)=\frac{pr}{2tE}(2-\nu),\\ \varepsilon_l&=\frac{1}{E}\left(\sigma_l-\nu\sigma_h\right)=\frac{pr}{2tE}(1-2\nu).\end{aligned}\]

In words: each strain is "its own stress over \(E\)", minus Poisson's ratio times "the other stress over \(E\)" (stretching one way makes the material shrink the other way). (\(\sigma_r\approx0\) contributes nothing.)

Reading the formula
\(\sigma_h/E\)
the stretch round the skin that the hoop stress alone would give.
\(-\nu\sigma_l/E\)
the narrowing round the skin caused by the pull along it (Poisson's effect).
\(\sigma_l/E-\nu\sigma_h/E\)
along the sausage: a small stretch, mostly cancelled by the narrowing from the big hoop stress.
\(\dfrac{pr}{2tE}(2-\nu)\), \(\dfrac{pr}{2tE}(1-2\nu)\)
the same two strains with \(\sigma_h=pr/t\) and \(\sigma_l=pr/2t\) put in.

From strain to change in size

What is this? (from strain to change in size)

Wrap a belt round the sausage. If the belt gets 1% longer, the circle it makes is 1% wider. So the stretch of the skin round the sausage is also the stretch of its radius and diameter.

The hoop strain is the stretch of the circumference: \(\varepsilon_h=\dfrac{2\pi(r+\Delta r)-2\pi r}{2\pi r}=\dfrac{\Delta r}{r}\). So the hoop strain is also the strain of the radius and diameter.

Result · change in diameter, length and volume (cylinder) \[\begin{aligned}\Delta d&=\varepsilon_h\,d,\qquad\Delta r=\varepsilon_h\,r,\\ \Delta L&=\varepsilon_l\,L,\\ \frac{\Delta V}{V}&=2\varepsilon_h+\varepsilon_l=\frac{pr}{2tE}(5-4\nu).\end{aligned}\]

In words: diameter change = hoop strain × diameter; length change = longitudinal strain × length; volume strain = twice the hoop strain plus the longitudinal strain (since \(V=\pi r^2L\), and small changes add: \(\Delta V/V=2\Delta r/r+\Delta L/L\)).

Reading the formula
\(\Delta d=\varepsilon_h\,d\)
the belt picture: the diameter grows by the hoop strain.
\(2\varepsilon_h\)
the cross-section gets wider in two directions across the sausage, each by the hoop strain, so its area grows by twice that.
\(+\,\varepsilon_l\)
the length grows by the longitudinal strain.
\((5-4\nu)\)
\(2(2-\nu)+(1-2\nu)\): the two strain formulas above added in this way.
Result · sphere \[\begin{aligned}\varepsilon&=\frac{\sigma}{E}(1-\nu)=\frac{pr}{2tE}(1-\nu),\\ \Delta r&=\varepsilon r,\qquad\frac{\Delta V}{V}=3\varepsilon.\end{aligned}\]

In words: both in-plane stresses are equal, so the Poisson shrinkage from one cancels part of the stretch from the other.

Worked example · written for these notes · steel tank

A closed steel cylinder: inner radius 1.2 m, wall 12 mm, length 6 m, gauge pressure 1.0 MPa, \(E=200\u{GPa}\), \(\nu=0.3\). Find the stresses, strains, and the changes in diameter, length and volume (ignore the ends' volume change).

  1. Thin? \(r/t=1200/12=100\) ✓.
  2. \(\sigma_h=\dfrac{1.0(1200)}{12}=100\u{MPa}\); \(\sigma_l=50\u{MPa}\).
  3. \(E=200\u{GPa}=200\,000\u{MPa}\) (keep everything in MPa and mm). MPa divided by MPa gives a pure number; mixing GPa and MPa makes the strain 1000 times wrong. \[\begin{aligned}\varepsilon_h&=\frac{100-0.3(50)}{200\,000}=4.25\times10^{-4},\\ \varepsilon_l&=\frac{50-0.3(100)}{200\,000}=1.0\times10^{-4}.\end{aligned}\]
  4. \(\Delta d=\varepsilon_hd=4.25\times10^{-4}\times2400\u{mm}=1.02\u{mm}\); \(\Delta L=1.0\times10^{-4}\times6000\u{mm}=0.60\u{mm}\).
  5. \(V=\pi(1.2)^2(6)=27.14\u{m^3}\); \(\Delta V/V=2(4.25)+1.0=9.5\times10^{-4}\); \(\Delta V=0.0258\u{m^3}\approx25.8\) litres. Twice the hoop strain, because the cross-section widens in both directions across the vessel.
  6. Dimension check: \(\Delta d=\dfrac{\text{MPa}}{\text{MPa}}\times\text{mm}\) = mm ✓. Sense check: \(\varepsilon_l\) is small and positive because \(\nu=0.3\lt0.5\): the Poisson shrinkage from the big hoop stress almost cancels the longitudinal stretch.

\(\boxed{\sigma_h=100,\ \sigma_l=50\u{MPa};\ \Delta d=1.02\u{mm},\ \Delta L=0.60\u{mm},\ \Delta V=0.0258\u{m^3}}\)

You can now: find the hoop and longitudinal strains with the 2D Hooke's law; from them, the change in diameter, length and volume of a closed cylinder; and the same for a sphere.

7Stress on a weld or any inclined plane ~5 min

What is this?

Draw a slanted line on the sausage skin: that is a helical weld. The pull round and the pull along both tug across the line, each partly, so the pull across the weld is a blend of the two. Because the two pulls are unequal, they also try to slide the two sides of the line past each other: a shear along the weld. A weld straight round the sausage feels only the pull along it; a weld straight along feels only the pull round.

Many vessels are made by rolling a steel strip into a helix (spiral) and welding the seam (Dec 2016 A3(b), May 2014 A3(c)–(d)). The weld is weaker than the plate, so we need the normal stress across the weld and the shear stress along it. This is plane-stress transformation from S3, made easy because \(\sigma_h\) and \(\sigma_l\) are already principal (no shear on them).

Let the weld make angle \(\beta\) with a transverse plane (a plane cutting straight across). Then the normal to the weld makes angle \(\beta\) with the vessel axis. With \(x\) along the axis (\(\sigma_x=\sigma_l\)), \(y\) round the hoop (\(\sigma_y=\sigma_h\)), \(\tau_{xy}=0\), the transformation equations of S3 give:

Result · stresses on a helical weld \[\begin{aligned}\sigma_w&=\sigma_l\cos^2\beta+\sigma_h\sin^2\beta\\ &=\tfrac{\sigma_h+\sigma_l}{2}-\tfrac{\sigma_h-\sigma_l}{2}\cos2\beta,\\ \tau_w&=\tfrac{\sigma_h-\sigma_l}{2}\sin2\beta,\\ \sigma_{\parallel}&=\sigma_h+\sigma_l-\sigma_w.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the normal stress on the weld blends the axial and hoop stresses (all axial when \(\beta=0\), a circumferential weld; all hoop when \(\beta=90^\circ\), a longitudinal weld). The shear along the weld is the in-plane Mohr radius times \(\sin2\beta\). \(\sigma_\parallel\) is the normal stress along the weld direction (the sum of normal stresses on perpendicular planes is constant).

Reading the formula
\(\sigma_l\cos^2\beta\)
the share of the pull along the vessel felt across the weld: all of it when \(\beta=0\) (a weld straight round).
\(\sigma_h\sin^2\beta\)
the share of the pull round the vessel: all of it when \(\beta=90^\circ\) (a weld straight along).
\(\tau_w\)
the sliding along the weld. It comes from the two pulls being unequal, so it has the factor \(\sigma_h-\sigma_l\); it is zero for both straight welds and largest at \(45^\circ\).
\(\sigma_\parallel\)
the pull along the weld line itself; the pulls across and along always add to \(\sigma_h+\sigma_l\).

With Mohr's circle (what the papers ask you to sketch)

  1. Do: Plot \(L=(\sigma_l,0)\) and \(H=(\sigma_h,0)\) on the \(\sigma\)-axis. The circle has centre \(C=\tfrac12(\sigma_h+\sigma_l)\) and radius \(R=\tfrac12(\sigma_h-\sigma_l)\). \(L\) and \(H\) are the pull along and the pull round, with no shear; every slanted line on the skin is a point on the circle through them.
  2. Do: The axial face (normal along the axis) is point \(L\). The weld face's normal is turned \(\beta\) from the axis, so rotate \(2\beta\) round the circle from \(L\), in the same sense. A face turned through \(180^\circ\) is the same face again, so half a turn on the skin is one full turn on the circle: angles double.
  3. Do: Read off \(\sigma_w=C-R\cos2\beta\) and \(|\tau_w|=R\sin2\beta\). The diametrically opposite point gives the face along the weld: \(\sigma_\parallel=C+R\cos2\beta\). A point's distance along the \(\sigma\)-axis is the pull across that face, and its height is the sliding along it.

Try it · weld angle on Mohr's circle (\(\sigma_h=100\), \(\sigma_l=50\u{MPa}\))

Left: Mohr's circle with the axial point \(L\), the hoop point \(H\), and the weld point \(W\) at \(2\beta\) from \(L\). Right: the element aligned with the weld, showing \(\sigma_w\) across it and \(\tau_w\) along it. The shear on the weld is largest at \(\beta=45^\circ\).

Worked example · written for these notes · 35° weld

The tank of Section 6 (\(\sigma_h=100\), \(\sigma_l=50\u{MPa}\)) is welded along a helix at \(35^\circ\) to a transverse plane. Find the stresses on the weld.

  1. \(C=75\u{MPa}\), \(R=25\u{MPa}\), \(2\beta=70^\circ\).
  2. \(\sigma_w=75-25\cos70^\circ=66.4\u{MPa}\) (check: \(50\cos^235^\circ+100\sin^235^\circ=33.55+32.90=66.45\) ✓).
  3. \(\tau_w=25\sin70^\circ=23.5\u{MPa}\).
  4. Along the weld: \(\sigma_\parallel=150-66.4=83.6\u{MPa}\).

\(\boxed{\sigma_w=66.4\u{MPa}\ \text{(tension across)},\ \ \tau_w=23.5\u{MPa}\ \text{(along the weld)}}\)

Direction of \(\tau_w\): on the weld face whose outward normal points (partly) along the \(+\)axis, \(\tau_w\) acts along the weld in the sense that has a component in the \(+\)hoop direction, i.e. towards the larger stress (from the traction \((\sigma_l\cos\beta,\ \sigma_h\sin\beta)\) resolved along the weld). The element in the figure above is drawn this way.

You can now: find the normal stress across and the shear stress along a helical weld, by formula or by Mohr's circle; measure the weld angle from the transverse plane; and find the angle of largest weld shear.

8Where the cap meets the cylinder ~2 min

What is this?

Under pressure each part of the vessel swells by its own amount. The cylinder body, pulled twice as hard round as along, swells more than a hemispherical cap of the same thickness. But they are welded together and must stay the same size at the join. So the end of the cylinder is held in by the cap and bends near the weld, and extra bending stresses appear there.

May 2014 A3(b) asks you to compare the radius increase of hemispherical end caps with that of the cylinder they are welded to. From Section 6:

\[\begin{aligned}\Delta r_{\rm cyl}&=\frac{pr^2}{2tE}(2-\nu),\\ \Delta r_{\rm cap}&=\frac{pr^2}{2t_{\rm cap}E}(1-\nu).\end{aligned}\]

In words: with the same thickness, the cylinder grows more than twice as much as the cap. At the joint they must have the same radius, so the joint is forced to bend: local discontinuity stresses (bending stresses) appear near the weld.

Result · matching expansion

Setting \(\Delta r_{\rm cap}=\Delta r_{\rm cyl}\) gives the cap thickness that removes the mismatch:The \(p\), \(r^2\) and \(E\) are the same on both sides and cancel, leaving only the thicknesses and the Poisson terms.

\[t_{\rm cap}=t\,\frac{1-\nu}{2-\nu}\quad(=0.41\,t\ \text{for}\ \nu=0.3).\]

In words: a thinner cap stretches more, so making the hemispherical cap about 40% of the cylinder's thickness lets both grow by the same amount. (The price: the thin cap's membrane stress \(\tfrac{pr}{2t_{\rm cap}}=\tfrac{2-\nu}{2(1-\nu)}\,\tfrac{pr}{t}\) is then about \(1.2\,\sigma_h\) for \(\nu=0.3\), higher than anywhere in the cylinder; designers balance the two.)

Beyond the handouts · numbers from May 2014

Mean radius 400 mm, cylinder 10 mm, caps 8 mm, \(p=1.2\u{MPa}\), \(E=200\u{GPa}\), \(\nu=0.3\): cap stress \(30\u{MPa}\), \(\Delta r_{\rm cap}=0.042\u{mm}\); cylinder \(\sigma_h=48\), \(\sigma_l=24\u{MPa}\), \(\Delta r_{\rm cyl}=0.0816\u{mm}\). The cylinder grows almost twice as much; the caps would need about \(0.41\times10=4.1\u{mm}\) to match. Full solution in the exam taster.

You can now: compare the radius increase of a hemispherical cap with that of the cylinder; explain the discontinuity (bending) stresses at the joint; and find the cap thickness that matches the growth.

9Bolts, rivets and joints ~7 min

What is this?

Think of the lid of a pressure cooker. The gas pushes on the lid with pressure × the area inside the seal, whatever the lid's shape, and the bolts are the hands that hold it down. Each bolt can only pull so hard, so divide the push by what one bolt can carry. Seams work the same way: the skin's pull arriving at a seam must pass through the rivets.

The ends of a vessel must be held on, and plates must be joined. Four past-paper parts (May 2013, Dec 2014, May 2019) ask how many bolts or rivets are needed, or what pressure a joint can take. All use one idea: the force the pressure puts on a part = pressure × the projected area it acts on (Section 2), and that force must be carried by the fasteners.

Bolted end caps

  1. Do: Force on the cap: \(F=p\times\dfrac{\pi}{4}D^2\), with \(D\) the diameter inside the seal (gasket or vessel bore). This is the push of the gas on the lid: only the area the gas can reach, inside the seal, counts.
  2. Do: Capacity of one bolt: \(F_b=\sigma_{\rm allow}A_b\), with \(A_b\) the bolt's (net) tensile area. Each bolt is one hand that can safely pull at most its allowable stress times its area.
  3. Do: Number of bolts: \(n\ge F/F_b\), rounded up to a whole number (often to a multiple suiting the pattern). Rounding down would leave the push bigger than the bolts can safely hold.
Result · number of bolts \[n\ \ge\ \frac{p\,\tfrac{\pi}{4}D^2}{\sigma_{\rm allow}\,A_b}\]

In words: total pressure force on the cap ÷ what one bolt can safely carry, rounded up.

Reading the formula
\(p\,\tfrac{\pi}{4}D^2\)
the push of the gas on the lid (pressure × projected area).
\(\sigma_{\rm allow}A_b\)
the safe pull of one bolt. With pretension, use only the stress still available (below).
\(\ge\)
the bolts together must at least match the push, so round up.
What is this? (bolt pretension)

Screw a jar lid on tight before anything pushes on it: the seal is already squashed. When the pressure inside rises, it first just eases that squeeze instead of opening a gap, so the jar does not leak. The price is that the bolts are already partly loaded before the pressure arrives.

Definition · bolt pretension (initial tightening)

Bolts are tightened before the vessel is pressurised, so they already carry an initial stress, and they squeeze the gasket (a soft sealing ring) between the flanges. Why (Dec 2014 A2(c)):

  • to keep the gasket compressed, so the joint stays tight and does not leak when the pressure tries to push the cap off;
  • to stop the flanges separating, which would let the full pressure load cycle on and off in the bolts (fatigue) and let the cap knock;
  • the pressure then mostly reduces the gasket compression instead of adding all of its load to the bolts.

In the textbook simplification used in the papers, only the part of the allowable stress not already used by the pretension is available for the pressure load: \(\sigma_{\rm available}=\sigma_{\rm allow}-\sigma_{\rm initial}\).

Worked example · Dec 2014 A2(a) · 5 marks

Compressed-air tank, gauge pressure 3 MPa, outside diameter 500 mm, wall 10 mm, flat end caps bolted on with a gasket. How many Ø40 mm bolts are needed if the allowable bolt stress is 100 MPa, of which 60 MPa is the initial stress?

  1. Inner diameter \(D=500-2(10)=480\u{mm}\). Force on a cap: \(F=3\times\tfrac\pi4(480)^2=542.9\times10^3\u{N}=542.9\u{kN}\).
  2. Stress available for the pressure: \(100-60=40\u{MPa}\).The bolts already use 60 MPa keeping the gasket squeezed; only the rest is free for the pressure (the reading in the flag below). Bolt area \(\tfrac\pi4(40)^2=1257\u{mm^2}\); one bolt carries \(40\times1257=50.27\u{kN}\).
  3. \(n\ge542.9/50.27=10.8\).

\(\boxed{n=11\ \text{bolts}}\) (12 if an even pattern is wanted).

"of which 60 MPa is the initial stress" is read in the Pytel–Singer way: the pretension uses 60 MPa, leaving 40 MPa for the pressure. If the full 100 MPa were available, \(n=4.32\to5\).

Riveted seams

What is this? (riveted seams)

Picture the skin made of two strips that overlap along a seam and are pinned together. The skin's pull arriving at the seam has to pass through the pins. A seam along the sausage carries the big pull round it; a seam round the sausage carries the smaller pull along it.

Definitions · lap joint, pitch, single shear

In a single-lap joint two plates overlap and are joined by one row of rivets. The pitch \(s\) is the spacing between rivet centres along the seam. Each rivet is cut by one plane between the plates (single shear), so it can carry \(\tau_{\rm allow}\times\tfrac\pi4d^2\).

  • Longitudinal seam (along the cylinder): it carries the hoop force. One pitch length \(s\) of seam carries \(\sigma_h t s=p\,r\,s\).
  • Circumferential seam (round the cylinder, e.g. where the end is attached): it carries the longitudinal force, \(\sigma_l t s=\tfrac12p\,r\,s\) per pitch.
Result · pressure a riveted seam can hold \[\begin{aligned}\text{longitudinal seam: }&p\,r\,s=\tau_{\rm allow}\tfrac\pi4d^2,\\ \text{circumferential seam: }&\tfrac12p\,r\,s=\tau_{\rm allow}\tfrac\pi4d^2.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the wall force arriving in one pitch of seam must be carried by the one rivet in that pitch. Check both seams; the smaller pressure governs.

Worked example · May 2013 A1(b) · 9 marks · older format

A closed cylindrical steel vessel of 1.8 m diameter, 10 mm sheet, made of two semi-cylinders joined by longitudinal single-lap seams with 12 mm rivets at 36 mm pitch; dished ends attached by circumferential single-lap seams with 8 mm rivets at 30 mm pitch. Rivet shear stress must not exceed 60 MPa. Find the maximum pressure and the resulting wall stresses.

  1. \(r=900\u{mm}\) (diameter taken as the thin-wall diameter).
  2. Longitudinal seam: one rivet \(60\times\tfrac\pi4(12)^2=6786\u{N}\). So \(p(900)(36)=6786\Rightarrow p=0.209\u{MPa}\).
  3. Circumferential seam: one rivet \(60\times\tfrac\pi4(8)^2=3016\u{N}\). So \(\tfrac12p(900)(30)=3016\Rightarrow p=0.223\u{MPa}\).
  4. The smaller governs: \(p_{\max}=0.209\u{MPa}\).Each seam gives the pressure it can hold; the vessel is only as strong as its weaker seam. Then \(\sigma_h=0.209(900)/10=18.8\u{MPa}\), \(\sigma_l=9.42\u{MPa}\).

\(\boxed{p_{\max}=0.209\u{MPa};\ \ \sigma_h=18.8\u{MPa},\ \ \sigma_l=9.42\u{MPa}}\)

The low wall stresses show that the rivets, not the plate, are the weak link: a reason modern vessels are welded.

Beyond the handouts · joint efficiency

Codes allow for a weaker seam with a joint efficiency \(\eta\) (0 to 1): \(\sigma_h=\dfrac{pr}{\eta t}\). Example: \(p=1\u{MPa}\), \(r=1000\u{mm}\), \(t=10\u{mm}\), \(\eta=0.8\) gives \(125\u{MPa}\) instead of 100.

You can now: find the force on an end cap from pressure × projected area; find the number of bolts, allowing for pretension and rounding up; explain why bolts are pretensioned; and find the pressure a riveted seam can hold.

10Composite and shrink-fit cylinders ~5 min

What is this?

Slide one sausage skin tightly inside another and pressurise. The two skins touch all the way round, so they must stretch by the same amount, like two springs side by side pulled together. The push is shared between them, and the stiffer skin, which needs more force for the same stretch, takes the bigger share.

Dec 2017 A3(b): an aluminium tube is shrunk onto a steel tube (heated so it expands, slid on, then cooled so it grips), and the pair is pressurised. How is the pressure shared?

Definitions · composite cylinder, interface pressure

A composite cylinder is two (or more) tubes, one tightly inside the other, acting together. The pressure between them is the interface pressure \(\bar p\). It pushes the outer tube outward (hoop tension) and the inner tube inward (it reduces the inner tube's tension).

For two thin tubes of nearly the same radius \(r\), inner tube (material 1, thickness \(t_1\)) and outer tube (material 2, \(t_2\)), internal pressure \(p\), ends free (\(\sigma_l=0\)):

  1. Do: Equilibrium (cut both tubes along a diameter, as in Section 2): the total hoop force carries the whole pressure, \[\sigma_1t_1+\sigma_2t_2=pr.\] The lengthwise cut of Section 2, but now two layers of skin share the push on the cut face.
  2. Do: Compatibility (the tubes stay in contact, so their radii grow by the same amount): equal hoop strains. With \(\sigma_l=0\), \(\varepsilon_h=\sigma/E\), so \[\frac{\sigma_1}{E_1}=\frac{\sigma_2}{E_2}.\] Equilibrium gives one equation for two unknown stresses; the equal stretch of the two skins gives the second.
  3. Do: Solve the two equations. Interface pressure from the outer tube alone: \(\sigma_2=\bar p\,r/t_2\). The only pressure on the outer tube is the interface pressure, so it is a thin cylinder with \(\bar p\) inside.
Result · sharing the pressure \[\begin{aligned}\sigma_1&=\frac{E_1\,pr}{E_1t_1+E_2t_2},\\ \sigma_2&=\frac{E_2\,pr}{E_1t_1+E_2t_2}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the tubes share the hoop load in proportion to "stiffness × thickness". The stiffer tube takes more stress, because both must stretch the same amount. This is the same reasoning as a statically indeterminate bar (S1 Section 11): equilibrium alone is not enough, so a deformation condition is added.

Reading the formula
\(pr\)
the total hoop force per unit length on the lengthwise cut (Section 2), to be shared.
\(E_1t_1+E_2t_2\)
the combined stiffness of the two skins: material stiffness × thickness for each.
\(E_1\) or \(E_2\) on top
each tube's stress is in proportion to its own \(E\): for the same stretch, the stiffer material is pulled harder.
Worked example · written for these notes · steel liner in a brass tube

A steel tube (\(t_1=5\u{mm}\), \(E_1=200\u{GPa}\)) fits tightly inside a brass tube (\(t_2=3\u{mm}\), \(E_2=100\u{GPa}\)); mean radius 250 mm; internal pressure 3 MPa; ends free. Find the hoop stresses and the interface pressure.

  1. Equilibrium: \(5\sigma_s+3\sigma_b=3(250)=750\u{N/mm}\).
  2. Compatibility: \(\sigma_s/200=\sigma_b/100\Rightarrow\sigma_s=2\sigma_b\).
  3. \(10\sigma_b+3\sigma_b=750\Rightarrow\sigma_b=57.7\u{MPa}\), \(\sigma_s=115.4\u{MPa}\).
  4. \(\bar p=\sigma_bt_2/r=57.7(3)/250=0.692\u{MPa}\). Check the inner tube: net pressure \(3-0.692\), \(\sigma_s=(2.308)(250)/5=115.4\u{MPa}\) ✓.

\(\boxed{\sigma_{\rm steel}=115.4\u{MPa},\ \sigma_{\rm brass}=57.7\u{MPa},\ \bar p=0.692\u{MPa}}\)

Beyond the handouts · stresses from the shrink fit itself

If the outer tube's inner radius is made smaller than the inner tube's outer radius by a radial interference \(\delta\), assembling them creates an interface pressure even with no internal pressure. The outer tube grows by \(\bar pr^2/(E_2t_2)\) and the inner shrinks by \(\bar pr^2/(E_1t_1)\); together they make up \(\delta\):

\[\delta=\bar p\,r^2\left(\frac{1}{E_1t_1}+\frac{1}{E_2t_2}\right).\]

In words: the misfit is shared between stretching the outer tube and squashing the inner. Example: two steel tubes, \(r=100\u{mm}\), \(t=5\u{mm}\) each, \(\delta=0.05\u{mm}\) give \(\bar p=2.5\u{MPa}\) and hoop stresses \(\pm50\u{MPa}\) (outer tension, inner compression). Dec 2017 asks only for the additional stress due to pressure, which the method above gives; the fit stresses simply add on.

You can now: write the equilibrium and equal-strain equations for two tubes; solve for each tube's hoop stress and the interface pressure; and (beyond the handouts) find the interface pressure from a shrink-fit interference.

12Common mistakes

✗Using the diameter in \(pr/t\). ✓\(r\) is the radius. A "1.5 m diameter" vessel has \(r=750\u{mm}\). ✗Mixing GPa and MPa in the strain (getting strains of 0.5 instead of \(5\times10^{-4}\)). ✓Write \(E=200\u{GPa}=200\,000\u{MPa}\); keep stresses in MPa and lengths in mm. ✗\(\varepsilon_h=\sigma_h/E\), forgetting the Poisson term. ✓\(\varepsilon_h=(\sigma_h-\nu\sigma_l)/E\): the wall is stressed both ways. ✗Giving only the in-plane maximum shear \(pr/4t\). ✓The absolute maximum is \(pr/2t\) (with \(\sigma_r=0\)); give both and label them. ✗Measuring the weld angle from the axis when the paper measures it from the transverse plane. ✓Angle from the transverse plane = angle of the weld's normal from the axis: \(\sigma_w=\sigma_l\cos^2\beta+\sigma_h\sin^2\beta\). ✗Rotating \(\beta\) (not \(2\beta\)) on Mohr's circle. ✓Angles double on the circle. ✗Rounding the number of bolts down (10.8 → 10). ✓Always round up: 10 bolts would be over-stressed. ✗Using \(\sigma_l=pr/2t\) when the ends are free to slide. ✓No end thrust on the wall ⇒ \(\sigma_l=0\) (Dec 2017).

13Find the mistake

1 · Change in diameter

"Dec 2018 numbers: \(r=1000\u{mm}\), \(t=10\u{mm}\), \(p=0.8\u{MPa}\), \(E=200\u{GPa}\), \(\nu=0.25\). \(\sigma_h=80\u{MPa}\), so \(\varepsilon_h=80/200\,000=4\times10^{-4}\) and \(\Delta d=4\times10^{-4}\times2000=0.80\u{mm}\)."

Hint

What does the longitudinal stress do to the hoop strain?

Answer

The Poisson effect of \(\sigma_l=40\u{MPa}\) is missing: \(\varepsilon_h=(80-0.25\times40)/200\,000=3.5\times10^{-4}\), so \(\Delta d=0.70\u{mm}\).

2 · Sphere shear

"In a sphere \(\sigma_1=\sigma_2=pr/2t\), so the maximum shear stress is \((\sigma_1-\sigma_2)/2=0\). Spheres have no shear anywhere."

Hint

Is that the only pair of principal stresses?

Answer

That is only the in-plane maximum. With \(\sigma_r=0\) as the third principal stress, the absolute maximum is \((pr/2t-0)/2=pr/4t\), on planes at \(45^\circ\) through the thickness.

3 · The weld

"Dec 2016: \(\sigma_h=75\), \(\sigma_a=37.5\u{MPa}\), weld at \(30^\circ\) to a transverse plane. \(\sigma_w=\sigma_a\sin^230^\circ+\sigma_h\cos^230^\circ=9.4+56.3=65.6\u{MPa}\)."

Hint

When \(\beta=0\) (a circumferential weld), which stress should act across the weld?

Answer

Sine and cosine are swapped. A circumferential weld (\(\beta=0\)) is pulled across by the axial stress, so \(\sigma_w=\sigma_a\cos^2\beta+\sigma_h\sin^2\beta=28.1+18.75=46.9\u{MPa}\).

14Concept checks

Written for these notes.

1 · Doubling

A cylinder's radius is doubled with the same wall and pressure. What happens to \(\sigma_h\) and to \(\Delta d\)?

Answer

\(\sigma_h=pr/t\) doubles; the strain doubles too, and \(\Delta d=\varepsilon_hd\) has \(d\) doubled as well, so \(\Delta d\) becomes four times larger.

2 · Which seam?

A cylinder is made with one longitudinal weld and two circumferential welds of equal strength. Which is more likely to fail, and why?

Answer

The longitudinal weld: it is pulled apart by the hoop stress, which is twice the longitudinal stress acting across the circumferential welds.

3 · Longitudinal strain sign

For what Poisson's ratio would a closed cylinder not change length under pressure?

Answer

\(\varepsilon_l=\tfrac{pr}{2tE}(1-2\nu)=0\) when \(\nu=0.5\) (an incompressible material, like rubber). For metals (\(\nu\approx0.3\)) it gets slightly longer.

4 · Best weld angle

At what helix angle \(\beta\) is the shear along a spiral weld largest, and how big is it?

Answer

At \(\beta=45^\circ\) (\(\sin2\beta=1\)): \(\tau_w=\tfrac12(\sigma_h-\sigma_l)=pr/4t\), the in-plane maximum.

5 · Projected area

A hemispherical cap of radius \(r\) is bolted to a cylinder. What total force do the bolts carry from a gauge pressure \(p\)?

Answer

\(p\pi r^2\): the pressure's net force equals \(p\) times the projected area, whatever the cap's shape (flat, dished or hemispherical).

6 · Vacuum

A thin cylinder has a vacuum inside (\(p=-0.1\u{MPa}\) gauge). What changes?

Answer

The formulas still hold with \(p\) negative: the wall is in compression, \(\sigma_h=-0.1r/t\). But a thin wall in compression can buckle (crumple) long before it yields, which thin-wall stress theory does not cover.

Dec 2023 A1(c)9 marksalso Dec 2022 A3(a)–(b), Dec 2018 A3(b)S7.1 thin cylinder, Δd

Consider a closed cylindrical steel pressure vessel. The radius of the cylinder is 3 m and its wall thickness is 10 mm. Young's modulus of steel is 200 GPa and Poisson's ratio is 0.25. Assume that it is a thin wall vessel. (i) Determine the hoop and longitudinal stresses in the cylindrical wall caused by an internal pressure 0.9 MPa. [3] (ii) Calculate the change in diameter of the cylinder caused by pressurization. [6]

Hint

Radius 3000 mm. Strain needs both stresses and \(\nu\); then \(\Delta d=\varepsilon_hd\) with \(d=6000\u{mm}\).

Answer

Consider a thin-walled spherical pressure vessel with inner radius \(r\) and wall thickness \(t\). It is subjected to an internal gage pressure \(p\). (i) Derive vessel formulas for membrane stresses \(\sigma_\varphi\) and \(\sigma_t\). [4] (ii) Determine the maximum shear stresses \(\tau_{\max}\) for in-plane rotation of the stress element. [2] (iii) Neglecting stress in radial direction, determine the maximum shear stresses \(\tau_{\max}\) for out-of-plane rotation of the stress element. [4]

Hint

Hemisphere + gas as the free body (Section 4). Then Mohr's circles with \(\sigma_r=0\) (Section 5).

Answer

(i) Cut through the centre; FBD of a hemisphere and its gas: \(\sigma(2\pi rt)=p(\pi r^2)\Rightarrow\sigma_\varphi=\sigma_t=\dfrac{pr}{2t}\) (same for any cutting plane by symmetry).

(ii) \(\tau_{\max}=\dfrac{\sigma_\varphi-\sigma_t}{2}=0\): Mohr's circle for in-plane rotation is a point.

(iii) With \(\sigma_r=0\): \(\boxed{\tau_{\max}=\dfrac{pr/2t-0}{2}=\dfrac{pr}{4t}}\), on planes at \(45^\circ\) to the surface.
