Stress, Strain and Mohr's Circle

Every slide of the Statics chapter 3 handout (56 slides) explained from scratch: normal and shear stress, strain, Young's modulus, thermal strain, Poisson's ratio, shear and bulk moduli, Hooke's law in three dimensions, the stress transformation equations, principal stresses and Mohr's circle. Every worked example is solved in full, with sketches of the loaded part and of the stress element. Mohr's circle questions appear on almost every paper, so the interactive circle in Section 18 and the exam taster at the end are worth the time.

0Where this chapter sits

In S2 you found the internal forces \(N\), \(V\), \(M\) in a beam: the totals of what one piece does to the other across a cut. This chapter asks how those totals are spread over the cut (stress), how the material stretches in response (strain), and how the push-and-pull on a tiny square changes when you look at it from a different angle (Mohr's circle). The handout lists five topics (slide 2): (1) normal and shear stress; (2) strain, Young's modulus and thermal strain; (3) Hooke's law; (4) transformation of stress; (5) Mohr's circle.

What comes next uses this chapter directly: bending stress in S4 is a normal stress, torsion (S6) gives shear stress, and in pressure vessels (S7) you combine two normal stresses and use Mohr's circle on a weld.

Definition · stress

Stress is internal force per unit area on a cut: how hard the material on one side of the cut is pushing, pulling or dragging the material on the other side, per square metre. Unit: pascal, \(1\u{Pa}=1\u{N/m^2}\). Engineers usually use \(1\u{MPa}=10^6\u{Pa}=1\u{N/mm^2}\) (handy: newtons divided by square millimetres gives MPa directly).

Sign convention for this chapter
  • Normal stress \(\sigma\) (sigma): positive in tension (arrow pointing out of the face), negative in compression (arrow pointing into the face). Strain \(\varepsilon\) positive when the material lengthens.
  • Shear stress \(\tau_{xy}\) (tau): the stress on the face whose outward normal is \(+x\), acting in the \(y\)-direction. It is positive when, on the \(+x\) face, it points \(+y\). Then the pair of shear arrows on the two \(x\)-faces turns the element anticlockwise, which is how the handout states it (slides 37 and 48: "\(\tau_{xy}\) is assumed to be +ve for anti-clockwise direction"). On the \(+y\) face the matching \(\tau_{yx}=\tau_{xy}\) then points \(+x\).
  • Angles \(\theta\) of a rotated element are measured anticlockwise from \(x\) to the new axis \(x'\).
  • Mohr's circle: \(\sigma\) along the horizontal axis (right +), \(\tau\) up. Plot \(P(\sigma_x,\ \tau_{xy})\) for the \(x\)-face and \(P'(\sigma_y,\ -\tau_{xy})\) for the \(y\)-face. Turning the element by \(\theta\) anticlockwise moves the point by \(2\theta\) clockwise round the circle (slide 52).

Some textbooks (e.g. Hibbeler) plot \(\tau\) downward so both rotations are in the same sense. Do not mix the two; in the exam, draw the little sign sketch the handout asks for (slide 48: "must draw this to show +ve direction").

1Normal stress (slides 2–3)

Pull a straight bar with a force \(F\) at each end and imagine cutting it at \(c\)–\(c\), at right angles to its axis (slide 3). The left piece is in equilibrium only if the right piece pulls on it across the whole cut face. Those spread-out internal forces are the stresses.

Definition · normal stress (slide 3)

If the stresses act perpendicular to the cross-section of area \(A\) and are uniformly distributed over it, they are called normal stress:

\[\sigma=\frac{N}{A}=\frac{F}{A}.\]

In words: normal stress = normal force ÷ area of the cut. Twice the force on the same bar doubles the stress; the same force on a bar twice as thick halves it.

\(N\) is the normal force of S2 (here \(N=F\)). For a bar in compression \(N\) and \(\sigma\) are negative.

2Stress on an inclined cut (slides 4–6)

Now cut the same bar along a line tilted at angle \(\varphi\) (phi) to the cross-section (slide 4). The internal force on that cut is still \(F\) along the bar, but it is no longer perpendicular to the cut face. Split the stress on the inclined face into

  • a component \(\sigma\) perpendicular to the cut (normal stress), and
  • a component \(\tau\) along the cut (shear stress: it tries to slide one piece past the other, like the blades of scissors).

The inclined face is longer than the straight cross-section, so its area is \(A^*=A/\cos\varphi\) (slide 4). Equilibrium of the left piece (slide 5), resolving along the bar (→) and across it (↑):

\[\begin{aligned}\rightarrow:&\quad \sigma A^*\cos\varphi+\tau A^*\sin\varphi-F=0,\\ \uparrow:&\quad \sigma A^*\sin\varphi-\tau A^*\cos\varphi=0.\end{aligned}\]

Solving the two equations with \(A^*=A/\cos\varphi\):

Result · stresses on an inclined section (slides 5–6) \[\sigma=\frac FA\cos^2\varphi,\qquad\tau=\frac FA\sin\varphi\cos\varphi.\]

With \(\cos^2\varphi=\tfrac12(1+\cos2\varphi)\), \(\sin\varphi\cos\varphi=\tfrac12\sin2\varphi\) and \(\sigma_0=F/A\) (the stress on the straight cross-section):

\[\sigma=\frac{\sigma_0}{2}(1+\cos2\varphi),\qquad\tau=\frac{\sigma_0}{2}\sin2\varphi.\]

In words: as the cut tilts, the normal stress falls from \(\sigma_0\) to zero and a shear stress appears. \(\sigma_{\max}=\sigma_0\) on the straight cut (\(\varphi=0\)); \(\tau_{\max}=\sigma_0/2\) on the cut at \(\varphi=\pi/4=45^\circ\) (slide 6).

Try it · stresses on a cut at angle \(\varphi\), in units of \(\sigma_0=F/A\)

\(\sigma/\sigma_0=\cos^2\varphi\)\(\tau/\sigma_0=\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\)

The shear curve peaks at \(45^\circ\) with half the axial stress. This is why ductile metals pulled in a test machine fail by slipping along planes at about \(45^\circ\).

Example · written for these notes

A bar carries \(\sigma_0=100\u{MPa}\) in tension. On a cut at \(\varphi=30^\circ\):

\[\begin{aligned}\sigma&=100\cos^230^\circ=\boxed{75\u{MPa}},\\ \tau&=\tfrac{100}{2}\sin60^\circ=\boxed{43.3\u{MPa}}.\end{aligned}\]

Slide 35 repeats this result as the starting point for principal stresses (Section 14): one force gives \(\sigma_{\max}=\sigma_0\) at \(\varphi=0\) and \(\tau_{\max}=\sigma_0/2\) at \(\varphi=\pi/4\).

3Allowable stress and a tapered bar (slides 7–8)

If the cross-section changes along the bar, so does the stress (slide 7):

\[\sigma(x)=\frac{N(x)}{A(x)}.\]
Definition · allowable stress (slide 7)

In design, the size of the stress must not exceed an allowable stress \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}\): a safe limit set for the material, well below the stress at which it would yield or break.

\[|\sigma|\le\sigma_{\text{allow}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad A_{\text{req}}=\frac{|N|}{\sigma_{\text{allow}}}.\]

In words: the smallest cross-section you may use is the force divided by the allowable stress.

Example · written for these notes

A round steel tie carries \(N=50\u{kN}\); \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}=160\u{MPa}\). Then \(A_{\text{req}}=50\,000\u{N}/160\u{N/mm^2}=312.5\u{mm^2}\), so \(d=\sqrt{4A/\pi}=\boxed{19.9\u{mm}}\): choose a 20 mm bar.

Worked example · slide 8 · tapered bar in compression

A solid round bar of length \(l\) widens linearly from radius \(r_0\) to \(2r_0\). It is squeezed by a force \(F\) at each end. Find the stress at any section.

  1. Radius at distance \(x\): \(r(x)=r_0+\dfrac{r_0}{l}x=r_0\left(1+\dfrac xl\right)\); area \(A(x)=\pi r^2(x)\).
  2. Every cut carries the same normal force \(N=-F\) (compression).
  3. \[\sigma(x)=\frac{N}{A(x)}=\boxed{\frac{-F}{\pi r_0^2\left(1+\dfrac xl\right)^2}}\]

In words: the stress is largest at the thin end (\(x=0\)) and falls to a quarter of that at the thick end, because the area there is four times bigger.

4Strain (slides 9–10)

Definition · strain (slide 9) \[\varepsilon=\frac{\Delta l}{l}\]

Strain \(\varepsilon\) (epsilon) is the elongation \(\Delta l\) divided by the original (undeformed) length \(l\). It has no units (m/m); values are tiny, e.g. \(10^{-3}\), sometimes quoted as "1000 microstrain" (\(\mu\varepsilon\)).

In words: strain is the fractional change of length: a 2 m bar that grows by 1 mm has strain \(0.5\times10^{-3}\).

Local strain (slide 10). If the cross-section varies, or the bar carries forces along its length (such as its own weight), different bits stretch by different amounts. Let \(u(x)\) be the displacement of the cross-section that started at \(x\) (how far it moves along the bar). A short piece \(\mathrm dx\) becomes \(\mathrm dx+\mathrm du\) long, so

\[\varepsilon(x)=\frac{\mathrm du}{\mathrm dx}.\]

In words: the local strain is the rate at which the displacement changes along the bar. If the whole bar stretches evenly, \(u\) grows linearly and \(\varepsilon\) is constant, giving back \(\Delta l/l\).

5The stress–strain diagram and Young's modulus (slides 11–12)

Pull a test specimen (slide 11 shows a dog-bone bar of length \(l\), area \(A\)) and plot stress against strain:

Stress–strain diagram of a ductile metal (sketch after slide 11)

Red: engineering stress \(\sigma=F/A\) with the original area. Dashed grey: true stress \(\sigma_t=F/A_a\) with the actual (shrinking) area. Green dashed: unloading runs back parallel to the first straight line, leaving a permanent strain \(\varepsilon_{pl}\).

  • Proportional limit \(\sigma_P\): up to here the graph is a straight line through the origin.
  • Yield stress \(\sigma_Y\): above this the material flows (deforms permanently) without much more stress.
  • Unloading after yielding follows a line parallel to the elastic line; the strain left over at zero stress is the plastic strain \(\varepsilon_{pl}\).
  • True stress \(\sigma_t=F/A_a\), with \(A_a\) the actual area, keeps rising; the engineering stress \(F/A\) drops near the end because the bar necks (gets thinner locally).
Law · Hooke's law (slide 11) \[\sigma=E\varepsilon\]

\(E\) is the modulus of elasticity, or Young's modulus: the slope of the straight part of the diagram. It has the units of stress (MPa or GPa), because \(\varepsilon\) has none. The slide notes that this form ignores the change of cross-sectional area.

In words: in the elastic range, stress is proportional to strain; a stiff material (big \(E\)) needs a big stress for a small stretch. Like a spring: force ∝ extension, with \(E\) as a "spring constant" for the material itself.

6Thermal strain and the change in length (slides 13–14)

Definition · thermal strain (slide 13) \[\varepsilon_T=\alpha_T\,\Delta T\]

A temperature rise \(\Delta T\), assumed uniform over the section and along the bar, makes the material expand freely by a strain proportional to \(\Delta T\). \(\alpha_T\) is the coefficient of thermal expansion (1/°C or 1/K).

The total strain is the elastic part plus the thermal part (slide 13):

\[\varepsilon=\frac{\sigma}{E}+\alpha_T\Delta T.\]

With \(\varepsilon=\Delta l/l\) and \(\sigma=F/A\) for a uniform bar:

Result · change in length (slides 13–14) \[\begin{aligned}\Delta l&=\frac{Fl}{EA}+\alpha_T\Delta T\,l;\\ \Delta T=0:\ \ \Delta l&=\frac{Fl}{EA}.\end{aligned}\]

If the area or the force varies along the bar (slide 14):

\[\Delta l=\int_0^l\left(\frac{N}{EA}+\alpha_T\Delta T\right)\mathrm dx.\]

In words: a bar gets longer by "force × length ÷ (stiffness × area)" plus "expansion coefficient × temperature rise × length". \(EA\) is called the axial rigidity: the bigger it is, the less the bar stretches.

Examples · written for these notes
  1. Free expansion. A 2 m steel rod (\(\alpha_T=1.2\times10^{-5}\)/°C) is heated by 40 °C with no force: \(\Delta l=1.2\times10^{-5}\times40\times2000\u{mm}=\boxed{0.96\u{mm}}\).
  2. Fully restrained. The same rod between rigid walls cannot grow: \(\Delta l=0\Rightarrow \sigma/E=-\alpha_T\Delta T\), so \(\sigma=-E\alpha_T\Delta T=-210\,000\times1.2\times10^{-5}\times40=\boxed{-100.8\u{MPa}}\) (compression). Note the length cancels.
  3. Tapered bar of slide 8. With \(N=-F\) and \(A=\pi r_0^2(1+x/l)^2\), the integral gives \(\Delta l=-\dfrac{Fl}{2\pi Er_0^2}\): half the shortening of a bar of constant radius \(r_0\).

Example 3 is beyond the slides (they stop at the stress); it shows how the integral formula is used.

7Example: a steel cylinder inside a copper tube (slides 15–17)

A solid steel cylinder (area \(A_S\), modulus \(E_S\), length \(l\)) sits inside a copper tube (\(A_C\), \(E_C\), same length \(l\)). A rigid plate on top squeezes the assembly onto a rigid floor with a force \(F\). Find the stresses in each part and the shortening (slides 15–16).

This problem is statically indeterminate (S1 Section 11): equilibrium gives one equation for two unknown forces. The missing equation comes from the deformation: the plate is rigid, so both parts shorten by the same amount. This "equilibrium + compatibility" recipe returns in S5, S6 and S7.

  1. Equilibrium of the plate (forces taken as compressive, positive): \(F_C+F_S=F\).
  2. Each part's shortening (Section 6): \(\Delta l_C=\dfrac{F_Cl}{E_CA_C}\), \(\Delta l_S=\dfrac{F_Sl}{E_SA_S}\).
  3. Compatibility \(\Delta l_C=\Delta l_S\): \[\frac{F_Cl}{E_CA_C}=\frac{F_Sl}{E_SA_S}\ \Rightarrow\ F_S=\frac{E_SA_S}{E_CA_C}F_C.\]
  4. Substitute into equilibrium: \(F_C\left(1+\dfrac{E_SA_S}{E_CA_C}\right)=F\).
Result · slide 17 \[\begin{aligned}F_C&=\frac{E_CA_C}{E_CA_C+E_SA_S}F,\\ F_S&=\frac{E_SA_S}{E_CA_C+E_SA_S}F,\\ \sigma_C&=\frac{E_C}{E_CA_C+E_SA_S}F,\\ \sigma_S&=\frac{E_S}{E_CA_C+E_SA_S}F,\\ \Delta l_C&=\Delta l_S\\&=\frac{Fl}{E_CA_C+E_SA_S}.\end{aligned}\]

(The stresses are compressive; magnitudes shown.)

In words: the parts share the load in proportion to their axial rigidities \(EA\): the stiffer part takes more. The assembly behaves like one bar of rigidity \(E_CA_C+E_SA_S\) (two springs side by side: stiffnesses add).

Numbers · written for these notes

Steel cylinder \(d=40\u{mm}\): \(A_S=1257\u{mm^2}\), \(E_S=200\u{GPa}\). Copper tube 60 mm outside, 44 mm inside: \(A_C=1307\u{mm^2}\), \(E_C=110\u{GPa}\). \(F=200\u{kN}\), \(l=300\u{mm}\).

\[\begin{aligned}E_CA_C&=1.438\times10^8\u{N},\\ E_SA_S&=2.513\times10^8\u{N},\\ \text{sum}&=3.951\times10^8\u{N},\\ F_C&=72.8\u{kN},\quad F_S=127.2\u{kN},\\ \sigma_C&=55.7\u{MPa},\\ \sigma_S&=101.2\u{MPa},\\ \Delta l&=\frac{200\,000\times300}{3.951\times10^8}\\&=\boxed{0.152\u{mm}}.\end{aligned}\]

(Both stresses compressive.)

Check: \(\sigma_S/\sigma_C=E_S/E_C=1.82\) ✓, since both parts have the same strain.

8Four elastic constants; the Poisson effect (slides 18–20)

Slide 18 lists four numbers that measure how stiff a material is:

Young's modulus \(E\)resistance to stretching under a normal stress (Section 5).
Poisson's ratio \(\nu\)how much the material shrinks sideways when stretched (this section).
Shear modulus \(G\)resistance to distortion (change of shape) under shear stress (Section 10).
Bulk modulus \(k\)resistance to uniform compression from all sides (Section 12).

Pull a rectangular block with a tensile stress \(\sigma_x\) (slide 19). It gets longer in \(x\) (strain \(\varepsilon_x\)) and also thinner in \(y\) and \(z\). This sideways shrinking is the lateral contraction or Poisson effect.

Definition · Poisson's ratio (slide 19) \[\begin{aligned}\nu&=-\frac{\text{lateral strain}}{\text{axial strain}},\\ \varepsilon_y&=-\nu\varepsilon_x,\quad\varepsilon_z=-\nu\varepsilon_x.\end{aligned}\]

\(\nu\) ("nu") is a positive number, so the minus sign makes the lateral strain negative (shrinking) when the axial strain is positive.

In words: stretch a rubber band and it gets thinner. Poisson's ratio says how many units thinner (as a strain) for each unit longer.

9Multi-axial stress and strain (slides 21–22)

When \(\sigma_x\), \(\sigma_y\) and \(\sigma_z\) all act at once (slide 21), each one stretches the material along its own direction and, by the Poisson effect, shrinks it along the other two. For a linear material the effects simply add (superposition, slide 22):

Result · strains under three normal stresses (slide 22) \[\begin{aligned}\varepsilon_x&=\frac{1}{E}\left(\sigma_x-\nu\sigma_y-\nu\sigma_z\right),\\ \varepsilon_y&=\frac{1}{E}\left(\sigma_y-\nu\sigma_x-\nu\sigma_z\right),\\ \varepsilon_z&=\frac{1}{E}\left(\sigma_z-\nu\sigma_x-\nu\sigma_y\right).\end{aligned}\]

In words: the strain in one direction = (its own stress minus \(\nu\) times each of the other two stresses) ÷ \(E\).

Watch (slide 22)

Inside the derivation the slide writes \(\varepsilon_y'\), \(\varepsilon_z'\) for the partial strains caused by \(\sigma_y\) alone and \(\sigma_z\) alone. You cannot put the total \(\varepsilon_z\) back in their place. And the \(\sigma\)'s here are normal stresses only: shear stresses do not change lengths along \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) (for an isotropic material).

Beyond the handouts · plane stress and the inverse form

Plane stress means \(\sigma_z=0\) (and no shear on the \(z\)-faces): the usual state in a thin plate loaded in its own plane. Then \(\varepsilon_z=-\frac{\nu}{E}(\sigma_x+\sigma_y)\ne0\): the plate still changes thickness. Solving the first two equations for the stresses gives the form you need when strain gauges are measured (May 2019 A2(a), in the exam taster):

\[\begin{aligned}\sigma_x&=\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}\left(\varepsilon_x+\nu\varepsilon_y\right),\\ \sigma_y&=\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}\left(\varepsilon_y+\nu\varepsilon_x\right).\end{aligned}\]

In words: to get a stress from two measured strains, add \(\nu\) times the other strain, and multiply by \(E/(1-\nu^2)\).

10Shear stress, shear strain, shear modulus (slides 23–25)

Glue a block to the floor and push its top face sideways with a force \(F\) spread over the top area \(A\) (slide 23). The block does not get longer; it leans: its right angles become slightly less than \(90^\circ\).

Definitions · shear strain and shear modulus (slide 23) \[\begin{aligned}\tau_{xy}&=\frac FA\overset{\text{def}}{=}G\gamma_{xy},\\ \gamma_{xy}&=\frac{\Delta x}{l}=\tan\theta\approx\theta.\end{aligned}\]

The shear strain \(\gamma_{xy}\) (gamma) is the change of the right angle, in radians: sideways shift \(\Delta x\) over height \(l\). For small angles \(\tan\theta\approx\theta\). The shear modulus \(G\) is the shear version of \(E\).

In words: shear stress = shear modulus × angle of lean. Like pushing the top of a stack of paper: the stiffer the stack, the less it leans.

The link between \(E\), \(G\) and \(\nu\) (slide 25)

Slide 25 draws a square under pure shear \(\tau\) with its two diagonals: one diagonal is stretched (labelled "tension"), the other squashed ("compression"). Seen on the diagonals, pure shear is a pull of size \(\tau\) one way and a push of size \(\tau\) at right angles (you can check this with the transformation equations of Section 15 at \(\theta=45^\circ\)). Working out the diagonal's stretch both ways gives:

Result · slide 25 (isotropic, linear elastic) \[G=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}\]

In words: the three constants are not independent: know two, and the third follows. For metals with \(\nu\approx0.3\), \(G\approx0.38E\). (Check against the table: aluminium \(72.4/2.62=27.6\u{GPa}\) ✓.)

Definition · isotropic

An isotropic material has the same properties in every direction (most metals). Wood is not: it is much stiffer along the grain.

11Hooke's law for multi-axial loading (slide 26)

Result · generalised Hooke's law with temperature (slide 26) \[\begin{aligned}\varepsilon_x&=\tfrac1E\left(\sigma_x-\nu\sigma_y-\nu\sigma_z\right)+\alpha_T\Delta T,\\ \varepsilon_y&=\tfrac1E\left(\sigma_y-\nu\sigma_x-\nu\sigma_z\right)+\alpha_T\Delta T,\\ \varepsilon_z&=\tfrac1E\left(\sigma_z-\nu\sigma_x-\nu\sigma_y\right)+\alpha_T\Delta T,\\ \gamma_{xy}&=\tfrac1G\tau_{xy},\quad\gamma_{xz}=\tfrac1G\tau_{xz},\quad\gamma_{yz}=\tfrac1G\tau_{yz}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: normal strains come from the three normal stresses (with the Poisson cross-terms) plus the same thermal expansion in every direction; each shear strain comes only from its own shear stress. A temperature change never produces shear strain.

12Bulk modulus and the limits on \(\nu\) (slides 27–30)

The bulk modulus measures how hard a material resists being squeezed into a smaller volume (slide 27).

Volume change (slide 28). Take a unit cube (\(1\times1\times1\)). Under \(\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z\) its sides become \(1+\varepsilon_x\), \(1+\varepsilon_y\), \(1+\varepsilon_z\), so

\[\begin{aligned}V&=(1+\varepsilon_x)(1+\varepsilon_y)(1+\varepsilon_z)\\ &=1+\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y+\varepsilon_z+\varepsilon_x\varepsilon_y\\&\quad+\varepsilon_y\varepsilon_z+\varepsilon_x\varepsilon_z+\varepsilon_x\varepsilon_y\varepsilon_z\\ &\approx1+\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y+\varepsilon_z,\end{aligned}\]

because products of strains (like \(10^{-3}\times10^{-3}\)) are far smaller than the strains themselves.

Definition · volumetric strain (slide 28) \[e=V-1=\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y+\varepsilon_z\]

In words: the fractional change of volume is the sum of the three normal strains.

Slide 29. Adding the three lines of Hooke's law (no temperature change):

\[e=\frac{1-2\nu}{E}\left(\sigma_x+\sigma_y+\sigma_z\right).\]

Under a uniform hydrostatic pressure \(p\) (equal squeeze from all sides, like deep under water), \(\sigma_x=\sigma_y=\sigma_z=-p\):

Result · bulk modulus (slide 29) \[\begin{aligned}e&=-\frac{3(1-2\nu)}{E}p=-\frac pk,\\ k&=\frac{E}{3(1-2\nu)}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: volumetric strain = − pressure ÷ bulk modulus. For steel (\(E=200\u{GPa}\), \(\nu=0.29\)): \(k\approx159\u{GPa}\).

Why \(\nu\lt\tfrac12\) (slide 30). A stable material squeezed from all sides can only lose volume, so \(e\lt0\) whenever \(p\gt0\). That needs \(1-2\nu\gt0\):

\[0\lt\nu\lt\tfrac12\quad\text{for any engineering material}.\]

For most metals \(\nu\) is around 0.3. (\(\nu\to\tfrac12\) means incompressible, like rubber.)

13Example: a circle scribed on a plate (slides 31–33)

Worked example · slides 31–33

A circle of diameter \(d=200\u{mm}\) is scribed on an unstressed \(360\times350\u{mm}\) aluminium plate of thickness \(t=18\u{mm}\). In-plane stresses \(\sigma_x=150\u{MPa}\) and \(\sigma_y=85\u{MPa}\) (both tension) are then applied. Assuming plane stress (\(\sigma_z=0\)), find the change in (a) the diameters \(AB\) (vertical) and \(CD\) (horizontal), (b) the thickness, (c) the plate volume.

the question does not state \(E\) and \(\nu\); the solution on slide 32 uses \(E=70\u{GPa}\) and \(\nu=1/3\) (the "/3" in its working).

  1. Strains (slide 32), with \(\sigma_z=0\): \[\begin{aligned}\varepsilon_x&=\tfrac1E(\sigma_x-\nu\sigma_y)\\&=\tfrac{(150-85/3)\times10^6}{70\times10^9}=1.738\times10^{-3},\\ \varepsilon_y&=\tfrac1E(\sigma_y-\nu\sigma_x)\\&=\tfrac{(85-150/3)\times10^6}{70\times10^9}=0.5\times10^{-3},\\ \varepsilon_z&=\tfrac1E(-\nu\sigma_x-\nu\sigma_y)\\&=\tfrac{-(85+150)/3\times10^6}{70\times10^9}=-1.119\times10^{-3}.\end{aligned}\]
  2. (a) \(AB\) lies along \(y\): \(\Delta_{AB}=\varepsilon_yd=0.5\times10^{-3}\times200\u{mm}=\boxed{100\u{\mu m}}\). \(CD\) lies along \(x\): \(\Delta_{CD}=\varepsilon_xd=\boxed{348\u{\mu m}}\).
  3. (b) \(\Delta t=\varepsilon_z t=-1.119\times10^{-3}\times18\u{mm}=\boxed{-20.1\u{\mu m}}\) (thinner, although \(\sigma_z=0\): the Poisson effect).

14Stress at a point depends on the axes (slides 34–36)

Slide 34 starts the second lecture of the chapter. Section 2 showed that one force on a bar gives \(\sigma_{\max}=\sigma_0\) on the straight cut and \(\tau_{\max}=\sigma_0/2\) on the \(45^\circ\) cut (slide 35). With several forces we want the same answers: on which cut is the normal stress largest or smallest, and how big is it? The tool is a coordinate transformation, and Mohr's circle is a picture of it (slide 35).

Definition · stress element, plane stress

A stress element is a tiny square (cube, in 3D) drawn around one point of a body, with the stresses on its faces drawn as arrows. In plane stress only the in-plane stresses \(\sigma_x\), \(\sigma_y\) and \(\tau_{xy}=\tau_{yx}\) act.

The stress components at a point depend on the chosen axes (slide 36). Slide 36 draws the same point \(P\) twice: once as a cube aligned with \(x\)–\(y\), with \(\sigma_x\), \(\sigma_y\), \(\tau_{xy}\), \(\tau_{yx}\); once as a cube turned by \(\theta\) about \(z\), with different values \(\sigma_{x'}\), \(\sigma_{y'}\), \(\tau_{x'y'}\). Both describe the same physical state.

Analogy: a wind blowing north-east is "so much north plus so much east", or "all north-east and nothing across". Same wind, different components. A stress state is the same idea one level up: turning the element changes how the stress is split into normal and shear parts.

The bar of Section 2 shows it: one stress \(\sigma_0\) on the axis-aligned element becomes normal and shear stresses on an element turned by \(30^\circ\):

Fact · shear stresses come in pairs

\(\tau_{yx}=\tau_{xy}\). Otherwise the shear arrows on the four faces of the tiny square would form an unbalanced couple and it would spin (moment equilibrium of the element). The handout uses this on slide 40.

15The stress transformation equations (slides 37–42)

Take a wedge of thickness \(t\) cut from the element (slides 37–39): a vertical face \(OQ\) (an \(x\)-face), a horizontal face \(OR\) (a \(y\)-face) and a sloping face \(QR\) whose outward normal is the new axis \(x'\), at angle \(\theta\) anticlockwise from \(x\). Let the sloping face have area \(\mathrm dA\) (\(=\mathrm dy'\,t\), slide 38). Then the vertical face has area \(\mathrm dA\cos\theta\) and the horizontal face \(\mathrm dA\sin\theta\).

Slide 37 repeats the sign convention: \(\sigma_x\), \(\sigma_y\) positive in tension, \(\tau_{xy}\) positive anticlockwise. Arrows in the sketch are drawn positive.

Force balance (slide 40). Force = stress × area. Resolve along \(x'\) and along \(y'\):

\[\begin{aligned}x':\ &\sigma_{x'}\mathrm dA-\sigma_x\mathrm dA\cos\theta\cos\theta\\&-\tau_{xy}\mathrm dA\cos\theta\sin\theta\\&-\sigma_y\mathrm dA\sin\theta\sin\theta\\&-\tau_{yx}\mathrm dA\sin\theta\cos\theta=0,\\ y':\ &\tau_{x'y'}\mathrm dA+\sigma_x\mathrm dA\cos\theta\sin\theta\\&-\tau_{xy}\mathrm dA\cos\theta\cos\theta\\&-\sigma_y\mathrm dA\sin\theta\cos\theta\\&+\tau_{yx}\mathrm dA\sin\theta\sin\theta=0.\end{aligned}\]

In words: each term is (stress) × (area of its face) × (cosine of the angle between the force and the direction we resolve in). Two cosines appear: one to get the area, one to project the force.

With \(\tau_{yx}=\tau_{xy}\) and dividing by \(\mathrm dA\) (slide 41):

\[\begin{aligned}\sigma_{x'}&=\sigma_x\cos^2\theta+\sigma_y\sin^2\theta+2\tau_{xy}\sin\theta\cos\theta,\\ \tau_{x'y'}&=-(\sigma_x-\sigma_y)\cos\theta\sin\theta\\&\quad+\tau_{xy}(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta),\\ \sigma_{y'}&=\sigma_x\sin^2\theta+\sigma_y\cos^2\theta-2\tau_{xy}\sin\theta\cos\theta.\end{aligned}\]

(\(\sigma_{y'}\) is \(\sigma_{x'}\) with \(\theta\) replaced by \(\theta+90^\circ\): the \(y'\)-face is the \(x'\)-face turned a further right angle.)

Double angles (slide 42). Using \(\cos^2\theta=\tfrac12(1+\cos2\theta)\), \(\sin^2\theta=\tfrac12(1-\cos2\theta)\), \(2\sin\theta\cos\theta=\sin2\theta\), \(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta=\cos2\theta\):

Result · transformation equations (slide 42) \[\begin{aligned}\sigma_{x'}&=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2}+\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\cos2\theta\\&\quad+\tau_{xy}\sin2\theta,\\ \sigma_{y'}&=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2}-\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\cos2\theta\\&\quad-\tau_{xy}\sin2\theta,\\ \tau_{x'y'}&=-\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\sin2\theta+\tau_{xy}\cos2\theta.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the normal stress on a face turned by \(\theta\) is the average stress plus a part that swings with \(2\theta\); the shear stress swings with \(2\theta\) too, a quarter-cycle out of step. Everything repeats every \(180^\circ\) of element rotation, because a face and the opposite face carry the same stress.

Ready answer · "what is \(\sigma_{y'}\)?" (Dec 2015 A2(b))

Replace \(\theta\) by \(\theta+90^\circ\) in \(\sigma_{x'}\): \(\cos(2\theta+180^\circ)=-\cos2\theta\), \(\sin(2\theta+180^\circ)=-\sin2\theta\), giving the \(\sigma_{y'}\) line above. Note \(\sigma_{x'}+\sigma_{y'}=\sigma_x+\sigma_y\).

16Principal stresses and principal directions (slides 43–45)

Which angle makes \(\sigma_{x'}\) largest or smallest (slide 43)? Set the derivative to zero:

\[\frac{\mathrm d\sigma_{x'}}{\mathrm d\theta}=-(\sigma_x-\sigma_y)\sin2\theta+2\tau_{xy}\cos2\theta=0\]

(and \(\mathrm d\sigma_{y'}/\mathrm d\theta\) is the same with the opposite sign). Call the solution \(\theta'\):

Result · principal directions (slides 43–44) \[\tan2\theta'=\frac{2\tau_{xy}}{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}.\]

Because \(\tan2\theta'=\tan(2\theta'+\pi)\), there are always two solutions, \(\theta'\) and \(\theta'+90^\circ\): two faces at right angles. These are the principal directions.

The extreme values (slides 44–45). From \(\tan2\theta'\), draw a right triangle with sides \(\sigma_x-\sigma_y\) and \(2\tau_{xy}\):

\[\begin{aligned}\cos2\theta'&=\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{\sqrt{(\sigma_x-\sigma_y)^2+4\tau_{xy}^2}},\\ \sin2\theta'&=\frac{2\tau_{xy}}{\sqrt{(\sigma_x-\sigma_y)^2+4\tau_{xy}^2}}.\end{aligned}\]

Substituting into \(\sigma_{x'}\) (slide 45):

Result · principal stresses (slide 45) \[\begin{aligned}\sigma_{\max/\min}&=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2}\\&\quad\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: principal stress = average normal stress ± the "radius" \(\sqrt{(\text{half the difference})^2+\text{shear}^2}\).

Definitions · principal stress, principal coordinate system (slide 45)

\(\sigma_{\max}\) and \(\sigma_{\min}\) (also written \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\)) are the principal stresses, acting on faces at the principal angles \(\theta'\) and \(\theta'+\pi/2\). The shear stress on those faces is zero. Axes pointing along the principal directions form the principal coordinate system: in it the element is only pulled or pushed, never sheared.

In words (exam-ready, Dec 2015 A2(a)): the principal stresses are the largest and smallest normal stresses at a point over all orientations; they act on two perpendicular planes that carry no shear. The principal coordinate system is the pair of axes normal to those planes.

Watch · which angle carries which stress?

\(\tan^{-1}\) only gives an angle between \(-90^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\), so \(\tfrac12\tan^{-1}\) cannot tell you whether \(\theta'\) carries \(\sigma_{\max}\) or \(\sigma_{\min}\). Always substitute \(\theta'\) back into \(\sigma_{x'}\) (or read it off Mohr's circle, which shows it at a glance). Example 1 in Section 19 is a case where the calculator angle gives \(\sigma_{\min}\).

17Maximum shear stress and mean stress (slides 46–47)

Doing the same for the shear stress (slide 46):

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\mathrm d\tau_{x'y'}}{\mathrm d\theta}&=-(\sigma_x-\sigma_y)\cos2\theta\\&\quad-2\tau_{xy}\sin2\theta=0\\ \Rightarrow\ \tan2\theta''&=-\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2\tau_{xy}}.\end{aligned}\]
Result · maximum shear stress (slide 46) \[\begin{aligned}\tau_{\max}&=\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2}\\&=\pm\tfrac12\left(\sigma_{\max}-\sigma_{\min}\right).\end{aligned}\]

The two values differ only in sign (sense of direction), so both are called the maximum shear stress.

In words: the biggest shear is half the difference between the principal stresses. Since \(\tan2\theta''=-1/\tan2\theta'\), the angles \(2\theta''\) and \(2\theta'\) differ by \(90^\circ\): the planes of maximum shear are at \(45^\circ\) to the principal planes.

Result · the mean stress does not change (slide 47) \[\begin{aligned}\sigma_M&=\tfrac12\left(\sigma_{x'}+\sigma_{y'}\right)=\tfrac12\left(\sigma_x+\sigma_y\right)\\&=\tfrac12\left(\sigma_{\max}+\sigma_{\min}\right).\end{aligned}\]

In words: however you turn the element, the average of the two normal stresses stays the same. On the planes of maximum shear the normal stress equals this average (not zero).

These formulas are hard to remember and easy to get wrong. Mohr's circle packs them all into one picture (slide 47).

18Mohr's circle (slides 48–53)

Square and add the transformation equations: \(\left(\sigma_{x'}-\sigma_M\right)^2+\tau_{x'y'}^2=r^2\) for every \(\theta\). So as the element turns, the point \((\sigma_{x'},\tau_{x'y'})\) runs round a circle with centre \((\sigma_M,0)\) and radius \(r\). That is Mohr's circle.

Building it (slides 48–50)

  • Axes: normal stress \(\sigma\) to the right, shear stress \(\tau\) up. Draw the small element beside it with its positive shear direction (slide 48 insists on this: "anti-clockwise direction is referred as +ve").
  • \(P(\sigma_x,\ \tau_{xy})\) represents the \(x\)-face; \(P'(\sigma_y,\ -\tau_{xy})\) the \(y\)-face. They are opposite ends of a diameter.
  • Centre \(M\) at \(\sigma_M=\tfrac12(\sigma_x+\sigma_y)\); horizontal distance from \(M\) to \(P\) is \(\tfrac12(\sigma_x-\sigma_y)\).
Result · radius and angle (slide 49) \[\begin{aligned}r&=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2}=\tau_{\max},\\ r\cos2\theta'&=\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2},\quad r\sin2\theta'=\tau_{xy},\\ \tan2\theta'&=\frac{2\tau_{xy}}{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the radius is the maximum shear stress, and the angle \(2\theta'\) at the centre between \(MP\) and the \(\sigma\)-axis is twice the principal angle.

Result · principal stresses on the circle (slide 50) \[\begin{aligned}S&=\sigma_M+r\\&=\frac{\sigma_{\max}+\sigma_{\min}}{2}+\frac{\sigma_{\max}-\sigma_{\min}}{2}\\&=\sigma_{\max},\\ S'&=\sigma_M-r=\sigma_{\min}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: where the circle cuts the \(\sigma\)-axis (zero shear) are the principal stresses; the top and bottom of the circle are \(\pm\tau_{\max}\), at normal stress \(\sigma_M\). Slide 51 marks all four: \(\sigma_{\max}\), \(\sigma_{\min}\), \(\tau_{\max}\), \(\tau_{\min}\).

Stresses on a rotated element (slides 52–53)

To get the stresses on an element turned by \(\theta\) anticlockwise (slide 52): rotate \(P\) by \(2\theta\) clockwise round the circle to \(Q(a,b)\), and \(P'\) likewise to \(Q'(a',b')\). With the angle of \(P\) being \(2\theta'\), \(Q\) sits at angle \(2\theta'-2\theta\):

\[\begin{aligned}a&=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2}+r\cos(2\theta'-2\theta)\\ &=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2}+r\cos2\theta'\cos2\theta\\&\quad+r\sin2\theta'\sin2\theta\\ &=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2}+\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\cos2\theta\\&\quad+\tau_{xy}\sin2\theta=\sigma_{x'},\\ a'&=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2}-r\cos(2\theta'-2\theta)\\&=\sigma_{y'},\\ b&=-b'=r\sin(2\theta'-2\theta)\\ &=-\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\sin2\theta+\tau_{xy}\cos2\theta\\&=\tau_{x'y'}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: reading the coordinates of \(Q\) gives back exactly the transformation equations. So the circle is the transformation equations, drawn.

Ready answer · general steps (slide 53)
  1. Get \(P(\sigma_x,\tau_{xy})\) and \(P'(\sigma_y,-\tau_{xy})\) from the given values (or by calculation). Read the signs off the drawn element carefully.
  2. Plot \(P\) and \(P'\) and draw the diameter \(PP'\).
  3. Locate the centre \(M\); calculate the radius \(r=\tau_{\max}\); read \(\sigma_{\max}=\sigma_M+r\) and \(\sigma_{\min}=\sigma_M-r\) on the \(\sigma\)-axis.
  4. Angles: \(2\theta'\) from \(MP\) to the \(\sigma\)-axis; on the element, turn by \(\theta'\) in the opposite sense to the circle. For a rotated element at \(\theta\), turn \(P\) by \(2\theta\) the opposite way.

There is no need to draw the circle to scale; a sketch will do (slide 53). Label the points and show the angles.

21Common mistakes

✗Reading \(\tau_{xy}\) off the top face and getting its sign wrong. ✓Look at the right (\(+x\)) face: shear pointing up → \(\tau_{xy}\gt0\); pointing down → \(\tau_{xy}\lt0\). ✗Turning the element by \(2\theta\), or turning \(P\) by \(\theta\). ✓Angles on the circle are double those on the element, and in the opposite sense (in this course's \(\tau\)-up convention). ✗Assuming \(\tfrac12\tan^{-1}\dfrac{2\tau_{xy}}{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}\) always gives \(\sigma_{\max}\). ✓Substitute back, or read it off the circle (slide 56 example: it gives \(\sigma_{\min}\)). ✗Saying the normal stress is zero on the maximum-shear planes. ✓It is \(\sigma_M=\tfrac12(\sigma_x+\sigma_y)\). ✗Forgetting the Poisson terms, e.g. \(\varepsilon_x=\sigma_x/E\) when \(\sigma_y\ne0\). ✓\(\varepsilon_x=(\sigma_x-\nu\sigma_y-\nu\sigma_z)/E\). ✗Taking \(\varepsilon_z=0\) in plane stress. ✓Plane stress has \(\sigma_z=0\), so \(\varepsilon_z=-\nu(\sigma_x+\sigma_y)/E\ne0\) (the plate gets thinner). ✗Mixing MPa and Pa, mm and m. ✓N/mm² = MPa; strain × mm = mm; keep one set of units throughout.

22Find the mistake

1 · A student's Mohr's circle for slide 54

"\(\sigma_x=-5\), \(\sigma_y=20\), and the shear arrow on the top face points left, so \(\tau_{xy}=+15\). \(P=(-5,15)\). Then \(\sigma_{\max}=27.03\) at \(25.10^\circ\) anticlockwise."

Hint

Which face defines \(\tau_{xy}\), and which way does its arrow point?

Answer

On the right (\(+x\)) face the arrow points down, so \(\tau_{xy}=-15\). With the wrong sign the principal values are unchanged (they depend on \(\tau_{xy}^2\)) but the angles flip: the student's element would be the mirror image. Correct: \(\sigma_{\min}=-12.03\u{MPa}\) at \(25.10^\circ\) anticlockwise, \(\sigma_{\max}=27.03\u{MPa}\) at \(64.90^\circ\) clockwise.

2 · A student's rotated element

"To get the stresses on the element turned \(30^\circ\) anticlockwise, I turn \(P\) by \(30^\circ\) anticlockwise on the circle."

Answer

Two errors: on the circle the angle is doubled (\(60^\circ\)) and, with \(\tau\) plotted up, the sense is reversed (clockwise) (slide 52).

3 · A student's tube and cylinder

"The steel and copper share the 200 kN equally because they have about the same area, so each carries 100 kN."

Answer

They share in proportion to \(EA\), not \(A\): both shorten by the same amount, and the stiffer steel needs more force for that. 127 kN steel, 73 kN copper (Section 7).

23Concept checks

Written for these notes.

1 · Pure shear

An element has \(\sigma_x=\sigma_y=0\), \(\tau_{xy}=50\u{MPa}\). Principal stresses and directions?

Answer

Centre at 0, radius 50: \(\sigma_{1}=50\u{MPa}\), \(\sigma_2=-50\u{MPa}\), on faces at \(45^\circ\) (\(\tan2\theta'=\infty\)). This is slide 25's picture: tension on one diagonal, compression on the other.

2 · A circle that is a point

When does Mohr's circle shrink to a point, and what does that mean?

Answer

When \(\sigma_x=\sigma_y\) and \(\tau_{xy}=0\) (equal biaxial stress). Every direction is then principal and no plane carries shear.

3 · Invariant

On an element turned by \(\theta\), \(\sigma_{x'}=40\) and \(\sigma_{y'}=-10\u{MPa}\). What is \(\sigma_x+\sigma_y\), and the centre of Mohr's circle?

Answer

\(\sigma_x+\sigma_y=\sigma_{x'}+\sigma_{y'}=30\u{MPa}\); centre at \(\sigma_M=15\u{MPa}\) (slide 47).

4 · Poisson's ratio of 0.6?

A data sheet lists an isotropic metal with \(\nu=0.6\). Why is it wrong?

Answer

Then \(1-2\nu\lt0\) and \(k\lt0\): squeezing it from all sides would make it grow. Stability needs \(0\lt\nu\lt\tfrac12\) (slide 30).

5 · Heating a clamped bar

Does the stress in a bar held between rigid walls and heated by \(\Delta T\) depend on its length or area?

Answer

Neither: \(\sigma=-E\alpha_T\Delta T\) (Section 6). The force \(\sigma A\) does depend on the area.

6 · Where is \(\tau_{\max}\)?

The principal stresses at a point are 80 and 20 MPa. What is \(\tau_{\max}\), and what normal stress acts on its plane?

Answer

\(\tau_{\max}=\tfrac12(80-20)=30\u{MPa}\), with \(\sigma=\sigma_M=50\u{MPa}\), on planes at \(45^\circ\) to the principal planes.
