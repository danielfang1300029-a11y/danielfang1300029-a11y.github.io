D3 exam guide · Dynamics of rockets

0How this chapter is examined

Verdict: predictable. From 2014 to 2024 rockets came as one Section B question (B2 or B3), almost always shared with orbit or re-entry parts. The same 4–10-mark derivation of \(\Delta v=v_{ex}\ln(M_0/M_1)\) opened the question in 6 of the 11 sittings, nearly word for word, and was followed by one or two "how much fuel / how fast at burnout" calculations. With orbits and re-entry removed, expect the rocket parts to stay and the rest of the question to change.

Exam format

3 h · any five of six 20-mark questions · at least two from each section (A statics, B dynamics) · about 36 min per question.

Formula sheet: Dec 2021–Dec 2024 print, at the start of Section B, the rigid-body relative-velocity lines, \(I=\int r^2\,\mathrm dm\), the orbit formulas, \(R_E=6370\) km and \(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\). They do not give the rocket equation, the thrust formula or anything else in this chapter: learn \(\Delta v=v_{ex}\ln(M_{ini}/M_{fi})-gt\) by heart and be ready to derive it. (Dec 2016–May 2019 sheets did print \(\Delta V=V_{ex}\ln(M_{initial}/M_{final})\), with \(-gt\) in 2018 and 2019; do not rely on that.)

2026/27 changes: orbital dynamics and re-entry are removed (D1 handout, slide 7), so most of the old rocket/orbit question is gone; control (C1) is added and has no past papers. How the 2026 paper will share its marks among these topics has not been announced.

Sign convention (same as the notes)

Upward (direction of flight) positive. \(M(t)\) = mass still on the rocket, so \(\mathrm dM/\mathrm dt\lt0\); burn rate \(\dot m=-\mathrm dM/\mathrm dt\gt0\). \(v_{ex}\gt0\) is the exhaust speed relative to the rocket; seen from the ground the exhaust moves at \(v-v_{ex}\). \(g\) constant (use the value the question gives); drag ignored unless stated.

D3.1Derive the rocket equation

D3.1Derive \(\Delta v=v_{ex}\ln(M_0/M_1)\)6 sittings · 35 marks (2014–2024)
Looks like
the control-volume figure (body \(M-\Delta m\) at \(v+\Delta v\), chunk \(\Delta m\) at \(v-v_{ex}\)) and "derive the differential equation … show by integration that …"; 4–10 marks.
Do
momentum before = momentum after (+ impulse \(-Mg\Delta t\) if gravity kept) → drop \(\Delta m\Delta v\) → \(\Delta m=-\Delta M\), divide by \(\Delta t\) → \(M\,\mathrm dv=-v_{ex}\,\mathrm dM\) → integrate with limits.
Watch
the minus sign from \(\Delta m=-\Delta M\) is what turns \(\ln(M_1/M_0)\) into \(\ln(M_0/M_1)\); state it. Write limits on both integrals.

Concept: Notes §3 · one small puff and §4 · thrust and integrating.

Ready answer · the four lines to write \[\begin{aligned}&(M-\Delta m)(v+\Delta v)+\Delta m(v-v_{ex})\\&\qquad=Mv-Mg\,\Delta t\\ &\Rightarrow M\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=-v_{ex}\frac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dt}-Mg\\ &g\to0:\ \int_{v_0}^{v_1}\mathrm dv=-v_{ex}\int_{M_0}^{M_1}\frac{\mathrm dM}{M}\\ &\Rightarrow\Delta v=v_{ex}\ln\frac{M_0}{M_1}\end{aligned}\]

In words: momentum after a puff = momentum before plus the impulse of gravity; in the limit this is "mass × acceleration = thrust − weight"; with no gravity the speed gain is the exhaust speed times the log of the mass ratio.

Dec 2024 B3(a)4 marksalso Dec 2023 B3(a) 4 · Dec 2022 B3(a) 4 · Dec 2021 B2(a) 5 · Dec 2015 B2(a) 8 · Dec 2014 B2(a) 10

In a space mission, a single stage rocket containing a space shuttle is launched into space from Earth's surface. During the launch from Earth, a chunk of mass \(\Delta m\) is ejected at a speed of \(v_{ex}\) relative to the rocket, causing the rest of the rocket to gain a velocity increment \(\Delta v\).

(a) Derive the differential equation for the rocket flight by ignoring gravity and air resistance. Show by integration that the total velocity increase from mass \(M_0\) to \(M_1\) is \(\Delta v=v_{ex}\ln(M_0/M_1)\). (b), (c) orbits, (d) re-entry → removed

Older wordings: Dec 2014 (10 marks) asks for the equation "involving only the instantaneous mass \(M\) and velocity \(v\) with \(v_{ex}\) as a parameter" and calls the masses \(M_1\to M_2\); Dec 2015 (8 marks) draws the rocket as an oval and writes \(M_{initial}\to M_{final}\). Same answer; for 8–10 marks keep the gravity term until the last step and explain each line.

Hint

Type D3.1. First step: write the momentum of the system at \(t\) and at \(t+\Delta t\) from the figure.

Solution
  1. System = everything inside the dashed box. Momentum at \(t\): \(Mv\). At \(t+\Delta t\): \((M-\Delta m)(v+\Delta v)+\Delta m(v-v_{ex})\).
  2. Gravity and air resistance ignored, so no external impulse and momentum is conserved: \[\begin{aligned}&(M-\Delta m)(v+\Delta v)\\&\quad+\Delta m(v-v_{ex})=Mv\\ &\Rightarrow M\Delta v-\Delta m\,\Delta v=v_{ex}\Delta m.\end{aligned}\]
  3. \(\Delta m\,\Delta v\) is a product of two small quantities: drop it. The rocket's mass changes by \(\Delta M=-\Delta m\). Divide by \(\Delta t\) and let \(\Delta t\to0\): \[M\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=-v_{ex}\frac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dt}.\] (With gravity kept: \(M\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt=-v_{ex}\,\mathrm dM/\mathrm dt-Mg\).)
  4. Separate and integrate from \((v_0,M_0)\) to \((v_1,M_1)\): \[\begin{aligned}\int_{v_0}^{v_1}\mathrm dv&=-v_{ex}\int_{M_0}^{M_1}\frac{\mathrm dM}{M}\\ v_1-v_0&=-v_{ex}\ln\frac{M_1}{M_0}.\end{aligned}\]
Marker note

The marks are for the momentum balance, the "\(\Delta m\Delta v\) negligible" remark and the sign \(\Delta m=-\Delta M\); writing the final formula alone scores little.

\(\boxed{\Delta v=v_1-v_0=v_{ex}\ln\dfrac{M_0}{M_1}}\)

Equivalent forms: \(M_1=M_0e^{-\Delta v/v_{ex}}\); \(\dfrac{M_0}{M_1}=e^{\Delta v/v_{ex}}\).

D3.2Rocket-equation numbers: fuel, burnout, staging

D3.2Fuel needed, burnout speed, staging8 sittings · 54 marks (2014–2024)
Looks like
"total initial weight … kg", "exhaust speed \(v_{ex}=\ldots\) km/s", a speed to reach (old papers took it from the orbit part); or a burn rate in kN/s plus "average \(g\)"; or masses for two stages.
Do
sketch the stack and FBD → no gravity: \(M_{fi}=M_{ini}e^{-\Delta v/v_{ex}}\), fuel \(=M_{ini}-M_{fi}\) · with gravity: \(F_{th}=v_{ex}\dot m\), \(t_b=m_{fuel}/\dot m\), \(v_b=v_{ex}\ln(M_{ini}/M_{fi})-gt_b\) · stages: one rocket equation per stage, drop the empty shell in between, add.
Watch
"weight in kg" means mass; a rate in kN/s is a weight rate (÷ \(g\) for kg/s, but mass ratios can stay in kN); fuel = \(M_{ini}-M_{fi}\), not \(M_{fi}\); forgetting to drop the shell.

Concept: Notes §4 · thrust, burnout speed, §6 · Saturn V worked example, §9 · multi-stage.

Ready answer · recurring forms \[\begin{aligned}m_{fuel}&=M_{ini}\left(1-e^{-\Delta v/v_{ex}}\right)\\ F_{th}&=v_{ex}\dot m,\quad t_b=\frac{m_{fuel}}{\dot m}\\ v_b&=v_{ex}\ln\frac{M_{ini}}{M_{fi}}-g\,t_b\\ \Delta v_{stages}&=v_{ex}\sum_k\ln\frac{M_{ini}^{(k)}}{M_{fi}^{(k)}}\end{aligned}\]

In words: fuel = launch mass × (1 − e−(speed gain ÷ exhaust speed)); thrust = exhaust speed × burn rate; burnout time = fuel ÷ burn rate; burnout speed = exhaust speed × ln(mass ratio) − gravity × burn time; stages simply add their speed gains.

From 2026/27 the orbit parts that used to supply \(\Delta v\) are removed, so a new paper will most likely give the speed directly. Below, where an old part needed an orbit result, it is quoted as "from (b)" with one line of working so you can do the rocket part.

Hint

Type D3.2. First step: \(\Delta v=3074\u{m/s}\) from rest; solve the rocket equation for the final mass.

Dec 2018 B3(a)–(e)18 marks (4 + 2 + 4 + 4 + 4)

The initial weight of a single stage rocket is 500 kN, of which 420 kN is fuel. The weight of payload weight is 40 kN and the structural mass of the booster is 40 kN. It is launched vertically from the surface of the earth. It burns fuel at a constant rate of 3 kN/s and has a constant exhaust speed of 3 km/s. Assume that the average value of the gravitational acceleration is 9.6 m/s² and the rocket start from rest.

(a) Determine the average thrust of the rocket engine to burnout. [4] (b) Determine the time of burnout. [2] (c) Determine the speed at the burnout. [4]

The rocket is redesigned and a two stage booster is used. The payload weight is the same (40 kN). The weight of fuel is 420 kN fuel and is stored in stage 1 booster (300 kN fuel and 30 kN structural booster mass) and stage 2 booster (120 kN fuel and 10 kN structural booster mass), respectively. The fuel burn rate and the exhaust speed are still the same. Assume that the average value of the gravitational acceleration is 9.6 m/s² and the rocket start from rest.

(d) Determine the speed at the first burnout. [4] (e) Determine the speed at the second burnout. [4] (f) compare → D3.4

(d)–(e) are staging calculations; slide 92 marks the two-stage formula "Not examined" for 2026/27, yet the papers set staging. They need only the rocket equation used twice, so they are kept as lower priority practice.

Hint

Type D3.2. First step: turn the weight rate 3 kN/s into a mass rate with \(g=9.6\); for the two-stage part, the first burn takes 300 kN of fuel.

Solution
  1. (a) \(\dot m=3000\u{N/s}\div9.6\u{m/s^2}=312.5\u{kg/s}\); \(F_{th}=v_{ex}\dot m=3000\times312.5=937.5\u{kN}\) (constant, so it is also the average).
  2. (b) \(t_b=420\u{kN}\div3\u{kN/s}=140\u{s}\).
  3. (c) Burnout weight \(500-420=80\u{kN}\): \[\begin{aligned}v_b&=3000\ln\tfrac{500}{80}-9.6(140)\\&=5498-1344=4154\u{m/s}.\end{aligned}\]
  4. (d) Stage 1 burns 300 kN in 100 s, 500 → 200 kN: \[\begin{aligned}v_1&=3000\ln2.5-9.6(100)\\&=1789\u{m/s}.\end{aligned}\]
  5. (e) Drop the 30 kN shell: 170 kN. Stage 2 burns 120 kN in 40 s, 170 → 50 kN: \[\begin{aligned}v_2&=1789+3000\ln3.4-9.6(40)\\&=1789+3671-384\\&=5076\u{m/s}.\end{aligned}\]
Marker note

Mass ratios may be taken directly from weights (the \(g\) cancels), but the burn rate in (a) must be in kg/s.

\(\boxed{937.5\u{kN};\ 140\u{s};\ 4154\u{m/s};\ 1789\u{m/s};\ 5076\u{m/s}}\)

D3.3Variable-mass thrust and acceleration

D3.3Variable-mass ODE: burn rate, acceleration at an instant1 sitting · 20 marks (2014–2024)
Looks like
"maintain a constant acceleration \(a_0\) … prove that \(\mathrm dm/\mathrm dt=\ldots\)" or "fuel consumed at \(x\) kg/s … find the acceleration when fired and just before burnout".
Do
FBD → \(M\dot v=v_{ex}\dot m-Mg\) → for constant \(a_0\): separate \(\mathrm dM/M\), integrate → \(M(t)\), then \(\dot m=-\mathrm dM/\mathrm dt\) · for an instant: \(a=v_{ex}\dot m/M-g\) with the mass at that instant.
Watch
the 2017 printed answer has the wrong sign in the exponent (prove the decaying form and say so); the rocket's current speed does not enter \(a\).

Concept: Notes §4 · thrust and §7 · varying mass beyond rockets (Dec 2017 B3(a) worked).

Ready answer · constant-acceleration burn \[\begin{aligned}M(t)&=m_0e^{-(a_0+g)t/v_{ex}}\\ \dot m&=\frac{m_0(a_0+g)}{v_{ex}}e^{-(a_0+g)t/v_{ex}}\end{aligned}\]

In words: to keep the acceleration constant, burn fuel in proportion to the mass still on board, so mass and burn rate both decay exponentially.

Dec 2017 B3(a), (b)12 + 8 marks

(a) The rocket has an initial mass \(m_0\) including the fuel. For practical reasons desired for the crew, it is required that it maintain a constant upward acceleration \(a_0\) as shown in Figure B3(a). If the fuel is expelled from the rocket at a constant relative speed \(v_{e/r}\), prove that the rate at which the fuel should be consumed \(\mathrm dm/\mathrm dt\) to maintain the desired motion as follows: \(\dfrac{\mathrm dm}{\mathrm dt}=\dfrac{m_0(a_0+g)}{v_{e/r}}e^{(a_0+g)t/v_{e/r}}\). Neglect air resistance, and assume that the gravitational acceleration \(g\) is constant. [12]

(b) The second stage B of the two-stage rocket has a mass of 5 Mg (empty) and is launched from the first stage A with an initial velocity of 600 km/h as shown in Figure B3(b). The fuel in the second stage has a mass of 0.7 Mg and is consumed at the rate of 4 kg/s. If it is ejected from the rocket at the rate of 3 km/s, measured relative to B, determine the acceleration of B at the instant the engine is fired and just before all the fuel is consumed. Neglect the effects of gravitation and air resistance. [8]

(a): the printed result has \(+\) in the exponent. A lighter rocket needs a falling burn rate for constant acceleration, so the correct exponent is negative (RESOLVED, same as the notes). Prove the decaying form and point out the sign in one line.

Hint

Type D3.3. (a) First step: put \(\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt=a_0\) into the rocket ODE with gravity. (b) First step: thrust \(=v_{e/r}\dot m\) is constant; which mass do you divide by at each instant?

Solution
  1. (a) Rocket ODE (upward +) with \(\dot v=a_0\): \(Ma_0=-v_{e/r}\dfrac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dt}-Mg\).
  2. Separate: \(\dfrac{\mathrm dM}{M}=-\dfrac{a_0+g}{v_{e/r}}\,\mathrm dt\); integrate from \(M=m_0\) at \(t=0\): \[M=m_0e^{-(a_0+g)t/v_{e/r}}.\]
  3. Rate of consumption \(\dfrac{\mathrm dm}{\mathrm dt}=-\dfrac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dt}=\dfrac{m_0(a_0+g)}{v_{e/r}}e^{-(a_0+g)t/v_{e/r}}\). Check at \(t=0\): thrust \(v_{e/r}\dot m=m_0(a_0+g)\) = weight + \(m_0a_0\) ✓.
  4. (b) \(F_{th}=v_{e/r}\dot m=3000\u{m/s}\times4\u{kg/s}=12{,}000\u{N}\). At firing \(M=5000+700=5700\u{kg}\): \(a=12{,}000/5700=2.11\u{m/s^2}\).
  5. Just before burnout \(M=5000\u{kg}\): \(a=12{,}000/5000=2.40\u{m/s^2}\). The 600 km/h does not enter (burn time \(700/4=175\u{s}\), not asked).
Marker note

In (a) state the sign of \(\mathrm dM/\mathrm dt\) explicitly; reproducing the printed \(+\) exponent without comment shows the step was copied, not derived.

\(\boxed{\dot m=\dfrac{m_0(a_0+g)}{v_{e/r}}e^{-(a_0+g)t/v_{e/r}};\quad a_B=2.11\to2.40\u{m/s^2}}\)

D3.4Why multi-stage rockets low priority

Answer skeleton · 4 sittings · 17 marks (2014–2024)
  1. Diagram to draw: single-stage stack next to a two-stage stack, stage-1 shell falling away (1 mark if asked to "explain").
  2. Dead mass: a single stage carries its empty tanks and engines all the way to burnout; fuel is spent accelerating mass that is no longer useful (1–2 marks).
  3. Mass ratio: dropping each empty stage makes every later burn start from a lighter rocket, so \(\Delta v=v_{ex}\ln\prod_k M_{ini}^{(k)}/M_{fi}^{(k)}\) beats \(v_{ex}\ln(M_0/M_{fi})\) with the same fuel (1 mark; quote a number if the paper gave one).
  4. Why not more than 3: gains shrink with each extra stage while engines, separation hardware, structure, failure risk and cost all grow (1–2 marks).

Model answer (exam length, 4 marks): A single-stage rocket must accelerate its empty tanks and engines all the way to burnout, so part of the fuel is wasted pushing dead mass. A multi-stage rocket throws each stage away once its fuel is used, so the remaining fuel accelerates a lighter vehicle and each stage achieves a larger mass ratio; the speed gains add, \(\Delta v=v_{ex}\ln(R_1R_2\cdots)\), giving a higher burnout speed for the same fuel. In theory infinitely many stages would be best, but each extra stage adds engines, separation mechanisms and structure, increasing cost, complexity and the chance of failure, while the benefit becomes small; so practical designs stop at about three stages.

Slide 92 marks the two-stage formula "Not examined" for 2026/27, yet staging was set in Dec 2018, Dec 2015, May 2014 and May 2013. The written "why" is cheap to learn; keep it, at low priority.

Concept: Notes §9 · multi-stage rockets.

Dec 2014 B2(b)5 marks

Stem in D3.2 (Dec 2014 B2). (b) Explain why three-stage rocket is preferred in many designs.

Hint

Type D3.4. Why more than one, and why not more than three.

Solution
  1. More than one: dropping empty stages saves accelerating dead mass, so each burn has a better mass ratio and the final speed for the same fuel is higher.
  2. Beyond Reaching orbit costs about 9–10 km/s including losses; with \(v_{ex}\approx3\)–4 km/s one stage would need a mass ratio of roughly 10–30, which a real structure cannot reach with a useful payload.
  3. Not more than three: each extra stage adds engines, separation systems and structure; the gain gets smaller while cost and failure risk rise. Three stages is the usual balance.

\(\boxed{\text{3 stages: most of the staging gain, without the cost of more}}\)

D3.5Launch and flight practicalities

Answer skeleton · 2 sittings · 8 marks (2014–2024)
  1. Name the physics term the question is about (gravity loss \(gt_b\); drag \(\tfrac12C_D\rho Av^2\); impulse between two bodies).
  2. One design measure per term, each with its reason (1 mark each): burn fast / high thrust-to-weight → small \(gt_b\); slender streamlined body, climb vertically out of dense air, then tilt over gradually, throttle down near maximum dynamic pressure → less drag; staging → lighter later.
  3. For "does X change?" questions: state yes/no, then the momentum argument (what impulse acts, and on what).

Model answer (exam length, 4 marks): Gravity costs \(g\) metres per second of speed for every second of burning, so rockets are designed with a high thrust-to-weight ratio and burn their fuel quickly, and they drop empty stages so the later stages accelerate harder. Drag grows with air density and the square of speed, so the body is slender with a pointed nose fairing; it climbs vertically to leave the dense lower atmosphere while still slow, then turns over gradually towards horizontal (a "gravity turn"), and the engines are throttled back through the region of maximum aerodynamic pressure.

Concept: Notes §4 · design guidelines, §7 · when does losing mass push?, §14 · concept check 3.

Dec 2023 B3(d)4 marks

Stem in D3.2 (Dec 2023 B3). (d) In reality, gravity and air resistance cannot be neglected. Describe what engineering design strategies are used to minimise these effects in a real space shuttle launch.

Hint

Type D3.5. Gravity loss is \(gt_b\); drag grows with \(\rho v^2\). One strategy for each, with the reason.

Solution
  1. Gravity: high thrust-to-weight ratio so the burn is short (small \(gt_b\), slide 86 guideline 3); staging so dead mass is shed and later stages accelerate harder.
  2. Drag: slender streamlined body and nose fairing; vertical climb through the dense lower air while slow, then gradual pitch-over (gravity turn); throttle down through maximum dynamic pressure.
  3. Beyond launch eastward near the equator to borrow Earth's rotation (about 0.46 km/s).

\(\boxed{\text{burn fast + stage (gravity);\ streamline + climb out + throttle (drag)}}\)

Dec 2016 B2(d)4 marks

Stem in D3.2 (Dec 2016 B2: satellite with a booster, 20,000 kg, \(V_{ex}=4\) km/s). (d) Suppose that the shell of the booster is ejected a few minutes after the booster finishes the firing and propulsion, will the speed of the satellite change and will the orbit change due to the discarding of the shell? Whatever your answer, briefly explain why.

Hint

Type D3.5. With what velocity, relative to the satellite, does the shell leave?

Solution
  1. The engine has stopped, so the shell is simply released: its velocity relative to the satellite is zero.
  2. Momentum before \(=(m_s+m_{sh})V\); after \(=m_sV'+m_{sh}V\). Equal ⇒ \(V'=V\): no impulse acts between them.
  3. Same position, same velocity ⇒ same orbit (an orbit is fixed by position and velocity, not by mass). If springs push the shell off, a small \(\Delta V=m_{sh}u/(m_s+m_{sh})\) results, negligible for gentle separation.

\(\boxed{\text{No change in speed or orbit}}\)

✓Last-minute checklist

  • The rocket equation is not on the 2021–2024 formula sheet: write \(\Delta v=v_{ex}\ln(M_{ini}/M_{fi})-gt\) from memory.
  • Derivation: momentum before = after, drop \(\Delta m\Delta v\), \(\Delta m=-\Delta M\), integrate with limits.
  • Fuel = \(M_{ini}(1-e^{-\Delta v/v_{ex}})\), not the final mass.
  • kN/s is a weight rate: divide by \(g\) for kg/s before \(F_{th}=v_{ex}\dot m\).
  • Gravity loss \(=g\,t_b\) with \(t_b\) = fuel ÷ burn rate; use the \(g\) the question gives.
  • Staging: one rocket equation per stage; drop the empty shell between stages.
  • Acceleration at an instant \(=F_{th}/M-g\) with the mass at that instant; the current speed does not matter.
  • Releasing a shell with zero relative velocity changes nothing; only mass thrown out fast pushes.
