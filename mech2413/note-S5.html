Deflection of Beams

0Where this chapter sits

In S2 you found the bending moment \(M(x)\) inside a beam; in S4 you turned it into bending stress. This chapter asks the last question about a beam: how far does it bend?. Deflection is the geometric result of a beam being stressed. Picture a plank laid across a stream: stand on it and it sags into a smooth curve, and we want that curve point by point: how far each point drops (the sag) and how much the plank tilts there (the slope). Sag matters in its own right (a floor that sags 3 cm feels unsafe even when the steel is far from breaking), and it gives the missing equation for a beam with "too many" supports (S1 Section 11).

Every worked example has a sketch, a free-body diagram and a plotted deflection curve.

Definitions · deflection, slope, elastic line

As a beam bends, each point of its axis moves sideways (across the beam) by a small distance \(w(x)\), the deflection. The bent axis is called the deflection curve or elastic line. Its gradient \(w'(x)=\mathrm dw/\mathrm dx\) is the slope: for small bending it equals the angle (in radians) through which the beam has turned at \(x\).

Sign convention for this chapter
  • \(x\) runs along the beam from the left end (or from the support named in the example), pointing right.
  • Bending moment \(M\): positive sagging (smile), exactly as in S2. Shear \(V\): positive clockwise; load \(q\): positive down.
  • Deflection \(w\): positive upward. So a beam that sags has \(w\lt0\), and the law is written \(EIw''=M\). A negative answer means the point moves down.
  • Slope \(w'\): positive when the beam rises to the right (turned anticlockwise).

many figures draw the \(z\)-axis and \(w\) pointing down, but the standard equations (\(EIw''=M\), \(w_{\max}=-Fl^3/3EI\) for a cantilever pushed down) only agree with sagging-positive \(M\) if \(w\) is measured up. These notes use \(w\) up so that these equations and answers keep their standard form. If you prefer \(w\) down, write \(EIw''=-M\); every deflection keeps its size and only changes sign. In the exam, state which you use.

1Ordinary bending: equilibrium and stress resultants ~3 min

What is this?

Cut the loaded plank anywhere in your mind. The two halves still hold each other up, so something must pass across the cut. For a sagging plank the top layers on the two sides push against each other and the bottom layers pull on each other. Added up, these pushes and pulls make a turning effect, the bending moment, and it is this moment that bends the plank. This section is a recap of S2 and S4; it only collects the equations the rest of the chapter needs.

Definition · ordinary (uniaxial) bending

Ordinary bending is bending in one plane only: the loads act in the \(x\)–\(z\) plane, which is a plane of symmetry of the cross-section, so the beam bends in that plane without twisting. Every beam in this course is of this kind.

Recall the slice of length \(\mathrm dx\) from S2: shear \(V\) and moment \(M\) on its left face, \(V+\mathrm dV\) and \(M+\mathrm dM\) on its right face, and the load \(q\) on top. Equilibrium of the slice gave

\[\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dx}=-q,\qquad\frac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dx}=V.\]

In words: a downward load makes the shear fall; the shear is the slope of the moment diagram (S2 Section 9).

Recall where \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) come from. Over the cut face, each small patch \(\mathrm dA\) at height \(z\) carries a normal stress \(\sigma\) (pulling along \(x\)) and a shear stress \(\tau\) (along \(z\)). Adding them up:

\[\begin{aligned}N&=\int\sigma\,\mathrm dA,\qquad V=\int\tau\,\mathrm dA,\\ M&=\int z\,\sigma\,\mathrm dA.\end{aligned}\]
Reading the formula
\(\sigma\,\mathrm dA\)
the push or pull carried by one small patch of the cut face.
\(\int\sigma\,\mathrm dA=N\)
all those pushes and pulls added up. In a plank that is only bent the pushes and pulls cancel, so there is no net stretching force.
\(z\)
the lever arm: how far the patch is from the neutral axis.
\(\int z\,\sigma\,\mathrm dA=M\)
the turning effects added up. Pushes at the top and pulls at the bottom do not cancel here: they turn the same way, and this is the moment that bends the plank.

In words: the normal force is the total of the normal stresses; the shear force is the total of the shear stresses; the bending moment is the total turning effect of the normal stresses about the axis (each stress times its lever arm \(z\)).

Definitions · stress

Stress is force per unit area (unit Pa = N/m², usually MPa). Normal stress \(\sigma\) acts perpendicular to a surface (stretching or squashing it); shear stress \(\tau\) acts along the surface (trying to slide it). See S3.

You can now: state \(\mathrm dV/\mathrm dx=-q\) and \(\mathrm dM/\mathrm dx=V\); and explain \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) as the totals of the stresses on a cut, with \(M\) the total turning effect.

2Strain, stress and the neutral axis ~3 min

What is this?

Bend a thick foam bar into a smile between your hands. The top face gets shorter and the bottom face gets longer. Somewhere in between is a layer that keeps its original length. To describe this we need to know how far each little piece of the bar moves, how much it stretches (strain), and how hard the material resists the stretch (stress).

Inside the beam, describe the movement of each point by two displacements: \(u(x,z)\) along \(x\) and \(w(x,z)\) along \(z\). The figure below shows how a small element \(\mathrm dx\times\mathrm dz\) deforms:

Definitions · strain and shear strain \[\varepsilon=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x},\qquad\gamma=\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}.\]
Reading the formula
\(\varepsilon=\partial u/\partial x\)
how much a layer of the bar stretches per unit length (negative if it is squashed).
\(\partial w/\partial x\)
how much a horizontal edge of a small square tilts as the bar bends.
\(\partial u/\partial z\)
how much a vertical edge of the same square tilts, because layers at different heights slide by different amounts.
\(\gamma\)
the two tilts together: how far the square has been squeezed into a lozenge.

In words: the strain \(\varepsilon\) is the stretch per unit length: the element of length \(\mathrm dx\) gets longer by \(\mathrm du=\varepsilon\,\mathrm dx\). The shear strain \(\gamma\) is the loss of the right angle at a corner (in radians): one edge tilts by \(\partial w/\partial x\), the other by \(\partial u/\partial z\).

(\(\partial/\partial x\) is a partial derivative: differentiate with respect to \(x\) while holding \(z\) fixed.)

For an elastic material, stress is proportional to strain (Hooke's law, S3):

\[\begin{aligned}\sigma&=E\varepsilon=E\frac{\partial u}{\partial x},\\ \tau&=G\gamma=G\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\right).\end{aligned}\]

In words: stress = stiffness × strain. \(E\) is Young's modulus (stiffness in stretching, about 200 GPa for steel); \(G\) is the shear modulus (stiffness in shearing).

What is this? (neutral axis)

In the bent foam bar, the layer in the middle that neither stretches nor shortens is the neutral axis. Above it the bar is squashed, below it stretched (for a smile). We measure every height \(z\) from this layer.

Definition · neutral axis

The neutral axis is the line in the cross-section along which there is no longitudinal stress or strain: fibres there neither stretch nor shorten. If the section is symmetric, the material is isotropic (same properties in every direction) and the beam is straight before bending, the neutral axis passes through the centroid of the section (S1 Section 8).

Section 4 shows why it is the centroid. We measure \(z\) from the neutral axis, so \(z=0\) on it.

You can now: define strain, shear strain and the neutral axis; write Hooke's law \(\sigma=E\varepsilon\), \(\tau=G\gamma\); and say when the neutral axis passes through the centroid.

3Two kinematic assumptions ~3 min

What is this?

Draw straight vertical lines across the side of the foam bar with a ruler, then bend it. Two things stay true. The bar is as thick as before, so every point of one line moves up or down by the same amount. And each line is still straight; it has only tilted. These two observations are the two assumptions, and they turn the movement of the whole cross-section into just two numbers: how far it moves sideways (\(w\)) and how much it tilts (\(\psi\)).

To find the deflection we need to know how the cross-sections move. Take a point \(P\) at depth \(z\) in a section; after bending it has moved to \(P'\): down by \(w\), and sideways because the section has tilted by an angle \(\psi\). The theory makes two assumptions:

Assumption 1 · the beam does not get thinner or thicker

Every point of a cross-section has the same deflection, so \(w\) depends on \(x\) only:

\[w=w(x),\qquad\varepsilon_z=\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}=0.\]

In words: the height of the beam does not change when it bends; the whole section moves sideways together.

Assumption 2 · plane sections remain plane

A cross-section that was a flat plane stays a flat plane; it only rotates through a small angle \(\psi(x)\). So a point at distance \(z\) from the axis moves along the beam by

\[u(x,z)=\psi(x)\,z,\qquad\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}=\psi(x).\]
Reading the formula
\(\psi(x)\)
how much the drawn line at position \(x\) has tilted (a small angle in radians).
\(z\)
how far the point is from the neutral axis along that line.
\(u=\psi z\)
how far the point slides along the bar: zero on the neutral axis, growing straight in proportion to \(z\), in opposite directions on the two sides. That is what a straight tilted line looks like.

In words: the sideways shift grows linearly with distance from the neutral axis, like the pages of a closed book that slide over each other when you bend it: the further a page is from the middle, the more it slides.

Both assumptions are accurate for a slender beam (long compared with its depth) of constant cross-section, or one that tapers only slightly. A small angle means \(\psi\) in radians is much less than 1, so \(\sin\psi\approx\tan\psi\approx\psi\).

Going further · names

These are the assumptions of Euler–Bernoulli beam theory (also called "engineering beam theory" or "ordinary bending theory"). Adding the rule that sections stay perpendicular to the bent axis (shear deformation neglected) is what gives \(w'=\psi\) in Section 4.

You can now: state the two kinematic assumptions (\(w=w(x)\); plane sections stay plane, \(u=\psi z\)); and say when they hold (slender beam, small angles).

4The moment–curvature law \(EIw''=M\) ~5 min

What is this?

Bend a plastic ruler between your hands. Push harder (a bigger moment) and it curls more tightly; a steel ruler of the same size (a stiffer one) curls less for the same push. The law \(EIw''=M\) says exactly this: how sharply the beam curves at a point is the moment there divided by its bending stiffness. And if you know how sharply the plank curves at every point, you can rebuild its whole shape: the curvature tells you how the tilt changes along the plank, and the tilt tells you how the height changes.

Put the displacement \(u=z\psi\) into the stress–strain laws of Section 2:

\[\begin{aligned}\sigma&=E\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=E\frac{\partial(\psi z)}{\partial x}\\&=Ez\psi',\\ \tau&=G\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\right)\\&=G(w'+\psi).\end{aligned}\]

In words: the bending stress grows linearly with distance \(z\) from the neutral axis, at a rate set by how fast the sections rotate, \(\psi'\).

Now add up the stresses over the section:

\[N=\int\sigma\,\mathrm dA=E\psi'\int z\,\mathrm dA.\]

In pure bending there is no normal force, \(N=0\), so \(\int z\,\mathrm dA=0\). A beam that is only bent is not pulled as a whole, so the stretched layers below the neutral axis and the squashed layers above it must balance exactly. That integral is zero exactly when \(z\) is measured from the centroid of the section: this is why the neutral axis passes through the centroid (Section 2). For the moment:

\[M=\int z\sigma\,\mathrm dA=E\psi'\int z^2\,\mathrm dA=EI\psi'.\]
Definitions · second moment of area, flexural rigidity

\(I=\int z^2\,\mathrm dA\) is the second moment of area of the cross-section about its neutral axis (unit m⁴; see S4). For a rectangle of width \(b\) and depth \(h\), \(I=bh^3/12\).

The product \(EI\) (unit N·m²) is the flexural rigidity or bending stiffness: how hard the beam is to bend. Doubling the depth of a rectangular beam multiplies \(EI\) by 8.

Result · constitutive equation for the bending moment \[M=EI\psi'\]

In words: the bending moment equals the bending stiffness times the rate at which the cross-sections rotate along the beam.

Going further · link to S4

Eliminating \(\psi'\) between \(\sigma=Ez\psi'\) and \(M=EI\psi'\) gives the bending-stress formula of S4, \(\sigma=\dfrac{Mz}{I}\). The quantity \(\psi'\approx w''\) is the curvature \(1/\rho\) of the bent axis (\(\rho\) = radius of curvature), so the law is also written \(\dfrac1\rho=\dfrac{M}{EI}\).

From rotation to deflection

If shear deformation is neglected (slender beam, Section 3), each cross-section stays perpendicular to the bent axis, so the angle it turns through equals the slope of the elastic line: \(w'=\psi\) (the section and the tangent to the axis turn through the same angle). Then \(\psi'=w''\) and

Result · differential equation of the elastic line \[\begin{aligned}EI\,w''(x)&=M(x),\\ EI\,w'(x)&=\int_0^xM\,\mathrm dx+C_1,\\ EI\,w(x)&=\int_0^x\!\mathrm dx\int_0^xM\,\mathrm dx\\&\quad+C_1x+C_2.\end{aligned}\]

In words: bending stiffness × curvature = bending moment. Integrate the moment once to get the slope, twice to get the deflection; two constants of integration, \(C_1\) and \(C_2\), are fixed by the supports.

Reading the formula
\(EI\)
how stiff the plank is: a thick steel beam has a large \(EI\), a thin plastic ruler a small one.
\(w''\)
how sharply the plank curves at \(x\): positive for a smile, negative for a frown.
\(M(x)\)
the bending effort at \(x\) (sagging positive). Same sign as \(w''\), so a sagging moment gives a smile.
\(EIw'=\int_0^xM\,\mathrm dx+C_1\)
the tilt at \(x\) is the tilt at the start plus all the curving that happened on the way. \(C_1\) is \(EI\) times the tilt at \(x=0\).
\(EIw=\cdots+C_1x+C_2\)
the height at \(x\) is the starting height plus all the rising and falling on the way. \(C_2\) is \(EI\) times the height at \(x=0\).
\(C_1,\ C_2\)
the moment alone cannot know where the plank starts or how it is tilted there; the supports tell us (Section 5).

Sign check · sagging moment, upward curvature

Left: \(M\gt0\) (sagging) gives \(w''\gt0\): the curve is concave up, a smile. Right: \(M\lt0\) (hogging) gives \(w''\lt0\): a frown. With \(w\) measured up, the equation needs no minus sign.

You can now: derive \(M=EI\psi'\) and \(EIw''=M\) from the two assumptions; explain why the neutral axis is at the centroid; and read the sign: sagging \(M\), smile, \(w''\gt0\) with \(w\) up.

5Boundary conditions ~3 min

What is this?

Supports are rules about the shape of the curve at one point. The ends of a plank bridge rest on the banks: they cannot drop, but the plank can tilt there freely (a pin or roller). A diving board built into concrete at its base can neither drop nor tilt there (a clamp). The far end of the diving board is free: it drops and tilts as much as the load makes it. Each rule fixes the height or the tilt of the curve at that point, and that is what pins down \(C_1\) and \(C_2\).

The two constants need two facts about the beam's ends. There are two kinds:

Definitions · geometrical and statical boundary conditions
  1. Geometrical (kinematic) boundary conditions state something about the movement: the deflection \(w\) or the slope \(w'\). Example: a pin stops the end moving, \(w=0\).
  2. Statical boundary conditions state something about the stress resultants \(V\) or \(M\). Example: a free end carries no moment, \(M=0\).

In words: supports tell you where the beam cannot go (geometry) or what it cannot carry (statics). In this chapter's method \(M(x)\) is already known from statics, so it is the geometrical conditions that fix \(C_1\) and \(C_2\).

Boundary-condition table
Support\(w\)\(w'\)\(M\)\(V\)
pin (or roller)0\(\ne0\)0\(\ne0\)
parallel motion (slides vertically, cannot turn)\(\ne0\)0\(\ne0\)0
fixed end (clamped)00\(\ne0\)\(\ne0\)
free end\(\ne0\)\(\ne0\)00

In words: every support gives exactly two zeros, one per row. A pin stops movement but lets the beam turn; a clamp stops both; a free end can move and turn but carries nothing; a parallel-motion support lets the end slide but not turn.

Watch

The free end of a cantilever has no geometrical condition (it moves and turns). Both constants then come from the fixed end: \(w=0\) and \(w'=0\) there. Students often wrongly write \(w'=0\) at a pin; a pin lets the beam rotate.

How to apply them (one region):

  1. Do: for each support, read its row of the boundary-condition table and keep the geometrical zeros: \(w=0\), \(w'=0\) or both. These are the points where the curve is pinned in height (bank, pin) or in tilt (wall, clamp).
  2. Do: check that you have exactly two: pin and roller give \(w=0\) at both ends; a clamp gives \(w=0\) and \(w'=0\) at the same point; a free end gives none. Two constants means two unknowns about the curve: its starting height and its starting tilt.
  3. Do: put each condition into the integrated \(w\) or \(w'\) at the support's \(x\) and solve for \(C_1\), \(C_2\). With a symmetric load, \(w'=0\) at mid-span may replace one condition (concept check 5). On a symmetric plank bridge the lowest point is in the middle, where the plank is level.

You can now: tell geometrical from statical boundary conditions; give the two zeros for a pin, a clamp, a free end and a parallel-motion support; and choose the two conditions that fix \(C_1\) and \(C_2\).

6Single region: cantilevers ~5 min

What is this?

A diving board with a diver standing on its tip. At the concrete it is level and does not drop; further out it drops more and more and tilts more and more, and the tip drops the most. One formula for the moment covers the whole board, so one curve with two constants describes the whole shape.

If one formula for \(M(x)\) holds along the whole beam, one integration does the job: single-region calculation.

Worked example · cantilever with an end load

A cantilever of length \(l\) and bending stiffness \(EI\) is clamped at \(x=0\) and carries a downward force \(F\) at its free end \(x=l\). Find the slope and deflection.

System

Left piece of a cut at \(x\) (wall reactions \(F\) up, \(Fl\) anticlockwise)

  1. Do: Moment. Keep the piece to the right of the cut (only \(F\) acts on it). \(F\) is a distance \(l-x\) to the right of the cut and points down, so it bends the beam into a frown (hogging): \[M=-F(l-x).\] (From the left piece: \(M=F x-Fl\), the same.) Bigger \(|M|\) means a tighter curl: the board bends hardest at the wall, where the lever arm \(l-x\) is longest, and not at all at the tip.
  2. Do: Integrate twice: \[\begin{aligned}EIw''&=F(x-l),\\ EIw'&=F\left(\frac{x^2}{2}-lx\right)+C_1,\\ EIw&=F\left(\frac{x^3}{6}-\frac{lx^2}{2}\right)+C_1x+C_2.\end{aligned}\] The first integration turns the curl into tilt, the second turns tilt into height; each adds an unknown (a starting tilt, then a starting height).
  3. Do: Boundary conditions at the clamp: \(w'(0)=0\Rightarrow C_1=0\); \(w(0)=0\Rightarrow C_2=0\). The board leaves the concrete level and at zero height, which fixes both unknowns at once.
  4. Do: Result: \[\begin{aligned}w'(x)&=\frac{F}{EI}\left(\frac{x^2}{2}-lx\right),\\ w(x)&=\frac{F}{EI}\left(\frac{x^3}{6}-\frac{lx^2}{2}\right).\end{aligned}\] Both are negative for every \(x\gt0\): beyond the wall the board slopes down and has dropped.
  5. Do: Maximum values, at the free end \(x=l\): \[w'_{\max}=-\frac{Fl^2}{2EI},\qquad w_{\max}=-\frac{Fl^3}{3EI}.\] The curl is a frown everywhere, so the tilt only gets steeper going out: the tip has both the biggest drop and the steepest slope.
  6. Do: Dimension check: \(\dfrac{\mathrm N\cdot\mathrm m^3}{\mathrm N\,\mathrm m^2}=\mathrm m\) ✓. The minus signs mean the tip moves down and the beam slopes down to the right ✓. A lost \(EI\) or a wrong power of \(l\) shows up at once, and the signs must match the picture of a board bending down.

\(\boxed{w_{\max}=-\dfrac{Fl^3}{3EI}\ \ \text{(down)},\qquad w'_{\max}=-\dfrac{Fl^2}{2EI}}\)

In words: the tip of a cantilever drops by "force × length³ ÷ (3 × bending stiffness)". Double the length and the drop is 8 times bigger.

Reading the formula
\(F\)
a heavier diver: the drop grows in proportion.
\(l^3\)
a longer board: a longer lever (more moment) acting over a longer length that keeps curling, so length counts three times over.
\(EI\)
a stiffer board drops less.
\(3\)
comes from integrating the straight-line moment twice.
minus sign
the tip moves down (\(w\) is measured up).

Deflection curve \(w\,EI/(Fl^3)\) against \(x/l\): zero value and zero slope at the wall, steepest at the tip, \(-1/3\) at the tip.

Worked example · past paper Dec 2021 A3(b) · 10 marks · cantilever with a uniform load

The cantilever beam \(AB\) of length \(L\) carries a uniformly distributed load of intensity \(w_0\), which includes the weight of the beam. The free end \(A\) is on the left (\(x=0\)) and the wall \(B\) on the right (\(x=L\)). Calculate the deflection of the beam.

System

Left piece of a cut at \(x\)

  1. Moment from the left piece (only the load \(w_0x\), acting at \(x/2\) from the cut, hogging): \(M=-\dfrac{w_0x^2}{2}\).
  2. Integrate: \(EIw'=-\dfrac{w_0x^3}{6}+C_1\), \(EIw=-\dfrac{w_0x^4}{24}+C_1x+C_2\).
  3. Conditions at the wall \(x=L\): \(w'(L)=0\Rightarrow C_1=\dfrac{w_0L^3}{6}\); \(w(L)=0\Rightarrow C_2=\dfrac{w_0L^4}{24}-\dfrac{w_0L^4}{6}=-\dfrac{w_0L^4}{8}\). Here the board is built into the wall on the right, so it is level and at zero height at \(x=L\); the free end \(A\) just follows.
  4. Result: \[w(x)=-\frac{w_0}{24EI}\left(x^4-4L^3x+3L^4\right).\]
  5. Check: \(w(L)=-\frac{w_0}{24EI}(L^4-4L^4+3L^4)=0\) ✓; free end \(w(0)=-\dfrac{w_0L^4}{8EI}\) (down) ✓.

\(\boxed{w(x)=-\dfrac{w_0}{24EI}\left(x^4-4L^3x+3L^4\right),\quad w_A=-\dfrac{w_0L^4}{8EI}}\)

Here \(w_0\) is a load per length (kN/m), not a deflection; the paper's notation. Measured from the wall instead, the same curve is \(w=-\dfrac{w_0x^2}{24EI}(6L^2-4Lx+x^2)\).

Ready results · cantilevers (length \(l\), tip values, downward loads)
  • End force \(F\): tip deflection \(\dfrac{Fl^3}{3EI}\) down, tip slope \(\dfrac{Fl^2}{2EI}\).
  • Uniform load \(q\) over the whole length: tip deflection \(\dfrac{ql^4}{8EI}\) down, tip slope \(\dfrac{ql^3}{6EI}\).

You can now: find \(w(x)\) and \(w'(x)\) of a cantilever with an end force or a uniform load, with the wall on either side; and quote the tip values \(Fl^3/3EI\) and \(ql^4/8EI\).

7Multiple regions: a point load on a simply supported beam ~5 min

What is this?

Back to the plank bridge, with you standing off-centre. Under your feet the moment changes its formula, so the left part and the right part of the plank need separate curves. But it is still one plank: at your feet the two curves must meet at the same height (no break) and with the same tilt (no kink). Those two facts are the matching conditions.

When the formula for \(M\) changes along the beam (at a point force, a couple, or where a distributed load starts or stops), integrate separately in each region. Each region brings two constants, so extra conditions are needed: the matching conditions at the point where the regions meet.

Worked example · force \(F\) at \(x=a\)

A simply supported beam (pin \(A\), roller \(B\)), span \(l=a+b\), bending stiffness \(EI\), carries a downward force \(F\) at \(x=a\). Find \(w\) and \(w'\).

System

FBD: \(A=Fb/l\), \(B=Fa/l\) (S2 Section 2)

  1. Do: Moment. The shear force jumps at \(x=a\), so \(M\) has two formulas (from S2 Section 2): \[M(x)=\begin{cases}F\dfrac bl\,x, & 0\le x\le a,\\[4pt] F\dfrac al\,(l-x), & a\le x\le l.\end{cases}\] Region I is \(0\le x\le a\), region II is \(a\le x\le l\). Your weight splits the plank into two stretches that curl by different rules, so no single formula fits.
  2. Do: Integrate in each region:
    Region I:\[\begin{aligned}EIw_{\rm I}''&=F\tfrac bl x,\\ EIw_{\rm I}'&=F\tfrac bl\tfrac{x^2}{2}+C_1,\\ EIw_{\rm I}&=F\tfrac bl\tfrac{x^3}{6}+C_1x+C_2;\end{aligned}\]Region II:\[\begin{aligned}EIw_{\rm II}''&=F\tfrac al(l-x),\\ EIw_{\rm II}'&=-F\tfrac al\tfrac{(l-x)^2}{2}+C_3,\\ EIw_{\rm II}&=F\tfrac al\tfrac{(l-x)^3}{6}\\&\quad-C_3(l-x)+C_4.\end{aligned}\] (Region II is integrated in powers of \((l-x)\), so that the roller condition at \(x=l\) is easy. Writing \(-C_3(l-x)\) instead of \(C_3x\) just moves a constant into \(C_4\).) Each stretch gets its own curve with its own starting tilt and height: four unknowns in all.
  3. Do: Boundary conditions: \(w_{\rm I}(0)=0\Rightarrow C_2=0\); \(w_{\rm II}(l)=0\Rightarrow C_4=0\). Each bank holds its end of the plank at zero height. The ends may tilt, so there is no slope condition there.
  4. Do: Matching conditions. A real beam does not break or kink at the load, so the deflection and the slope are continuous at \(x=a\): \[\begin{aligned}w_{\rm I}(a)&=w_{\rm II}(a):\\ &F\tfrac bl\tfrac{a^3}{6}+C_1a=F\tfrac al\tfrac{b^3}{6}-C_3b,\\ w_{\rm I}'(a)&=w_{\rm II}'(a):\\ &F\tfrac bl\tfrac{a^2}{2}+C_1=-F\tfrac al\tfrac{b^2}{2}+C_3.\end{aligned}\] Without these, the two curves could meet in a step or a sharp corner under your feet; with them, four equations fix the four constants.
  5. Do: Solve the two equations: \[\begin{aligned}C_1&=-\frac{Fab(a+2b)}{6l},\\ C_3&=\frac{Fab(b+2a)}{6l}.\end{aligned}\] \(C_1/EI\) is the tilt at the left bank (negative: the plank dips going in) and \(C_3/EI\) the tilt at the right bank (positive: it climbs out), as in the end slopes below.
Definition · matching (continuity) conditions for deflection

At the boundary between two regions: \(w\) and \(w'\) are always continuous (the beam stays in one piece and has no kink) unless there is an internal hinge, where \(w'\) may jump. Compare S2 Section 11, where \(V\) and \(M\) were matched.

Final result (by substituting the constants; \(a(a+2b)=l^2-b^2\)):

\(0\le x\le a\):

\[w_{\rm I}(x)=-\frac{Fbx}{6lEI}\left(l^2-b^2-x^2\right),\]

\(a\le x\le l\):

\[\begin{aligned}w_{\rm II}(x)&=-\frac{Fa(l-x)}{6lEI}\\&\quad\times\left[l^2-a^2-(l-x)^2\right].\end{aligned}\]

In words: region II is region I seen from the other end: swap \(a\) and \(b\) and measure from \(B\) instead of \(A\).

Key values

Under the load:

\[w(a)=-\frac{Fa^2b^2}{3lEI}.\]

End slopes:

\[\begin{aligned}w'(0)&=-\frac{Fab(l+b)}{6lEI},\\ w'(l)&=\frac{Fab(l+a)}{6lEI}.\end{aligned}\]

Central load (\(a=b=\tfrac l2\)):

\[w_{\max}=-\frac{Fl^3}{48EI}.\]

In words: a central load on a simply supported beam deflects it by \(Fl^3/48EI\): 16 times less than the same load on a cantilever of the same length.

Reading the formula
\(a^2b^2\)
how far you stand from each bank. Near a bank one of them is small and the plank hardly sags under you; the product is largest when you stand in the middle.
\(l\) and \(EI\) underneath
a stiffer plank sags less; for a fixed position the sag grows like \(l^3\) (put \(a=b=l/2\)).
minus sign
the plank goes down under your feet.

If \(a\ge b\), the largest deflection is in region I, where \(w_{\rm I}'=0\):

\[\begin{aligned}x_m&=\sqrt{\frac{l^2-b^2}{3}},\\ w_{\max}&=-\frac{Fb\,(l^2-b^2)^{3/2}}{9\sqrt3\,lEI}.\end{aligned}\]

The maximum is not under the load unless the load is central; it is always within about \(0.077l\) of mid-span.

8Macaulay brackets for deflection Going further ~4 min

What is this?

Instead of separate curves glued together at every load, write one formula with switches in it. A bracket \([x-a]\) is a switch that stays off (zero) until \(x\) passes \(a\), and then turns on. After integration the switch turns on gently, with zero height and zero tilt at \(a\), so it never puts a break or a kink into the plank. The gluing then takes care of itself.

Going further · why the exam needs this

The matching method grows fast: with three or more load points the matching method needs 6, 8, … constants. Dec 2014 A1(c) asks for a deflection on a beam written with Macaulay brackets (S2 Section 7), and the bracket method keeps two constants for the whole beam.

Rule · integrating a Macaulay bracket \[\int[x-a]^n\,\mathrm dx=\frac{[x-a]^{n+1}}{n+1}\qquad(n\ge0).\]

In words: integrate a bracket like an ordinary power of \((x-a)\), and keep the bracket: never multiply it out. Because each bracket is zero (with zero slope) until \(x=a\), the matching conditions \(w_{\rm I}=w_{\rm II}\), \(w'_{\rm I}=w'_{\rm II}\) are satisfied automatically, and the same two constants \(C_1,C_2\) hold in every region.

  1. Do: Write \(M(x)\) as one Macaulay expression, from the left end (S2 Section 7 table: force \(-P[x-a]\), clockwise couple \(+M_0[x-a]^0\), uniform load \(-\tfrac q2[x-a]^2\)). Each load adds its own switch at the point where it acts, so one line describes every stretch of the plank.
  2. Do: \(EIw''=M\); integrate twice, bracket by bracket, adding \(C_1x+C_2\) once. Each switched term integrates to one that turns on with zero height and zero tilt, so no break or kink appears and no new constants are needed.
  3. Do: Use the two support conditions (usually \(w=0\) at each support) to find \(C_1,C_2\). The whole plank is one curve with one starting tilt and one starting height, so two support facts fix it.
  4. Do: Evaluate \(w\) where asked: brackets with \(x\lt a\) are zero. Loads further right have not switched on yet at that point; they still count, through \(C_1\).
Worked example · the S2 Macaulay beam

The beam of S2 Section 7: span 10 m, pin at \(x=0\), roller at \(x=10\u{m}\), \(30\u{kN}\) at 3 m, \(50\u{kN}\) at 7 m; reactions \(36\u{kN}\) and \(44\u{kN}\). Find the deflection at 3 m, 5 m and 7 m and the largest deflection.

System

  2. Integrate: \[\begin{aligned}EIw'&=18x^2-15[x-3]^2\\&\quad-25[x-7]^2+C_1,\\ EIw&=6x^3-5[x-3]^3\\&\quad-\tfrac{25}{3}[x-7]^3+C_1x+C_2.\end{aligned}\]
  3. \(w(0)=0\): every bracket is zero, so \(C_2=0\). \(w(10)=0\): \(6000-5(343)-\tfrac{25}{3}(27)+10C_1=0\Rightarrow 6000-1715-225+10C_1=0\Rightarrow C_1=-406\u{kN\,m^2}\).
  4. Values (\(EI\) in kN·m², \(w\) in m): \[\begin{aligned}EIw(3)&=162-1218\\&=-1056,\\ EIw(5)&=750-40-2030\\&=-1320,\\ EIw(7)&=2058-320-2842\\&=-1104.\end{aligned}\]

9The method of superposition ~4 min

What is this?

Two people stand on the plank bridge. The sag with both of them is the sag the first one makes alone plus the sag the second one makes alone. This works because the plank responds in proportion: twice the load, twice the sag, and the two effects do not interfere.

Suppose a simply supported beam carries a line load \(q_1\) over its left part and a force \(F_2\) further right. Instead of a new calculation, split it into two simpler beams, one with only \(q_1\) and one with only \(F_2\), and add the results:

Principle · superposition \[\begin{aligned}w&=w_1+w_2,\quad w'=w_1'+w_2',\\ M&=M_1+M_2,\quad V=V_1+V_2.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the effect of several loads together is the sum of their effects one at a time.

  1. Do: split the loading into simple cases that you know (the ready table below). Each case is the plank with one person on it, whose sag is already worked out.
  2. Do: write each case's \(w(x)\), or its value at the point asked, with the same \(x\) and the same sign convention. You are adding heights at the same spot on the same plank; a different origin or sign would add the wrong things.
  3. Do: add: \(w=w_1+w_2\), and likewise \(w'\), \(M\), \(V\). Each part fits its own moment and the same supports, so the sum fits the total moment and the supports too.
  4. Do: for the largest deflection, add the functions and then look for \(w'=0\); add the maxima directly only if they sit at the same point. Two dips in different places do not stack into one dip as deep as both.
Going further · why it works, and when it does not

The equation \(EIw''=M\) is linear: if \(w_1\) solves it for \(M_1\) and \(w_2\) for \(M_2\), then \(w_1+w_2\) solves it for \(M_1+M_2\), with the same (zero) boundary conditions. This needs small deflections and a linear-elastic material. It fails if the beam yields, or if it deflects so much that the geometry changes (then the small-angle assumption of Section 3 is gone).

A simply supported beam of span 6 m carries a uniform load \(4\u{kN/m}\) over the whole span and \(10\u{kN}\) at mid-span. Find the mid-span deflection.

10Statically indeterminate beams Going further ~7 min

What is this?

Prop the tip of the diving board on a post. Now the wall and the post share the load, and equilibrium cannot say how: the wall could hold more and the post less, or the other way round. The board's shape settles it. The post holds the tip at zero height, so it must push up exactly hard enough to cancel the drop the load would cause.

Going further · why this section is here

A beam with more supports than equilibrium can handle (degree of indeterminacy \(x\ge1\), S1 Section 11) cannot be solved with statics alone. Past papers ask for propped cantilevers (Dec 2018 A1(b), Dec 2015 A1(c)) and a spring-supported beam (May 2013 A3). The extra equation always comes from deflection: the beam must fit its supports. This is called a compatibility condition.

Method A · integrate with the redundant reaction kept as an unknown

Worked example · propped cantilever with a uniform load

A beam of span \(l\) is clamped at \(A\) (\(x=0\)) and rests on a roller at \(B\) (\(x=l\)); it carries a uniform load \(q_0\). Unknowns: \(A_V\), \(M_A\), \(B\) (three), but only two useful equilibrium equations: degree 1.

System

FBD with the unknown roller force \(B\)

  1. Moment in terms of \(B\). Keep the right piece (length \(l-x\)): \(B\) up at distance \(l-x\) (sagging), the load \(q_0(l-x)\) at half that distance (hogging): \[M=B(l-x)-\tfrac12q_0(l-x)^2.\]
  2. Integrate (in powers of \(l-x\)): \[\begin{aligned}EIw'&=-\tfrac B2(l-x)^2+\tfrac{q_0}{6}(l-x)^3\\&\quad+C_1,\\ EIw&=\tfrac B6(l-x)^3-\tfrac{q_0}{24}(l-x)^4\\&\quad-C_1(l-x)+C_2.\end{aligned}\]
  3. Three geometrical conditions for three unknowns \(C_1,C_2,B\): \(w(l)=0\Rightarrow C_2=0\); \(w'(0)=0\Rightarrow C_1=\tfrac B2l^2-\tfrac{q_0}6l^3\); \(w(0)=0\Rightarrow \tfrac B6l^3-\tfrac{q_0}{24}l^4-C_1l=0\). The roller force is one more unknown, and the roller gives one more fact about the shape: the height there is zero.
  4. Substituting \(C_1\): \(\tfrac B6l^3-\tfrac B2l^3-\tfrac{q_0}{24}l^4+\tfrac{q_0}{6}l^4=0\Rightarrow -\tfrac B3+\tfrac{q_0l}{8}=0\Rightarrow B=\tfrac38q_0l\).
  5. Now statics finishes the job: \(A_V=q_0l-B=\tfrac58q_0l\); \(M(0)=\tfrac38q_0l^2-\tfrac12q_0l^2=-\tfrac18q_0l^2\) (hogging at the wall). Once \(B\) is known the beam is an ordinary determinate one.

\(\boxed{B=\tfrac38q_0l,\quad A_V=\tfrac58q_0l,\quad M_A=\tfrac18q_0l^2\text{ (hogging)}}\)

Also: \(V=q_0\left(\tfrac58l-x\right)\); the largest sagging moment is \(\tfrac{9}{128}q_0l^2\) at \(x=\tfrac58l\) (where \(V=0\)); the largest deflection is \(\dfrac{q_0l^4}{185EI}\) at \(x=0.578l\).

Top: \(M/(q_0l^2)\): \(-\tfrac18\) at the clamp, \(+\tfrac9{128}\) at \(\tfrac58l\), 0 at the roller. Bottom: \(wEI/(q_0l^4)\).

Alternative · integrate the load four times

Dec 2018 A1(b) says "using the integral method". Then start from \(q\): \(V=-q_0x+C_1\), \(M=-\tfrac12q_0x^2+C_1x+C_2\), \(EIw'=\int M\,\mathrm dx+C_3\), \(EIw=\iint M+C_3x+C_4\), and use four conditions: \(w(0)=0\), \(w'(0)=0\) (clamp), \(w(l)=0\), \(M(l)=0\) (roller). It gives the same \(C_1=\tfrac58q_0l\), \(C_2=-\tfrac18q_0l^2\). (This is the exam taster below.)

Method B · compatibility by superposition

Remove the extra support, call its force \(B\) (the redundant), and demand that the deflections it removes add up to what the support allows.

  1. Do: remove the extra support and put its unknown force \(B\) in its place. Without the post the board is an ordinary cantilever, whose sag is in the ready table.
  2. Do: find the deflection at that point from the load alone and from \(B\) alone. By superposition (Section 9) the real deflection is the sum of the two.
  3. Do: set the sum equal to what the support allows: 0 for a rigid prop, the spring's compression \(R/k\) for a spring. This is the compatibility condition: the board must end up where the support holds it.
\[\underbrace{-\frac{q_0l^4}{8EI}}_{\text{load alone}}+\underbrace{\frac{Bl^3}{3EI}}_{B\text{ alone}}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad B=\frac38q_0l.\]

In words: the roller must push the tip back up by exactly as much as the load pushed it down. Both terms come straight from the ready table (Section 9).

Worked example · past paper May 2013 A3 · 20 marks older format · spring support

Figure A3 shows a uniformly distributed simply supported beam with an additional spring support at \(B\), which deflects 0.0005 mm per kgf. Assume that the elastic modulus and the second moment of area of the beam are 204 GPa and 9350 cm⁴, respectively. Determine (a) the deflection at \(B\) [14]; and (b) the maximum bending moment and its location [6].

Pin at \(A\), roller at \(C\), span 9 m (4.5 m + 4.5 m), spring at mid-span \(B\), uniform \(16\u{kN/m}\) over the whole span.

  1. Data in SI. \(EI=204\times10^9\u{Pa}\times9350\times10^{-8}\u{m^4}=1.907\times10^7\u{N\,m^2}\). A kgf (kilogram-force) is the weight of 1 kg, \(9.807\u{N}\), so the spring stiffness is \(k=\dfrac{9.807\u{N}}{0.0005\times10^{-3}\u{m}}=1.961\times10^7\u{N/m}\).
  2. Compatibility. Remove the spring, replace it by its upward force \(R\). The beam's mid-span deflection must equal the spring's compression \(R/k\): The beam and the top of the spring move together, so the spring is squashed by exactly as much as the beam sags there. \[\frac{5qL^4}{384EI}-\frac{RL^3}{48EI}=\frac Rk.\]
  3. Numbers: \(\dfrac{5qL^4}{384EI}=\dfrac{5(16000)(9^4)}{384(1.907\times10^7)}=0.07166\u{m}\); \(\dfrac{L^3}{48EI}=7.96\times10^{-7}\u{m/N}\); \(\dfrac1k=5.10\times10^{-8}\u{m/N}\). \[\begin{aligned}R&=\frac{0.07166}{7.96\times10^{-7}+5.10\times10^{-8}}\\&=84.6\u{kN}.\end{aligned}\]
  4. (a) \(\delta_B=\dfrac Rk=\dfrac{84.58\times10^3}{1.961\times10^7}=4.31\times10^{-3}\u{m}\).
  5. (b) End reactions \(A=C=\tfrac12(16\times9-84.6)=29.7\u{kN}\). On \(AB\): \(M=29.7x-8x^2\) (kN·m). \(V=0\) at \(x=29.7/16=1.86\u{m}\): \(M=+27.6\u{kN\,m}\). At \(B\): \(M=29.7(4.5)-8(4.5)^2=-28.3\u{kN\,m}\) (hogging).

\(\boxed{\delta_B=4.31\u{mm}\ \text{down};\quad |M|_{\max}=28.3\u{kN\,m}\ \text{(hogging) at }B}\)

Sense check: a rigid prop (\(k\to\infty\)) would take \(\tfrac58qL=90\u{kN}\); the springy prop takes slightly less, 84.6 kN ✓. Without it the beam would sag 71.7 mm; the spring cuts that to 4.3 mm.

"deflects 0.0005 mm per kgf" is read as the spring's flexibility (compression per unit force), so \(k=1\u{kgf}/0.0005\u{mm}\).

You can now: recognise a statically indeterminate beam; find the redundant reaction by integrating with it as an unknown (or the load four times), or by compatibility with the ready table; and handle a spring support with \(\delta=R/k\).

"Simply supported beam, \(l=6\u{m}\): 10 kN at 2 m from \(A\) and 10 kN at 4 m. Each alone gives \(w_{\max}=Fa^2b^2/3lEI=10(4)(16)/(18EI)=35.6/EI\), so together the maximum is \(71.1/EI\)."

Hint

Is \(Fa^2b^2/3lEI\) the deflection at mid-span?

Answer

\(Fa^2b^2/3lEI\) is the deflection under each load, not at mid-span, and the two loads sit at different points. By symmetry the maximum is at mid-span: each load gives there \(EIw_{\rm I}(3)\) with \(b=2\): \(\dfrac{10(2)(3)}{36}(36-4-9)=38.3\), so the total is \(\boxed{76.7/EI}\) (down) at mid-span.

15Concept checks

1 · Which is stiffer?

Two beams of equal length carry the same central load: one simply supported, one a cantilever loaded at its tip. Ratio of their largest deflections?

Answer

\(\dfrac{Fl^3/3EI}{Fl^3/48EI}=16\). The cantilever deflects 16 times more.

2 · Doubling the depth

A rectangular beam's depth is doubled (width unchanged). By what factor does its deflection change?

Answer

\(I=bh^3/12\) grows 8 times, so the deflection falls to \(\tfrac18\).

3 · Inflection point

Where does the deflected shape of a propped cantilever under uniform load change from frown to smile?

Answer

Where \(M=0\) (because \(w''=M/EI\)): \(-\tfrac12q_0x^2+\tfrac58q_0lx-\tfrac18q_0l^2=0\Rightarrow x=\tfrac l4\).

4 · Counting constants

A beam has three regions and you integrate region by region. How many constants, and where do the equations come from?

Answer

6 constants: 2 boundary conditions + 2 matching conditions (\(w\), \(w'\)) at each of the 2 region boundaries = 6.

5 · Symmetry

A simply supported beam with a symmetric load: which condition at mid-span can replace one boundary condition?

Answer

\(w'(l/2)=0\): the beam is horizontal at its line of symmetry. Then integrate over half the beam only.

This is the Section 6 cantilever exactly. For (e) you need \(I=wt^3/12\) (part (d)) in m⁴.

(c) \(M=-F_1(L-x)\); clamp \(w(0)=w'(0)=0\):

Hint

Free end at \(x=0\), wall at \(x=L\): left piece gives \(M=Px-\tfrac12qx^2\). Both conditions at \(x=L\).

Answer

(c) \(EIw'=\tfrac P2x^2-\tfrac q6x^3+C_1\), \(EIw=\tfrac P6x^3-\tfrac q{24}x^4+C_1x+C_2\); \(w'(L)=0\Rightarrow C_1=\tfrac q6L^3-\tfrac P2L^2\); \(w(L)=0\Rightarrow C_2=\tfrac P3L^3-\tfrac q8L^4\).

Dec 2017 A3(a)10 marksS5.2 simply supported deflection

Provided the flexural rigidity \(EI\) is known, solve the deflection for a simply supported beam that carries a uniformly distributed load. Determine the deflection in the middle of the beam when \(x=l/2\).

Hint

Reactions \(\tfrac12ql\) each; \(M=\tfrac q2x(l-x)\); conditions \(w(0)=w(l)=0\).

\(EIw''=\tfrac q2(lx-x^2)\Rightarrow EIw'=\tfrac q2\left(\tfrac{lx^2}{2}-\tfrac{x^3}{3}\right)+C_1\), \(EIw=\tfrac q2\left(\tfrac{lx^3}{6}-\tfrac{x^4}{12}\right)+C_1x+C_2\).

(down). End slopes \(\mp\dfrac{ql^3}{24EI}\). Symmetry check: \(w'(l/2)=0\) ✓.
