How Do We Design a Controller?

Disturbance rejection with a spring–mass–damper: model the task, choose the PID gains, and verify the actual response.

0Where this chapter sits

C1 introduced the PID law and tuned it by feel on a water tank. This chapter uses the same PID law but chooses the gains with mathematics: model the plant, predict its motion, place its poles, check stability, then verify the design against a written specification. One task runs through the whole chapter: a spring–mass–damper (\(m=1\u{kg}\), \(c=2\u{N\,s/m}\), \(k=4\u{N/m}\)) is hit by a constant load \(d=0.12\u{N}\), and the actuator must bring it back to its target \(x=0\).

Chapter on one page

  2. §4–7 The Laplace transform turns the motion equation into algebra: \(X(s)=G(s)\,[F(s)+D(s)]\); partial fractions bring back \(x(t)\). Uncontrolled: a 30 mm offset.
  3. §8–9 Poles set the motion: real part = decay rate, imaginary part = oscillation frequency; \(\omega_n\) and \(\zeta\) classify the damping. Likely exam calculation
  4. §10–11 Specifications and the final-value theorem: peak below 5 mm, back within ±0.5 mm by 2.5 s, force below 0.15 N; final error from \(\lim_{s\to0}sX(s)\).
  5. §12 Feedback: \(L=CG\), \(S=1/(1+L)\), and the load map \(G_d=G/(1+CG)\).
  6. §13–16 P adds stiffness, D adds damping, I stores the steady force; PID gives a third-order characteristic polynomial.
  7. §17–18 Stability and Routh–Hurwitz: the cubic is stable when all coefficients are positive and \((c+K_d)(k+K_p)\gt mK_i\). Likely exam calculation
  8. §19–21 Pole placement: match coefficients to chosen poles (\(K_p=22\), \(K_i=24\), \(K_d=7\)), then verify peak, recovery and force by inverse transform. Likely exam calculation
  9. §22–24 Frequency response: Bode plots, the Nyquist plot and criterion \(Z=P+N\), gain and phase margins.
  10. §25 Heavier mass: the same method gives new gains (\(48, 48, 16\)) that meet the same specification.

Sign convention and units for this chapter
  • Position \(x\) positive to the right, measured from the target; forces positive to the right. Spring force \(-kx\), damper force \(-c\dot x\).
  • Error \(e=r-x\) with fixed target \(r=0\), so \(e=-x\). Negative feedback: the measurement enters the summing junction with a minus sign.
  • s-plane: \(s=\sigma+j\omega\); real part \(\sigma\) (s⁻¹) on the horizontal axis, imaginary part \(\omega\) (rad/s) on the vertical. Left half-plane (\(\sigma\lt0\)) = decaying = stable.
  • SI units in every calculation (m, N, s); displacements are quoted in mm only at the end. Gains: \(K_p\) in N/m, \(K_i\) in N/(m·s), \(K_d\) in N·s/m. (This chapter writes the gains as \(K_p\), \(K_i\), \(K_d\); C1 wrote \(K_{\rm p}\) etc. They are the same.)

1The task and the apparatus ~4 min

What is this?

Picture a shopping trolley tied to a wall by an elastic cord, with a shock absorber alongside. Someone leans on it with a steady push. The cord stretches until it holds the push, so the trolley ends up a little away from where it started. Your job is to stand by it with your hand on it and push back so cleverly that it hardly moves and quickly returns to its mark. Your hand is the actuator; your eyes on the mark are the sensor; the rule you use to push is the controller.

This chapter has three aims: limit the displacement after a constant external load, return to the fixed target quickly, and design and verify a basic PID controller. This is disturbance rejection: limiting the effect of an outside influence while the target stays fixed. (C1's "tracking" was the other job: following a changing target.)

Left: the apparatus. Spring \(k\) and damper \(c\) tie the mass to a fixed wall; the actuator force \(F\) and the external load \(d\) both act on the mass; a sensor measures \(x\) relative to the target \(r=0\). Right: the free-body diagram of the mass, drawn for \(x\gt0\), \(v\gt0\): spring force \(kx\) and damper force \(cv\) pull to the left (they are \(-kx\) and \(-cv\) in the positive-right convention); \(F\) and \(d\) are drawn positive to the right.

Definitions · the quantities
  • Disturbance \(d\): an external force we do not choose (here a step of 0.12 N). It enters independently of the controller.
  • Actuator force \(F\): the controller's output, the only thing we can choose.
  • Displacement \(x\): the deviation from the fixed target; the controller reads it.
  • Baseline parameters: \(m=1\u{kg}\), \(c=2\u{N\,s/m}\), \(k=4\u{N/m}\); load step \(d=0.12\u{N}\).

The one test used throughout: rest at the target, then a 0.12 N load step at \(t=0\), controller off (\(F=0\))

Top: the load. Bottom: without a controller the mass swings to a peak of 34.89 mm at 1.81 s and settles at 30 mm. It settles, but not at the target \(x=0\). This uncontrolled curve is the starting comparison for every design.

Key idea: every time-response comparison in the chapter uses the same load step, the same target and the same initial state (rest at \(x=0\)). Only the controller changes, so differences in the curves come from the controller alone.

You can now: state the disturbance-rejection task; name \(d\), \(F\), \(x\) and \(r\) on the apparatus; and draw the free-body diagram of the mass with the spring force \(-kx\), damper force \(-cv\), actuator \(F\) and load \(d\).

2State, order and the linear time-invariant model ~4 min

What is this?

To predict where a thrown ball goes next, a photo of where it is now is not enough: you also need how fast it is moving. Position plus speed is enough; nothing else about the past matters. That minimum list is the state. A mass on a spring needs exactly two numbers, so it is a "second-order" system.

Definitions · state and order
  • State: a sufficient set of variables for predicting the future motion from the known inputs.
  • System order: the number of independent state variables.

For the mass: \(x_1=x\) (position) and \(x_2=v=\dot x\) (velocity). Position sets the spring force; velocity sets the damper force and the rate of change of position. So two states, order 2:

\[\begin{aligned}\dot x_1&=x_2,\\ \dot x_2&=\frac{F+d-cx_2-kx_1}{m}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: position changes at the velocity; velocity changes at (net force)/(mass). The test starts from \(x=0\), \(v=0\): the load changes the acceleration first, then velocity, then position.

The motion equation

Newton's second law on the free-body diagram of Section 1 (sum of forces to the right = \(m\ddot x\)):

Result · the plant model \[\begin{aligned}m\ddot x&=F+d-c\dot x-kx\\ \Rightarrow\ m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx&=F+d.\end{aligned}\]

With the baseline values in SI units: \(\ddot x+2\dot x+4x=F+d\).

Reading the formula
\(m\ddot x\)
mass × acceleration: what the net force produces.
\(c\dot x\)
the damper's resistance, growing with speed (like pushing a spoon through honey).
\(kx\)
the spring's pull back, growing with stretch.
\(F+d\)
the two outside pushes: ours (actuator) and the world's (load).

In words: mass × acceleration + damping force + spring force = actuator force + load. Units: kg·m/s² = N·s/m·m/s = N/m·m = N ✓.

Definitions · linear and time-invariant
  • Linear: responses obey scaling and addition. Starting from rest, doubling the load doubles the response, and the responses to two separate inputs add. Here every state and input appears to the first power (no \(x^2\), no \(\sin x\)).
  • Time-invariant: shifting an input in time shifts its response by the same amount. Here \(m\), \(c\), \(k\) stay constant during a test, so the same experiment run later gives the same curve, just later.
  • LTI = linear and time-invariant. Everything in this chapter (Laplace transforms, transfer functions, poles) relies on it.

The question for the rest of the chapter: predict \(x(t)\) after the load appears, first with \(F=0\), then with feedback.

You can now: define state and order and say why the mass has two states; write \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F+d\) from the free-body diagram; and explain linearity and time invariance with an example.

3Initial state and the equilibrium force balance ~3 min

What is this?

If someone leans on the trolley with a steady 0.12 N and you want it to stay exactly on its mark, the elastic cord is not stretched and the shock absorber is not moving, so neither helps. Your hand must push back with exactly 0.12 N, for as long as they lean. "Back on the mark" therefore needs a permanent push from you, not just a quick correction.

Definitions · initial condition and equilibrium
  • Initial condition: the starting state. Here, before the load appears: \(x(0)=\dot x(0)=0\), and \(d(0^-)=F(0^-)=0\) (\(0^-\) = just before \(t=0\)).
  • Equilibrium: a state where the state variables are constant, so their derivatives are zero.

At equilibrium \(\ddot x=\dot x=0\), so the motion equation becomes a force balance:

Result · equilibrium force balance \[kx_{\rm eq}=F_{\rm eq}+d_0.\]

For the fixed zero target, \(x_{\rm eq}=0\), so

\[F_{\rm eq}=-d_0=-0.12\u{N}.\]

In words: at rest the damper gives no force; on the target the spring gives no force; so the actuator alone must cancel the load, for ever.

  • A mass at rest has zero damping force; to stay at the original target its spring force is also zero.
  • So the actuator must supply a persistent −0.12 N. The chapter then tests which controller structures can provide that force at zero position error.

Key idea: returning to the target needs the correct steady force as well as stable transient motion. Keep this in mind: it is why P alone fails and I succeeds (Sections 13–15).

You can now: write the zero initial state; use the equilibrium force balance \(kx_{\rm eq}=F_{\rm eq}+d_0\); and explain why holding \(x=0\) against a constant load needs a constant actuator force of −0.12 N.

4Complex numbers and exponential motion ~4 min

What is this?

A plucked guitar string does two things at once: it vibrates (a pitch) and it fades (gets quieter). One complex number \(s=\sigma+j\omega\) stores both facts: \(\omega\) is how fast it vibrates, \(\sigma\) is how fast it fades (negative) or grows (positive). Then \(e^{st}\) is the whole sound in one symbol.

Definitions · complex numbers
  • A complex number \(s=\sigma+j\omega\) has a real part \(\sigma\) and an imaginary part \(\omega\), with \(j^2=-1\). (Engineers write \(j\), not \(i\), because \(i\) is used for current.)
  • Conjugates share the real part and have opposite imaginary parts: \(\sigma+j\omega\) and \(\sigma-j\omega\).
  • The s-plane is the plane with \(\sigma\) across and \(\omega\) up; each complex number is a point.
Result · exponential of a complex number (Euler's formula) \[e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t}=e^{\sigma t}\left[\cos(\omega t)+j\sin(\omega t)\right].\]
Reading the formula
\(e^{\sigma t}\)
the envelope: decays if \(\sigma\lt0\), grows if \(\sigma\gt0\), constant if \(\sigma=0\).
\(\cos\omega t+j\sin\omega t\)
a point going round the unit circle at \(\omega\) rad/s: the oscillation.
\(\sigma\) (s⁻¹)
exponential growth or decay rate.
\(\omega\) (rad/s)
angular frequency.

In words: the real coordinate controls growth or decay; the imaginary coordinate sets the angular frequency.

A conjugate pair \(-1\pm j\sqrt3\) in the s-plane

\(e^{-t}\cos(\sqrt3\,t)\) inside its envelope \(\pm e^{-t}\)

An example, \(\sigma=-1\), \(\omega=\sqrt3\): the real signal oscillates inside a decaying envelope (dashed). \(\sigma=-1\) gives the decay, \(|\omega|=\sqrt3\) the oscillation rate.

Why conjugate pairs matter: a real motion cannot have an imaginary part. Adding the two conjugate exponentials cancels the imaginary parts:

\[\begin{aligned}&\tfrac12\left(e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t}+e^{(\sigma-j\omega)t}\right)\\ &\quad=e^{\sigma t}\cos\omega t.\end{aligned}\]

In words: a conjugate pair combines into one real decaying (or growing) oscillation. These exponential pieces will describe the transient part of the load response.

You can now: read \(\sigma\) and \(\omega\) off a point in the s-plane; expand \(e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t}\) with Euler's formula; and explain how a conjugate pair gives a real decaying oscillation.

5The Laplace transform, transform pairs and linearity ~5 min

What is this?

Logarithms turned multiplication into addition, so people multiplied big numbers by looking up logs, adding, and looking back. The Laplace transform does the same trick for differential equations: look up the transform of each term in a table, solve the resulting algebra, and look the answer back up to get the motion in time.

Definition · one-sided Laplace transform \[\begin{aligned}\mathcal L\{f(t)\}&=F(s)\\ &=\int_0^\infty f(t)\,e^{-st}\,\mathrm dt,\\ s&=\sigma+j\omega.\end{aligned}\]
Reading the formula
\(\int_0^\infty\)
"one-sided": only \(t\ge0\) counts; what happened before the experiment is carried by the initial conditions.
\(e^{-st}\)
an exponential weight that makes late times count less, so the integral can converge.
\(F(s)\)
a function of the complex variable \(s\), not of time.

In words: weight the signal by \(e^{-st}\) and add up over all positive time; the answer depends on \(s\). It exists where that weighting makes the integral converge.

Worked example · the load's transform

The load is \(d(t)=0.12\u{N}\) for \(t\ge0\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}D(s)&=\int_0^\infty0.12\,e^{-st}\,\mathrm dt\\ &=0.12\left[\frac{e^{-st}}{-s}\right]_0^\infty=\frac{0.12}{s},\end{aligned}\]

valid for \({\rm Re}(s)\gt0\). \(e^{-st}\to0\) as \(t\to\infty\) only if the real part of \(s\) is positive; that is the region where the integral converges. Units: N·s (the transform of a force carries an extra second).

Key idea: \(s\) belongs to the transformed description; time \(t\) remains the variable of the physical motion. The derivative rules (Section 6) turn the motion equation into algebra, and the inverse transform (Section 7) recovers \(x(t)\).

Transform pairs and linearity

Ready table · the transform pairs used in this chapter
Time signal \(f(t)\), \(t\ge0\)Transform \(F(s)\)Where it appears
\(1\) (unit step)\(\dfrac1s\)the constant load, the final offset
\(e^{-bt}\)\(\dfrac{1}{s+b}\)a real pole at \(-b\)
\(e^{-bt}\cos\omega t\)\(\dfrac{s+b}{(s+b)^2+\omega^2}\)damped cosine (complex pole pair)
\(e^{-bt}\sin\omega t\)\(\dfrac{\omega}{(s+b)^2+\omega^2}\)damped sine (complex pole pair)

In words: read left to right to transform a signal; read right to left to recover it.

Show the calculation: the pair \(e^{-bt}\leftrightarrow 1/(s+b)\) and the damped cosine
  1. \(\int_0^\infty e^{-bt}e^{-st}\,\mathrm dt=\int_0^\infty e^{-(s+b)t}\,\mathrm dt\). The two exponentials multiply by adding their exponents.
  2. \(=\left[\dfrac{e^{-(s+b)t}}{-(s+b)}\right]_0^\infty=\dfrac{1}{s+b}\) for \({\rm Re}(s)\gt-b\). Same integral as the step, with \(s\) replaced by \(s+b\).
  3. Write \(\cos\omega t=\tfrac12(e^{j\omega t}+e^{-j\omega t})\), so \(e^{-bt}\cos\omega t=\tfrac12\left(e^{-(b-j\omega)t}+e^{-(b+j\omega)t}\right)\). Euler's formula (Section 4) splits the cosine into two complex exponentials.
  4. Use step 2 twice: \(\tfrac12\left(\dfrac{1}{s+b-j\omega}+\dfrac{1}{s+b+j\omega}\right)\). Each piece is an exponential with "\(b\)" replaced by \(b\mp j\omega\).
  5. Common denominator: \(\dfrac{(s+b)}{(s+b)^2+\omega^2}\). \((s+b-j\omega)(s+b+j\omega)=(s+b)^2+\omega^2\); the numerators add to \(2(s+b)\).
  6. The sine pair follows the same way with \(\sin\omega t=\tfrac1{2j}(e^{j\omega t}-e^{-j\omega t})\), giving \(\omega/[(s+b)^2+\omega^2]\).
Result · linearity of the transform \[\mathcal L\{a f+b g\}=aF(s)+bG(s).\]

In words: the transform of a weighted sum is the same weighted sum of the transforms. Scaling the load scales its transform; the transform of a sum is the sum of the transforms.

Worked example

Transform \(f(t)=3-2e^{-4t}\). By linearity and the first two pairs: \(F(s)=\dfrac3s-\dfrac{2}{s+4}\). Check at \(s=1\): \(3-0.4=2.6\), and the integral \(\int_0^\infty(3-2e^{-4t})e^{-t}\mathrm dt=3-2/5=2.6\) ✓.

You can now: state the definition of the Laplace transform; find \(D(s)=0.12/s\); use the four pairs in either direction; and apply linearity.

6Derivative rules and the transfer function ~5 min

What is this?

In the transformed world, "take the derivative" becomes "multiply by \(s\)" (plus a correction for how things started). So the motion equation, full of dots, becomes a plain multiplication. A transfer function is then just the exchange rate between what goes in and what comes out: so many metres of displacement per newton of force, at each \(s\).

Result · derivative rules \[\begin{aligned}\mathcal L\{\dot x\}&=sX(s)-x_0,\\ \mathcal L\{\ddot x\}&=s^2X(s)-sx_0-v_0.\end{aligned}\]
Reading the formula
\(sX(s)\)
differentiating in time = multiplying by \(s\) in the transform.
\(x_0,\ v_0\)
initial position and velocity: the rule keeps a memory of the starting state.

In words: each derivative multiplies by \(s\) and subtracts the starting value of what was differentiated.

Show the calculation: why \(\mathcal L\{\dot x\}=sX-x_0\)
  1. \(\mathcal L\{\dot x\}=\int_0^\infty \dot x\,e^{-st}\,\mathrm dt\). Definition.
  2. Integrate by parts with \(u=e^{-st}\), \(\mathrm dv=\dot x\,\mathrm dt\): \(=\left[x\,e^{-st}\right]_0^\infty+s\int_0^\infty x\,e^{-st}\,\mathrm dt\). \(\mathrm du=-se^{-st}\mathrm dt\), so the minus signs combine to \(+s\).
  3. \(=(0-x_0)+sX(s)\). \(e^{-st}\) kills the upper limit where the transform converges; the lower limit gives \(x(0)\).
  4. Apply the rule to \(\dot x\) itself: \(\mathcal L\{\ddot x\}=s\,\mathcal L\{\dot x\}-v_0=s(sX-x_0)-v_0\). \(\ddot x\) is the derivative of \(\dot x\), whose starting value is \(v_0\).

Our test starts at rest on the target, \(x_0=v_0=0\), so the initial-state terms vanish. Transforming \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F+d\) term by term (linearity):

\[(ms^2+cs+k)\,X(s)=F(s)+D(s).\]

In words: the differential equation has become algebra containing the known load \(D\), the actuator output \(F\) and the displacement \(X\) we want to predict. Zero initial conditions are used in every transfer-function calculation and every plotted load test.

Definition · transfer function

The transfer function of an LTI system is the ratio of the transformed output to the transformed input, with zero initial state.

For the mass, the input is the net applied force \(u(t)=F(t)+d(t)\), \(U(s)=F(s)+D(s)\); the output is \(x\):

\[\begin{aligned}G(s)&=\frac{X(s)}{U(s)}=\frac{1}{ms^2+cs+k}\\ &=\frac{1}{s^2+2s+4}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: \(G\) is the plant's rule for turning net force into displacement (m/N). It belongs to the mechanics and does not change when we add a controller.

Block-diagram reading of the plant. A block multiplies its input by its transfer function; a summing junction (Σ) adds its inputs with the signs shown. With the controller off (\(F=0\)): \(X/D=G(s)\).

  • Controller off: \(F=0\Rightarrow X/D=G(s)=1/(s^2+2s+4)\). The disturbance alone produces the uncontrolled motion.
  • Feedback will choose \(F\) from the measured position. The mechanical transfer function \(G\) stays the same, while the disturbance response changes (Section 12).
  • A transfer function always names an input–output pair and assumes zero initial state.

You can now: apply the derivative rules with initial conditions; transform the motion equation to \((ms^2+cs+k)X=F+D\); and define and write the plant transfer function \(G(s)\).

7The inverse transform: the uncontrolled 30 mm offset ~6 min

What is this?

The transformed answer \(X(s)\) is like a parcel of mixed goods. Partial fractions unpack it into standard items, each of which is in the table; then each item is translated back into time and the pieces are added. A constant piece is the final offset; the decaying oscillating pieces are the wobble that dies away.

Definitions
  • Inverse Laplace transform: recovers the time signal from its transformed expression.
  • Partial fractions: rewriting a ratio of polynomials as a sum of simple fractions that match the transform pairs.
Worked example · uncontrolled response to the 0.12 N step

Given \(F=0\), \(D=0.12/s\), \(G=1/(s^2+2s+4)\), zero initial state. Find \(x(t)\) and its final value.

  1. Do: multiply: \(X(s)=G(s)D(s)=\dfrac{0.12}{s(s^2+2s+4)}\). Output transform = transfer function × input transform.
  2. Do: complete the square: \(s^2+2s+4=(s+1)^2+3\). That puts the quadratic in the form \((s+b)^2+\omega^2\) of the damped pairs, with \(b=1\), \(\omega=\sqrt3\).
  3. Do: partial fractions: \[\begin{aligned}X(s)&=\frac{0.03}{s}-0.03\,\frac{s+1}{(s+1)^2+3}\\ &\quad-0.03\,\frac{1}{(s+1)^2+3}.\end{aligned}\] Each term now matches a pair: step, damped cosine, damped sine (needs the factor \(\sqrt3\) on top).
  4. Do: invert term by term: \[\begin{aligned}x(t)&=0.03\Big[1-e^{-t}\Big(\cos\sqrt3\,t\\ &\qquad+\frac{\sin\sqrt3\,t}{\sqrt3}\Big)\Big]\ {\rm m}.\end{aligned}\] \(\dfrac{1}{(s+1)^2+3}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{(s+1)^2+3}\leftrightarrow\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}e^{-t}\sin\sqrt3\,t\).
  5. Do: check: \(x(0)=0.03[1-1]=0\) ✓ (starts on target); as \(t\to\infty\), \(e^{-t}\to0\) so \(x\to0.03\u{m}=\boxed{30\u{mm}}\). The constant term is the final offset; the damped sine and cosine are the transient.
Show the calculation: the partial-fraction coefficients
  1. Write \(\dfrac{0.12}{s(s^2+2s+4)}=\dfrac{A}{s}+\dfrac{Bs+C}{s^2+2s+4}\). A simple factor \(s\) gets a constant on top; an irreducible quadratic gets a linear numerator.
  2. Multiply through: \(0.12=A(s^2+2s+4)+(Bs+C)s\).
  3. Put \(s=0\): \(0.12=4A\Rightarrow A=0.03\). The second term vanishes at \(s=0\) (the "cover-up" trick).
  4. Compare \(s^2\): \(0=A+B\Rightarrow B=-0.03\). Compare \(s^1\): \(0=2A+C\Rightarrow C=-0.06\).
  5. Split \(Bs+C=-0.03s-0.06=-0.03(s+1)-0.03\). To match the pairs, the numerator must be written in powers of \((s+1)\), the shifted variable.
  6. So \(X=\dfrac{0.03}{s}-0.03\dfrac{s+1}{(s+1)^2+3}-0.03\dfrac{1}{(s+1)^2+3}\) ✓.

Key idea: at \(t=0\) the displacement is zero; later the transient decays, but the mass stays 30 mm from its zero target. The uncontrolled system is stable, but its final position does not satisfy the control objective. (The curve is the one plotted in Section 1.)

You can now: multiply a transfer function by \(D(s)=0.12/s\); split the result into partial fractions by completing the square; invert with the pairs table; and read the final offset from the constant term.

8Poles and natural modes ~3 min

What is this?

Tap a wine glass and it rings at its own note, whatever you tapped it with. That "own way of moving" is a natural mode, and its pitch and fade rate are fixed by the glass. The poles are where that information lives: each pole is the \(s\) of one mode \(e^{st}\). The tap (here, the load) only decides how loud each mode is.

Definitions · pole and natural mode
  • Pole: a root of the denominator of a reduced transfer function (common factors of top and bottom cancelled first). At a pole the transfer function becomes infinite.
  • Natural mode: a component of the homogeneous response (the motion with no input) associated with a pole \(p\): \(e^{pt}\).

The uncontrolled disturbance map \(G_d(s)=X/D=1/(s^2+2s+4)\) has denominator roots

\[p_{1,2}=-1\pm j\sqrt3,\]

so the modes in the load response are

\[\begin{aligned}e^{p_{1,2}t}&=e^{-t}\left[\cos(\sqrt3\,t)\right.\\ &\qquad\left.\pm j\sin(\sqrt3\,t)\right].\end{aligned}\]

Poles of the uncontrolled map (dots). Distance to the left of the imaginary axis (1 s⁻¹) = decay rate; height (\(\sqrt3\) rad/s) = oscillation frequency.

  • The negative real part gives decay; the imaginary part gives the oscillation frequency of the transient after the load appears.
  • The load amplitude and the zero initial state fix the coefficients of these terms (the 0.03's of Section 7); the poles fix their shapes.

Key idea: poles govern the motion modes. Stable poles alone do not guarantee zero position error: these poles are stable, yet the offset is 30 mm.

You can now: find the poles of a transfer function; write the natural mode of each pole; and read decay rate and oscillation frequency from a pole's position.

9Natural frequency, damping ratio and the four damping cases ~6 min

What is this?

A car's suspension. With worn-out shock absorbers the car bounces several times after a bump (underdamped). With very stiff ones it creeps back slowly with no bounce (overdamped). The best setting returns quickly with at most a small bounce. Two numbers describe every such system: how fast it would bounce with no damping at all (\(\omega_n\)) and how much damping it has compared with "just enough to stop bouncing" (\(\zeta\)).

Definitions · natural frequency and damping ratio
  • Natural frequency \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\): stiffness relative to mass; the oscillation frequency with no damping.
  • Critical damping \(c_{\rm cr}=2\sqrt{km}\): the threshold between oscillatory and non-oscillatory natural modes.
  • Damping ratio \(\zeta=c/c_{\rm cr}=c/(2\sqrt{km})\): how much damping there is compared with critical.
  • Damped frequency (underdamped only) \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\): the frequency the system actually oscillates at.
Result · the standard second-order denominator \[ms^2+cs+k=m\left(s^2+2\zeta\omega_n s+\omega_n^2\right).\]

Baseline: \(\omega_n=\sqrt{4/1}=2\u{rad/s}\), \(\zeta=2/(2\sqrt{4})=0.5\), \(\omega_d=2\sqrt{0.75}=\sqrt3\u{rad/s}\).

Reading the formula
\(\omega_n^2=k/m\)
the constant term (after dividing by \(m\)).
\(2\zeta\omega_n=c/m\)
the \(s\) coefficient: damping per unit mass.
poles
\(-\zeta\omega_n\pm j\omega_d\) when \(\zeta\lt1\): decay rate \(\zeta\omega_n\), frequency \(\omega_d\).

In words: divide the denominator by \(m\); the constant term is \(\omega_n^2\), the middle coefficient is \(2\zeta\omega_n\). Two different frequencies: \(\omega_n\) (undamped) and \(\omega_d\) (what you see).

Check with Section 8: \(-\zeta\omega_n\pm j\omega_d=-1\pm j\sqrt3\) ✓. These describe the same load-response transient.

Four damping cases

\(\zeta=0\): undampedpoles \(\pm j\omega_n\) on the imaginary axis; sustained oscillation around the static equilibrium.
\(0\lt\zeta\lt1\): underdampedcomplex pair in the left half-plane; decaying oscillation, overshoot.
\(\zeta=1\): criticala repeated real pole \(-\omega_n\); fastest return with no overshoot.
\(\zeta\gt1\): overdampedtwo distinct real poles; slow creep, no overshoot.

Going further · second-order step-response formulas

For an underdamped system \(\omega_n^2/(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2)\) given a step, textbooks (and the related-course papers) use:

10Measuring success: peak, recovery band and actuator force ~3 min

What is this?

A goalkeeper is judged by how far the ball gets past them (peak), how soon they are back in position (recovery), and how much effort it costs (force). A specification writes those three judgements as numbers before any design starts, so that "good" can be checked, not argued.

Definitions · performance measures \[\begin{aligned}x_{\rm peak}&=\max_{t\ge0}|x(t)|,\\ |x(t)|&\le\varepsilon\ \ \text{for all}\ t\ge t_{\rm rec},\\ e_{\rm ss}&=-\lim_{t\to\infty}x(t),\\ F_{\rm peak}&=\max_{t\ge0}|F(t)|.\end{aligned}\]
  • Peak displacement: the largest deviation from the target.
  • Recovery time \(t_{\rm rec}\): the final entry into a band \(\pm\varepsilon\) around the target (after which the mass stays inside). Here \(\varepsilon=0.5\u{mm}\), an absolute band (not a percentage).
  • Steady-state error \(e_{\rm ss}\): with \(r=0\), \(e=-x\), so \(e_{\rm ss}=-x_\infty\).
  • Actuator effort: the largest force magnitude the actuator must deliver.

In words: how far it goes, when it comes back for good, where it ends up, and how hard the actuator pushes.

The design brief for this chapter
  • peak displacement below 5 mm;
  • recovery into ±0.5 mm within 2.5 s;
  • peak actuator force below 0.15 N (an illustrative limit).

The uncontrolled response never recovers to the band: it settles at 30 mm. A curve settling around a nonzero offset does not complete the task, however smooth it looks. Use the same absolute band and the same load test for every controller comparison.

11Zeros, DC gain, the final-value theorem and steady-state error ~6 min

What is this?

Poles say how a system moves; zeros say which inputs it blocks. A zero at \(s=0\) means "a constant input gets through as nothing", like a capacitor blocking direct current. And the DC gain is the answer to the simplest question of all: if I push steadily, how far does it end up? The final-value theorem lets you read that answer straight from the transform, without solving for \(x(t)\).

Definition · finite zero

A finite zero is a root of the numerator of a reduced transfer function, for a specified input and output. Write \(G_d(s)=X(s)/D(s)=N_d(s)/Q_d(s)\).

Controller off: \(G_d=\dfrac{1}{s^2+2s+4}\), \(N_d(s)=1\), a nonzero constant, so no finite zeros. Its zero-frequency load gain is \(G_d(0)=1/4\u{m/N}\): a constant load leaves a static offset. After adding integral feedback we will return to this same input–output map and find a zero at the origin (Section 15). Always keep the input \(d\) and output \(x\) fixed when comparing zeros before and after feedback.

Definitions · DC gain and steady-state error
  • DC gain: the transfer function at zero frequency, \(G(0)\). ("DC", direct current, means a constant signal.)
  • Steady-state error: the limiting difference between target and output, \(e_{\rm ss}=\lim_{t\to\infty}[r-x(t)]\).
Result · final-value theorem \[x_\infty=\lim_{t\to\infty}x(t)=\lim_{s\to0}sX(s),\]

provided every pole of \(sX(s)\) lies strictly in the left half-plane.

Reading the formula
\(sX(s)\)
multiply the output transform by \(s\) first (this cancels the \(1/s\) of a step).
\(s\to0\)
zero frequency: the long-time, steady behaviour.
condition
the theorem only works if \(x(t)\) actually settles. A pole on the imaginary axis (oscillation forever) or in the right half-plane (growth) gives a wrong answer.

In words: if the response settles, its final value is \(s\) × transform, evaluated at \(s=0\). For a step load of size \(d_0\): \(x_\infty=d_0\,G_d(0)\), the DC gain times the step.

Worked example · uncontrolled final offset \[\begin{aligned}x_\infty&=\lim_{s\to0}s\cdot G_d(s)\frac{0.12}{s}\\ &=0.12\,G_d(0)=0.12\times\tfrac14\\ &=0.03\u{m}.\end{aligned}\]

Condition: \(sX=0.12/(s^2+2s+4)\) has poles \(-1\pm j\sqrt3\), both in the left half-plane ✓. Error at the fixed target: \(r=0\), \(e(t)=-x(t)\), \(\boxed{e_{\rm ss}=-0.03\u{m}}\) — it matches the constant term in Section 7.

Watch · the condition matters

With \(c=0\) (Section 9) the formula still gives 0.03 m, but the mass oscillates between 0 and 60 mm for ever and never settles. \(sX=0.12/(s^2+4)\) has poles \(\pm2j\) on the axis, so the theorem does not apply.

Key idea: the desired final value is always \(x=0\); the uncontrolled final value is an error. With feedback, a zero final error requires zero DC gain from load to displacement, while the actuator must still supply −0.12 N.

Going further · system type and error constants

Textbooks call these \(K_p,K_v,K_a\); do not confuse \(K_p\) here with the proportional gain.

12The feedback loop: \(L=CG\), sensitivity and the disturbance map ~6 min

What is this?

Your hand on the trolley: you watch how far it has moved and push back accordingly. The push goes through the trolley's own mechanics, which changes the motion, which changes what you see, which changes your push — a loop. The algebra of the loop tells you, in one formula, how much of the original shove still reaches the trolley's position once you are helping.

Negative feedback uses the error \(e=r-x\) to oppose deviation from the target. Here \(r\) is fixed at zero, so \(e=-x\). The controller \(C(s)\) senses \(x\) and produces an opposing force; the disturbance pushes on the mass at a second summing point.

The disturbance-rejection loop. The task input is \(d\), the measured response is \(x\), the controller output is \(F\). The measured displacement returns to the negative input of the first summing junction.

Derivation · the disturbance map
  1. Do: fixed target and control force: \(r=0\), \(E=-X\), \(F=CE=-CX\). Read the diagram from the left: the error is target minus measurement, and the controller multiplies it by \(C\).
  2. Do: substitute into the plant: \(X=G(F+D)=G(-CX+D)\). The plant sees the sum of both forces (second Σ).
  3. Do: collect \(X\): \((1+CG)X=GD\). \(X\) appears on both sides because of the loop; move it to one side.
  4. Do: divide: \[G_d=\frac XD=\frac{G}{1+CG}=GS.\] This one map describes displacement caused by the same external load, with feedback active.
Definitions · loop transfer and sensitivity
  • Loop transfer \(L=CG\): what a signal is multiplied by on one trip round the loop.
  • Sensitivity \(S=\dfrac{1}{1+L}\): the factor by which feedback changes the plant's disturbance response, \(G_d=GS\).
  • Closed-loop poles: the roots of \(1+CG=0\) after clearing denominators. The physical plant \(G\) stays unchanged.

In words: with feedback the load's effect is the open-loop effect \(G\) divided by \(1+L\). Where \(|L|\) is large, \(|S|\) is small and the load is strongly rejected.

Key idea: use \(G_d\) to predict the load response; use \(L\) to examine feedback stability (Sections 22–24).

Going further · block-diagram reduction rules

Related-course papers ask you to reduce a diagram to one transfer function. Four rules cover almost everything:

  • Series (one block feeds the next): multiply, \(G_1G_2\).
  • Parallel (two blocks share an input, outputs added): add, \(G_1\pm G_2\).
  • Feedback loop (forward \(G\), return path \(H\), negative feedback): \(\dfrac{G}{1+GH}\). Positive feedback: \(\dfrac{G}{1-GH}\).
  • Moving a summing point past a block \(G\) (downstream): multiply the side input by \(G\); moving it upstream: divide by \(G\). Moving a pickoff point downstream: divide the branch by \(G\); upstream: multiply.

In words: forward path over (1 + loop) is the master rule; the others just rearrange the diagram until that rule applies. The disturbance map \(G/(1+CG)\) is this rule with forward path \(G\) (from \(d\)) and loop \(CG\).

Worked example

\(G_1=\dfrac1{s+1}\) and \(G_2=\dfrac2{s+3}\) in series, with unity negative feedback. Series: \(G=\dfrac{2}{(s+1)(s+3)}\). Loop: \(T=\dfrac{G}{1+G}=\dfrac{2}{(s+1)(s+3)+2}=\boxed{\dfrac{2}{s^2+4s+5}}\). DC gain \(2/5=0.4\).

You can now: draw the disturbance-rejection loop; derive \(G_d=G/(1+CG)\) step by step; define \(L\) and \(S\); and say what \(1+CG=0\) gives. (Going further) reduce series, parallel and feedback blocks.

What is this?

A P controller pushes back in proportion to how far the mass has moved: that is exactly what a spring does. So P control is an extra, invisible spring. A stiffer spring lets the load push the mass less far (smaller offset) and makes it bounce faster, but it adds no damping, so the bounce dies away no faster than before.

With \(C=K_p\): \(F=K_pe=-K_px\). Put this in \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F+d\):

In words: proportional feedback increases the effective stiffness, which shrinks the offset and speeds up the oscillation, but leaves the exponential decay rate alone.

For \(1+KL_0(s)=0\) with \(K\) from 0 to ∞, \(n\) open-loop poles and \(m\) open-loop zeros:

  1. Do: branches start at the open-loop poles (\(K=0\)) and end at the zeros or go to infinity (\(K\to\infty\)). \(1+KL_0=0\) means \(L_0=-1/K\): infinite at poles when \(K\to0\), zero at zeros when \(K\to\infty\).
  2. Do: real-axis segments: a point on the real axis is on the locus if the number of real poles plus zeros to its right is odd. Only then do the angles add up to 180°.
  3. Do: \(n-m\) asymptotes at angles \(\dfrac{(2q+1)180°}{n-m}\), meeting the real axis at the centroid \(\sigma_a=\dfrac{\sum p-\sum z}{n-m}\).
  4. Do: breakaway points: solve \(\mathrm dK/\mathrm ds=0\) with \(K=-1/L_0(s)\), keep roots that lie on a real-axis segment.
  5. Do: imaginary-axis crossing: the gain that makes a Routh row zero (Section 18), and \(\omega\) from the auxiliary equation.
  6. Do: departure angle from a complex pole: \(180°-\sum(\text{angles from other poles})+\sum(\text{angles from zeros})\).

What is this?

A D controller pushes back in proportion to how fast the mass is moving: that is what a shock absorber does. So D is an extra, invisible damper. 15PI control stores the steady force ~4 min

What is this?

Back to your hand on the trolley. If it has been off its mark for a while, you lean in a bit more, and a bit more, and you keep leaning. When it is finally back on its mark you are still leaning with exactly the force needed to cancel the shove. That memory is the integral: it stores the steady force that P could only provide by leaving an error.

Definition · integral action

Integral action accumulates error over time. PI control adds this stored contribution to the proportional force. The PI law (\(K_p=8\), \(K_i=12\)):

\[F=8e+I,\qquad \dot I=12e.\]

In words: the force is the proportional part plus a stored part \(I\) that grows at a rate proportional to the error.

  • Why the offset can vanish: at zero error the proportional force is zero, but the stored integral force can stay nonzero and balance the constant load.
  • An extra state: the integral of the error is a third state variable (\(x\), \(v\), and \(\int e\)). For a stable PI loop its steady value supplies −0.12 N and the displacement returns to zero.
Worked example · the PI disturbance map

The numerator is now \(s\): a zero at the origin, so \(G_d(0)=0\) and \(x_\infty=0.12\,G_d(0)=0\) (the loop is stable, see Section 18). Multiplying top and bottom by \(s\) to clear the \(1/s\) of the integrator puts the factor \(s\) on top. 17Asymptotic and BIBO stability ~3 min

What is this?

A marble in a bowl: nudge it and it rolls back to the bottom (stable). A marble on an upturned bowl: nudge it and it rolls away (unstable). A marble on a flat table just stays where you nudge it, or keeps rolling (on the boundary). Two questions are asked: does it come back after a nudge, and does a bounded push always give a bounded motion?

Definitions · two kinds of stability
  • Asymptotic stability: nearby initial states converge to the equilibrium.
  • BIBO stability (bounded-input, bounded-output): every bounded input gives a bounded zero-state output.

In words: asymptotic = "returns to rest after a nudge"; BIBO = "never runs away under any limited push". For the LTI models here both hold exactly when every closed-loop pole has a negative real part.

18The Routh–Hurwitz test and the marginal case ~7 min

What is this?

Solving a cubic exactly is a chore, and you only want a yes/no answer: are all the roots on the safe side? The Routh–Hurwitz test is a short arithmetic table built from the coefficients that answers exactly that, like checking a cheque's total without itemising every purchase.

Definition · Routh–Hurwitz test

A test that decides from the coefficients alone whether every root of a polynomial lies strictly in the left half-plane, without solving for the roots. Build the Routh array; the number of sign changes down its first column equals the number of roots in the right half-plane.

Going further · Routh special cases and relative stability
  • Zero in the first column (rest of the row not all zero): replace the 0 by a small \(\varepsilon\gt0\), continue, and count sign changes as \(\varepsilon\to0^+\).

  • Relative stability: to test whether all roots lie left of \(s=-a\), substitute \(s=z-a\) and apply Routh to the polynomial in \(z\).

19Pole placement: PD and PID by matching coefficients ~6 min

What is this?

Instead of twiddling knobs and watching, decide first where the poles should be (how fast each mode should die away), write down the polynomial with those roots, and choose the gains so that the actual polynomial is the same one. It is like ordering a suit to measurements rather than trying on suits until one fits.

Definition · pole placement

Pole placement chooses controller parameters so that the closed-loop characteristic polynomial has specified roots.

  1. Do: write the actual closed-loop polynomial with the gains as unknowns. Its roots are the closed-loop poles (Section 16).
  2. Do: choose the desired poles and multiply out the desired polynomial, with the same leading coefficient. Two polynomials with the same leading coefficient have the same roots only if all coefficients match.
  3. Do: match coefficients of equal powers of \(s\) and solve for the gains. One equation per unknown gain.
  4. Do: verify the result against the specification by the actual load test. Pole locations are a design choice; they do not guarantee the peak, recovery or force limits.
Worked example · two-pole design with PD

Given the mass with PD control; desired poles \(-3\) and \(-4\). Find \(K_p\), \(K_d\).

Match: \(2+K_d=7\Rightarrow K_d=5\u{N\,s/m}\); \(4+K_p=12\Rightarrow K_p=8\u{N/m}\).

\(\boxed{K_p=8\u{N/m},\ K_d=5\u{N\,s/m}}\). This changes the transient (two real poles: no overshoot), but PD still leaves a 10 mm static offset (\(0.12/12\)). It illustrates two-pole placement; integral action is needed to complete the zero-error task.

What is this?

Worked example · the designed PID under the 0.12 N step
  1. Do: the load-to-displacement map: \(\dfrac{X(s)}{D(s)}=\dfrac{s}{(s+2)(s+3)(s+4)}\). \(G_d=s/D_{\rm cl}\) (Section 16) with the placed poles.
  2. Do: apply the step: \(X(s)=\dfrac{0.12}{(s+2)(s+3)(s+4)}\). The \(s\) on top cancels the \(1/s\) of the step: the zero at the origin.
  3. Do: partial fractions (cover-up): \(X=\dfrac{0.06}{s+2}-\dfrac{0.12}{s+3}+\dfrac{0.06}{s+4}\). Each coefficient is \(0.12\) divided by the other two factors at that pole, e.g. at \(-2\): \(0.12/(1\cdot2)\).

What is this?

"Zero" only means something once you say which transfer function. The controller has its own zeros (they shape how it reacts), and the load-to-position map has different ones (they decide what load signals are blocked). Mixing them up is like confusing a person's height with the height of the house they live in.

Constant-load rejection: \(G_d(0)=0\Rightarrow x_\infty=0.12\,G_d(0)=0\).

22Frequency response and Bode plots ~6 min

What is this?

Push the mass back and forth sinusoidally. A slow push it simply follows; a very fast push it cannot follow at all (its inertia filters it out); near its natural frequency it may swing more than you push. A frequency response is that "how much gets through, and how late" answer, for every frequency at once. A Bode plot is its graph, drawn on log scales so that a huge range fits on one page.

Definition · frequency response

The frequency response of a transfer function is \(G(j\omega)\): the transfer function evaluated at \(s=j\omega\). For a sinusoidal input of angular frequency \(\omega\), a stable LTI system eventually responds with the same frequency, amplitude multiplied by \(|G(j\omega)|\) and shifted by the phase \(\angle G(j\omega)\).

\(|G_d(j\omega)|\), load-to-position gain in m/N, \(G_d=G/[1+C(s)G(s)]\), fixed target \(r=0\); active gains \(K_p=8\), \(K_i=12\), \(K_d=3\)

PPDPIPID

Log–log axes. P and PD keep a nonzero DC load gain (1/12 m/N, flat at low frequency); PI and PID fall towards zero as \(\omega\to0\) (slope +1 decade per decade, from the zero at the origin): they reject slowly varying loads. All curves meet at high frequency, \(|G_d|\approx1/(m\omega^2)\): the mass's inertia filters fast loads whatever the controller.

  • \(|G_d(j\omega)|\) measures displacement per unit external force at each frequency. Low-frequency behaviour reveals the response to slowly varying loads.
  • Frequency plots analyse this same loop; every time-response comparison still uses the same 0.12 N step.

Bode plots

Definition · Bode plot

A Bode plot shows magnitude in decibels, \(20\log_{10}|L(j\omega)|\), and phase in degrees, both against angular frequency on a logarithmic axis.

In words: 0 dB means a magnitude of 1 (unit amplitude ratio); +6.02 dB means ×2; −20 dB means ×0.1. Each factor of 10 in magnitude is 20 dB.

23The Nyquist plot and the Nyquist criterion ~8 min

What is this?

Walk a dog on a long lead around a lamp post. Whether the lead ends up wrapped round the post depends only on how many times the dog circled it. The Nyquist criterion does the same counting: trace the loop's frequency response as a curve in the complex plane, and count how many times it winds round the "lamp post" \(-1\). Each wrap tells you about an unstable closed-loop pole, without ever solving for the poles.

Definition · Nyquist plot

A Nyquist plot traces the complex values of \(L(j\omega)\) (real part across, imaginary part up) as the frequency runs through the negative and positive ranges, \(-\infty\lt\omega\lt\infty\). The negative-frequency half is the mirror image (in the real axis) of the positive half, because \(L(-j\omega)\) is the conjugate of \(L(j\omega)\).

Worked example · the P-controlled oscillator

\(K_p=8\): \(L=\dfrac{8}{s^2+2s+4}\). At \(\omega=2\u{rad/s}\), \(L(j2)=-2j\) (Section 22): magnitude 2, phase −90°. Two more points students can calculate: \(\omega=0\): \(L=2\) (on the positive real axis); \(\omega\to\infty\): \(L\approx-8/\omega^2\to0\), arriving from the left (phase → −180°).

The Nyquist stability criterion

Result · Nyquist criterion \[Z=P_r+N\]
Reading the formula
\(P_r\)
number of open-loop poles (poles of \(L\)) inside the right half-plane.
\(N\)
net number of clockwise encirclements of \(-1\) by the complete mapped contour (anticlockwise counts negative).
\(Z\)
number of unstable closed-loop poles (roots of \(1+L=0\) in the RHP). Stable needs \(Z=0\).

In words: unstable closed-loop poles = unstable open-loop poles + clockwise wraps of the curve round −1. Check separately for poles on the imaginary axis (the curve passing through −1).

What the detour maps to, for \(L\) with \(1/s\)

Left: the contour runs up the imaginary axis and round a huge semicircle, enclosing the whole right half-plane clockwise. Show the calculation: where the detour arc goes
  1. On the detour, \(s=\varepsilon e^{j\theta}\) with \(\theta\) from −90° to +90° and \(\varepsilon\) tiny. A small half-circle round the origin, on the right side.
  2. Near \(s=0\), \(L=\dfrac{K_ds^2+K_ps+K_i}{s(s^2+2s+4)}\approx\dfrac{K_i}{4s}=\dfrac{K_i}{4\varepsilon}e^{-j\theta}\). Every other factor tends to its value at \(s=0\).
  3. So \(|L|=K_i/(4\varepsilon)\to\infty\) and \(\angle L=-\theta\) runs from +90° to −90°: a huge half-circle swept clockwise through the positive real axis. The angle decreases.
  4. It passes on the far right, so it never crosses the negative real axis left of −1; it only joins the two halves of the plot.
Counting rule (practical)

Draw a ray from \(-1\) to the left along the real axis. Each time the complete curve crosses it going upward (from below to above) count +1 (clockwise); going downward count −1. The total is \(N\). A curve that never crosses the real axis left of −1 has \(N=0\).

  2. Do: phase \(=-90°-\arctan\omega\), between −90° and −180°: the curve stays in the third quadrant and reaches the origin from the direction −180° as \(\omega\to\infty\). It never crosses the negative real axis, so it can never wrap round −1.
  3. Do: the detour round the origin pole maps to a huge clockwise half-circle on the right, joining the two mirror halves.
  4. Do: \(P_r=0\), \(N=0\Rightarrow Z=0\): stable for every \(K\gt0\). Check: \(s^2+s+K\) has positive coefficients ✓.

\(K=2\): solid \(\omega\gt0\), dashed \(\omega\lt0\); dotted line: the asymptote \({\rm Re}=-2\). The infinite detour arc on the right is not drawn.

You can now: sketch a Nyquist plot from a few computed points; state \(Z=P_r+N\) and its terms; handle the detour round an integrator; count encirclements with a ray; and confirm the \(K_i=60\) boundary by Nyquist. (Going further) sketch \(K/[s(s+1)]\).

24Gain and phase margins ~5 min

What is this?

A lorry passing under a bridge: it fits, but by how much? The margins answer "how far is this loop from instability?". The gain margin says how many times stronger the loop could be made before it hits −1; the phase margin says how much extra delay (phase lag) it could take before it hits −1.

Definitions · margins
  • Gain crossover \(\omega_{\rm gc}\): where \(|L|=1\) (0 dB). Phase margin: the extra phase lag needed there to reach the critical point, \[{\rm PM}=180°+\angle L(j\omega_{\rm gc}).\]
  • Phase crossover \(\omega_{\rm pc}\): where \(\angle L=-180°\) (on the relevant continuous branch). Gain margin: the gain multiplier that would bring \(|L|\) to 1 there, \[{\rm GM}=\frac{1}{|L(j\omega_{\rm pc})|}.\]

In words: PM = how many more degrees of lag at the 0 dB frequency; GM = how many times more gain at the −180° frequency (in dB: \(-20\log_{10}|L(j\omega_{\rm pc})|\)).

Worked example · baseline PID

\(L=\dfrac{3s^2+8s+12}{s(s^2+2s+4)}\). At \(\omega=\sqrt{12}=3.464\u{rad/s}\):

\[\begin{aligned}|N|&=|-24+j27.71|=36.66,\\ |D|&=3.464\times|-8+j6.93|=36.66,\end{aligned}\]

so \(|L|=1\): \(\omega_{\rm gc}=3.464\u{rad/s}\). Phase: \(\angle N-\angle D=130.9°-229.1°=-98.2°\). \(\angle N=180°-\arctan(27.71/24)\); \(\angle D=90°+[180°-\arctan(6.93/8)]\).

\(\boxed{{\rm PM}=180°-98.2°=81.8°}\). The phase of this ideal loop stays between about −98° and −82° for all \(\omega\gt0\): there is no finite −180° crossing, so \(\boxed{{\rm GM}=\infty}\).

Bode plot of the baseline PID loop (\(K_p=8\), \(K_i=12\), \(K_d=3\)). 0 dB at 3.464 rad/s, where the phase is −98.2°: PM = 81.8° (orange arrow). The phase never reaches the −180° line: no finite phase crossover, infinite gain margin in this ideal model.

Watch

An infinite gain margin does not imply unlimited tolerance to delay or model changes: a time delay adds phase lag that grows with frequency, and an unmodelled resonance can change the curve.

Going further · reading GM and PM off a given Bode plot
  1. Do: on the magnitude plot find where the curve crosses 0 dB: that is \(\omega_{\rm gc}\). Drop down to the phase plot and read \(\angle L\); PM = 180° + that phase. PM is measured at unit magnitude.
  2. Do: on the phase plot find where the curve crosses −180°: \(\omega_{\rm pc}\). Go up to the magnitude plot and read \(|L|\) in dB; GM (dB) = −(that value). GM is measured at −180° phase.
  3. Do: both positive (PM \(\gt0\), GM \(\gt0\) dB) ⇒ stable, for an open-loop-stable \(L\) with a single crossover each.
Worked example

\(L=\dfrac{2}{s(s+1)(s+2)}\). Phase \(-90°-\arctan\omega-\arctan(\omega/2)=-180°\) at \(\omega_{\rm pc}=\sqrt2\) (then \(\arctan\omega+\arctan(\omega/2)=90°\), i.e. \(\omega\cdot\omega/2=1\)). \(|L(j\sqrt2)|=2/(\sqrt2\sqrt3\sqrt6)=1/3\) ⇒ GM = 3 = 9.54 dB. \(|L|=1\) at \(\omega_{\rm gc}=0.749\u{rad/s}\), phase \(-147.4°\) ⇒ PM = 32.6°. Routh check: \(s^3+3s^2+2s+2g\) is stable for \(g\lt3\) ✓ (gain may be tripled).

Dotted lines: \(\omega_{\rm gc}=0.749\) and \(\omega_{\rm pc}=1.414\) rad/s. GM is the gap below 0 dB at \(\omega_{\rm pc}\); PM is the gap above −180° at \(\omega_{\rm gc}\).

You can now: define gain and phase crossover; compute PM and GM; show the baseline PID has PM ≈ 81.8° and infinite GM; and explain what margins do not guarantee. (Going further) read both margins off a Bode plot.

What is this?

Load the trolley with shopping so it weighs twice as much. The same hand-pushing rule would now give a different, more sluggish motion. The design method does not change, though: write the new polynomial, match it to the same chosen poles, and check the same three numbers.

Worked example · redesign for \(m=2\u{kg}\)

What changes: \(m: 1\u{kg}\to2\u{kg}\). What stays: spring, damper, the 0.12 N load step, the zero target, and the desired poles \(-2,-3,-4\u{s^{-1}}\). Find new continuous-time PID gains and check the 5 mm peak, ±0.5 mm recovery within 2.5 s and 0.15 N force requirements.

  1. Do: actual polynomial: \(2s^3+(2+K_d)s^2+(4+K_p)s+K_i\). With \(m=2\) the leading coefficient doubles.
  2. Do: desired polynomial with the same leading coefficient: \(2D_*=2s^3+18s^2+52s+48\). Matching needs equal leading coefficients, so double \(D_*\), not just copy it.

27Common mistakes

✗Using the final-value theorem without checking that \(sX(s)\) is stable. ✓Check the poles of \(sX\) first: with \(c=0\) or \(K_i\ge60\) the "final value" does not exist. ✗Expecting P or D to remove the offset under a constant load. ✓P only adds stiffness (offset \(0.12/(4+K_p)\)); D is zero at rest. Only I stores the −0.12 N. ✗Dropping \(m\) in the characteristic polynomial (or in pole placement with \(m\ne1\)). ✓Match \(ms^3+\dots\) to \(m\,D_*\), with equal leading coefficients. ✗Writing the Routh condition as \(a_2a_1\gt a_0\) without dividing by \(m\), or forgetting "all coefficients positive". ✓\((c+K_d)(k+K_p)\gt mK_i\) and \(c+K_d\gt0\), \(k+K_p\gt0\), \(K_i\gt0\). ✗Calling the controller's zeros "the zeros of the system". ✓Always name the transfer function: zeros of \(C\) differ from zeros of \(G_d\). ✗Defining recovery as the first time \(|x|\) drops below 0.5 mm. ✓It is the final entry: after it, \(x\) stays inside the band. ✗In a Nyquist count, ignoring the detour round the integrator pole, or counting anticlockwise turns as positive. ✓Include the mapped detour arc; \(N\) counts net clockwise encirclements. ✗Reading GM at the 0 dB frequency, PM at the −180° frequency. ✓PM at \(|L|=1\); GM at \(\angle L=-180°\). ✗Partial fractions with the quadratic left as \(s^2+2s+4\). ✓Complete the square, \((s+1)^2+3\), and write the numerator in powers of \(s+1\) to match the damped pairs.

1 · A student's stability limit

"Mass with PID, \(K_p=8\), \(K_d=3\). The closed loop is \(s^3+(c+K_d)s^2+(k+K_p)s+K_i\). By Routh, stable if \(ck\gt K_i\), so \(K_i\lt8\)."

Hint

Which coefficients appear in the \(s^1\) entry of the Routh array?

Answer

The condition uses the closed-loop coefficients, \((c+K_d)(k+K_p)\gt mK_i\): \(5\times12=60\), so \(0\lt K_i\lt60\). The student used the plant's \(c\) and \(k\) alone (\(2\times4=8\)).

2 · A student's pole placement for \(m=2\)

"Desired \((s+2)(s+3)(s+4)=s^3+9s^2+26s+24\). Match to \(2s^3+(2+K_d)s^2+(4+K_p)s+K_i\): \(K_d=7\), \(K_p=22\), \(K_i=24\) — the same gains as before."

Do the two polynomials have the same leading coefficient?

Answer

Coefficients can only be matched when the leading coefficients agree. Match to \(2D_*=2s^3+18s^2+52s+48\): \(K_d=16\), \(K_p=48\), \(K_i=48\). With the old gains the poles move to \(-1.380\) and \(-1.560\pm j2.502\), not the chosen \(-2,-3,-4\).

"With \(c=0\) and \(F=0\): \(x_\infty=\lim_{s\to0}s\cdot\dfrac{0.12}{s(s^2+4)}=0.03\u{m}\), so the mass settles at 30 mm."

\(sX=0.12/(s^2+4)\) has poles \(\pm2j\) on the imaginary axis, so the final-value theorem does not apply. The mass oscillates between 0 and 60 mm for ever (Section 9); 30 mm is only the centre of the swing.

30Concept checks

1 · Bigger P

With P control only, what \(K_p\) brings the steady offset down to 5 mm? Does the decay rate change?

Answer

\(0.12/(4+K_p)=0.005\Rightarrow K_p=20\u{N/m}\). No: the poles are \(-1\pm j\sqrt{3+K_p}\), real part −1 whatever \(K_p\) (Section 13).

2 · Critical damping by D

With \(K_p=8\), what \(K_d\) makes the PD loop critically damped?

Answer

\(s^2+(2+K_d)s+12\): \(\zeta=1\) needs \(2+K_d=2\sqrt{12}=6.93\), so \(K_d=4.93\u{N\,s/m}\). (The PD design of Section 19, \(K_d=5\), is just past critical: two real poles −3 and −4.)

3 · Which zero?

Why does the PID loop end with \(x_\infty=0\) under a constant load, while the PD loop does not? Answer in terms of \(G_d\).

Answer

With I, \(G_d=s/D_{\rm cl}\) has a zero at the origin, so \(G_d(0)=0\) and \(x_\infty=0.12\,G_d(0)=0\) (stable loop). PD has \(G_d=1/(s^2+(2+K_d)s+12)\), \(G_d(0)=1/12\u{m/N}\), so \(x_\infty=10\u{mm}\).

4 · Halfway to the boundary

With \(K_p=8\), \(K_d=3\), is \(K_i=30\) stable? What happens if \(K_d\) is raised to 5 with \(K_i=90\)?

Answer

\(K_i=30\lt60\): stable. With \(K_d=5\): bound \((2+5)(4+8)=84\lt90\): still unstable. (Raising \(K_d\) to 6 gives bound 96, and 90 would then be stable.)

5 · Decibels

\(|G_d(j\omega)|\) of a design is 0.1 m/N at some frequency. Express this in dB and say what it means for a 0.2 N sinusoidal load at that frequency.

Answer

\(20\log_{10}0.1=-20\u{dB}\). The steady oscillation has amplitude \(0.1\times0.2=0.02\u{m}=20\u{mm}\).

6 · Nyquist count

An open-loop-stable loop's Nyquist plot encircles −1 once anticlockwise. How many unstable closed-loop poles? Is that possible?

Answer

\(Z=P_r+N=0+(-1)=-1\), impossible (\(Z\) counts poles). So the count is wrong: either a branch or the detour arc was missed, or \(P_r\ne0\). With \(P_r=0\), only \(N\ge0\) can occur.

Answer
