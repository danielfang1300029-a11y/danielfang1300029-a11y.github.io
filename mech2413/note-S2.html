Force and Moment Resultants in Beams

The whole of Statics chapter 2 explained from scratch: the normal force, shear force and bending moment inside a beam, how to draw their diagrams for point forces, couples and distributed loads, Macaulay brackets, and the integral method with boundary and matching conditions. Every worked example is solved in full with a free-body diagram and plotted diagrams. This chapter carries the most Section A marks after S7, so the exam taster at the end matters.

0Where this chapter sits

In S1 you found the forces the supports put on a beam. This chapter looks inside it. Cut the beam anywhere and ask how hard the two pieces must push and twist each other, the way a door's hinge must resist your push, and resist it harder the farther from the hinge you push. Those internal forces decide whether the beam breaks. Beams are among the most important elements in structural engineering, and these internal forces feed straight into stress (S3), bending stress (S4) and deflection (S5). The chapter has three parts: concentrated loads, then distributed loads, then dual loading.

Definition · beam

A beam is a long, slender member loaded across its length, so that it bends (S1 Section 9). We draw it as a line along the \(x\)-axis, from the left end \(x=0\) to the right end \(x=l\).

Sign convention for this chapter
  • \(x\) runs along the beam from the left end, pointing right. In the integral method the \(z\)-axis points down (so a downward load \(q\) is positive).
  • Normal force \(N\): positive in tension (pulling away from the cut face).
  • Shear force \(V\): positive if it tends to rotate a small slice of the beam clockwise. On the cut face of the left piece, positive \(V\) points down; on the cut face of the right piece it points up.
  • Bending moment \(M\): positive if it bends the beam into a sagging shape (a smile, concave up). On the cut face of the left piece, positive \(M\) is anticlockwise; on the right piece, clockwise.
  • Distributed load \(q(x)\) (N/m or kN/m): positive pointing down.
  • Moment equations for reactions are written with the sense shown each time (↺ anticlockwise + or ↻ clockwise +). Unknown reactions are drawn in an assumed direction; a negative answer means the other way.

1Stress resultants: \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) ~5 min

What is this?

Push a door near its handle and it swings easily. Push with the same force close to the hinge and it hardly moves. The turning effect is force times distance from the hinge. Now cut a beam anywhere and treat the cut as a hinge for the piece on the left. Every load on that piece tries to slide it across the cut and swing it round the cut. The other piece must hold it still with three things: a pull along the beam (\(N\)), a push across it (\(V\)) and a twist (\(M\)).

Take a beam carrying everything at once: inclined forces \(F_1, F_2, \dots, F_n\), a couple \(M_1\) and a distributed load \(q\), on a pin and a roller. Imagine cutting it (the green dashed "cut"). On the cut face of the left piece the right piece pushes and pulls with a spread of small forces \(p\). By S1 Section 6, any force system can be replaced by one resultant force \(R\) acting at a chosen point \(C\) plus one couple \(M^{(C)}\). Take \(C\) at the centre of the cut face, and split \(R\) into a part along the beam and a part across it:

Definitions · the three stress resultants
  • Normal force \(N\): the component of \(R\) along the beam axis. It stretches or squashes the beam.
  • Shear force \(V\): the component of \(R\) across the beam. It tries to slide one piece past the other, like scissors.
  • Bending moment \(M\): the couple \(M^{(C)}\). It tries to bend the beam.

Together they are called stress resultants (or internal forces), because each is the total of the stresses on the cut face (S3, S4).

Analogy: hold a ruler at both ends. Pull your hands apart: that is \(N\). Push one hand up and the other down: that is \(V\). Twist both wrists to curl the ruler into a smile: that is \(M\).

Which way is positive?

What is this? (sign convention)

Take a plank resting on two supports. Look at the left piece of a cut, with the cut as its hinge. The left support pushes it up, so the right piece must push it down at the cut to stop it sliding: that is positive \(V\). The same upward push, an arm's length from the cut, swings the left piece clockwise about the cut, so the right piece must hold it back with an anticlockwise twist: that is positive \(M\), and it is what makes the plank sag into a smile.

The internal forces on the two faces of a cut are an action–reaction pair (S1 Section 3), so they point opposite ways on the two faces. A sign convention therefore cannot say "right is positive"; it says what the pair does to the material:

(a) Positive \(N\) / (b) negative \(N\)Positive pulls both faces away from the cut: tension. Negative pushes them together: compression.
(c) Positive \(V\) / (d) negative \(V\)Positive: left face of the right piece pushed up, right face of the left piece pushed down. The pair turns a small slice clockwise.
(e) Positive \(M\) / (f) negative \(M\)Positive bends the beam into a smile (sagging): top squashed, bottom stretched. Negative gives a frown (hogging).
Going further · two quick rules that follow from the convention

For the left piece of a cut at \(x\), equilibrium gives

\[\begin{aligned}V(x)&=\text{(sum of upward forces}\\&\qquad\text{on the left piece)},\\ M(x)&=\text{(sum of clockwise moments}\\&\qquad\text{about the cut, left piece)}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: stand at the cut and look left. Every upward force adds to \(V\); every force or couple that would turn the left piece clockwise about you adds to \(M\). (Looking right instead: downward forces add to \(V\), anticlockwise moments add to \(M\).) This is how the equations in this chapter come out; use it to check your signs.

Diagrams

Cut at every \(x\) and you get \(N(x)\), \(V(x)\), \(M(x)\) as functions of position. Plotted against \(x\) they are the normal-force diagram, the shear-force diagram (SFD) and the bending-moment diagram (BMD). Positive values are drawn above the axis and marked ⊕, negative below and marked ⊖. The largest \(|M|\) usually decides where a beam fails, which is why the BMD is the most important of the three.

You can now: name the three stress resultants on a cut and say what each does to the beam; draw them in their positive directions on either face; and find \(V\) and \(M\) on the left piece as the upward forces and the clockwise moments about the cut.

2One inclined force on a simply supported beam ~5 min

What is this?

Think of the beam as a door with its hinge at \(A\), held shut by a hand at \(B\). The load pushes at \(D\), a distance \(a\) from the hinge. The hand at \(B\) pushes back from the far end, with the longer arm \(l\), so it needs only the fraction \(a/l\) of the load. Swap roles (hinge at \(B\)) and you get \(A\)'s share. Then we cut the beam either side of the load and see how the push and twist inside change as the cut passes it.

First example: a beam \(AB\) of length \(l\), pin at \(A\), roller at \(B\), loaded by a single force \(F\) at point \(D\), a distance \(a\) from \(A\) and \(b\) from \(B\) (\(a+b=l\)). \(F\) is inclined at angle \(\alpha\) to the beam, pointing down and to the left. Split it: \(F_V=F\sin\alpha\) downward and \(F_H=F\cos\alpha\) to the left.

System

FBD of the whole beam

Step 1 · Whole-beam equilibrium

\[\begin{aligned}\rightarrow:&\quad A_H-F_H=0\ \Rightarrow\ A_H=F_H,\\ \text{↺}A:&\quad lB-aF_V=0\ \Rightarrow\ B=\tfrac{a}{l}F_V,\\ \text{↺}B:&\quad -lA_V+bF_V=0\ \Rightarrow\ A_V=\tfrac{b}{l}F_V.\end{aligned}\]

Taking moments about \(A\) treats \(A\) as the door's hinge: \(A\)'s own unknown forces pass through it and drop out, leaving \(B\) as the only unknown.

In words: each support carries a share of the vertical load in proportion to the distance to the other support: the closer the load to \(A\), the more \(A\) carries. The pin alone resists the horizontal push. Check: \(A_V+B=\frac{a+b}{l}F_V=F_V\) ✓.

Step 2 · Cut between \(A\) and \(D\), \(0\lt x\lt a\)

Keep the left piece. On its cut face draw \(N\), \(V\), \(M\) in their positive directions (Section 1): \(N\) to the right, \(V\) down, \(M\) anticlockwise.You do not need to guess the real directions: the equations return a negative value whenever the true one is opposite, as \(N\) does here.

\[\begin{aligned}\rightarrow:&\quad A_H+N=0\\ &\Rightarrow N=-A_H=-F_H,\\ \uparrow:&\quad A_V-V=0\\ &\Rightarrow V=A_V=\tfrac{b}{l}F_V,\\ \text{↺}C:&\quad -xA_V+M=0\\ &\Rightarrow M=xA_V=x\tfrac{b}{l}F_V.\end{aligned}\]

Step 3 · Cut between \(D\) and \(B\), \(a\lt x\lt l\)

The left piece now also carries \(F\) (components \(F_H\) left, \(F_V\) down at distance \(x-a\) from the cut):With the cut as the hinge, \(F_V\) pushes on the piece at arm \(x-a\), so it swings the piece the opposite way to \(A_V\) and starts to cancel its twist.

\[\begin{aligned}\rightarrow:&\quad A_H-F_H+N=0\ \Rightarrow\ N=0,\\ \uparrow:&\quad A_V-F_V-V=0\\ &\Rightarrow V=\tfrac{b-l}{l}F_V=-\tfrac{a}{l}F_V=-B,\\ \text{↺}C:&\quad -xA_V+(x-a)F_V+M=0\\ &\Rightarrow M=xA_V-(x-a)F_V\\ &\qquad\ =\left(1-\tfrac{x}{l}\right)aF_V.\end{aligned}\]

Step 4 · The diagrams

Diagrams for the inclined force (drawn for \(a=0.4l\), \(F_V=F_H=1\))

Top: \(N\) (⊖ from \(A\) to \(D\), zero after). Middle: \(V\) (⊕ \(A_V\), then ⊖ \(-B\)). Bottom: \(M\), a triangle peaking at \(D\).

  • Shear. \(A_V\) and \(B\) both come from \(F\). From \(A\) to \(D\) nothing new acts, so \(V\) stays at \(A_V\). At \(D\), \(F\sin\alpha\) is bigger than \(A_V\) and overpowers it, so \(V\) jumps down by \(F_V\) and turns negative, \(-B\). At \(B\) the reaction cancels the remaining shear and \(V\) returns to zero.
  • Normal force. \(A_H\) also comes from \(F\). From \(A\) to \(D\) the beam is squashed between the pin pushing right and \(F_H\) pushing left: \(N=-F_H\) (compression), constant. At \(D\), \(F\cos\alpha=A_H\) cancels it, and \(N=0\) from \(D\) to \(B\). (The roller cannot take horizontal force, so that part of the beam carries none.)
  • Moment. At \(A\) (a pin) there is no bending moment. From \(A\) to \(D\), \(M\) grows linearly. At \(D\) the force starts a counter-moment, and from \(D\) to \(B\) \(M\) falls linearly back to zero at the roller. The peak is
\[M_{\max}=M(a)=\frac{ab}{l}F_V.\]
Reading the formula
\(\tfrac{b}{l}F_V\)
the left support's share of the load, \(A_V\).
\(a\)
its arm: how far that upward push sits from a cut just at the load.
\(\tfrac{ab}{l}F_V\)
push × arm, the twist the cut must resist. It is largest when \(a\) and \(b\) are equal, so a load at mid-span bends the beam most.

In words: the biggest bending moment under a single point load on a simply supported beam is "load × the two distances ÷ span", right under the load. For a load at mid-span it is \(F_Vl/4\).

You can now: find the reactions of a pin–roller beam under one inclined force; cut once on each side of the load and write \(N\), \(V\), \(M\) for each region; draw the three diagrams; and quote \(M_{\max}=\frac{ab}{l}F_V\).

3General procedure and Example 1: several vertical forces ~3 min

What is this?

Before you can say what a door's hinge feels, you need every push on the door, including the hand holding it shut. A beam is the same: first find the support forces, then move a cut along the beam like a sliding hinge. Between two loads nothing new acts on the left piece, so one cut tells you the whole stretch. Each time the cut passes a load, the push and twist inside change.

Recipe · statically determinate beam
  1. Do: Equilibrium of the whole beam: (a) take moments about a support to get the other support's reaction; (b) then use the force sums (↑ and →) for the rest. The reactions are loads on the beam too, so no cut can be balanced until they are known. Moments about one support treat it as the door's hinge, so its own unknowns drop out.
  2. Do: Method of sections: cut once in every region between load points; draw positive \(N, V, M\) on the cut; write equilibrium of one piece. Inside one region the left piece carries the same loads wherever you cut, so one formula in \(x\) covers it; drawing the unknowns positive lets the sign of the answer give their real direction.
  3. Do: Draw the force and moment diagrams. The diagrams show at a glance where the beam is pushed and twisted hardest, which is where it would fail.

Take a pin–roller beam with three forces at spacing \(a\) (span \(4a\)). The case worked through: \(F\) down at \(x=a\), \(2F\) down at \(2a\), and \(F\) up at \(3a\).

Example 1

FBD

Reactions

\[\begin{aligned}\text{↺}A:&\ -aF-2a(2F)+3aF\\ &\quad+4aB=0\ \Rightarrow B=\tfrac12F,\\ \text{↺}B:&\ -4aA+3aF+2a(2F)\\ &\quad-aF=0\ \Rightarrow A=\tfrac32F,\\ \uparrow:&\ A-F-2F+F+B=0\\ &\Rightarrow A+B=2F\ ✓\end{aligned}\]

There are no horizontal loads, so \(A_H=0\) and \(N=0\) everywhere.

Shear force: one cut per region

Draw the free-body diagram of the left piece for a cut in each region. Each time, \(V\) = upward forces on the left piece:

\[\begin{aligned}0\lt x\lt a:&\ V=A=\tfrac32F,\\ a\lt x\lt 2a:&\ V=A-F=\tfrac12F,\\ 2a\lt x\lt 3a:&\ V=A-3F=-\tfrac32F,\\ 3a\lt x\lt 4a:&\ V=A-2F=-\tfrac12F.\end{aligned}\]

Bending moment

Moments about the cut \(C\) for the left piece (each force times its distance \(x-a_i\) to the cut):The cut is the hinge: a load far to the left of it has a long arm and swings the piece hard, like a push near a door's handle.

\(0\le x\le a\):

\[M=xA=\tfrac32xF,\]

\(a\le x\le 2a\):

\[M=xA-(x-a)F=\left(a+\tfrac12x\right)F,\]

\(2a\le x\le 3a\):

\[\begin{aligned}M&=xA-(x-a)F-(x-2a)2F\\&=\left(5a-\tfrac32x\right)F,\end{aligned}\]

\(3a\le x\le 4a\):

\[\begin{aligned}M&=xA-(x-a)F-(x-2a)2F\\&\quad+(x-3a)F=\left(2a-\tfrac12x\right)F.\end{aligned}\]

Example 1 · shear-force and bending-moment diagrams, in units of \(F\) and \(aF\)

\(V\): \(\tfrac32F\), \(\tfrac12F\), \(-\tfrac32F\), \(-\tfrac12F\). \(M\): 0, \(\tfrac32aF\), \(2aF\) (maximum, where \(V\) changes sign), \(\tfrac12aF\), 0.

Watch

The maximum bending moment sits where the shear force changes sign (here at \(x=2a\)). That is no accident: Section 4 shows \(V\) is the slope of \(M\).

You can now: follow the three-step recipe for a statically determinate beam; find the reactions by taking moments about each support; write \(V\) and \(M\) region by region from the left piece; and spot the maximum moment where \(V\) changes sign.

4Concentrated forces and couples in general ~4 min

What is this?

Turn a wheel nut with a two-handed spanner (a wheel brace): one hand pushes up, the other pulls down, equally hard. The two pushes cancel, so the nut is not shoved anywhere; it is only twisted. That pure twist is a couple. Its turning effect is the same about every point, so it has no "distance". Bolted onto a beam, a couple adds nothing to the push across a cut (\(V\)), but as the cut passes it, the twist inside (\(M\)) changes all at once.

Now let a pin–roller beam of span \(l\) carry \(n\) vertical forces \(F_i\) at distances \(a_i\) from \(A\) and \(k\) couples \(M_j\) at distances \(b_j\). The couples are separate loads: they are not caused by the forces. (In this general case each \(M_j\) is drawn anticlockwise.)

Whole beam

\[\begin{aligned}\text{↺}A:&\ lB-\textstyle\sum a_iF_i+\sum M_j=0\\ &\Rightarrow B=\tfrac1l\left[\textstyle\sum a_iF_i-\sum M_j\right],\\ \text{↺}B:&\ -lA+\textstyle\sum(l-a_i)F_i+\sum M_j=0\\ &\Rightarrow A=\tfrac1l\left[\textstyle\sum(l-a_i)F_i+\sum M_j\right].\end{aligned}\]

In words: a couple appears in the moment equation with its full value, wherever it sits on the beam. It does not have a "distance".

Left piece of a cut at \(x\)

\[\begin{aligned}\uparrow:&\ A-\textstyle\sum F_i-V=0,\\ \text{↺}C:&\ -xA+\textstyle\sum(x-a_i)F_i+\sum M_j+M=0,\end{aligned}\]

where the sums include only the loads to the left of the cut. Hence

Result · shear and moment from the left \[\begin{aligned}V&=A-\textstyle\sum F_i,\\ M&=xA-\textstyle\sum(x-a_i)F_i-\sum M_j.\end{aligned}\]

In words: shear = left reaction minus every load you have passed; moment = left reaction × distance, minus each passed load × its distance to the cut, minus each passed anticlockwise couple.

Reading the formula
\(A-\sum F_i\)
the net upward push on the left piece: what the cut must cancel by pushing down.
\(xA\)
the left reaction's swing about the cut: push × arm \(x\), clockwise.
\(-\sum(x-a_i)F_i\)
each downward load already passed swings the other way, with its own arm \(x-a_i\).
\(-\sum M_j\)
each anticlockwise couple passed counts in full, with no arm: a spanner twist is the same about every point.

What the diagrams look like

  • SFD: a staircase. It starts at \(A\), steps down by \(F_i\) at each downward force, and ends at \(-B\). Couples do not change \(V\) at all.
  • BMD: straight lines between load points. At each couple \(M_j\) it jumps (down by \(M_j\) for an anticlockwise couple, up for a clockwise one). Differentiating the formula for \(M\):
\[\frac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dx}=A-\textstyle\sum F_i=V.\]

In words: the slope of the bending-moment diagram equals the shear force. Where \(V\) is big, \(M\) changes fast; where \(V=0\), \(M\) is flat, which is where its maximum or minimum is.Slide the cut one small step right: every push on the left piece gets an arm one step longer, so the twist grows by (net upward push) × (step). That net push is \(V\).

Try it · a point load and a couple on a 10 m simply supported beam

\(F=10\u{kN}\) downward. Watch: the couple never changes the SFD's shape (only shifts it via the reactions), but makes the BMD jump; the two BMD lines either side of a couple stay parallel.

You can now: write the reactions of a pin–roller beam with forces and couples; give \(V\) and \(M\) at any cut from the loads to its left; predict that forces step the SFD and couples make the BMD jump; and use \(\mathrm dM/\mathrm dx=V\).

5Example 2: an applied couple, inclined roller ~3 min

What is this?

Clamp a spanner onto the middle of a plank and twist it. Nothing pushes the plank up or down overall, but it wants to spin. The two supports stop the spin by twisting back: one pushes up and the other pulls down, equally hard, so together they form an opposite couple. Because a twist is the same wherever you apply it, these support forces do not depend on where the spanner sits.

A beam \(AB\) of span \(l\) has a pin at \(A\) and, at \(B\), a roller on a surface inclined at \(45^\circ\). A clockwise couple \(M_0\) acts at \(x=a\) (\(b=l-a\)). The inclined roller can only push perpendicular to its surface, so its reaction \(B\) acts at \(45^\circ\): \(B_H=B_V\).

System

FBD

Whole beam

\[\begin{aligned}\text{↻}A:&\ M_0-lB_V=0\ \Rightarrow\ B_V=B_H=\tfrac{M_0}{l},\\ \text{↻}B:&\ lA_V+M_0=0\ \Rightarrow\ A_V=-\tfrac{M_0}{l},\\ \rightarrow:&\ A_H-B_H=0\ \Rightarrow\ A_H=\tfrac{M_0}{l}.\end{aligned}\]

\(M_0\) appears in full in both moment equations with no distance, because a couple has the same moment about every point.

\(A_V\) is negative: the pin pulls the beam down. The two vertical reactions form a couple \(\frac{M_0}{l}\times l=M_0\) that balances the applied one. Because the bending moment jumps at \(x=a\), two regions are needed.

The two regions

Cut at \(x\lt a\)

Cut at \(x\gt a\)

\[\begin{aligned}x\lt a:&\ \ N=-A_H=-\tfrac{M_0}{l},\ \ V=A_V=-\tfrac{M_0}{l},\\ &\ \ M=xA_V=-\tfrac{x}{l}M_0;\\ x\gt a:&\ \ N=-\tfrac{M_0}{l},\ \ V=-\tfrac{M_0}{l},\\ &\ \ M-xA_V-M_0=0\\ &\ \ \Rightarrow M=-\tfrac{xM_0}{l}+M_0=\tfrac{l-x}{l}M_0.\end{aligned}\]

Example 2 · \(N\), \(V\), \(M\), drawn for \(a=0.6l\)

\(N=-M_0/l\) (compression) and \(V=-M_0/l\) everywhere. \(M\) falls from 0 to \(-\frac{a}{l}M_0\), jumps up by \(M_0\) to \(+\frac{b}{l}M_0\), and falls to 0 at \(B\).

  • Normal force: produced only by the support reactions (the inclined roller pushes the beam towards the pin); constant along the whole beam, \(-M_0/l\).
  • Shear force: the same value in both regions. The reactions, and so \(V\), do not depend on where the couple is applied.
  • Moment: a jump discontinuity of size \(M_0\) at \(x=a\). The two straight lines have the same slope because the shear force is the same on both sides.

You can now: handle an inclined roller (its reaction is perpendicular to the surface); find reactions when the only load is a couple; and draw \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) with the jump of \(M_0\) at the couple and equal slopes either side.

6Concept questions ~1 min

1 · Moments about a pivot with a couple there

A 4 m beam is pinned at \(A\), with a roller \(B\) at the far end. A force \(F\) acts down 2 m from \(A\), and a clockwise couple \(M_2\) acts at \(A\) itself. Taking clockwise as positive, which moment equation about \(A\) is correct? (a) \(2F-4B=0\); (b) \(M_2+2F-4B=0\).

Hint

Does a couple's moment depend on the point you take moments about?

Answer

(b). A couple has the same moment about every point (S1 Section 5), including the point where it is applied, so \(M_2\) must appear. The pin's own reaction passes through \(A\) and drops out; the couple does not.

2 · Symmetric or not?

Three equal forces \(F\) at \(a\), \(2a\), \(3a\) on a pin–roller beam of span \(4a\), plus a clockwise couple \(M\) at mid-span. Are the reactions, the SFD and the BMD symmetric?

Hint

Find \(A\) and \(B\) with the couple included.

Answer
\[A=\tfrac32F-\tfrac{M}{4a},\qquad B=\tfrac32F+\tfrac{M}{4a}.\]

Not symmetric. The forces alone would give \(A=B=\tfrac32F\) and symmetric diagrams. The couple adds an extra \(-M/4a\) to the whole SFD (it slides down uniformly), and makes the BMD jump up by \(M\) at mid-span, so the moment is larger on the right half than on the left.

7Macaulay brackets ~7 min

What is this?

Slide the cut, our hinge, from the left end towards the right. A load only swings the left piece once it is on the left piece, that is, once the cut has passed it. From then on its arm is the distance from the load to the cut. A Macaulay bracket is a switch that writes exactly this: off before the load, "distance to the cut" after it. So one formula covers the whole beam.

With many loads, writing \(V\) and \(M\) region by region gets long. Macaulay's notation writes them as one expression valid along the whole beam. It is also the easy way to integrate these piecewise curves later, to get slopes and deflections (S5).

Definition · Macaulay bracket \[[x-a]^n=\begin{cases}0, & x\lt a,\\ (x-a)^n, & x\ge a,\end{cases}\]

for any power \(n\ge0\).

In words: the bracket is "switched off" (zero) before \(x=a\) and behaves like an ordinary power of \((x-a)\) after it. In particular \([x-a]^0\) is 0 before \(a\) and 1 after: a switch that turns a constant on at \(x=a\).

Many books write \(\langle x-a\rangle^n\) with angle brackets; MECH2413 uses square brackets.

The first three Macaulay brackets, \(a=2\)

\([x-2]^0\)\([x-2]^1\)\([x-2]^2\)

Why it helps: a cantilever

A cantilever of length \(L\) is fixed at the right-hand wall; its free left end carries a couple \(m\), and a force \(P\) acts at distance \(l\) from the free end. With \(x\) measured from the free end, the moment is

\[\begin{aligned}l\gt x\gt0:&\quad M=-m,\\ L\gt x\gt l:&\quad M=-m+P(x-l),\end{aligned}\]

two formulas for two regions. With the bracket \([x-l]\), which is zero until the cut passes \(P\), both become one:

\[M=-m+P[x-l].\]

In words: write the moment as if every load were already to the left of the cut, and put each load's distance in a Macaulay bracket so that it only counts once the cut has passed it.

With the arrows as drawn (\(m\) clockwise, \(P\) downward), these signs match a hogging-positive moment. In this course's sagging-positive convention the same beam gives \(M=m-P[x-l]\). The bracket idea is identical either way; in the exam, state your convention.

8Distributed loads: the geometry method ~5 min

What is this?

Push a door with your whole flat palm instead of one fingertip. The push is spread over your hand, but the door swings exactly as if one push of the same total size acted at the middle of your palm. A distributed load is a spread push like that. For equilibrium we may replace it by one force: its total, placed at its "middle", which for an uneven spread sits nearer the heavier part.

Definition · distributed load

A distributed load is applied across a length or an area rather than at one point: snow on a roof, the weight of a floor slab, water pressure on a wall. Along a beam it is described by its intensity \(q(x)\), force per unit length (N/m or kN/m). For example, a beam might be loaded at 1000 N/m along its whole length.

There are two ways to deal with it:

  1. By geometry: replace the distributed load by a single concentrated force. Do this separately for the whole beam (to get reactions) and for each cut piece (using only the part of the load on that piece).
  2. By integration: find \(V\) and \(M\) by integrating, with boundary conditions (Sections 9–13).

In the geometry method a triangular load on a beam is replaced by one downward arrow at a specific point.

Result · resultant of a distributed load \[F_r=\int_0^L q(x)\,\mathrm dx,\qquad\bar x=\frac{\int_0^L q(x)\,x\,\mathrm dx}{\int_0^L q(x)\,\mathrm dx}.\]

In words: the resultant equals the area under the load curve, and it acts at the centroid ("centre of mass") of that area (S1 Section 7).

Reading the formula
\(q(x)\,\mathrm dx\)
the push on one thin strip of the beam.
\(\int_0^L q\,\mathrm dx\)
all the strips added: the total push, the area under the load curve.
\(\int_0^L q\,x\,\mathrm dx\)
each strip's push × its arm from the left end, added: the total turning effect about \(x=0\).
\(\bar x=\dfrac{\cdots}{\cdots}\)
turning effect ÷ total push: the arm at which one single push gives the same turning, like the middle of your palm on the door.
Example · uniform load

\(10\u{kN/m}\) over 2 m, starting 1 m from the left end of a 4 m beam.

\(F_r=10\times2=\boxed{20\u{kN}}\) at \(\bar x=x_0+0.5b=1+0.5(2)=\boxed{2\u{m}}\): the middle of the loaded length.

Example · triangular load

A load falling from \(5\u{kN/m}\) at the left end to 0 over 4 m (on an 8 m beam). For a triangle the resultant is the triangle's area, \(\tfrac12\times\text{base}\times\text{height}\), at one third of the length from the larger end.Most of the triangle's area, and so most of the push, is near the tall end, so the single equivalent push sits closer to it than the middle.

\[\begin{aligned}F_r&=\tfrac12bh=\tfrac12(4)(5)=\boxed{10\u{kN}},\\ \bar x&=x_0+\tfrac13b=0+\tfrac43=\boxed{\tfrac43\u{m}}.\end{aligned}\]

For more complicated loads, such as a wavy \(q(x)\), use the integrals. Two examples:

Example · \(q(x)=x^{1/2}\) over 5 m
\[\begin{aligned}F_r&=\int_0^5x^{1/2}\,\mathrm dx=\tfrac23\left[x^{3/2}\right]_0^5\\ &=\boxed{7.45\u{kN}},\\ \bar x&=\frac{\int_0^5x^{3/2}\,\mathrm dx}{7.45}=\frac{\tfrac25\left[x^{5/2}\right]_0^5}{7.45}\\ &=\boxed{3.0\u{m}}.\end{aligned}\]

Units: \(q\) is taken in kN/m with \(x\) in m, which gives the answer 7.45 kN.

Example · \(q(y)=y^2\) on a 5 m vertical wall

The load acts horizontally on a vertical member, growing with \(y\) measured down from the top (like water pressure, but quadratic).

\[\begin{aligned}F_r&=\int_0^5y^2\,\mathrm dy=\tfrac13\left[y^3\right]_0^5\\ &=\boxed{41.7\u{kN}},\\ \bar y&=\frac{\int_0^5y^3\,\mathrm dy}{41.7}=\frac{\tfrac14\left[y^4\right]_0^5}{41.7}\\ &=\boxed{3.75\u{m}}\ \text{from the top}.\end{aligned}\]

The faster the load grows, the further the resultant moves towards the heavy end: \(\tfrac23L\) for a linear load, \(\tfrac34L\) for \(y^2\), \(\tfrac35L\) for \(\sqrt x\).

Watch

The resultant is for equilibrium of a whole piece. When you cut through a distributed load, the piece you keep carries only part of it: replace that part by its own resultant (its own area, at its own centroid). Never put the whole beam's resultant on a cut piece.

You can now: replace a uniform, triangular or curved distributed load by its resultant (area under the curve) at its centroid; use the integrals for \(F_r\) and \(\bar x\); and use only the part of the load on a cut piece.

9The integral method: \(q\), \(V\) and \(M\) are linked ~5 min

What is this?

Keep sliding the cut, our hinge, to the right in tiny steps. Each step puts a little more downward load onto the left piece, so the net upward push on it (\(V\)) drops by that bit of load. Each step also makes every push on the left piece one step farther from the hinge, so the twist (\(M\)) grows by the net push times the step, just as a push on a door turns it more the farther it is from the hinge. These two facts, written for a tiny step, are the whole integral method.

When \(q(x)\) varies continuously along the beam, cut out a tiny slice of length \(\mathrm dx\) and look at its equilibrium.

On the slice: shear \(V\) and moment \(M\) on the left face, \(V+\mathrm dV\) and \(M+\mathrm dM\) on the right face (all drawn positive), and the load \(\mathrm dF=q\,\mathrm dx\) acting at the middle, \(\tfrac{\mathrm dx}2\) from \(C\) (the right face). Equilibrium:

\[\begin{aligned}\uparrow:&\ V-q\,\mathrm dx-(V+\mathrm dV)=0\\ &\Rightarrow q\,\mathrm dx+\mathrm dV=0,\\ \text{↺}C:&\ -M-\mathrm dx\,V+\tfrac{\mathrm dx}{2}q\,\mathrm dx+M+\mathrm dM=0\\ &\Rightarrow -V\,\mathrm dx+\mathrm dM+\tfrac12q\,(\mathrm dx)^2=0.\end{aligned}\]

Divide by \(\mathrm dx\) and let \(\mathrm dx\to0\) (the \((\mathrm dx)^2\) term vanishes faster than the others):

Result · the differential relations \[\begin{aligned}\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dx}&=-q,\qquad\frac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dx}=V,\\ \frac{\mathrm d^2M}{\mathrm dx^2}&=-q.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the slope of the shear diagram is minus the load intensity (a downward load makes \(V\) fall); the slope of the moment diagram is the shear force. So the load is the "acceleration" of the moment diagram.

Reading the formula
\(\dfrac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dx}=-q\)
per metre the cut moves, the left piece picks up \(q\) of downward load, so its net upward push falls by \(q\).
\(\dfrac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dx}=V\)
per metre the cut moves, every push on the left piece gains one metre of arm, so the twist grows by the net push \(V\).
\(\dfrac{\mathrm d^2M}{\mathrm dx^2}=-q\)
the two together: the load decides how the moment diagram bends. Downward load makes it curve downwards, like the top of a hill.

Shape rules

constantlinearquadratic parabola
linearquadratic parabolacubic parabola

In words: each integration raises the power by one. Use this table to check the shape of every diagram you draw.

A second method

Integrating the relations gives \(V\) and \(M\) directly, without cutting the beam into pieces:

\[V=-\int q\,\mathrm dx+C_1,\qquad M=\int V\,\mathrm dx+C_2.\]
  1. Do: integrate the load once, with a minus sign, for \(V\), keeping a constant \(C_1\). Adding up the load the cut has passed tells you how far the net upward push has fallen; \(C_1\) is the push you started with.
  2. Do: integrate \(V\) for \(M\), keeping a constant \(C_2\). Adding up push × each small step of extra arm gives the twist; \(C_2\) is the twist at \(x=0\).
  3. Do: fix \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) from what you know at the ends (the boundary conditions below). Integration gives only the shape of each curve; the supports say where the curves start.

In words: integrate the load once (with a minus sign) for the shear, integrate the shear for the moment, and fix the two constants from what you know at the ends.

Boundary conditions

What is this? (boundary conditions)

A door on its hinges swings freely: the hinge holds the door up but cannot resist any twist. A pin or roller at a beam's end is the same, so \(M=0\) there. At a free end nothing touches the beam at all, so there is neither push nor twist: \(V=0\) and \(M=0\). A folding door has a hinge between its two leaves, and that hinge cannot pass a twist from one leaf to the other either.

The constants \(C_1,C_2\) come from boundary conditions: values of \(V\) or \(M\) that a support or a free end forces.

End type\(V\)\(M\)Why

parallel motion (vertical slide)0\(\ne0\)it lets the end slide up and down, so it cannot hold a vertical force
sliding sleeve (horizontal slide)\(\ne0\)\(\ne0\)it stops vertical movement and turning

In words: only the bold zeros are usable conditions; "\(\ne0\)" means the value is unknown (a reaction), so it gives no equation.

Joints inside a structure

Some structures are several beams joined together. At an internal joint:

Connecting member\(V\)\(M\)
hinge (pin) \(G\)\(\ne0\)0

A single internal pin cannot transmit a moment, so \(M=0\) there: one extra condition for every hinge.

You can now: derive \(\mathrm dV/\mathrm dx=-q\) and \(\mathrm dM/\mathrm dx=V\) from a small slice; predict the shape of each diagram from the load; integrate \(q\) for \(V\) and \(M\); and pick the boundary and joint conditions that give equations.

What is this?

The same load on three beams held in different ways. Integrating the load gives the same two formulas every time; only the ends differ. A pin end is like a door on hinges: free to turn, so no twist there. A fixed end is like a door screwed shut into its frame: it cannot turn, so it can take a twist. The ends decide the constants, and so the diagrams.

11Matching conditions: loads given piece by piece ~4 min

What is this?

Slide the cut, our hinge, past the point where the load starts. Nothing grabs the beam at that point: no sudden push and no spanner twist. So the push and twist inside just before the point and just after it are the same; only how fast they change is different. That "same on both sides" is a matching condition, and it gives the extra equations the extra constants need.

If \(q(x)\) is given by different functions in different portions of the beam, the beam must be divided into regions and the integration done separately in each.

  1. Do: split the beam into regions wherever the formula for \(q\) changes. One integration only works where the load follows one formula.
  2. Do: integrate in each region, with its own two constants. Each region's curves have their own starting values, which are not yet known.
  3. Do: write the boundary conditions at the ends. These are the zeros the supports and free ends force.
  4. Do: write two matching conditions at every point where regions meet. They tie the pieces of each curve together, with a jump only where a point force or couple acts.
  5. Do: solve for all the constants. Two constants per region need exactly two conditions per region, and the ends plus the joints supply them.

A cantilever, fixed at \(x=0\), free at \(x=l\). Region I (\(0\le x\lt a\)) is unloaded; region II (\(a\lt x\lt l\)) carries a uniform load \(q_0\):

12What each load does to the diagrams ~3 min

What is this?

Walk the cut along the beam and notice what happens as it passes each load. A point force is a sharp push at one spot: the net push on the left piece changes all at once, so \(V\) jumps, while the twist only starts growing at a new rate. A couple is a spanner twist at one spot: the twist changes all at once, so \(M\) jumps, but no push is added, so \(V\) carries on. A spread load is a gentle, steady push: nothing jumps, the lines only bend.

To set the right matching conditions you need to know what each kind of load does to \(V\) and \(M\) at the point where it acts:

point force \(F\)jump by \(F\) (down for a downward force, moving right)change in slope (a kink)

In words: forces make the shear jump and the moment kink; couples make the moment jump and leave the shear alone; distributed loads only bend the lines.

Worked example · mixed loads

A 10 m simply supported beam \(AB\) (pin \(A\), roller \(B\)): \(20\u{kN/m}\) over \(AC\) (first 4 m) and \(10\u{kN}\) at \(D\), 8 m from \(A\) (\(CD=4\u{m}\), \(DB=2\u{m}\)).

System

FBD (80 kN resultant at 2 m)

13Matching example: point force and triangular load ~3 min

What is this?

This is Section 12's walk turned into equations. As the cut passes a sharp push, the push inside jumps by that force, but the twist does not jump, because the force has no arm about its own point. As the cut passes a spanner twist, the twist inside jumps by that couple, and the push carries on unchanged.

Here is how the table turns into matching conditions. On a pin–roller beam with a downward force \(F\) at \(x=a\) and an anticlockwise couple \(M_0\) at \(x=b\), there are three regions, and

\[\begin{aligned}V_{\rm II}(a)&=V_{\rm I}(a)-F,\\ M_{\rm II}(a)&=M_{\rm I}(a),\\ V_{\rm III}(b)&=V_{\rm II}(b),\\ M_{\rm III}(b)&=M_{\rm II}(b)-M_0.\end{aligned}\]

In words: crossing a downward force, \(V\) drops by \(F\) and \(M\) carries on; crossing an anticlockwise couple, \(V\) carries on and \(M\) drops by \(M_0\).

Reading the formula
\(V_{\rm II}(a)=V_{\rm I}(a)-F\)
once the cut passes the force, the left piece carries one more downward push of size \(F\).
\(M_{\rm II}(a)=M_{\rm I}(a)\)
right at the force its arm to the cut is zero, so it adds no twist yet.
\(V_{\rm III}(b)=V_{\rm II}(b)\)
a couple is a pure twist: it adds no push.
\(M_{\rm III}(b)=M_{\rm II}(b)-M_0\)
the anticlockwise couple counts in full the moment the cut passes it; with this sign convention it lowers \(M\).
Worked example · force plus triangular load

A pin–roller beam, span \(l=a+b\). A force \(F\) acts at \(x=a\); a triangular load rises from 0 at \(x=a\) to \(q_0\) at the roller. Use two local coordinates: \(x_1\) from \(A\) in region I, and \(x_2\) from the load point in region II (\(0\le x_2\le b\)).Measuring from where the triangle starts keeps its formula simple, \(q_0x_2/b\), and makes the matching conditions sit at \(x_2=0\).

14Dual loading ~2 min

What is this?

Push on the end of a spanner fitted to a nut. The nut feels two things: your push, passed along the spanner, and a twist equal to your push times the spanner's length. A load hung on a bracket fixed to a beam works the same way. The bracket is the spanner, and the beam, at the bracket's foot, feels the force plus a couple.

A single load may impose both a force and a moment at a point because of the shape of the structure. Example: a bracket fixed to the beam rises and reaches 3 m sideways; a force \(F\) hangs on its end. Moving \(F\) onto the beam at the bracket's foot needs an added couple equal to \(F\times\) (distance moved) (S1 Section 6):

Load on a bracket

Equivalent loads on the beam

\[\text{force } F\ \text{down}\quad+\quad\text{couple } 3F\ (\text{clockwise}).\]

The couple's sense is the way \(F\) would swing the bracket about its foot, just as the push on a spanner decides which way the nut turns.

In words: a load on an arm is a force plus a twist at the arm's root: twist = force × arm length, in the direction the arm would turn.

Watch

Past papers use this repeatedly (Dec 2017 A2, Dec 2014 A1: a 40 kN load on a 0.5 m bracket gives 40 kN plus a 20 kN·m clockwise couple). The SFD then jumps by \(F\) at the bracket's foot, and the BMD jumps by \(Fd\) at the same point. Forgetting the couple loses most of the marks.

You can now: move a load off a bracket onto the beam as a force plus a couple \(Fd\) with the right sense; and show both its jumps, in \(V\) and in \(M\), at the bracket's foot.

17Find the mistake

1 · Shear in the cantilever of Section 11

"Cut at \(x\) in region II and keep the left piece. The load on the left piece is \(q_0(x-a)\) and the wall gives \(q_0(l-a)\) up, so \(V_{\rm II}=q_0(l-a)-q_0(x-a)=q_0(l-x)\). At the wall, then, \(V=q_0l\).

Hint

Is the formula for \(V_{\rm II}\) valid at \(x=0\)?

Answer

\(V_{\rm II}=q_0(l-x)\) is right, but only for \(a\lt x\lt l\). At the wall you must use region I: \(V_{\rm I}=q_0(l-a)\). With the example's numbers that is 10 N, not 30 N. Each formula belongs to its own region.

2 · Macaulay example 1

"\(M=36x-30[x-3]-50[x-7]\). At \(x=5\): \(M=36(5)-30(2)-50(-2)=220\u{kN\,m}\)."

Hint

What is \([5-7]\)?

Answer

\([x-7]=0\) for \(x\lt7\), not \(-2\). \(M(5)=180-60=120\u{kN\,m}\), which agrees with \(6x+90\).

3 · Example 2 with the couple turned round

"If \(M_0\) at \(x=a\) is made anticlockwise, the moment diagram still jumps up by \(M_0\) at \(x=a\), because a couple always increases the bending moment."

Hint

Look at the effect table and at the formula \(M=\dots-\sum M_j\) in Section 4.

Answer

An anticlockwise couple makes \(M\) jump down by \(M_0\) (moving left to right). All the reactions also reverse, so the whole diagram is Example 2's turned upside down.

18Concept checks

1 · Reading a shear diagram

An SFD is a horizontal line at \(+5\u{kN}\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=2\u{m}\). What does the BMD do there, and what load acts on that stretch?

Answer

No load acts (\(\mathrm dV/\mathrm dx=-q=0\)). The BMD is a straight line rising with slope \(5\u{kN\,m}\) per metre, so \(M\) increases by 10 kN·m over the 2 m.

2 · Where is the maximum?

Why does the bending moment have its maximum or minimum where the shear force is zero or changes sign?

Answer

Because \(\mathrm dM/\mathrm dx=V\). Where the slope of \(M\) is zero or changes sign, \(M\) has a turning point. (Also check the ends and the jumps at couples.)

3 · Boundary conditions

A beam is fixed at \(x=0\) and has a pin at \(x=l\). How many boundary conditions does the integral method get from the ends, and is it enough?

Answer

Only one: \(M(l)=0\) at the pin (the fixed end gives none). Two constants need two conditions, so equilibrium alone cannot finish: the beam is statically indeterminate (S1 Section 11). S5 supplies the missing condition from deflection.

4 · Shape

A load grows linearly along a beam. What shapes are the SFD and BMD there?

Answer

Quadratic and cubic parabolas.

5 · Macaulay

Write \(V\) and \(M\) for a cantilever fixed at \(x=0\) (wall reactions \(A\) up, \(M_A\) anticlockwise on the beam) with a downward force \(P\) at \(x=c\), as single expressions.

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}V&=A-P[x-c]^0,\\ M&=-M_A+Ax-P[x-c],\end{aligned}\]

with \(A=P\), \(M_A=Pc\), so \(M=-P(c-x)\) for \(x\lt c\) and 0 after.
