C2.6Routh–Hurwitz: stable range, marginal gain, special cases, relative stability

19 parts · 88 marks · MECH2004 2005–2011 (every paper) · ELEC2245 2022 and 2024 · Learn it · note §18

Predictable · how to spot it
Looks like
“Use Routh tabulation / the Routh array to find the range of \(K\) for the closed loop to be stable.” “Find the value of \(K\) (and the frequency) at which the closed loop is marginally stable.” “Check the stability of \(p(s)=\dots\) using the Routh–Hurwitz criterion.” “Check the relative stability with respect to the line \(s=-1\).” Usually the last part of a question that first asks for the closed-loop transfer function or a root locus of the same loop. The gain may be called \(K\), \(K_c\), \(K_B\), \(k_p,k_I\) or even a sensor constant \(T_T\).
Do
  1. Write the characteristic polynomial (the polynomial whose roots are the closed-loop poles): set \(1+L(s)=0\), clear fractions, write it in descending powers with a positive leading coefficient. (\(L\) = everything round the loop.)
  2. Quick screen: a missing or non-positive coefficient means “not stable” straight away.
  3. Cubic → the one-line test (ready answer). Order 4 or more → build the full array (ready answer).
  4. Require every first-column entry \(\gt0\); each gives an inequality in the gain; intersect them. The number of sign changes in the first column = the number of roots in the right half-plane.
  5. Marginal: the gain that makes the \(s^1\) entry zero; the \(s^2\) row then gives the auxiliary polynomial whose roots \(\pm j\omega\) are the oscillation frequency (ready answer).
  6. Special cases: a single zero in the first column → \(\varepsilon\) method (ready answer); a whole row of zeros → differentiate the auxiliary polynomial (ready answer). Relative stability → substitute \(s=z-\sigma\) first (ready answer).
Watch
  • Do not forget the last entry \(a_0\gt0\): it is where the lower limit (usually \(K\gt0\)) comes from.
  • Strict inequalities: at the marginal gain the loop oscillates for ever, which is not stable.
  • “Stable for every \(K\gt0\)” is a legitimate answer: three MECH2004 parts below have no upper limit. Say so and show why (the \(s^1\) entry is positive for every \(K\gt0\)).
  • Open-loop unstable does not mean closed-loop unstable (and vice versa): test the polynomial that was asked about (E24D Q1(b)).
  • Routh tells you how many roots are in the right half-plane, not where they are.
Beyond the slides · toolbox

The handout (slides 27–28) applies Routh–Hurwitz only to a cubic and shows one marginal case. Four tools on this page go further: the full array for order 4 and 5, the \(\varepsilon\) method, the row-of-zeros rule, and the axis shift for relative stability. They are taught in the orange Beyond box of note §18; the Ready answers below give each one in exam form, and the parts that need them carry the Beyond the slides label.

Ready answer · cubic (slide 27) \[a_3s^3+a_2s^2+a_1s+a_0,\quad a_3\gt0\]
RowColumn 1Column 2
\(s^3\)\(a_3\)\(a_1\)
\(s^2\)\(a_2\)\(a_0\)
\(s^1\)\(\dfrac{a_2a_1-a_3a_0}{a_2}\)\(0\)
\(s^0\)\(a_0\)
\[\begin{aligned}\text{stable}\iff{}&a_3,a_2,a_1,a_0\gt0\\&\text{and}\ a_2a_1\gt a_3a_0.\end{aligned}\]

In words: a cubic is stable exactly when all four coefficients are positive and “middle two multiplied” beats “outer two multiplied”. On slide 27 (mass \(m\), damping \(c\), stiffness \(k\), PID gains) this reads \((c+K_d)(k+K_p)\gt mK_i\).

Ready answer · building the array (any order)

Row 1: \(a_n,\ a_{n-2},\ a_{n-4},\dots\) Row 2: \(a_{n-1},\ a_{n-3},\dots\) (pad with zeros). Every later entry uses the two rows above it:

\[\begin{aligned}b_1&=\frac{a_{n-1}a_{n-2}-a_na_{n-3}}{a_{n-1}},\\ b_2&=\frac{a_{n-1}a_{n-4}-a_na_{n-5}}{a_{n-1}}.\end{aligned}\]

Same pattern all the way down: (pivot × entry above-right) − (entry above pivot × entry right of pivot), all over the pivot. You get \(n+1\) rows, \(s^n\) down to \(s^0\); the last entry is always \(a_0\).

In words: each new entry is a little cross-multiplication of the two rows above, divided by the first entry of the row just above. Stable ⇔ no sign change down the first column; each sign change is one right-half-plane root. A whole row may be multiplied by a positive number to tidy it.

Ready answer · marginal gain and crossing frequency

Cubic with gain in it: set the \(s^1\) entry to zero, \(a_2a_1=a_3a_0\), and solve for the marginal gain \(K^*\). Then the \(s^2\) row is the auxiliary polynomial:

\[\begin{aligned}A(s)&=a_2s^2+a_0=0\\ \Rightarrow s&=\pm j\omega,\quad \omega=\sqrt{a_0/a_2}.\end{aligned}\]

Check: \(p(s)=(a_3s+a_2)(s^2+\omega^2)\) at \(K^*\); the third pole is \(-a_2/a_3\).

In words: at the marginal gain two poles sit exactly on the imaginary axis; the row just above the zero row, read as a polynomial, has those two poles as its roots, so its square root ratio is the frequency of the never-dying oscillation.

Ready answer · zero in the first column (\(\varepsilon\) method) · beyond the slides

If a first-column entry is 0 but the rest of its row is not, replace the 0 by a small positive number \(\varepsilon\), carry on building the array, then let \(\varepsilon\to0^+\) and read the signs.

  • An entry like \(\dfrac{3\varepsilon-15}{\varepsilon}\) → \(-\infty\): negative.
  • Signs \(+,+,+,-,+\) → two sign changes → two right-half-plane roots → unstable.
  • A zero in the first column already means “not stable”: at best marginal, usually unstable. The \(\varepsilon\) count tells you how many roots are on the wrong side.

In words: the zero is only a division-by-zero accident; nudging it to a tiny positive number lets the array finish and the sign pattern either side of it counts the bad roots.

Ready answer · a whole row of zeros · beyond the slides

A whole row of zeros (typical at the marginal gain) means the polynomial has roots placed symmetrically about the origin, for example \(\pm j\omega\). Take the row above it as the auxiliary polynomial \(A(s)\), replace the zero row by the coefficients of \(dA/ds\), and carry on.

\[\begin{aligned}&s^3+2s^2+s+2:\ s^1\ \text{row}=0,\\&A=2s^2+2,\ \tfrac{dA}{ds}=4s,\\&\text{column }1,2,4,2:\ \text{no sign change.}\end{aligned}\]

No sign change and a zero row → no right-half-plane roots but a pair on the axis, \(A(s)=0\Rightarrow s=\pm j1\): marginally stable.

In words: a row of zeros flags a mirror-image pair of roots; the row above is that pair's own polynomial, and its derivative lets the array finish so the remaining roots can still be counted.

Ready answer · relative stability (shift the axis) · beyond the slides

To test whether every root lies to the left of the vertical line \(\operatorname{Re}s=-\sigma\): substitute

\[\begin{aligned}&s=z-\sigma\\&(\text{line }s=-1:\ s=z-1;\\&\ \text{line }s=1:\ s=z+1),\end{aligned}\]

expand in \(z\), and apply Routh–Hurwitz to the new polynomial. No sign change ⇔ every root has \(\operatorname{Re}s\lt-\sigma\). The number of sign changes = number of roots to the right of the line.

Answer skeleton for “describe how to proceed” (2 marks): (1) substitute \(s=z-\sigma\) in the characteristic equation; (2) the line becomes the imaginary axis of the \(z\)-plane; (3) apply Routh to the polynomial in \(z\); (4) all first-column entries positive ⇔ all roots left of the line.

In words: moving the origin onto the line turns “left of the line” into “left half-plane”, which is the only question Routh can answer. A line further left asks for a faster-decaying, better-damped system.

ELEC2245 Dec 2024 Q1(b)5 marksRelated course

(b) Consider the following closed-loop system.

Fig. 1. The system for Q1(b) (redrawn).

Suppose \(D_c(s)=1\), and \(G(s)=\dfrac{3s+5}{s^2+s-2}.\) Is the open loop system stable? How about the closed-loop system? How do you get your answer?

Q1(a) is on guide Cx (system classification); Q1(c)–(d) are on guide C2a · C2.3.

Hint

Two different polynomials: the open loop's poles are the roots of the denominator of \(G\); the closed loop's are the roots of \(1+D_cG=0\).

Solution
  1. Open loop: poles are the roots of \(s^2+s-2=(s+2)(s-1)\): \(s=-2\) and \(s=+1\). A pole in the right half-plane gives a mode \(e^{t}\) that grows, so the open loop is unstable.
  2. Closed loop: \(1+G=0\Rightarrow (s^2+s-2)+(3s+5)=0\): \[s^2+4s+3=(s+1)(s+3)=0.\]
  3. Poles \(s=-1\), \(s=-3\): both in the left half-plane, so the closed loop is stable. (Routh for a quadratic: all coefficients 1, 4, 3 positive ⇔ stable.)
  4. Closed-loop transfer function for reference: \[T(s)=\frac{3s+5}{s^2+4s+3}.\]

“How do you get your answer” wants the method named: poles of \(G\) for the open loop, roots of \(1+D_cG\) for the closed loop.

Open loop: poles \(+1,\,-2\) → unstable. Closed loop: \(s^2+4s+3=0\), poles \(-1,\,-3\) → stable. Feedback has stabilised an unstable plant.

C2.7Pole placement: choose P/PD/PI/PID gains for specs

8 parts · 47 marks · MECH2004 May 2010 (Q2, Q4), Aug 2010 (Q2), Dec 2011 (Q3, Q4) · ELEC2245 May 2022, May 2023 (Q3(c) both) · Learn it · note §19

This is the type closest to the MECH2413 handout: slides 29–30 (and the heavier-mass task, slides 41–42) do exactly this, choosing poles and matching coefficients. The specifications change from paper to paper (overshoot, rise, peak or settling time, repeated poles, a disturbance to reject), so the type gets a decision procedure rather than a card.

Beyond the slides · toolbox for this type

The slides give the desired poles directly. The related-course papers give specifications instead, so you need the textbook second-order formulas (taught in the orange box of note §9), plus three extras:

  • Settling band. The envelope is \(e^{-\sigma t}\) with \(\sigma=\zeta\omega_n\), so the time to fall inside a band of \(\pm\)band is \(t_s\approx-\ln(\text{band})/\sigma\): \(4.6/\sigma\) (1%), \(3.9/\sigma\approx4/\sigma\) (2%), \(3.0/\sigma\) (5%). \(e^{-\sigma t}=0.05\) gives \(\sigma t=3.0\).
  • Third pole. A PID loop on a second-order plant has three poles. Put the third one at least 5 times further left than the pair's real part (\(p\ge5\sigma\)). Its mode \(e^{-pt}\) then dies out long before the pair's, so the pair still decides the response ("dominant poles").
  • Controller zeros. The reference-to-output map \(CG/(1+CG)\) has the controller's zeros on top. A zero about as near the origin as the poles (or nearer) gives more overshoot and a faster rise than the \(\zeta\) formulas predict. It adds a multiple of the response's derivative, which is large early on. So treat the formulas as a first guess and check by simulation (slide 30: "the original load test decides").
Decision procedure
  1. Turn each specification into a pole condition (ready answer).
    • "overshoot less than \(M_p\)" → \(\zeta\ge\zeta(M_p)\) (10% → 0.591, 20% → 0.456, 5% → 0.690);
    • "rise time less than \(t_r\)" → \(\omega_n\ge1.8/t_r\);
    • "settling time less than \(t_s\)" → \(\sigma\ge4.6/t_s\), \(4/t_s\) or \(3/t_s\) for a 1%, 2% or 5% band;
    • "peak time \(t_p\)" → \(\omega_d=\pi/t_p\), then \(\omega_n=\omega_d/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\);
    • "critically damped", "two repeated real poles" → \(\zeta=1\) (discriminant zero);
    • poles named outright (slides 29–30: \(-3,-4\) or \(-2,-3,-4\)) → go straight to step 4.
  2. Choose the controller by counting knobs. The closed-loop polynomial needs one free gain for every coefficient below the leading one.
    • "reject a step disturbance", "zero steady-state error" → it must contain I (PI or PID). P and PD leave an offset (note §15).
    • Second-order plant + PD: 2 gains, 2 coefficients ✓. Second-order plant + PID: 3 gains, 3 coefficients → the dominant pair plus a third fast pole.
    • First-order plant + PI: 2 gains, 2 coefficients ✓. Type-1 plant \(b/[s(s+a)]\) + PI: 3 coefficients, 2 gains, so the \(s^2\) coefficient is stuck at \(a\) (ready answer).
  3. Write the actual characteristic polynomial: clear the fractions in \(1+C(s)G(s)=0\) (note §16). Keep the leading coefficient (\(m\)) or divide by it; either way the two polynomials must have the same leading coefficient (slide 42).
  4. Write the desired polynomial: \(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2\), times \((s+p)\) if there is a third pole. When the question sets limits, choose values with some margin above them. The formulas are approximate and controller zeros add overshoot.
  5. Match coefficients of equal powers of \(s\) and solve for the gains. "Ranges" questions (ELEC2245 Q3(c)): write \(\omega_n\) and \(\zeta\) (or \(\sigma\)) in terms of the gains; each specification becomes an inequality; combine them and say which one is binding.
  6. Check the zero. PD: \(-K_p/K_d\). PI: \(-K_i/K_p\). PID: roots of \(K_ds^2+K_ps+K_i\). If it is near the poles, say the real overshoot will be larger, and simulate it if you can. If it is too large: raise \(\zeta\), move the poles, or (PI) cancel the slow plant pole.
  7. Check the rest: gains positive; stable (automatic if every chosen pole is in the left half-plane; Routh as a check); steady state by the final-value theorem on \(G_d\) and \(E\) (note §11); actuator effort: large gains mean a large force or throttle, and the actuator may saturate (slide 33, note §20).

Ready answer · PI on a first-order or a type-1 plant

First-order \(G=b/(s+a)\) (cruise control, tank level):

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{Y}{R}&=\frac{b(K_Ps+K_I)}{s^2+(a+bK_P)s+bK_I},\\ \omega_n&=\sqrt{bK_I},\quad\zeta=\frac{a+bK_P}{2\sqrt{bK_I}},\\ K_P&=\frac{2\zeta\omega_n-a}{b},\quad K_I=\frac{\omega_n^2}{b}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: \(K_P\) sets how fast the error envelope decays (\(\sigma\)), \(K_I\) how fast it oscillates (\(\omega_n\)). The zero at \(-K_I/K_P\) adds overshoot. Choosing \(K_I/K_P=a\) cancels the plant pole and leaves a first-order loop, \(bK_P/(s+bK_P)\), with no overshoot.

Type-1 \(G=b/[s(s+a)]\): \(s^3+as^2+bK_ps+bK_i\). The \(s^2\) coefficient cannot be changed, so the three poles always add up to \(-a\): you can place two of them, and the third follows. Routh: stable only if \(aK_p\gt K_i\).

Predictable · how to spot it

Beyond the slides · the two conditions behind every rule

A point \(s\) is a closed-loop pole for some gain \(K\gt0\) exactly when \(1+KL(s)=0\), that is \(L(s)=-1/K\), a negative real number. Split that into an angle and a size:

\[\begin{aligned}&\text{angle: }\angle L(s)=\pm180^\circ,\ \pm540^\circ,\dots\\&\text{magnitude: }K=\frac{1}{|L(s)|}.\end{aligned}\]

With \(L=\dfrac{\prod(s-z_j)}{\prod(s-p_i)}\), the angle of \(L\) is \(\sum\angle(s-z_j)-\sum\angle(s-p_i)\): each term is the direction of the arrow from a zero or pole to the test point, measured anticlockwise from the positive real axis.

In words: the angle condition decides where the locus is (it gives the real-axis rule, the asymptotes and the departure angles); the magnitude condition then tells you which gain puts a pole at a chosen point on it.

Decision procedure · sketching a root locus
  1. Get the form \(1+KL(s)=0\). If \(L\) is given (“loop transfer function \(L=K\dots\)”), \(K\) is the gain. If a controller is given, write the characteristic equation and collect every term with the gain: \(a(s)+K\,b(s)=0\Rightarrow L=b/a\). PI: \(D_c=k_p\left(1+\frac1s\right)=k_p\frac{s+1}{s}\), so \(L=\dfrac{s+1}{s\cdot(\text{plant denominator})}\). Make every factor monic (\(s+p\), not \(ps+1\)); constants go into \(K\).
  2. Poles and zeros. Factor \(L\): poles \(p_i\) (\(n\) of them, drawn ×), zeros \(z_j\) (\(m\), drawn ○). \(s^2+2s=s(s+2)\) is two real poles; \(s^2+2s+5=(s+1)^2+4\) gives \(-1\pm2j\). There are \(n\) branches; each starts at a pole (\(K=0\)), \(m\) of them end at the zeros and \(n-m\) go to infinity (\(K\to\infty\)). The picture is symmetric about the real axis.

  4. Asymptotes (ready answer): \(n-m\) straight lines at \(\frac{(2q+1)180^\circ}{n-m}\) from the centroid \(\sigma_a=\frac{\sum p_i-\sum z_j}{n-m}\). Draw them dashed.
  5. Breakaway / break-in (ready answer): needed when a real segment lies between two poles (branches meet and leave the axis) or between two zeros (they arrive). Solve \(\mathrm dK/\mathrm ds=0\) with \(K=-1/L\); keep the real root that lies on that segment and gives \(K\gt0\). A segment from a pole to a zero (or a pole to \(-\infty\)) needs none.

Ready answer · imaginary-axis crossing by Routh

Cubic \(s^3+a_2s^2+a_1s+a_0\) (all \(a_i\gt0\)): stable \(\iff a_2a_1\gt a_0\); crossing when \(a_2a_1=a_0\), at \(\omega=\sqrt{a_1}\) (from \(a_2s^2+a_0=0\)).

Ready answer · reading ζ (the ζ-line)

A pole \(s=-\zeta\omega_n\pm j\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\) sits at angle \(\theta\) from the negative real axis with \(\zeta=\cos\theta\). The ζ-line is the ray \(s=a\,(-\zeta+j\sqrt{1-\zeta^2})\), \(a=\omega_n\gt0\). Handy forms:

\(\zeta\)ray\(\theta\)
0.5\(s=a(-1+j\sqrt3)\)\(60^\circ\)

  1. Substitute the ray into the characteristic equation; set the imaginary part to 0 (gives \(K\) in terms of \(a\)) and the real part to 0 (gives \(a\)).
  2. Accept only \(a\gt0\) and \(K\gt0\). Then \(s\) and \(K\) are the answer (check \(K\) with the magnitude condition).
  3. No such solution → the branch never meets that ζ. If the complex branch starts at the open-loop pair with damping \(\zeta_0\) and its ζ falls as \(K\) grows (departure angle pointing toward the imaginary axis), the largest ζ is \(\zeta_0\), approached as \(K\to0^+\) and not reached by any positive gain.

In words: damping is an angle in the \(s\)-plane; draw the ray with that angle and see whether, and at what gain, a branch meets it.

Ready answer · “Is it stable for all \(K\gt0\), and why?” (answer skeleton)
  1. Yes when \(n-m\le2\) with \(\sigma_a\lt0\) and no crossing: “all \(n\) branches stay in the left half-plane: the real branch lies on [segment], the complex branches approach the asymptotes \(\mathrm{Re}\,s=\sigma_a\lt0\); Routh: [condition] holds for every \(K\gt0\).”

In words: the far ends of the locus follow the asymptotes, so their direction decides whether high gain can push poles across the imaginary axis.

Beyond the slides · sketching rules for a Nyquist plot

The slides (37–39) define the plot and the count \(Z=P_r+N\) but do not teach how to predict its shape. Three quick limits do it; the note's Beyond box in §23 works the type-1 case \(K/[s(s+1)]\).

  • Low-frequency end (\(\omega\to0^+\)): keep only the lowest power of \(s\). With \(n\) poles at \(s=0\) (the type \(n\)), \(L\approx L_0/(j\omega)^n\): \(|L|\to\infty\) at phase \(-90^\circ n\), plus \(180^\circ\) if \(L_0\lt0\) (a factor written \((s/a-1)\) equals \(-1\) at \(s=0\)). For type 1 the curve runs along a vertical line \(\mathrm{Re}\,L=\lim_{\omega\to0}\mathrm{Re}\,L(j\omega)\). Why: near \(\omega=0\) the integrator dominates the size, the other factors only shift the real part.
  • High-frequency end (\(\omega\to\infty\)): with relative degree \(r\) (poles minus zeros), \(L\to0\) arriving from the direction \(-90^\circ r\): \(r=1\) from below, \(r=2\) from the left, \(r=3\) from above.
  • Real-axis crossings: solve \(\mathrm{Im}\,L(j\omega)=0\) for \(\omega\gt0\) and put that \(\omega\) into \(\mathrm{Re}\,L\).
  • Detour: each pole at \(s=0\) maps to a half-turn at infinity, swept clockwise, joining the \(\omega=0^-\) end to the \(\omega=0^+\) end.
Decision procedure · Nyquist questions
  1. Count the open-loop poles first. Factor \(L(s)\) (or \(G(s)\) if the gain \(K\) is outside). \(n\) = poles at \(s=0\) (type); \(P_r\) = poles in the right half-plane. Cue: a factor \((s/10-1)\) is a pole at \(s=+10\), so \(P_r=1\). Relative degree \(r\) = (number of poles) − (number of zeros).
  2. Low-frequency end. Type 1: compute \(\lim_{\omega\to0}\mathrm{Re}\,L(j\omega)\) (the vertical asymptote) and the sign of \(\mathrm{Im}\,L\) (does the \(\omega\gt0\) branch start at the bottom or the top?). Trap: with an RHP pole the start flips: \(\frac{s+1}{s(s/10-1)}\approx-\frac1s\) starts at the top.
  3. High-frequency end. From \(r\): the curve enters the origin from below (\(r=1\)), the left (\(r=2\)) or above (\(r=3\)).
  4. Real-axis crossings. Solve \(\mathrm{Im}\,L(j\omega)=0\); the crossing point \(x_c=\mathrm{Re}\,L(j\omega_c)\) is the number every stability answer hangs on. If \(K\) multiplies the plot, \(x_c\) scales with \(K\).
  5. “Which figure?” → match the three features (start, end, crossing) and say which feature rules the other figure out. “Draw” → plot the \(\omega\gt0\) branch with arrows, mirror it in the real axis for \(\omega\lt0\), add the detour arc, label the asymptote and the crossing.
  6. Gain outside the plot? The plot is of \(G\) (or of \(L\) with \(K=1\)). Closed-loop poles solve \(1+KG=0\), i.e. \(G=-1/K\): count encirclements of the point \(-1/K\), not \(-1\) (ready answer).
  7. Count \(N\) (clockwise positive): draw a ray from the critical point to the left; each branch crossing it upward counts +1, downward −1. Include the \(\omega\lt0\) branch and the detour arc.
  8. Conclude: \(Z=P_r+N\); stable iff \(Z=0\). So \(P_r=0\) needs \(N=0\); \(P_r=1\) needs \(N=-1\) (one anticlockwise turn). The boundary \(K\) is where the critical point sits on a crossing. Check with Routh on \(1+KG=0\) (two lines; slide 39 says the two boundaries agree).

Cues: “Nyquist plot”, “which figure”, “encirclement”, “as \(K\) changes from 0 to infinity”, points labelled \(-1/K_s\), \(-1/K_l\) (small and large \(K\)).

Traps:

  • Forgetting \(P_r\): with an open-loop RHP pole, “not encircled” means unstable (Dec 2024).
  • Counting only the \(\omega\gt0\) branch: its mirror crosses the ray too, so a type-1 plot that wraps \(-1\) has \(N=2\), not 1.
  • Saying “stable for \(K\gt2\)” because the crossing \(-K/2\) moved: the crossing must stay to the right of \(-1\) (equivalently \(-1/K\) to the left of \(-0.5\)).
  • Giving only the stable range when asked “as \(K\) changes from 0 to infinity”: also state the marginal \(K\) (poles on the \(j\omega\) axis) and \(Z\) above it.
Ready answer · critical gain from a Nyquist plot of \(G\) \[\begin{aligned}1+KG(s)=0\iff G(s)=-\frac1K\end{aligned}\]

If the plot of \(G\) crosses the negative real axis at \(x_c\) (at \(\omega=\omega_c\)), the critical point sits on the crossing when

\[K_c=-\frac{1}{x_c}=\frac1{|x_c|},\]

and the closed loop then has poles at \(s=\pm j\omega_c\).

In words: moving the gain is the same as sliding the critical point along the negative real axis; the stability boundary is the gain that puts it exactly on the curve.

Beyond the slides · Bode form, breakpoints, crossover, bandwidth

Slides 36 and 40 teach reading magnitude, phase and the two margins. Part (a) of every paper also asks for words the slides do not define:

  • Bode form: write every factor as \((s/p+1)\), or \(s^2/\omega_n^2+2\zeta s/\omega_n+1\) for a complex pair, and collect the constant in front. Example: \[\begin{aligned}\frac{K}{s(s+1)(s+4)}=\frac{K/4}{s(s+1)(s/4+1)}.\end{aligned}\]
  • Breakpoint (corner frequency): the factor \((s/p+1)\) breaks at \(\omega=p\); on the log–log magnitude plot the slope changes there by −1 (pole) or +1 (zero) decade per decade. A complex pair breaks at \(\omega_n\) (slope change −2) with a resonant bump of height about \(1/(2\zeta)\) when \(\zeta\) is small. An integrator \(1/s\) has no breakpoint: slope −1 from the start, phase −90°.
  • Crossover frequency \(\omega_c\): where \(|L(j\omega)|=1\) (slide 40 calls it the gain crossover).
  • Bandwidth \(\omega_{BW}\): strictly a closed-loop quantity, the frequency where \(|T(j\omega)|=\left|\frac{L}{1+L}\right|\) falls to \(0.707\) of its low-frequency value. For these loops it lies between \(\omega_c\) and about \(2\omega_c\). See the ready answer for what to quote from an open-loop plot.

Ready answer · reading GM and PM \[\begin{aligned}|L(j\omega_c)|&=1,\\ \mathrm{PM}&=180^\circ+\angle L(j\omega_c),\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}\angle L(j\omega_{180})&=-180^\circ,\\ \mathrm{GM}&=\frac{1}{|L(j\omega_{180})|}.\end{aligned}\]

For a loop with no right-half-plane poles whose phase crosses −180° once (every loop in these papers), the closed loop is stable iff GM \(\gt1\), equivalently PM \(\gt0\) (Nyquist: the curve passes to the right of −1).

In words: the phase margin is how much extra lag the loop can take where its gain is one; the gain margin is how many times bigger the gain can get where the phase is −180°.

Ready answer · \(K\) for a gain margin \(g\) (and neutral stability) \[\begin{aligned}K_{\rm new}&=\frac{K_0\,\mathrm{GM}_0}{g}\\&=\frac{K_0}{g\,|L_0(j\omega_{180})|}\end{aligned}\]

Neutral stability is \(g=1\): \(K_n=K_0\,\mathrm{GM}_0\).

In words: the −180° frequency stays put when \(K\) changes; only the height of \(|L|\) there scales with \(K\). Choose \(K\) so that this height is \(1/g\).

Ready answer · \(K\) for a phase margin \(\varphi\) \[\begin{aligned}&\angle L_0(j\omega_\varphi)=-180^\circ+\varphi\\&\Rightarrow\ K=\frac{K_0}{|L_0(j\omega_\varphi)|}\end{aligned}\]

In words: find the frequency where the phase already leaves the margin you want, then pick the gain that makes that frequency the crossover (\(|L|=1\) there).
