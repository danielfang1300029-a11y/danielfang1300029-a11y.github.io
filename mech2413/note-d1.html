Dynamics of Particles

0Where this chapter sits

This chapter is about how bodies move when forces act on them, with each body treated as a point. Two pictures run through it: a car on a road that speeds up, brakes, bends and banks; and a small ball on a string, hung inside a car or whirled round.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 Seen from far enough away, a car is a moving dot with mass.
  2. §2 Velocity is how fast position changes; acceleration is how fast velocity changes. Velocity always points along the path.
  3. §3 The suvat formulas hold only when acceleration is constant.
  4. §4 Otherwise add up small changes: integrate.
  5. §5 When acceleration depends on speed or position, use \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\).
  6. §6 Net force = mass × acceleration, but only seen from the ground. In a braking car you need a fictitious force.
  7. §7 One recipe for every problem: sketch, axes, FBD, components, \(\sum F=ma\), solve.
  8. §8 A parachute opening is the recipe plus \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\).
  9. §9 On a bend, axes ride with the car: along the road changes speed, into the bend (\(v^2/\rho\)) changes direction.
  10. §10 Some real force must supply the inward pull: friction, a banked road, a string. Dec 2023, Dec 2024 B1
  11. §11 A falling body speeds up until drag equals weight: terminal velocity.
  12. §12 Tiny, slow bodies: drag \(\propto V\).
  13. §13 Large, fast bodies: drag \(\propto V^2\). Know the shapes of the plots, not the formulas.
  14. §14 Drag exercises: floating droplets and a parachute jerk.

Newton's three laws

Laws · Newton's laws of motion
  1. First law (law of inertia). An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps moving with constant velocity, unless an external force acts on it.
  2. Second law. The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net (total) force on it and inversely proportional to its mass: \(\vb F=m\vb a\) (Section 6 gives the full form).
  3. Third law. For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction: the forces two bodies exert on each other are equal in size, opposite in direction and on the same line (S1 Section 3).

In words: no net force, no change of velocity; a net force changes velocity at a rate force ÷ mass; forces come in pairs acting on different bodies.

1Treating a body as a particle ~2 min

What is this?

Watch a car on a motorway from a footbridge. To say where it will be in ten minutes, its wheels, doors and shape do not matter: it is a dot moving along the road. The dot still carries the car's whole mass. That dot is a particle. If you ask how the wheels spin, the dot is no longer enough.

Definition · particle

Treating an object as a particle means modelling the whole object as a single point mass: it has mass but negligible size and shape, and we ignore its rotation and how its mass is spread inside it. Only its translational motion (moving from place to place) is studied.

Two examples: cars on a motorway (for traffic or a journey, a car is a moving dot) and the Soyuz MS-15 capsule landing under its parachute in April 2020 (for its descent speed, the capsule is a falling point). When rotation matters (a wheel, a crank, a flywheel) the body must be treated as a rigid body, which is D2.

Rule of thumb: a body can be a particle when every point of it moves in (almost) the same way, or when the question only asks about its overall path and speed.

You can now: decide when a body may be treated as a particle; say what the particle model ignores (size, shape, rotation, how the mass is spread).

2Displacement, velocity and acceleration ~3 min

What is this?

Drive along a winding road. Displacement is the straight arrow from where you started to where you are now; the road you actually followed is the path. Velocity is how fast that arrow is changing: the speedometer reading, plus the direction the car is pointing. Acceleration is how fast the velocity changes, and you cause it three ways: pressing the pedal, braking, or turning the steering wheel.

Definitions · kinematics
  • Kinematics: describing motion (where, how fast, how quickly speed changes) without asking what causes it. Kinetics (Section 6 on) links motion to forces.
  • Displacement \(\Delta\vb r\): the change of position, a vector (length and direction). It joins the start point to the end point; where the origin is does not matter.
  • Path: the actual route travelled. Different paths can give the same displacement, so displacement ≠ path.
  • Average velocity over a time \(\Delta t\): \(\vb v_{\rm avg}=\Delta\vb r/\Delta t\).
  • Instantaneous velocity: the limit as \(\Delta t\to0\):
\[\begin{aligned}\vb v&=\lim_{\Delta t\to0}\frac{\Delta\vb r}{\Delta t}=\frac{\mathrm d\vb r}{\mathrm dt}=\dot{\vb r},\\ \vb a&=\lim_{\Delta t\to0}\frac{\Delta\vb v}{\Delta t}=\frac{\mathrm d\vb v}{\mathrm dt}=\ddot{\vb r}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: velocity is the rate of change of position; acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. A dot over a symbol means "differentiate with respect to time" (\(\dot{\vb r}=\mathrm d\vb r/\mathrm dt\), \(\ddot{\vb r}=\mathrm d^2\vb r/\mathrm dt^2\)).

Reading the formula
\(\Delta\vb r\)
the short arrow from where the car was to where it is a moment later.
\(\Delta t\)
that moment.
\(\lim_{\Delta t\to0}\)
shrink the moment until the arrow lies along the road: this is why the velocity is tangent to the path.
\(\Delta\vb v\)
the change in the velocity arrow. It is not zero if only the direction changes, so a car turning at a steady speed is still accelerating.

Picture a particle moving along a curved path from \(A\) at time \(t\) to \(A'\) at time \(t+\Delta t\). Its position vector changes from \(\vb r\) to \(\vb r'=\vb r+\Delta\vb r\). As \(\Delta t\) shrinks, \(\Delta\vb r\) lines up with the path, so the velocity is always tangent to the path.

Straight-line (rectilinear) motion. On a line we only need one coordinate \(s\) (a point \(P\) moves to \(P'\) along the \(s\)-axis, \(\Delta s\) apart):

\[v=\frac{\mathrm ds}{\mathrm dt},\quad a=\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{\mathrm d^2s}{\mathrm dt^2}.\]

In words: on a straight line, velocity is the slope of the \(s\)–\(t\) graph and acceleration is the slope of the \(v\)–\(t\) graph.

You can now: tell displacement from path; get \(v\) and \(a\) by differentiating position; explain why the velocity is always tangent to the path; read \(v\) and \(a\) as slopes of the \(s\)–\(t\) and \(v\)–\(t\) graphs.

3Constant acceleration ~2 min

What is this?

Imagine a car whose acceleration is held exactly steady, or a ball dropped with air resistance ignored. The speed then rises by the same amount every second. Only in that case do the school "suvat" formulas hold: they are the integrals of a constant acceleration, done once for you.

If \(a=a_c\) is constant, integrate step by step, with \(v=v_0\) and \(s=s_0\) at \(t=0\):

Results · constant acceleration only \[\begin{aligned}&\textstyle\int_{v_0}^{v}\mathrm dv=\int_0^t a_c\,\mathrm dt\\ &\quad\Rightarrow\ v=v_0+a_ct,\\ &\textstyle\int_{s_0}^{s}\mathrm ds=\int_0^t(v_0+a_ct)\,\mathrm dt\\ &\quad\Rightarrow\ s=s_0+v_0t+\tfrac12a_ct^2,\\ &\textstyle\int_{v_0}^{v}v\,\mathrm dv=\int_{s_0}^{s}a_c\,\mathrm ds\\ &\quad\Rightarrow\ v^2=v_0^2+2a_c(s-s_0).\end{aligned}\]

In words: these are the HKDSE "suvat" equations. The third comes from the chain rule \(a=\dfrac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=\dfrac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm ds}\dfrac{\mathrm ds}{\mathrm dt}=v\dfrac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm ds}\), i.e. \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\) (Section 5).

Reading the formula
\(v^2-v_0^2\)
how much the speed squared has grown.
\(a_c\)
the steady acceleration, the same at every point of the road.
\(s-s_0\)
the distance covered along the line.
no \(t\)
use this one when the time is neither given nor asked.
Warning

Acceleration is often not constant in real life (drag, springs, engines, a driver's foot). Then these formulas are not valid. Never use them when \(a\ne\) constant; integrate instead (Sections 4–5).

Worked example · free fall from height \(h\)

Ball released from rest

FBD (air resistance neglected): weight only

Acceleration is measured as a function of time, \(a(t)=\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt\), so in general

\[\begin{aligned}v_2&=v_1+\int_{t_1}^{t_2}a(t)\,\mathrm dt,\\ s_2&=s_1+\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Big(v_1\\ &\qquad+\int_{t_1}^{t}a(t')\,\mathrm dt'\Big)\mathrm dt.\end{aligned}\]

Here, with \(s\) and \(v\) positive downward, \(a=g\) constant, \(u=v(0)=0\), \(s(0)=0\): Down is the direction of motion, so gravity enters as \(+g\): the chapter's falling convention.

\[\begin{aligned}v(t)&=\textstyle\int_0^tg\,\mathrm dt=gt,\\ s(t)&=\textstyle\int_0^tv\,\mathrm dt=\tfrac12gt^2,\\ h&=\tfrac12g\tau^2\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\tau=\sqrt{2h/g}},\\ &\boxed{v(\tau)=g\tau=\sqrt{2gh}}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the time to fall grows with the square root of the height; the impact speed is \(\sqrt{2gh}\) whatever the mass. Check: \(h=5\u{m}\) gives \(\tau=1.01\u{s}\), \(v=9.9\u{m/s}\).

Maths reminder: \(\int x^n\,\mathrm dx=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+\text{const}\) for \(n\ne-1\), but \(\int\frac1x\,\mathrm dx=\ln|x|+\text{const}\). The \(\ln\) case appears constantly with drag.

You can now: derive the suvat formulas by integrating a constant \(a\); find the fall time \(\sqrt{2h/g}\) and impact speed \(\sqrt{2gh}\); recognise when suvat must not be used.

4Varying acceleration: integrate ~4 min

What is this?

Real drivers do not hold the pedal still, so the acceleration changes from moment to moment, like the jagged trace from an accelerometer. Then suvat fails. Instead add up the small changes: in each short moment the speed changes by (acceleration × that moment). Adding all of them is integrating, which on a graph is the area under the curve.

When acceleration changes with time, the same integrals still work; you just cannot pull \(a\) out:

\[\begin{aligned}v_2&=v_1+\int_{t_1}^{t_2}a(t)\,\mathrm dt,\\ s_2&=s_1+\int_{t_1}^{t_2}v(t)\,\mathrm dt.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the change in velocity is the area under the \(a\)–\(t\) graph; the change in position is the area under the \(v\)–\(t\) graph. Going the other way, differentiate: \(a=\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt\), \(v=\mathrm ds/\mathrm dt\).

Reading the formula
\(v_1\), \(s_1\)
where the car was and how fast it went at the start, time \(t_1\).
\(a(t)\,\mathrm dt\)
the small speed gain in one short moment.
\(\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\)
add up all those small gains from \(t_1\) to \(t_2\).
\(v(t)\,\mathrm dt\)
the small distance covered in one short moment; adding them gives the distance.

Why integrate acceleration? Acceleration is the easiest of the three to measure. An accelerometer is a small mass sitting on a piezoelectric crystal (a crystal that produces a voltage when squeezed or sheared, with no power supply). When the structure accelerates, the crystal must push the mass with force \(F=m\ddot y\), and the voltage is proportional to that force, so it measures \(\ddot y\). Integrating a measured \(a(t)\) trace gives the velocity \(V=\int a\,\mathrm dt\). Our goal: if we know how acceleration, velocity or displacement change over time, we can model and predict the motion.

Worked example · car with \(v=0.9t^2+0.6t\)

A car moves in a straight line so that for a short time its velocity is \(v=(0.9t^2+0.6t)\u{m/s}\), \(t\) in s. Find its position and acceleration at \(t=3\u{s}\). At \(t=0\), \(s=0\).

Coordinate \(s\) from the fixed origin \(O\), positive to the right

  1. Coordinate system. \(s\) measured from the fixed origin \(O\), positive to the right. A position only means something once the origin and the positive direction are fixed; the answers below are measured from \(O\), to the right.
  2. Position (from \(v=\mathrm ds/\mathrm dt\), which links \(v\), \(s\), \(t\)): \[\begin{aligned}&\textstyle\int_0^s\mathrm ds=\int_0^t(0.9t^2+0.6t)\,\mathrm dt\\ &\Rightarrow\ s=0.3t^3+0.3t^2.\end{aligned}\] At \(t=3\): \(s=0.3(27)+0.3(9)=\boxed{10.8\u{m}}\).
  3. Acceleration (from \(a=\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt\), which links \(a\), \(v\), \(t\)): \(a=1.8t+0.6\). At \(t=3\): \(a=\boxed{6.0\u{m/s^2}}\) (to the right). Acceleration is the slope of the \(v\)–\(t\) graph, so here you differentiate; position is an area, so above you integrated.

Recipe: step 1, integrate or differentiate \(v(t)\) to get formulas for \(s(t)\) or \(a(t)\); step 2, put in the time to get the numbers.

You can now: get \(s(t)\) and \(a(t)\) from a given \(v(t)\) by integrating and differentiating, with the start conditions as limits; explain why integrating a measured acceleration gives the velocity.

5When acceleration depends on velocity or position: \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\) ~5 min

What is this?

Take your foot off the pedal and let the car coast. Air drag slows it hard at high speed and gently at low speed: the deceleration depends on how fast you are going, not on the clock. You cannot integrate over time directly, because you do not yet know the speed at each time. The trick is to measure progress by the speed itself, or by the distance covered, instead of by the clock.

Air drag makes the deceleration depend on speed, not on time. Then \(a=\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt\) can still be used for time (separate the variables), and for distance the chain rule gives the key trick:

Result · chain rule \[\begin{gathered}a=\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm ds}\cdot\frac{\mathrm ds}{\mathrm dt}=v\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm ds}\\ \Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds}\end{gathered}\]

In words: whenever the formula for \(a\) has no "time" in it (it depends on \(v\) or \(s\)), use \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\) to get distance, and \(\mathrm dt=\mathrm dv/a\) to get time.

Reading the formula
\(v\)
the car's speed right now.
\(\mathrm ds\)
a short stretch of road. The car takes time \(\mathrm ds/v\) to cover it.
\(a\,\mathrm ds\)
acceleration times that stretch; divided by \(v\) it is the speed gained over the stretch, \(\mathrm dv\).
\(v\,\mathrm dv\)
the change in \(\tfrac12v^2\). Integrated with constant \(a\) it gives the suvat formula \(v^2=v_0^2+2a_c(s-s_0)\).

Choosing the relation:

  1. Do: Look at what the given \(a\) depends on: \(t\), \(v\) or \(s\). You can only integrate with respect to a variable that appears in the formula, so this decides which differential to pair it with.
  2. Do: If \(a=a(t)\): integrate \(\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm dt\), then \(\mathrm ds=v\,\mathrm dt\) (Section 4). The clock is the input, so add up over time.
  3. Do: If \(a\) depends on \(v\) and you want a time: separate, \(\mathrm dt=\mathrm dv/a\), and integrate with matching limits. Each small speed change \(\mathrm dv\) takes time \(\mathrm dv/a\); adding these gives the time.
  4. Do: If \(a\) depends on \(v\) or \(s\) and you want a distance (or a speed at a given distance): use \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\), separate and integrate. Time never appears, so you never need the unknown \(v(t)\).
Definition · separating variables

Putting everything with \(v\) on one side and everything with \(t\) (or \(s\)) on the other, then integrating each side between matching limits. It works for \(\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt=f(v)\): \(\int\mathrm dv/f(v)=\int\mathrm dt\).

Worked example · F-117 with a drag parachute

After deploying its drag parachute, an aeroplane has acceleration \(a=-0.04v^2\u{m/s^2}\) (\(v\) in m/s). Air drag, and hence deceleration, is normally proportional to \(v^2\). (a) Find the time for \(v\) to fall from 80 m/s to 10 m/s. (b) What distance does the plane cover in that time?

FBD (horizontal): drag \(D=0.04mv^2\) opposes the motion

(a) Time. A time is asked and \(a\) contains only \(v\): step 3 above. \(\dfrac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=-0.04v^2\Rightarrow\displaystyle\int_{80}^{10}v^{-2}\,\mathrm dv=-0.04\int_0^t\mathrm dt\). Using \(\int v^{-2}\mathrm dv=-v^{-1}\):

\[\begin{aligned}&\Big[-\tfrac1v\Big]_{80}^{10}=-0.04t\\ &\Rightarrow\ t=\frac{1/10-1/80}{0.04}=\boxed{2.19\u{s}}.\end{aligned}\]

(b) Distance (tip: \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\)). \(v\,\mathrm dv=-0.04v^2\,\mathrm ds\Rightarrow\displaystyle\int_{80}^{10}\frac{\mathrm dv}{v}=-0.04\int_0^s\mathrm ds\). Using \(\int v^{-1}\mathrm dv=\ln v\):

\[\ln\tfrac{10}{80}=-0.04s\ \Rightarrow\ s=\tfrac{\ln8}{0.04}=\boxed{52.0\u{m}}.\]

How long to stop completely? Mathematically, putting \(v=0\) gives \(t=\infty\) and \(s=\infty\). Is that correct? No. The law \(a\propto-v^2\) only holds at high speed. At low speed the drag becomes proportional to \(v\) (\(a\propto-v\)) and other forces (rolling resistance, brakes) dominate, so the plane does stop. See Section 11.

Worked example · \(a=-cv^3\)

A point moves along a straight line with \(a=-cv^3\) (\(c\) a constant). If its velocity is \(v_0\), what distance does it travel before its velocity falls to \(v_0/2\)? How much time does it take? Hint: no "time" in \(a\), so use \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\).

\[\begin{aligned}&\text{distance: } v\,\mathrm dv=-cv^3\,\mathrm ds\\ &\Rightarrow\ \int_{v_0}^{v_0/2}\frac{\mathrm dv}{v^2}=-c\int_0^s\mathrm ds\\ &\Rightarrow\ \Big[-\tfrac1v\Big]_{v_0}^{v_0/2}=-\tfrac1{v_0}=-cs\\ &\Rightarrow\ \boxed{s=\frac1{cv_0}};\\ &\text{time: } \frac{\mathrm dv}{v^3}=-c\,\mathrm dt\\ &\Rightarrow\ \Big[-\tfrac1{2v^2}\Big]_{v_0}^{v_0/2}=-\tfrac{2}{v_0^2}+\tfrac{1}{2v_0^2}=-ct\\ &\Rightarrow\ \boxed{t=\frac{3}{2cv_0^2}}.\end{aligned}\]

Dimension check: \(a=-cv^3\) means \(c\) has units s²/m² (so \(cv^3\) is m/s²). Then \(1/(cv_0)\) has units m ✓ and \(1/(cv_0^2)\) has units s ✓.

You can now: choose between \(\mathrm dt=\mathrm dv/a\) and \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\) from what \(a\) depends on; separate variables with matching limits; find stopping times and distances under drag; say when a drag model stops being valid.

6Newton's second law and frames of reference ~4 min

What is this?

Push a car that has run out of fuel. The harder the net push, the faster its velocity changes; a heavier car changes more slowly for the same push. That is \(F=ma\): force is what changes motion, not what keeps it going.

Law · Newton's second law

The total force on a particle equals the rate of change of its linear momentum (mass × velocity):

\[\begin{gathered}\vb F=\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(m\vb V\big);\\ \text{if } m \text{ is constant: } \vb F=m\frac{\mathrm d\vb V}{\mathrm dt}=m\vb a.\end{gathered}\]

In words: net force = mass × acceleration, provided the mass does not change.

Reading the formula
\(\vb F\)
the vector sum of all the real forces on the body: the net push.
\(m\vb V\)
the momentum: how much motion the body carries.
\(\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\)
how fast that momentum changes. The net force sets this rate.
\(m\vb a\)
the same thing when the mass is fixed, as for a car (but not a rocket burning fuel).

Two important points when applying it:

  • It is only valid in an inertial frame. When analysing motion in a non-inertial (accelerating) frame you must add an "inertial force", e.g. centrifugal force.
  • In some problems the mass of the moving object changes with time (a rocket burning fuel), and then \(F\ne ma\). That is D3.
What is this? (frames of reference)

Hang a ball on a string from a car's mirror. When the car brakes hard, the ball swings forward, yet nothing pushed it forward. Someone on the pavement sees what really happens: the ball simply kept going while the car slowed beneath it. The pavement is an inertial frame; the braking car is not. To make \(F=ma\) work inside the car you add an imagined forward force, mass × the car's deceleration.

Definitions · frames of reference
  • A frame of reference is the set of axes (and the observer) from which motion is measured.
  • An inertial frame is one that is not accelerating: fixed to the ground is good enough for this course. Newton's laws hold in it as stated.
  • A non-inertial frame accelerates or rotates (fixed to a braking car, a lift starting up, a turning car).
  • A fictitious (inertial) force \(\vb F'=-m\vb a_{\rm frame}\) is an imaginary force added in a non-inertial frame so that "\(\sum\vb F=m\vb a'\)" works there. It has no partner in Newton's third law: nothing pushes it.
Worked example · standing in a lift (reminder from ENGG1300/ENGG1000)

You stand in a lift that starts to move up with acceleration \(a\) relative to the ground. (1) Find the normal force the floor exerts on you. (2) Evaluate Newton's second law in a frame fixed to the lift.

  1. Ground (inertial) frame, up positive: \(\sum F=N-mg=ma\Rightarrow\boxed{N=mg+ma}\). You feel heavier.
  2. Lift frame (axes \(x',y'\) moving with the lift): you are at rest relative to it, \(v'=0\), \(a'=0\). But \(\sum F=N-mg=ma\ne0\), so "\(\sum F=ma'\)" fails: the lift frame is not inertial (it accelerates at \(a\)).
  3. Fix it with a fictitious force \(F'=-ma\) (downward): \(\sum F=N-mg+F'=mg+ma-mg-ma=0=ma'\) ✓. The fictitious force makes Newton's law work in the non-inertial frame. The fictitious force is always \(-m\vb a_{\rm frame}\): the lift accelerates up, so it points down, opposite to the frame's acceleration.

You can now: state Newton's second law in momentum form and when it reduces to \(F=ma\); tell an inertial frame from a non-inertial one; add the fictitious force \(-m\vb a_{\rm frame}\) to work in an accelerating frame.

7The problem-solving recipe and a string in an accelerating car ~3 min

What is this?

Hang a ball on a string from the roof of a car. As the car speeds up, the ball swings back and settles at a steady angle. Why that angle? The string must pull the ball forward hard enough to give it the car's acceleration, while still holding up its weight. The six-step recipe turns that sentence into equations, and it works for every problem in this chapter.

Recipe · solving mechanics problems with Newton's laws
  1. Do: Understand and draw. Read carefully; sketch the situation with all moving objects and surfaces. You must see which way the car, and so the ball, accelerates before you can choose axes.
  2. Do: Choose a coordinate system. Standard \(x,y\) for each object; line one axis up with the motion or the acceleration if you can. With \(x\) along the car's acceleration, the ball has \(a_x=A\) and \(a_y=0\), so each equation has a simple right-hand side. On a bend use n–t axes instead (Section 9).
  3. Do: Free-body diagram. Isolate each object; draw all real forces (gravity, normal force, tension, friction, drag) acting on it. Only real pushes and pulls change motion: for the ball, just its weight and the string tension. The acceleration is drawn apart, as a green arrow, because it is the result, not a force.
  4. Do: Break forces into components along your axes (sines and cosines). Newton's law holds separately in each direction, so every force must be split along the same axes as the acceleration.
  5. Do: Apply Newton's second law separately on each axis: \(\sum F_x=ma_x\), \(\sum F_y=ma_y\). Set the acceleration to zero in a direction where the object does not move. The result is the equation of motion. Forward, the string's pull is the only thing accelerating the ball; upward, it must exactly cancel the weight because the ball neither rises nor falls.
  6. Do: Solve: plug in the given conditions (time, distance, speed), integrate if needed, and check that the answer makes physical sense. Limit checks, such as \(A=0\) giving a vertical string with \(T=mg\), catch sign and trigonometry slips.
Worked example · weight hanging in an accelerating car

A small weight of mass \(m\) hangs from a string in a car that accelerates at rate \(A\). What is the steady angle of the string from the vertical, and what is its tension? Analyse it both in the inertial (ground) frame and in a frame accelerating with the car.

Inertial frame (\(x\) forward, \(y\) up). The weight swings back until the string, at angle \(\theta\) from the vertical, pulls it forward hard enough to accelerate it with the car (\(a_x=A\), \(a_y=0\)):

\[\begin{aligned}\textstyle\sum F_x=ma_x:&\quad T\sin\theta=mA,\\ \textstyle\sum F_y=ma_y:&\quad T\cos\theta-mg=0.\end{aligned}\]

Divide, then square and add: Dividing removes \(T\) and leaves only the angle; squaring and adding uses \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\) to remove \(\theta\).

\[\boxed{\tan\theta=\frac Ag},\quad \boxed{T=m\sqrt{g^2+A^2}}.\]

Car frame. The weight is at rest relative to the car (\(a'=0\)), so add the fictitious force \(F'=mA\) backward. Now it is a statics problem: \(T\sin\theta-mA=0\), \(T\cos\theta-mg=0\), the same answers.

In words: the string hangs along the "effective gravity", the vector sum of real gravity and the backward fictitious force. This is how an accelerometer can be as simple as a pendulum. Limits: \(A=0\) gives \(\theta=0\), \(T=mg\) ✓; very large \(A\) gives \(\theta\to90^\circ\).

You can now: follow the six-step recipe from sketch to equation of motion; draw an FBD with real forces only; find the angle and tension of a string in an accelerating car in both the ground frame and the car frame.

8Example: the parachute opens ~3 min

What is this?

A sky diver is falling fast when his parachute opens. Suddenly the drag is large, and it depends on his speed, so the net force changes as his speed changes. It is the recipe of Section 7 in one direction: draw the forces, write \(\sum F=ma\), and because the force depends on speed, integrate with \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\).

Worked example · sky diver as the parachute opens

A sky diver and his parachute weigh 890 N. He is falling vertically at 30.5 m/s when his parachute opens. With the parachute open, the magnitude of the drag force (in newtons) is \(0.5v^2\). (a) What is the magnitude of his acceleration at the instant the parachute opens? (b) What is the magnitude of his velocity when he has descended 6.1 m from the point where the parachute opens?

FBD, downward positive

Mass: \(m=890/9.81=90.72\u{kg}\). Downward positive: This is the chapter's falling convention: the weight enters as \(+mg\), and drag, which opposes the downward velocity, as \(-0.5v^2\).

\[m\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=mg-0.5v^2.\]

(a) At \(v=30.5\u{m/s}\): drag \(=0.5(30.5)^2=465.1\u{N}\).

\[a=\frac{890-465.1}{90.72}=\boxed{4.68\u{m/s^2}}\ (\downarrow).\]

Sense check: his terminal speed is where \(0.5v^2=890\), i.e. \(v_T=\sqrt{1780}=42.2\u{m/s}\). He is slower than that, so the weight still wins and he keeps accelerating, but less than \(g\).

(b) No time in the force law, so use \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\), with \(k=0.5\u{N\,s^2/m^2}\):

\[\begin{aligned}v\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm ds}&=g-\frac km v^2\\ \Rightarrow\ &\int_{30.5}^{v}\frac{v\,\mathrm dv}{g-\frac km v^2}=\int_0^{6.1}\mathrm ds\\ \Rightarrow\ v^2&=v_T^2+\big(v_0^2-v_T^2\big)\,e^{-2ks/m}\\ &=1780+(930.25-1780)\\ &\qquad\times e^{-2(0.5)(6.1)/90.72}\\ &=1780-849.75(0.9350)\\ &=985.5\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2},\\ v&=\boxed{31.4\u{m/s}}.\end{aligned}\]
Going further · the \(v^2(s)\) formula

Substitute \(w=v^2\), so \(v\,\mathrm dv=\tfrac12\mathrm dw\): \(\tfrac12\frac{\mathrm dw}{\mathrm ds}=g-\frac kmw\), a first-order linear ODE (MATH1851) with solution

\[\begin{gathered}v^2=v_T^2+\big(v_0^2-v_T^2\big)\,e^{-2ks/m},\\ v_T^2=\frac{mg}{k}.\end{gathered}\]
Reading the formula
\(v_T^2\)
where the speed squared ends up: the terminal speed, where drag equals weight.
\(v_0^2-v_T^2\)
the starting gap. Negative here: he starts slower than terminal, so he still speeds up.
\(e^{-2ks/m}\)
the gap shrinks with every metre fallen, by a factor \(e\) every \(m/(2k)\) metres.

In words: for drag \(kv^2\), the square of the speed approaches \(v_T^2\) exponentially with distance, with "decay length" \(m/(2k)\). It reappears in Section 14 and in Dec 2021 B3(c).

You can now: write the equation of motion for a falling body with \(kv^2\) drag (downward positive); find the acceleration at the moment a parachute opens; find the speed after a given distance with \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\).

9Curvilinear motion: normal–tangential and polar coordinates ~8 min

What is this?

Drive a car round a bend. Even with the speedometer steady, you feel pushed sideways: the velocity keeps changing direction, and that takes an acceleration towards the inside of the bend. Curved motion is easiest to describe with axes that travel with the car: one pointing along the road, one pointing into the bend.

Now the particle moves along a curve: a car going round a hairpin bend, or a satellite in orbit (orbits are no longer examined). We still ignore the body's size. The main idea: choose the coordinate system that suits the problem (a reminder from ENGG1300/ENGG1000).

How to choose:

  1. Do: Straight-line motion, or a body whose acceleration has a fixed direction (the ball on a string in a car speeding up on a straight road): use Cartesian \(x,y\), with one axis along the acceleration. Then one equation has \(ma\) on the right and the other has zero.
  2. Do: Motion along a known path (a car on a bend or over a hill): use n–t, with \(\e{t}\) along the velocity and \(\e{n}\) towards the centre of curvature. Changes of speed then appear only in the \(t\) equation, and turning only in the \(n\) equation, as \(mv^2/\rho\).
  3. Do: Motion described by a distance and an angle from a fixed point (a ball whirled on a string, perhaps while the string is pulled in): use polar \(r,\theta\). The string length \(r\) and its direction \(\theta\) are exactly what you know or control.
  4. Do: On a banked track, where the acceleration is horizontal, use vertical and horizontal-towards-centre axes, not axes along the slope. The acceleration \(v^2/R\) is horizontal, so it then sits in one equation only.

Normal and tangential (n–t) coordinates

What is this? (n–t axes)

Freeze the car at one instant on the bend. One arrow points straight ahead along the road: that is \(\e{t}\). The other points sideways into the bend, towards the centre of the circle that best fits the road at that spot: that is \(\e{n}\). The pedal and the brake change the speed along \(\e{t}\); the steering wheel bends the velocity towards \(\e{n}\).

Definitions · n–t components
  • \(\e{t}\) (also written \(\vb u_t\)): unit vector tangent to the path, pointing along the velocity.
  • \(\e{n}\) (\(\vb u_n\)): unit vector perpendicular to \(\e{t}\), pointing towards the inside of the curve, i.e. towards the centre of curvature.
  • Radius of curvature \(\rho\): the radius of the circle that best fits the path at that point (the dashed circle). A gentle bend has a large \(\rho\); a straight line has \(\rho=\infty\).
\[\begin{aligned}\vb v&=v\,\e{t},\\ \vb a&=\frac{\mathrm d\vb v}{\mathrm dt}=\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}}_{a_t}\e{t}+\underbrace{\frac{v^2}{\rho}}_{a_n}\e{n}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the velocity is always along the path. The acceleration has two parts: \(a_t\) along the path changes the speed; \(a_n=v^2/\rho\) towards the centre changes the direction. The acceleration can have both components at once.

Reading the formula
\(\dfrac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}\)
how fast the speedometer reading changes: pedal or brake.
\(v^2\)
double the speed and the sideways acceleration is four times as big.
\(\rho\)
the radius of the best-fitting circle: a tighter bend (smaller \(\rho\)) needs more sideways acceleration.
\(\e{n}\)
always into the bend, whichever way the road turns.

For a path given as \(y(x)\), the radius of curvature is

\[\rho=\frac{\big[1+(\mathrm dy/\mathrm dx)^2\big]^{3/2}}{\big|\mathrm d^2y/\mathrm dx^2\big|}.\]
Reading the formula
\(\mathrm d^2y/\mathrm dx^2\)
how fast the slope of the road changes: how sharply it bends.
\(\mathrm dy/\mathrm dx\)
the slope of the road at that point.
\([1+(\mathrm dy/\mathrm dx)^2]^{3/2}\)
a correction for a tilted road: the same \(y''\) on a steep part means a gentler bend along the road itself.
\(|\cdots|\)
\(\rho\) is a length, so it is always positive; \(\e{n}\) tells you which side the centre is on.

In words: a curve bends sharply (small \(\rho\)) where its second derivative is large and its slope is small. Check: for a circle of radius \(R\) at its lowest point, slope 0 and \(|y''|=1/R\), so \(\rho=R\).

Polar coordinates (2D)

What is this? (polar coordinates)

Whirl a ball on a string round your hand. You can describe it by how long the string is (\(r\)) and which way it points (\(\theta\)). Even at a fixed length, the string must pull inward to keep the ball turning. If you also pull the string in while the ball goes round, its distance changes as it turns, and an extra sideways term appears.

Definitions · polar components

Position by distance \(r\) from a fixed origin \(O\) and angle \(\theta\) (in radians). \(\vb u_r\) points radially outward; \(\vb u_\theta\) is perpendicular to it, in the direction of increasing \(\theta\). \(\omega=\dot\theta\) is the angular velocity and \(\alpha=\dot\omega\) the angular acceleration.

\[\begin{aligned}\vb r&=r\,\vb u_r,\\ \vb v&=\dot r\,\vb u_r+r\omega\,\vb u_\theta,\\ \vb a&=\big(\ddot r-r\omega^2\big)\vb u_r\\ &\quad+\big(r\alpha+2\dot r\omega\big)\vb u_\theta.\end{aligned}\]

In words: radial acceleration = (rate of change of radial speed) − (centripetal term \(r\omega^2\)); transverse acceleration = (angular acceleration × radius, the "Euler" term) + (\(2\dot r\omega\), the Coriolis term, which appears when the distance from \(O\) changes while turning).

Reading the formula
\(\ddot r\)
the string being let out or pulled in faster and faster.
\(-r\omega^2\)
the inward acceleration needed just to keep the ball going round; the minus sign means towards \(O\), since \(\vb u_r\) points outward.
\(r\alpha\)
the ball being spun up (or slowed) around the circle.
\(2\dot r\omega\)
the Coriolis term: present only when the string length changes while the ball turns.

Newton's second law in n–t form

Result · equations of motion on a curve \[\begin{gathered}\textstyle\sum F_t\,\e{t}+\sum F_n\,\e{n}=m\big(a_t\e{t}+a_n\e{n}\big),\\ a_t=\dfrac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt},\quad \boxed{a_n=\dfrac{v^2}{\rho}}.\end{gathered}\]

So \(\sum F_t=m\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt\) and \(\sum F_n=mv^2/\rho\). Often there is no motion perpendicular to the plane of \(\e{t}\), \(\e{n}\), so the forces in that third direction balance. For rotation at constant radius \(R\):

\[v=\omega R,\qquad a_n=\frac{v^2}{R}=\omega^2R.\]

In words: to move on a curve, the net force must have a part pointing to the inside of the bend, equal to mass × \(v^2/\rho\). The normal component is the centripetal ("centre-seeking") acceleration.

Reading the formula
\(\sum F_t\)
the real forces along the road (driving force, friction, drag, a slope component): they change the speed.
\(\sum F_n\)
the real forces into the bend (friction, tension, a tilted normal force).
\(mv^2/\rho\)
what \(\sum F_n\) must add up to. It is a result to be supplied, not one more force to draw.
Watch

\(m v^2/\rho\) is not an extra force to add to the FBD in the ground frame. It is what the real forces (friction, normal force, tension, gravity) must add up to. Only in the non-inertial frame of the moving body do you add the outward centrifugal force \(mv^2/\rho\) (orange) and then use equilibrium.

Use the six-step recipe of Section 7 for every problem below.

You can now: choose Cartesian, n–t or polar coordinates to suit a problem; split acceleration into \(a_t=\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt\) and \(a_n=v^2/\rho\) towards the centre of curvature; find \(\rho\) for a path \(y(x)\); write the polar acceleration components; apply \(\sum F_n=mv^2/\rho\) with real forces only.

10Curves in practice: space station, off-ramp, maglev, banking ~7 min

What is this?

In every example here, some real force must supply the inward pull \(mv^2/R\). For a ball whirled on a string it is the tension. On a flat off-ramp only tyre friction can do it, and friction has a limit, so there is a top speed. Tilt (bank) the road and the road's push leans inward and helps. In a rotating space station the floor pushes the astronaut inward, and that push feels like weight.

Worked example · rotating space station

Future space stations may rotate to give their inhabitants simulated gravity. If the distance from the rotation axis to the occupied outer ring is \(R=100\u{m}\), what rotation rate gives one half of Earth's gravity?

Inertial frame. The only force on the astronaut is the floor's normal force \(N\), pointing towards the axis; it supplies the centripetal acceleration: \(N=m\omega^2R\). "Half gravity" means the floor pushes as hard as it would on Earth at \(g/2\): \(N=m g/2\).

\[\begin{aligned}\omega^2R&=\tfrac12g\ \Rightarrow\ \omega=\sqrt{\frac{g}{2R}}=\sqrt{\frac{9.81}{200}}\\ &=\boxed{0.221\u{rad/s}}=2.12\u{rev/min}.\end{aligned}\]

Non-inertial (rotating) frame. The astronaut is at rest, so add the outward centrifugal force \(m\omega^2R\); equilibrium \(N-m\omega^2R=0\) gives the same answer. To the astronaut, the centrifugal force feels exactly like weight pulling them onto the floor.

Worked example · freeway off-ramp

A preliminary design for a freeway off-ramp is circular with radius \(R=60\u{m}\) (flat, not banked). If the coefficient of static friction between tyres and road is at least \(\mu_s=0.4\), what is the maximum speed at which vehicles can enter the ramp without losing traction? (friction \(\le\mu N\))

Friction is the only horizontal force, so it alone supplies the inward pull; the most it can give is \(\mu_sN\).

\[\begin{aligned}\uparrow:&\quad N-mg=0\ \Rightarrow\ N=mg,\\ \text{to centre:}&\quad f=m\frac{v^2}{R}\ \le\ \mu_sN=\mu_smg\\ \Rightarrow\ v_{\max}&=\sqrt{\mu_sgR}=\sqrt{0.4(9.81)(60)}\\ &=\boxed{15.3\u{m/s}}\ (55\u{km/h}).\end{aligned}\]

Centrifugal force felt by the passengers. In the car's (non-inertial) frame each passenger feels an outward push \(mv^2/R\): at 15.3 m/s that is \(0.4\,mg\), 40% of their weight, sideways. It scales with \(v^2\): double the speed, four times the sideways push. Note the answer does not depend on the car's mass.

Worked example · maglev train on a banked track

An experimental magnetically levitated train is supported by magnetic repulsion forces \(M\) normal to the track. Sideways motion is prevented by lateral supports (force \(S\)). The 20 000 kg train travels at 30 m/s on a circular track of radius \(R=150\u{m}\) banked at 40°. Find \(M\) and \(S\).

Front view, centre of the curve to the left

Use vertical (no motion) and horizontal-towards-centre (\(a_n=v^2/R\)) directions. The train moves on a horizontal circle, so all its acceleration is horizontal and towards the centre; vertically it has none. \(M\) is perpendicular to the track (up and towards the centre); \(S\) acts along the track surface, up the slope (up and away from the centre):

\[\begin{aligned}\uparrow:&\ M\cos40^\circ+S\sin40^\circ=mg\ \ (1)\\ \leftarrow:&\ M\sin40^\circ-S\cos40^\circ=m\tfrac{v^2}{R}\ \ (2)\end{aligned}\]

with \(mg=196.2\u{kN}\) and \(mv^2/R=20\,000(900)/150=120\u{kN}\). Solving (multiply (1) by \(\cos40^\circ\), (2) by \(\sin40^\circ\) and add, etc.):

\[\begin{aligned}M&=mg\cos40^\circ+m\tfrac{v^2}{R}\sin40^\circ\\ &=\boxed{227.4\u{kN}},\\ S&=mg\sin40^\circ-m\tfrac{v^2}{R}\cos40^\circ\\ &=\boxed{34.2\u{kN}}.\end{aligned}\]

Note: axes normal and tangential to the track surface would make this harder, because the acceleration (horizontal) would then have components along both. Choose axes along the acceleration. \(S\gt0\): at 30 m/s the train is too slow for this bank and the lateral supports stop it sliding down-slope; with no lateral force needed, \(\tan\theta=v^2/(gR)\) would require 31.5°.

Going further · banked curves with and without friction, leaning

Exam questions (Dec 2024 B1, Dec 2023 B1) ask these, so here they are in general. A car (mass \(m\)) goes round a curve of radius \(R\) banked at \(\theta\), at speed \(v\).

No friction needed (normal force alone provides \(a_n\)): vertical \(N\cos\theta=mg\), towards centre \(N\sin\theta=mv^2/R\). Divide:

\[\tan\theta=\frac{v^2}{gR}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ v_0=\sqrt{gR\tan\theta}.\]
Reading the formula
\(v^2/R\)
the inward acceleration the car needs on this bend.
\(g\)
the weight the road must also hold up (per unit mass).
\(\tan\theta\)
the tilted normal force pushes inward and upward; its inward part ÷ upward part is \(\tan\theta\), and that ratio must equal \(v^2/(gR)\).
no \(m\)
mass cancels, so one bank angle suits every car at the design speed \(v_0\).

With friction \(f\) along the slope (positive down-slope), solving vertical and centre-ward equations:

\[\begin{aligned}f&=m\Big(\frac{v^2}{R}\cos\theta-g\sin\theta\Big),\\ N&=m\Big(g\cos\theta+\frac{v^2}{R}\sin\theta\Big).\end{aligned}\]

Faster than \(v_0\): friction acts down the slope (the car tends to slide up and out). Slower: friction acts up the slope. No slip while \(|f|\le\mu N\), giving

\[\begin{aligned}v_{\max}^2&=gR\frac{\sin\theta+\mu\cos\theta}{\cos\theta-\mu\sin\theta},\\ v_{\min}^2&=gR\frac{\sin\theta-\mu\cos\theta}{\cos\theta+\mu\sin\theta}.\end{aligned}\]

Leaning (cyclist, motorcyclist). On a flat road, friction \(f=mv^2/R\) (towards centre) and \(N=mg\) act at the tyre. For no tipping, their resultant must pass through the centre of mass, so the rider leans at angle \(\phi\) from the vertical with \(\tan\phi=f/N=v^2/(gR)\). Mass cancels: the lean angle does not depend on the rider's weight.

In words: the right bank angle and the right lean angle obey the same formula, \(\tan(\text{angle})=v^2/(gR)\): the surface (or the bike) is tilted so that the support force points along the "effective gravity" of real gravity plus centrifugal force.

Try it · car on a banked curve (Going further)

The friction arrow flips from down-slope to up-slope as the speed drops below the design speed \(v_0\). Set \(\theta=0\) for the flat off-ramp worked example (\(R=60\), \(\mu=0.4\): slips above 55 km/h).

You can now: say which real force supplies \(mv^2/R\) in each case; find the maximum speed on a flat bend from \(f\le\mu N\); resolve forces on a banked track vertically and towards the centre; find the friction-free bank angle and a lean angle from \(\tan(\text{angle})=v^2/(gR)\).

11Fluid drag and terminal velocity ~4 min

What is this?

Drop a ball from a tall building. At first the air barely resists, so it speeds up at about \(g\). The faster it goes, the harder the air pushes back, so the net force shrinks. Eventually drag equals weight: the ball stops speeding up and falls at a steady terminal speed.

What is this? (laminar and turbulent)

A dust grain drifting down through still air moves so slowly that the air slides past it in smooth layers: that is laminar flow, and doubling the speed doubles the drag. A car at motorway speed leaves a swirling wake of eddies behind it: that is turbulent flow, and doubling the speed quadruples the drag.

Definitions · drag, laminar, turbulent
  • Drag: the force a fluid (air, water) exerts on a body moving through it, opposite to the body's velocity. It depends on velocity in a complicated way.
  • Laminar flow: at low speed (or for tiny bodies), the fluid "creeps" past in orderly layers. Drag is proportional to speed: \(\text{Drag}=C_fV\).
  • Turbulent flow: at higher speed the flow loses stability and becomes chaotic with eddies (Fluid Mechanics, years 2/3). Drag is proportional to speed squared: \(\text{drag}=C_D\rho V^2A\), with \(\rho\) the fluid density and \(A\) the frontal area. (The coefficient cannot be worked out from theory for most shapes: it must be measured.)
  • \(C_f\) and \(C_D\) are constants for a given body and fluid.

For a body falling in a uniform fluid, with downward positive:

\[m\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}=mg-\text{Drag}\quad\text{(1)}\]
Reading the formula
\(mg\)
the weight: the same at every speed, positive because down is positive.
\(-\text{Drag}\)
the air pushing back, opposite to the downward velocity, and growing with speed.
\(m\,\mathrm dV/\mathrm dt\)
what is left over to speed the ball up. It falls to zero when drag has grown to equal the weight.
Definition · terminal velocity

When the drag has grown to balance the weight, \(mg=\text{drag}\), there is no more acceleration and the body falls at a constant speed, the terminal velocity \(V_T\).

(1) released; (2) drag grows with speed; (3) drag = weight at \(V_T\)

Starting from rest, the speed rises quickly at first and then levels off at \(V_T\) (the curve flattens because the net force shrinks as drag grows). What if the body starts faster than \(V_T\)? Then drag exceeds weight, the net force points up, and it slows down towards \(V_T\) from above. This is what happens to a satellite coming back from orbit into the atmosphere at high speed.

Recipe · falling against drag
  1. Do: Terminal velocity: set \(a=0\), so the net force is zero: \(mg-\text{drag}=0\); rearrange for \(V_T\). At terminal speed the ball no longer speeds up, so the forces on it must balance.
  2. Do: Full motion as a function of time: write and solve the ODE \(mg-\text{drag}=m\,\mathrm dV/\mathrm dt\) (or \(m\,\mathrm dV/\mathrm dt-mg+\text{drag}=0\)). The answer differs for laminar and turbulent drag. Before terminal speed the drag changes with speed, so the acceleration changes too; the ODE follows this moment by moment.

You can now: explain terminal velocity as drag = weight; tell laminar (\(\propto V\)) from turbulent (\(\propto V^2\)) drag; write the equation of motion of a falling body with downward positive; describe what happens when a body starts faster than \(V_T\).

12Laminar drag ~3 min

What is this?

A tiny water droplet in air is the laminar case: the air flows round it smoothly and the drag grows in proportion to speed. Its weight grows with the cube of its size, but the drag only in proportion to its size, so small drops fall far more slowly than big ones.

(1) Terminal velocity. For a perfect sphere of diameter \(d\) in a fluid of viscosity \(\mu\) (the fluid's "stickiness", unit kg/(m·s) = Pa·s):

\[\begin{gathered}\text{Drag}=C_fV,\quad C_f=3\pi\mu d\ \ (\text{Stokes}),\\ C_fV_T=mg\ \Rightarrow\ V_T=\frac{mg}{C_f}.\end{gathered}\]

With \(m=\rho_{\rm sphere}\tfrac16\pi d^3\):

Result · laminar terminal velocity of a sphere \[V_T^{\rm laminar}=\frac{\rho_{\rm sphere}\,g\,d^2}{18\mu}\quad\text{(2)}\]
Reading the formula
\(\rho_{\rm sphere}\,g\)
a denser ball is heavier for its size, so it falls faster.
\(d^2\)
weight \(\propto d^3\) divided by Stokes drag \(\propto d\).
\(\mu\)
a stickier fluid holds the ball back more, so it falls slower.
\(18\)
from \(\tfrac16\pi\) in the volume and \(3\pi\) in the drag: \(3\pi\div\tfrac16\pi=18\).

In words: terminal speed grows with the square of the diameter: a sphere ten times smaller falls a hundred times slower.

Worked example · raindrop 0.2 mm across

With \(\mu=1.5\times10^{-5}\u{kg/(m\,s)}\) (air) and \(\rho_{\rm water}=1000\u{kg/m^3}\):

\[\begin{aligned}V_T&=\frac{1000(9.81)(0.2\times10^{-3})^2}{18(1.5\times10^{-5})}\\ &=\boxed{1.45\u{m/s}}.\end{aligned}\]

For large raindrops (2 mm across) the drag is proportional to \(V^2\) and this result is not suitable: use Section 13.

Units: (kg/m³)(m/s²)(m²)/(kg/(m·s)) = m/s ✓.

(2) The full motion. Eq (1) with laminar drag is a standard inhomogeneous, first-order, linear ODE:

\[\begin{gathered}m\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}+C_fV=mg\\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}+\frac{C_f}{m}V=g.\end{gathered}\]

The standard form \(\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dt}+py=q\) has solution \(y(t)=e^{-pt}\big[\int qe^{pt}\mathrm dt+\text{const}\big]\) (integrating factor). Here \(p=C_f/m=g/V_T\), \(q=g\): From \(V_T=mg/C_f\), \(C_f/m=g/V_T\): writing \(p\) this way shows the time scale \(V_T/g\).

\[\begin{aligned}V(t)&=e^{-C_ft/m}\Big[\int ge^{C_ft/m}\mathrm dt+C\Big]\\ &=\frac{mg}{C_f}+Ce^{-C_ft/m}\\ &=V_T+Ce^{-gt/V_T}.\end{aligned}\]

With \(V(0)=V_0\): \(C=V_0-V_T\). Hence

Result · laminar fall \[\begin{gathered}\frac{V(t)}{V_T}=1+\Big(\frac{V_0}{V_T}-1\Big)e^{-gt/V_T}\\ \text{Eq (3)}\end{gathered}\]
Reading the formula
\(1\)
where the speed ends up: \(V=V_T\).
\(\dfrac{V_0}{V_T}-1\)
the starting gap: negative if the drop starts slower than \(V_T\), positive if faster.
\(e^{-gt/V_T}\)
the gap shrinks by a factor \(e\) every \(V_T/g\) seconds.

In words: the gap between the speed and the terminal speed shrinks exponentially, with time constant \(V_T/g\). After \(3V_T/g\) the speed is within 5% of \(V_T\).

You can now: find the laminar terminal velocity of a sphere from Stokes drag; explain why \(V_T\propto d^2\); solve the laminar equation of motion with an integrating factor and read the exponential approach to \(V_T\).

13Turbulent drag ~5 min

What is this?

A sky diver or a capsule under a parachute is the turbulent case: air tumbles in eddies behind it and drag grows with the square of the speed. Because drag climbs so steeply with speed, the faller reaches terminal speed sooner than in the laminar case. A bigger parachute means a slower landing.

Turbulent drag \(=C_D\rho V^2A\), where \(C_D\) (between 0 and 2) is dimensionless and \(A\) is the frontal area (the area the body presents to the flow).

Results · turbulent fall

(1) Terminal velocity from \(mg=\text{drag}\):

\[V_T^{\rm turbulent}=\sqrt{\frac{mg}{C_D\rho A}}\quad\text{(4)}\]
Reading the formula
\(mg\)
the weight the drag must balance.
\(C_D\)
the shape factor, measured, not calculated.
\(\rho\)
the density of the air (or other fluid): thicker fluid, lower terminal speed.
\(A\)
the frontal area, which the parachute designer controls.
\(\sqrt{\ }\)
because drag \(\propto V^2\): to land ten times slower you need a hundred times the area.

(2) Equation of motion: \(m\,\mathrm dV/\mathrm dt=mg-C_D\rho AV^2\), and dividing by \(m\) with Eq (4):

\[\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}=g\Big(1-\frac{V^2}{V_T^2}\Big)\quad\text{(5)}\]

Separating variables (partial fractions) gives the solution

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{gt}{V_T}&=\frac12\ln\left|\frac{V+V_T}{V-V_T}\right|\\ &\quad-\frac12\ln\left|\frac{V_0+V_T}{V_0-V_T}\right|\quad\text{(6)}\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{gathered}\frac{V}{V_T}=\frac{Ce^{2gt/V_T}-1}{Ce^{2gt/V_T}+1},\\ C=\frac{V_T+V_0}{V_T-V_0}\quad\text{(7)}\end{gathered}\]

In words: the speed again approaches \(V_T\), but because drag grows with \(V^2\) the approach is faster than in the laminar case (a tanh curve: for \(V_0=0\), \(V=V_T\tanh(gt/V_T)\)).

Going further · where Eq (6) comes from, and the ½

From Eq (5): \(\dfrac{\mathrm dV}{1-V^2/V_T^2}=g\,\mathrm dt\). Partial fractions: \(\dfrac{1}{1-V^2/V_T^2}=\dfrac{V_T}{2}\Big(\dfrac1{V_T+V}+\dfrac1{V_T-V}\Big)\), which integrates to \(\dfrac{V_T}2\ln\left|\dfrac{V_T+V}{V_T-V}\right|\). Evaluate between \(V_0\) and \(V\), divide by \(V_T\): Eq (6).

Most textbooks write turbulent drag as \(\tfrac12C_D\rho AV^2\). This course and several past papers (Dec 2016, May 2013) leave out the \(\tfrac12\); Dec 2023 includes it. The constant is just absorbed into \(C_D\): use the expression the question gives.

Worked example · spacecraft under a parachute

A spacecraft of mass \(m=5000\u{kg}\), \(C_D=1\), \(\rho=1\u{kg/m^3}\), frontal area \(A=20\u{m^2}\). Eq (4):

\[V_T=\sqrt{\frac{5000(9.81)}{1(1)(20)}}=\boxed{49.5\u{m/s}}.\]

Reducing \(V_T\) to 5 m/s (ten times less) requires a 100 times larger frontal area, because \(V_T\propto1/\sqrt A\).

Just after leaving the helicopter, drag ≈ 0

A sky diver of 100 kg just after jumping has only gravity, 1000 N, acting, so \(a=F_{\rm net}/m=1000/100=10\u{m/s^2}\) downward (taking \(g\approx10\)). The parachute area \(A\) is designed so that the terminal velocity is what the sky diver can tolerate on landing, normally \(V_T\approx3\text{–}5\u{m/s}\).

You can now: find the turbulent terminal velocity \(\sqrt{mg/(C_D\rho A)}\); write \(\mathrm dV/\mathrm dt=g(1-V^2/V_T^2)\); sketch and explain the laminar and turbulent \(V(t)\) curves from below and from above \(V_T\); use the drag expression the question gives.

14Drag exercises ~3 min

What is this?

Two exercises put the falling body to work: how long tiny droplets hang in a room, and how hard an opening parachute jerks a sky diver. Both start from the same two questions: where is the terminal speed, and how far from it is the body now?

Worked example · how long do droplets stay in the air?

The drag on small particles is \(3\pi\mu Vd\), with \(\mu=1.5\times10^{-5}\u{kg\,m^{-1}s^{-1}}\) the air viscosity and \(d\) the diameter. Estimate the time it takes water droplets of 1 µm and 10 µm diameter to fall 1 m.

FBD of a droplet at terminal velocity

Terminal velocity from Eq (2), \(V_T=\rho gd^2/(18\mu)\). The time to reach it, about \(V_T/g\), is microseconds, so the droplet falls the whole metre at \(V_T\): The droplet reaches terminal speed almost at once, so distance ÷ \(V_T\) gives the time with no need to solve the ODE.

\[\begin{aligned}&d=1\u{\mu m}:\\ &\ V_T=\frac{1000(9.81)(10^{-6})^2}{18(1.5\times10^{-5})}\\ &\quad\ \ =3.63\times10^{-5}\u{m/s},\\ &\ t=\frac{1\u{m}}{V_T}=\boxed{2.75\times10^{4}\u{s}\approx7.6\u{h}};\\ &d=10\u{\mu m}:\\ &\ V_T=3.63\times10^{-3}\u{m/s},\\ &\ t=\boxed{275\u{s}\approx4.6\u{min}}.\end{aligned}\]

Micro-droplets are "airborne". This is why small droplets matter for disease spread. Research on airborne transmission (Morawska & Cao, Environment International, 2020) shows that larger droplets carrying virus fall and deposit close to the person who emits them (droplet transmission), while smaller ones can travel metres or tens of metres in indoor air (aerosol transmission).

Worked example · sky diver, \(a=g-cv^2\)

A sky diver jumps from a helicopter and is falling straight down at 30 m/s when her parachute opens. From then on her downward acceleration is approximately \(a=g-cv^2\), where \(c\) is a constant. After an initial transient she descends at a nearly constant 5 m/s. (a) What is \(c\)? (b) What maximum acceleration or deceleration does she experience? (c) What is her downward speed when she has fallen 2 m from the point where the parachute opens?

FBD after opening, downward positive

(a) At the steady 5 m/s, \(a=0\): \(c=g/v_T^2=9.81/25=\boxed{0.392\u{m^{-1}}}\).

(b) \(|a|\) is largest when \(v\) is furthest from \(v_T\), i.e. at the moment of opening, \(v=30\u{m/s}\): The drag \(cv^2\) is largest at the highest speed, and the speed only falls from there, so the deceleration is greatest at the start.

\[a=9.81-0.392(30)^2=\boxed{-343\u{m/s^2}}.\]

(c) \(v\,\mathrm dv=(g-cv^2)\,\mathrm ds\) gives, as in Section 8, \(v^2=v_T^2+(v_0^2-v_T^2)e^{-2cs}\):

\[\begin{aligned}v^2&=25+(900-25)e^{-2(0.392)(2)}\\ &=25+875(0.208)=207.1\\ \Rightarrow\ v&=\boxed{14.4\u{m/s}}.\end{aligned}\]

Sense check: 35\(g\) is far more than a person can take; real parachutes open gradually, so \(c\) grows over a second or two instead of jumping.

You can now: estimate how long small droplets stay airborne from the laminar \(V_T\); find a drag constant from a given terminal speed; find the largest deceleration and the speed after a given distance for \(a=g-cv^2\).

16Common mistakes

✗Using \(v^2=v_0^2+2as\) or \(s=v_0t+\tfrac12at^2\) when \(a\) depends on \(v\), \(s\) or \(t\). ✓Those hold only for constant \(a\). Otherwise integrate: \(\mathrm dt=\mathrm dv/a\) or \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\). ✗Drawing "\(mv^2/R\)" as an extra force on the FBD in the ground frame. ✓In the inertial frame, real forces add up to \(mv^2/R\) towards the centre. The outward centrifugal force appears only in the car's (rotating) frame. ✗Resolving forces along and across a banked slope when the acceleration is horizontal. ✓Use vertical (\(\sum F=0\)) and horizontal-towards-centre (\(\sum F=mv^2/R\)) axes (Section 10). ✗Giving drag the same sign as gravity, or forgetting it reverses when the body moves the other way. ✓Drag always opposes the velocity. With downward positive: \(m\dot V=mg-\text{drag}\). ✗Forgetting the limits when separating variables (e.g. \(\int_0^t\) on one side, an indefinite integral on the other). ✓Matching limits on both sides: \(\int_{v_0}^{v}\dots=\int_0^t\dots\). ✗Concluding from \(a=-0.04v^2\) that the plane never stops, as a physical statement. ✓The model breaks down at low speed (drag becomes \(\propto v\), and other forces take over); see Section 5. ✗Thinking a heavier rider must lean more (or less) on a bend. ✓\(\tan\phi=v^2/(gR)\): mass cancels.

17Find the mistake

1 · The F-117 distance

"\(a=-0.04v^2\), \(v\) from 80 to 10 m/s. Average acceleration \(=-0.04\times\big(\tfrac{80+10}{2}\big)^2=-81\u{m/s^2}\). Then \(v^2=v_0^2+2as\) gives \(s=\dfrac{100-6400}{2(-81)}=38.9\u{m}\)."

Hint

Is the acceleration constant? Can you average it like that?

Answer

\(a\) changes with \(v\), so the constant-acceleration formula is invalid and "average \(v\), then square" is not the average acceleration either. Use \(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\): \(s=\ln 8/0.04=52.0\u{m}\) (Section 5).

2 · The off-ramp FBD

"Forces on the car going round the flat ramp: weight \(mg\) down, normal \(N\) up, friction \(f\) inwards, and the centripetal force \(mv^2/R\) inwards. Horizontal balance: \(f+mv^2/R=0\)."

Hint

Is the car in equilibrium horizontally? Is \(mv^2/R\) a force from anything?

Answer

There is no separate centripetal force: friction is what provides the centripetal acceleration. The correct equation is \(f=mv^2/R\) (net inward force = mass × \(a_n\)), with \(f\le\mu N\) (Section 10).

3 · Terminal speed in water

"A 2 mm steel ball sinks in oil with laminar drag. Its terminal speed is \(V_T=\sqrt{mg/(C_D\rho A)}\)."

Hint

Which drag law goes with laminar flow?

Answer

That formula is for turbulent drag (\(\propto V^2\)). Laminar drag \(C_fV=3\pi\mu dV\) gives \(V_T=mg/C_f=\rho_{\rm sphere}gd^2/(18\mu)\) (Section 12). (Strictly, buoyancy should also be subtracted in a liquid; it is neglected for air in Section 12.)

18Concept checks

1 · Which equation?

Acceleration is given as \(a=-kx\) (a spring). You want the speed at a given position. Which relation do you start from?

Answer

\(v\,\mathrm dv=a\,\mathrm ds\) (no time in \(a\)): \(\tfrac12(v^2-v_0^2)=-\tfrac12k(x^2-x_0^2)\).

2 · Constant speed, still accelerating?

A car goes round a roundabout at a steady 30 km/h. Is it accelerating? Which components of \(\vb a\) are non-zero?

Answer

Yes. \(a_t=\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt=0\), but \(a_n=v^2/R\ne0\): the direction of the velocity changes.

3 · Double the speed

On a flat bend, by what factor must the friction force grow if the speed doubles? What about the lean angle's tangent for a cyclist?

Answer

Both are \(\propto v^2\): four times the friction force, four times \(\tan\phi\).

4 · Starting faster than terminal

A body is thrown downward at \(2V_T\). Describe its speed afterwards and the sign of its acceleration.

Answer

Drag exceeds weight, so the acceleration points upward (it slows down), steeply at first, and the speed falls towards \(V_T\) from above, never crossing it (Sections 11–13).

5 · Halve the size

Two water droplets fall in air under laminar drag; one has half the diameter of the other. Compare their terminal speeds.

Answer

\(V_T\propto d^2\), so the smaller one falls at a quarter of the speed (and takes four times as long to fall 1 m).

6 · Fictitious or real?

In a braking bus you feel pushed forward. Which force is that in the bus frame, and what is the real explanation in the ground frame?

Answer

In the bus frame: the fictitious force \(-m\vb a_{\rm bus}\), pointing forward because the bus accelerates backward. In the ground frame there is no forward force: your body simply keeps moving (first law) while the bus slows beneath you, until friction from the seat or a handrail decelerates you.
