Basic Concepts of Statics

0Where this chapter sits

This chapter is about things that stay still: a plank resting on two stones across a stream, a ladder leaning on a wall, a bolt held tight by a spanner. Statics asks how hard each support must push, pull or twist so that nothing slides and nothing turns. Every later statics chapter (S2 to S7) starts from the free-body diagram and the equilibrium equations met here.

Definition · statics

Statics is the study of forces acting on bodies that are in equilibrium: bodies that are at rest, or moving at constant velocity, so that the forces on them balance.

Three working tools come first: 1 the method of sections, 2 the law of action and reaction, 3 the free-body diagram. The rest of the chapter defines nine basic terms: force, rigid body, free-body diagram, action and reaction, equilibrium, moment, system of forces, centroid, support. Each gets its own section below.

1Force, rigid body and the kinds of force ~7 min

What is this?

Picture tightening a bolt with a spanner. Your hand pushes on the handle: that push is a force. It has a size (how hard), a direction (which way) and a place (where your hand is). Push just as hard right next to the bolt and almost nothing happens; push at the far end of the handle and the bolt turns. For a force, the place matters as much as the size and the direction.

Force is a bound vector

Definition · force

A force is a push or a pull. It has a size (magnitude, in newtons N or kilonewtons kN), a direction, and a point of application (where it acts). The straight line through the point of application in the force's direction is its line of action.

Because the point of application matters, force is called a bound vector: a vector tied to a place. Moving it changes its effect. (A "free" vector, such as a displacement, can be slid anywhere.)

In Cartesian coordinates (perpendicular axes \(x, y, z\)) we write a force with unit vectors \(\e{x}, \e{y}, \e{z}\): arrows of length 1 pointing along the three axes.

\[\vb{F}=F_x\,\e{x}+F_y\,\e{y}+F_z\,\e{z}\]

In words: a force is "so much along \(x\)" plus "so much along \(y\)" plus "so much along \(z\)". \(F_x, F_y, F_z\) are the components (signed numbers).

Reading the formula
\(\vb F\)
your whole push on the spanner, as one arrow.
\(\e{x},\ \e{y},\ \e{z}\)
arrows of length 1 along the axes: they carry only the directions.
\(F_x,\ F_y,\ F_z\)
how much of the push goes along each axis. They are signed: a push to the left has \(F_x\lt0\), because \(+x\) points right.
the \(+\) signs
the three parts placed nose to tail rebuild the whole push.

Two pictures to keep in mind: a weight \(G\) hanging from a rope over a pulley, where the rope pulls with force \(F\) along the rope at angle \(\alpha\); and a force \(\vb F\) in a box with its three components \(F_x, F_y, F_z\) along the edges and the angles \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) it makes with the axes. Section 4 shows how to get the components from those angles.

Rigid body

What is this? (rigid body)

A steel spanner bends so little under your hand that you could never see it. Treating it as perfectly stiff loses nothing when you work out the forces on it. A rubber spanner would be different: it would bend, and where you pushed it would change what happens.

Definition · rigid body

A body is rigid if it does not deform (change shape) under forces. The distance between any two of its points never changes.

No real body is rigid, but steel parts deform so little (often less than 0.1%) that we can ignore the change of shape when we find the forces. Here is why the idea matters: on a rigid ball, a force \(F\) pulling at point \(A_1\) has the same effect as the same force pulling at point \(A_2\) on the same line of action \(f\). On a deformable (squashy) ball the two are different: pulling at \(A_1\) stretches the left side, pulling at \(A_2\) stretches the right side.

Consequence · line of action

On a rigid body, a force may be slid along its own line of action without changing its effect on the body's equilibrium. (This is called the principle of transmissibility.)

Going further · why this is allowed

From S3 onward we study how bodies do deform (stress and strain). That is not a contradiction: we first treat the body as rigid to find the forces (the deformation is too small to move the forces noticeably), then use those forces to find the small deformation. Sliding a force along its line is fine for finding reactions, but not for finding internal forces, because it changes which part of the body carries the load.

Kinds of force

What is this? (kinds of force)

Look at the spanner again. Its own weight is spread through all of its steel: a volume force. Your palm presses on a small patch of the grip: an area force. If that patch is tiny compared with the spanner, we treat it as one concentrated force at a point. And if we model the long, thin spanner as a line, its weight becomes so many newtons per metre along it: a line force.

Concentrated forceA single force with a line of action and a point of application. Idealises a load applied over a very small area: a thin wire or a needle point. Unit: N.
Volume (body) forceSpread through the volume of a body, such as weight. Each small volume \(\mathrm dV\) carries a small weight \(\mathrm dG\). Unit: N/m³.
Area forceSpread over a surface where two bodies touch, such as water pressure \(p\) on a dam wall. Unit: N/m² = Pa.
Line forceSpread along a line, such as a load \(q\) along a beam. Unit: N/m. In S2 this is the "distributed load" \(q(x)\).

Water pressure on a wall makes a triangle: pressure is zero at the water surface and grows linearly with depth. In S2 and in Section 7 below you will replace such spread-out loads by a single equivalent force.

You can now: say why a force is a bound vector and write it in components; explain when a body may be treated as rigid and why a force may then slide along its line of action; and name the four kinds of force with their units.

2Free-body diagrams and the method of sections ~4 min

What is this?

Picture a plank laid across a stream, resting on a stone at each bank, with you standing in the middle. To find how hard each stone pushes, draw the plank alone: rub out the stones, the water and the banks, and put an upward arrow where each stone was. That sketch, the plank plus every force acting on it, is the free-body diagram.

Definition · free-body diagram (FBD)

A free-body diagram is a sketch of one body cut free from everything around it. Every support and every contact is removed and replaced by the force (or moment) it exerts on the body. The FBD shows forces, not constraints.

The system: a beam on supports at \(A\) and \(B\), loaded by \(G\).

Its free-body diagram: the supports are gone; their forces \(A\) and \(B\) take their place.

Analogy: an FBD is like a police photo of one suspect against a blank wall, with arrows showing everyone who was pushing or pulling them. Nothing else in the picture.

Method of sections: making internal forces visible

What is this? (method of sections)

Now imagine sawing the plank in two somewhere over the water. Each half would drop unless something held it at the cut. Before the cut, that something was the other half of the plank. The forces across this imaginary cut are the internal forces: each half pushes and pulls on the other, equally and oppositely.

Definition · internal force, method of sections

Internal forces are the forces the parts of one body exert on each other. You cannot see them on the whole body, because they come in equal and opposite pairs and cancel. To see them, imagine cutting the body along a section. Each piece then has a free-body diagram of its own, and the forces across the cut appear on both faces. This is the method of sections.

An imaginary cut (green) through the loaded beam.

The two pieces ① and ②. The forces spread over the cut faces are equal and opposite.

Picture the forces on the cut faces as small arrows spread over the face: the cut face of piece ① feels some distribution, and the cut face of piece ② feels exactly the opposite distribution (Section 3 says why). In S2 these spread-out forces are replaced by their resultants: the shear force \(V\), normal force \(N\) and bending moment \(M\). In S3 they become stress (force per area).

Recipe · drawing a free-body diagram
  1. Do: Decide which body (or piece) you are isolating. Draw only its outline. Choosing the plank, not the stones, fixes whose balance you are checking; everything else becomes an arrow.
  2. Do: Draw the known loads where they act: point forces, couples, distributed loads, weight at the centre of gravity. Where a load acts decides how strongly it turns the body (Section 5), so it must sit at its true place.
  3. Do: At every place where the body touched something (support, other body, cut), draw the reactions that that contact can give (Section 10 lists them). Label each unknown with a letter. Each stone you rubbed out still pushes on the plank; leave one out and the balance equations are wrong.
  4. Do: Add dimensions and the axes you will use. The distances become the lever arms for moments, and the axes fix which directions count as positive.

You can now: draw a free-body diagram of a body, or of one piece of it, with every support and contact replaced by its reactions; and explain how an imaginary cut makes internal forces visible.

3The law of action and reaction ~2 min

What is this?

Stand on the plank and it presses down on each stone; each stone presses up on the plank just as hard. These two forces are a pair: same size, opposite directions, same line. They act on different bodies, one on the stone and one on the plank. So the plank's free-body diagram shows only the stone's push on the plank, never the plank's push on the stone.

Law · action and reaction (Newton's third law)

The forces two bodies exert on each other have the same magnitude, opposite directions, and lie on the same line of action.

In words: if I push you, you push me back exactly as hard, along the same line. Forces always come in pairs, and the two forces of a pair act on different bodies.

Two cases. (a) A man pushes on a wall. Cut between his hands and the wall: the wall feels a force \(F\) to the left, the man feels a force \(F\) to the right. (b) A small ball rests on a big ball. The small ball presses on the big one with force \(G\), and the big ball pushes back on the small one with \(G\) in the opposite direction, along the line joining their centres.

Watch

The two forces of an action–reaction pair never appear on the same free-body diagram, because they act on different bodies. If they did, they would cancel and the FBD would tell you nothing. They appear together only when you draw both bodies separately, as with pieces ① and ② in Section 2.

You can now: state the law of action and reaction; and put each force of an action–reaction pair on the free-body diagram of the body it acts on.

4Adding and splitting forces ~5 min

What is this?

Picture a lamp hanging from a knot, held by two ropes that run up to the ceiling at an angle. Each rope pulls the knot along its own length. Together the two pulls act like one single pull, straight up and equal to the lamp's weight. This section shows how to add forces like these into one, and how to split one force into sideways and up-and-down parts.

Parallelogram law

Law · parallelogram of forces

Two forces \(\vb F_1\) and \(\vb F_2\) acting at the same point \(A\) have the same effect as a single force, their resultant

\[\vb R=\vb F_1+\vb F_2,\]

which is the diagonal of the parallelogram whose sides are \(\vb F_1\) and \(\vb F_2\).

In words: to add two forces, put them tail-to-tail, complete the parallelogram, and the diagonal from the common tail is the sum. Equivalently, place them head-to-tail (a "triangle of forces"); the order does not matter.

Example

At point \(A\), \(F_1=6\u{kN}\) acts along \(+x\) and \(F_2=8\u{kN}\) along \(+y\). Find the resultant.

The parallelogram is a rectangle, so by Pythagoras

\[\begin{aligned}R&=\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=10\u{kN},\\ \theta&=\tan^{-1}\tfrac{8}{6}=53.1^\circ\ \text{above }+x.\end{aligned}\]

Many forces through one point: the force polygon

Definition · concurrent, coplanar

Forces are concurrent if their lines of action all pass through one point, and coplanar if they all lie in one plane.

For \(n\) coplanar concurrent forces, repeat the triangle construction: draw \(\vb F_1\), put the tail of \(\vb F_2\) at its head, then \(\vb F_3\), and so on (first \(\vb F_1+\vb F_2\), then \(\vb F_1+\vb F_2+\vb F_i\), up to \(\vb F_n\)). The arrow from the first tail \(a\) to the last head \(b\) is the resultant \(\vb R\). This chain is the force polygon. If the polygon closes (last head lands on first tail), \(\vb R=\vb 0\) and the forces balance.

Decomposition into components

What is this? (components)

Each rope pull can be split into a sideways part and an upward part. At the lamp's knot the two sideways parts point in opposite directions and cancel, and the two upward parts add up to the weight. Working with parts along the axes turns arrow drawings into simple sums of signed numbers.

Going the other way, any force can be split into components along the axes. If \(\vb F\) makes angles \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) with the \(x, y, z\) axes:

\[\begin{aligned}F_x&=F\cos\alpha,\quad F_y=F\cos\beta,\quad F_z=F\cos\gamma,\\ F&=\sqrt{F_x^2+F_y^2+F_z^2}.\end{aligned}\]
Reading the formula
\(F\cos\alpha\)
the share of the rope pull along \(x\). \(\cos\alpha\) is near 1 when the rope lies almost along \(x\), and near 0 when it runs almost square to \(x\).
\(\alpha\gt90^\circ\)
the pull leans towards \(-x\), so the cosine and the component come out negative, matching the sign convention.
\(\sqrt{F_x^2+F_y^2+F_z^2}\)
the full pull rebuilt from its three parts: Pythagoras in 3D.

In words: each component is the force times the cosine of its angle to that axis; the size of the force is the 3D Pythagoras of its components. \(\cos\alpha,\cos\beta,\cos\gamma\) are called the direction cosines; they satisfy \(\cos^2\alpha+\cos^2\beta+\cos^2\gamma=1\).

For several forces \(\vb F_i\) with components \(F_{ix},F_{iy},F_{iz}\), add component by component:

\[\begin{aligned}R_x&=\textstyle\sum F_{ix},\quad R_y=\sum F_{iy},\\ R_z&=\textstyle\sum F_{iz},\\ R&=\sqrt{R_x^2+R_y^2+R_z^2},\\ \cos\alpha_R&=\tfrac{R_x}{R},\ \cos\beta_R=\tfrac{R_y}{R},\ \cos\gamma_R=\tfrac{R_z}{R}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the resultant's \(x\)-component is the sum of all the \(x\)-components, and the same for \(y\) and \(z\). This is the algebraic version of the force polygon, and it is what you use in exams.

Example

\(\vb F=3\,\e{x}+4\,\e{y}+12\,\e{z}\ \mathrm{kN}\). Then \(F=\sqrt{9+16+144}=13\u{kN}\) and \(\cos\alpha=3/13\), \(\cos\beta=4/13\), \(\cos\gamma=12/13\), so \(\alpha=76.7^\circ\), \(\beta=72.1^\circ\), \(\gamma=22.6^\circ\). Check: \((9+16+144)/169=1\).

Equilibrium of concurrent forces

Condition · equilibrium of a point \[\begin{aligned}&\vb R=\textstyle\sum\vb F_i=\vb 0\\ \Longleftrightarrow\ &\textstyle\sum F_{ix}=0,\ \ \sum F_{iy}=0,\ \ \sum F_{iz}=0.\end{aligned}\]

In words: a set of forces through one point balances exactly when its resultant is zero, which means the forces in each direction add to zero (the force polygon closes). In a plane this gives 2 equations; in space, 3.

Reading the formula
\(\sum F_{ix}=0\)
the sideways parts cancel: the knot is not dragged left or right.
\(\sum F_{iy}=0\)
the upward parts of the rope pulls exactly balance the lamp's weight.
\(\sum F_{iz}=0\)
the same check towards and away from you (needed only in 3D).
\(\Longleftrightarrow\)
balancing in every direction is the same thing as the force polygon closing.

You can now: add forces with the parallelogram law or a force polygon; split a force into components using direction cosines and add forces component by component; and write the equilibrium equations for forces through one point.

5Moment and couple ~7 min

What is this?

Back to the bolt and spanner. How well your push turns the bolt depends on two things: how hard you push, and how far the line of your push passes from the bolt, measured square to that line. A longer spanner turns a stuck bolt more easily for the same push. That turning effect is the moment. A wheel brace (the cross-shaped spanner for car wheel nuts) used with two hands, one pushing up and one pulling down equally hard, applies a couple: the two pushes cancel as forces, so the nut is not shoved sideways, but their turning effects add.

Definition · couple and moment

A couple is two forces of equal magnitude, on parallel lines of action, in opposite directions. Their resultant force is zero, so they do not push the body anywhere; they only turn it. The turning effect is the moment of the couple:

\[M=hF\]

where \(h\) is the perpendicular distance between the two lines of action (the lever arm).

In words: moment = force × perpendicular distance. Unit: N·m (or kN·m). Same force, twice the distance → twice the turning effect.

Reading the formula
\(F\)
how hard you push on the spanner.
\(h\)
the perpendicular distance from the bolt to the line of your push, not the slanting length along the handle. Push along the handle and \(h=0\): no turning at all.
\(M\)
the turning effect on the bolt. Its sign follows the page's convention: anticlockwise \(+\), as seen with \(z\) pointing at you. Tightening a normal bolt while you look at its head is clockwise, so that moment is negative.

The same idea applies to one force about a point (a hinge): the moment of \(F\) about point \(0\) is \(F\) times the perpendicular distance \(h\) from \(0\) to the line of action of \(F\). The right-hand rule gives its direction: curl the fingers of your right hand in the sense of rotation; your thumb points along the moment vector \(\vb M\), perpendicular to the plane. In a plane problem every moment vector points straight out of or into the page, so we just give a sign: anticlockwise \(+\), clockwise \(-\).

Facts · couples
  • A couple has the same moment about every point (it is a "free" vector). Proof: for the couple \(F\), \(-F\) with lines a distance \(h\) apart, and a point at distance \(d\) from the line of \(-F\) (on the far side from \(F\)): \(F(d+h)-Fd=Fh\), independent of \(d\).
  • So a couple can be moved anywhere on a rigid body; only its moment \(M\) matters. Examples: turning a steering wheel with two hands, a screwdriver blade in a slot, and a wrench all apply couples \(M=hF\).

Moment equilibrium: a body that is not turning has \(\sum M_i=0\): the moments of all forces and couples about any point add to zero. Examples: the steering wheel held still by equal and opposite hand forces, a screw with balanced torques, and a cantilever whose wall supplies a moment balancing the moment of the load \(F\) at distance \(h\).

Moment as a vector: \(\vb M=\vb r\times\vb F\)

What is this? (moment as a vector)

In 3D a turning effect needs a direction too: the axis it turns about. The bolt shows it. Turn a normal (right-hand) thread the way the fingers of your right hand curl, and the bolt moves the way your thumb points. So the moment vector points along the bolt's axis, and its length says how strong the twist is.

In 3D, the moment of a force \(\vb F\) about a point \(0\) is the cross product

\[\vb M^{(0)}=\vb r\times\vb F,\]

where \(\vb r\) is the position vector from \(0\) to any point on the line of action of \(\vb F\).

Reading the formula
\(\vb r\)
the arrow from the bolt centre \(0\) to any point on the line of your push.
\(\vb F\)
your push.
\(\times\)
keeps only the part of the push that is square to \(\vb r\) (size \(rF\sin\theta=hF\)), and points the result along the turning axis by the right-hand rule.
"any point on the line"
sliding the push along its own line does not change the moment, as in Section 1.

In words: the moment vector is perpendicular to both \(\vb r\) and \(\vb F\) (right-hand rule) and its size is \(rF\sin\theta=hF\), because \(r\sin\theta\) is exactly the perpendicular distance \(h\).

In components (with \(\vb r=(x,y,z)\), \(\vb F=(F_x,F_y,F_z)\)):

\[\begin{aligned}M_x&=yF_z-zF_y,\\ M_y&=zF_x-xF_z,\\ M_z&=xF_y-yF_x.\end{aligned}\]

Take the special case of a force in the \(x\)–\(y\) plane: its moment about \(0\) points along \(z\), with size \(M_z^{(0)}=hF\). For several couples \(\vb M_i\) on one body, the resultant moment is the vector sum:

\[\begin{aligned}\vb M_R&=\textstyle\sum\vb M_i,\\ M_{Rx}&=\textstyle\sum M_{ix},\ \ M_{Ry}=\sum M_{iy},\\ M_{Rz}&=\textstyle\sum M_{iz},\end{aligned}\]

and moment equilibrium in 3D is \(\sum M_{ix}=0,\ \sum M_{iy}=0,\ \sum M_{iz}=0\).

Example

\(\vb F=(0,\,3,\,4)\u{N}\) acts at \(\vb r=(2,\,0,\,0)\u{m}\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}\vb M^{(0)}&=\begin{vmatrix}\e{x}&\e{y}&\e{z}\\2&0&0\\0&3&4\end{vmatrix}\\ &=(0\cdot4-0\cdot3)\,\e{x}-(2\cdot4-0)\,\e{y}\\ &\quad+(2\cdot3-0)\,\e{z}\\ &=(0,\,-8,\,6)\u{N\,m}.\end{aligned}\]

Check the size: \(|\vb M|=10\u{N\,m}\); and \(h\,F\) with \(F=5\u{N}\), \(h=2\u{m}\) (the line of \(\vb F\) is perpendicular to \(\vb r\)) is also 10 N·m.

You can now: find the moment of a force about a point as force × perpendicular distance, with the sign from the anticlockwise-positive convention; explain why a couple has the same moment about every point; and find a 3D moment from \(\vb r\times\vb F\) in components.

6Equilibrium of a rigid body in a plane ~9 min

What is this?

Back to the plank across the stream, with you standing on it. It stays still, so three things must hold: nothing pushes it sideways overall, the upward pushes of the stones balance the downward loads, and nothing turns it. Those are the three equilibrium equations of a body in a plane. If you walk towards one bank, that stone must push harder, or the plank would tip.

Reducing a coplanar system

Forces \(\vb F_1,\dots,\vb F_n\) on a body in one plane, not all through one point, can be replaced by an equivalent simpler system in three steps:

  1. Do: Pick a reference point \(A\). Slide each force to \(A\) and add a couple equal to its moment about \(A\), \(M_i^{(A)}\). (A force moved off its line must be accompanied by this couple to keep the same effect.) Moving a force sideways changes how it turns the body; the added couple puts that turning effect back.
  2. Do: Add the forces at \(A\) into one resultant \(\vb R\), and the couples into one moment \(M_R^{(A)}\): \[\begin{aligned}R_x&=\textstyle\sum F_{ix},\quad R_y=\sum F_{iy},\\ M_R^{(A)}&=\textstyle\sum M_i^{(A)}.\end{aligned}\] Once every force passes through one point they add like the rope pulls at a knot (Section 4), and couples, being free, simply add as signed numbers.
  3. Do: If \(\vb R\ne\vb 0\), the force \(\vb R\) at \(A\) plus the couple \(M_R^{(A)}\) equals the force \(\vb R\) alone, shifted sideways by the distance \(h=M_R^{(A)}/R\). Its line of action makes angle \(\alpha\) with the \(x\)-axis where \(\tan\alpha=R_y/R_x\). A force \(R\) shifted sideways by \(h\) has moment \(Rh\) about \(A\), so it can carry the couple by itself.

In words: any set of forces in a plane acts like one force \(R\) through a point \(A\) plus one twist \(M\); and if \(R\ne0\) you can even hide the twist by moving \(R\) sideways.

The four possible outcomes

CaseResultEffect on the body
a\(\vb R\ne\vb 0,\ M^{(A)}\ne0\)a single force \(R\) on a line that misses \(A\)
b\(\vb R\ne\vb 0,\ M^{(A)}=0\)a single force \(R\) whose line passes through \(A\)
c\(\vb R=\vb 0,\ M^{(A)}=M\ne0\)a pure couple \(M\) (same about every point)
d\(\vb R=\vb 0,\ M^{(A)}=0\)equilibrium

Three equivalent sets of equilibrium conditions

What is this? (the three sets)

You have three checks to spend on the plank. Each can be "no sliding in this direction" or "no turning about this point". A turning check about a point where an unknown force acts is handy, because that force has no lever arm there and drops out. The conditions on the points only stop two checks from asking the same question.

A rigid body under coplanar forces is in equilibrium (case d) when any one of these three sets holds:

Equilibrium conditions in a plane

(i) Two force conditions and one moment condition

\[\textstyle\sum F_{ix}=0,\quad\sum F_{iy}=0,\quad\sum M_i^{(A)}=0.\]

(ii) One force condition and two moment conditions (about points \(A\) and \(B\))

\[\textstyle\sum F_{ix}=0,\quad\sum M_i^{(A)}=0,\quad\sum M_i^{(B)}=0\]

with the line \(AB\) not perpendicular to \(x\);

or with \(\sum F_{iy}=0\) instead (then \(AB\) must not be perpendicular to \(y\)).

(iii) Three moment conditions about points \(A\), \(B\), \(C\)

\[\begin{aligned}&\textstyle\sum M_i^{(A)}=0,\quad\sum M_i^{(B)}=0,\\ &\textstyle\sum M_i^{(C)}=0\end{aligned}\]

with \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) not on one straight line.

In words: a flat body needs exactly three independent checks to be sure it neither slides nor turns. Two of them can be "no turning about another point" instead of "no sliding", as long as the points are well placed.

Reading the formula
\(\sum F_{ix}=0\)
the plank is not pushed left or right overall.
\(\sum F_{iy}=0\)
the stones' upward pushes equal the downward loads.
\(\sum M_i^{(A)}=0\)
no net turning about \(A\). Once the forces balance, the leftover turning effect is a couple, which is the same about every point, so one point is enough.

Why the conditions on the points? Use the four cases. If \(\sum M^{(A)}=0\), the system is either in equilibrium or reduces to a single force \(R\) through \(A\). If also \(\sum M^{(B)}=0\), any such \(R\) must lie along line \(AB\). A third check kills it: \(\sum F_x=0\) works if \(AB\) is not perpendicular to \(x\) (a force along \(AB\) would have an \(x\)-component); or \(\sum M^{(C)}=0\) works if \(C\) is off line \(AB\), since a force along \(AB\) has a moment about \(C\).

Watch

You never get more than three independent equations from one body in a plane. Writing \(\sum M\) about a fourth point gives nothing new: it is a combination of the others. This is why the "count the unknowns" test of Section 11 compares with 3.

Seven steps for any statics problem

  1. Do: Formulate the engineering problem (what is asked?). You must know which forces are wanted before you can choose which body to cut free.
  2. Do: Set up a mechanical model that keeps all the essential features of the real system (e.g. a crane becomes a beam on a pin and a roller). Real supports become ideal ones whose reactions are known (Section 10), so the unknowns can be named.
  3. Do: List the given and the unknown quantities. Comparing the number of unknowns with the three equations tells you early whether statics alone can solve it (Section 11).
  4. Do: Draw the free-body diagram. The equations are written for one body, so every force on that body must be in view.
  5. Do: Write the mechanical equations, e.g. the equilibrium conditions. These are the "no sliding, no turning" checks on the plank.
  6. Do: Write any geometric relations needed (angles, lengths). The equations need components and lever arms, which come from the angles and lengths.
  7. Do: Solve for the unknowns (and check the answer with an equation you did not use). A body at rest satisfies every equilibrium equation, so an unused one must also give zero; this catches sign and lever-arm slips.
Worked example 1 · pin and roller

A 6 m beam \(AB\) has a pin at \(A\) and a roller at \(B\). It carries \(12\u{kN}\) downward at 2 m from \(A\), and \(10\u{kN}\) at 4 m from \(A\) pointing down and to the right at \(60^\circ\) below the horizontal. Find the support reactions.

System

Free-body diagram (reactions in teal, assumed positive)

  1. Model and FBD. The pin gives \(A_x\) and \(A_y\); the roller gives only \(B_y\) (Section 10). Three unknowns, three equations: solvable.
  2. Split the inclined load. \(10\cos60^\circ=5\u{kN}\) to the right, \(10\sin60^\circ=8.660\u{kN}\) down.

The loads turn the beam clockwise about \(A\), so they enter with a minus sign.

Worked example 2 · cantilever

A 3 m cantilever is built into a wall at \(A\) (left). It carries a uniform load \(4\u{kN/m}\) over its whole length and \(5\u{kN}\) downward at the free end. Find the wall reactions.

7Centre of force and distributed loads ~5 min

What is this?

Now spread a load along the plank: say sand, heaped deeper at one end. For the support reactions, the stones only care about two things: how heavy the sand is in total, and where its balance point is. So we replace the spread-out sand by one force, its total weight, placed at that balance point. The same idea works for a few separate loads: their single equivalent force sits at their weighted average position.

Parallel forces

Take parallel forces \(G_1,\dots,G_n\) (all vertical, say, like the weights of parts of a plate) at points \((x_i,y_i)\). Their resultant is \(R=\sum G_i\). Where does it act? Answer by adding the opposite force \(H=-R\) on the same line, which must put the system in equilibrium. Any point \(C\) on that line is the centre of force (centre of the parallel forces).

Equilibrium of the plate with \(H\) at \((x_c,y_c)\), \(z\) vertical:

\[\begin{aligned}\textstyle\sum F_{iz}=0:&\quad H-\textstyle\sum G_i=0,\\ \textstyle\sum M_{ix}^{(0)}=0:&\quad y_cH-\textstyle\sum y_iG_i=0,\\ \textstyle\sum M_{iy}^{(0)}=0:&\quad -x_cH+\textstyle\sum x_iG_i=0,\end{aligned}\] \[\Rightarrow\quad x_c=\frac{\sum x_iG_i}{\sum G_i},\qquad y_c=\frac{\sum y_iG_i}{\sum G_i}.\]

In words: the centre of parallel forces is the force-weighted average position: each position counts in proportion to the force sitting there. Big forces pull the centre towards themselves.

Example

Downward forces \(2\u{kN}\) at \((1,1)\), \(3\u{kN}\) at \((4,1)\), \(5\u{kN}\) at \((2,3)\) (metres). \(R=10\u{kN}\); \(x_c=(2\cdot1+3\cdot4+5\cdot2)/10=2.4\u{m}\), \(y_c=(2\cdot1+3\cdot1+5\cdot3)/10=2.0\u{m}\).

Continuously distributed loads

What is this? (distributed load)

Draw the depth of sand along the plank as a graph: that is the load diagram \(q(x)\), in newtons per metre. A thin slice of sand weighs \(q\) times its width, so the total weight is the area under the graph. Where the sand is deeper the graph is taller, and the balance point moves towards the deep end.

Replace the separate forces by a line load \(q(x)\) (N/m) along a beam: the little piece \(\mathrm dx\) carries force \(q(x)\,\mathrm dx\), and sums become integrals:

\[R=\int q(x)\,\mathrm dx,\qquad x_c=\frac{\int x\,q(x)\,\mathrm dx}{\int q(x)\,\mathrm dx}.\]

In words: the resultant of a line load is the area under the load diagram, and it acts at the centroid of that area (its balance point along \(x\)).

Reading the formula
\(q(x)\,\mathrm dx\)
the weight of one thin slice of sand, of width \(\mathrm dx\).
\(\int q\,\mathrm dx\)
all slices added: the total weight \(R\), the area under the load diagram.
\(\int x\,q\,\mathrm dx\)
each slice's weight times its distance from the left end, added up: the total turning effect of the sand about the left end.
\(x_c\)
where the single force \(R\) must sit to give that same turning effect, since \(R\,x_c=\int x\,q\,\mathrm dx\).

For an area load \(p(x,y)\) (N/m², e.g. pressure on a plate), the small patch \(\mathrm dA\) carries \(p\,\mathrm dA\):

\[x_c=\frac{\int x\,p(x,y)\,\mathrm dA}{\int p(x,y)\,\mathrm dA},\qquad y_c=\frac{\int y\,p(x,y)\,\mathrm dA}{\int p(x,y)\,\mathrm dA}.\]
Worked example · triangular load

A simply supported beam of length \(l\) carries a load rising linearly from 0 at the left to \(q_0\) at the right: \(q(x)=q_0\,x/l\). Find the resultant and where it acts.

\[R=\int_0^l q_0\frac{x}{l}\,\mathrm dx=q_0\frac{x^2}{2l}\Big|_0^l=\tfrac12q_0l,\] \[\begin{aligned}\int_0^l x\,q(x)\,\mathrm dx&=\int_0^l q_0\frac{x^2}{l}\,\mathrm dx\\ &=q_0\frac{x^3}{3l}\Big|_0^l=\tfrac13q_0l^2,\end{aligned}\] \[x_c=\frac{\tfrac13q_0l^2}{\tfrac12q_0l}=\boxed{\tfrac23\,l}\ \text{ from the zero end.}\]

Most of the sand sits near the tall end, so the balance point lies beyond the middle, towards \(q_0\).

In words: a triangular load is equivalent to half of "peak × length", acting one third of the way in from the high end (two thirds from the pointed end).

8Centre of gravity, centre of mass and centroid ~8 min

What is this?

Balance a spanner on one finger. There is exactly one spot where it stays level: that is where its whole weight can be taken to act, the centre of gravity. Every bit of steel tries to tip the spanner one way or the other about your finger, and at the balance point those tipping effects cancel.

Centre of gravity

Weight is a parallel body force: every small volume \(\mathrm dV\) carries a small weight \(\mathrm d\vb G=f\,\mathrm dV\,\vb e\), all pointing the same way (down). Here \(f(x,y,z)\) is force per unit volume (N/m³). Applying Section 7 with integrals over the volume:

\[x_c=\frac{\int x\,f\,\mathrm dV}{\int f\,\mathrm dV},\quad y_c=\frac{\int y\,f\,\mathrm dV}{\int f\,\mathrm dV},\]\[z_c=\frac{\int z\,f\,\mathrm dV}{\int f\,\mathrm dV}.\]
Definition · centre of gravity

The centre of gravity \(C\) is the point where the whole weight \(G=\int f\,\mathrm dV\) can be taken to act. For a body made of several parts with weights \(G_i\) and centres \(C_i\), \(x_c=\sum x_iG_i/\sum G_i\), and the same for \(y_c, z_c\).

Each tiny piece's weight times its distance is its tipping effect; dividing the total tipping effect by the total weight gives the one place where the whole weight tips the body the same way.

With \(f=\varrho g\), where \(\varrho(x,y,z)\) is the density (kg/m³) and \(g\) the gravitational acceleration, \(g\) cancels:

\[x_c=\frac{\int x\,\varrho\,\mathrm dV}{\int\varrho\,\mathrm dV},\quad y_c=\frac{\int y\,\varrho\,\mathrm dV}{\int\varrho\,\mathrm dV},\]\[z_c=\frac{\int z\,\varrho\,\mathrm dV}{\int\varrho\,\mathrm dV}.\]

So for a homogeneous rigid body in a uniform, parallel gravity field, the weight can be treated as concentrated at this point.

Centre of mass and centroid

What is this? (centroid)

Cut a shape out of card of even thickness. Its balance point depends only on the shape, not on how heavy the card is: that point is the centroid. For a body made of one material near the Earth's surface, the centre of gravity, the centre of mass and the centroid are the same point.

Since \(\mathrm dm=\varrho\,\mathrm dV\) and \(m=\int\varrho\,\mathrm dV=\int\mathrm dm\):

\[x_c=\frac{\int x\,\varrho\,\mathrm dV}{\int\varrho\,\mathrm dV}=\frac{\int x\,\mathrm dm}{m}\ \ \overset{\varrho\ \text{const}}{=}\ \ \frac{\int x\,\mathrm dV}{V}.\]
Definitions · centre of mass, centroid
  • Centre of mass: \(x_c=\frac1m\int x\,\mathrm dm\) (and \(y_c, z_c\) likewise): the mass-weighted average position.
  • Centroid of a volume: \(x_c=\frac1V\int x\,\mathrm dV\): the purely geometric "middle" of a shape.
  • Centroid of a plane area (a flat plate of uniform thickness \(t\)): \(x_c=\frac1A\int x\,\mathrm dA,\ \ y_c=\frac1A\int y\,\mathrm dA\).
  • Centroid of a line (a thin wire, length \(l\), arc-length \(s\)): \(x_c=\frac1l\int x\,\mathrm ds,\ \ y_c=\frac1l\int y\,\mathrm ds\).

In words: centre of gravity = where the weight acts; centre of mass = average position of the mass; centroid = average position of the shape. They are the same point when gravity is uniform and parallel (all of them, near the Earth's surface) and, for the centroid, when the density is uniform.

Shortcut · symmetry

If a shape has an axis of symmetry, its centroid lies on that axis. A shape with two axes of symmetry has its centroid where they cross. Always use this before calculating.

Composite areas: the rectangular partition method

What is this? (composite areas)

A T or L section is just rectangles of card glued together. Each rectangle balances at its own middle. The whole shape balances at the average of those middles, with each rectangle counted in proportion to its area, because a bigger rectangle is more card. This is also why the balance point of an L can fall in the empty corner, off the card.

If the load (pressure) is uniform, \(x_c=\int x\,p\,\mathrm dA/\int p\,\mathrm dA=\int x\,\mathrm dA/\int\mathrm dA\), so the centre of force of the load is the centroid of the loaded area. For an area built from rectangles, the integral becomes a sum over the rectangles:

\[x_c=\frac{\sum A_ix_i}{\sum A_i},\qquad y_c=\frac{\sum A_iy_i}{\sum A_i}\]

In words: multiply each rectangle's centroid coordinate by its area, add, and divide by the total area. Each rectangle's own centroid is at its middle.

  1. Do: Use symmetry first; then split the shape into rectangles and choose one origin. An axis of symmetry gives one coordinate free, and a rectangle's balance point is simply its middle, so no integral is needed.
  2. Do: For each rectangle write its area \(A_i\) and its centroid \((x_i,y_i)\), with signs. A piece below or to the left of the origin pulls the balance point that way, so its coordinate is negative.
  3. Do: Compute \(x_c=\sum A_ix_i/\sum A_i\) and \(y_c=\sum A_iy_i/\sum A_i\). Bigger rectangles hold more card, so their middles count more in the average.
  4. Do: Check that the answer lies towards the bigger pieces and on any axis of symmetry. An unweighted average of the middles (a common mistake) fails this check.
Worked example · asymmetric section

Three rectangles: area 6 with centroid \((1,\,4.5)\); area 18 with centroid \((3,\,1.5)\); area 1 with centroid \((5.5,\,-0.5)\).

\(\boxed{(x_c,y_c)=(2.62,\ 2.14)}\). Note the negative \(y\) of the small piece below the axis: it pulls \(y_c\) down.

A hole is a rectangle with negative area: \(x_c=(\sum A_ix_i-A_hx_h)/(\sum A_i-A_h)\). The U-section in the exam taster below can be done either way: three rectangles, or a big rectangle minus the gap.

9Bars, beams, arches, frames, disks, plates and shells ~2 min

What is this?

Take a steel ruler. Pull its two ends apart and it hardly stretches: used like that it is a bar. Lay it across two books and press its middle and it bends: now it is a beam. A sheet of plywood is the same story in two dimensions: load it along its edge, in its own plane, and it is a disk; stand on it as a floor and it is a plate. Curve the sheet and it is a shell.

10Supports and support reactions ~6 min

What is this?

Think of the end of a beam, and list what its support does not allow. A beam end resting on a roller can roll along and can tilt, but cannot sink into the floor: one forbidden movement, so one push, square to the floor. A door hinge (a pin) lets the door swing but stops its edge moving in any direction: two force components. A diving board built into concrete (a fixed support) cannot move or turn at all: two force components and a twisting moment.

Definitions · support, reaction, degrees of freedom
  • A support connects a structure to its surroundings and holds it in a fixed position.
  • The forces and moments a support passes onto the structure are the support reactions.
  • A body in a plane has 3 degrees of freedom \(f\) (independent ways to move): slide in \(x\), slide in \(y\), turn. Each reaction removes one. With \(r\) reactions:
\[f=3-r.\]

In words: every support reaction is a movement the support forbids. A support that stops movement in a direction pushes back in that direction; a support that stops turning twists back with a moment.

Supports are classified by \(r\), the number of reactions:

One reaction, \(r=1\)

Ball, roller, sliding hinge, single strut. The body can slide along the surface and turn, but cannot move into (or away from) the surface. Reaction: one force perpendicular to the surface (or along the strut). A ball in a channel, a roller, a strut (a bar pinned at both ends) and a sliding hinge are all equivalent: the reaction \(A\) is a single force along one fixed line.

Two reactions, \(r=2\)

Hinge (pin), bounded ball/roller, two struts. The body can turn about the pin but cannot move. Reaction: a force of any direction, written as two components \(A_H\) (horizontal) and \(A_V\) (vertical), or as a magnitude \(A\) and an angle.

Parallel motion and sliding sleeve: the body cannot turn and cannot move in one direction, but can slide freely in the other. Reaction: one force plus one couple moment. A plate on rollers against a wall can slide vertically: reactions \(A_H\) and \(M_A\). A sleeve on a horizontal bar can slide horizontally: reactions \(A_V\) and \(M_A\).

Three reactions, \(r=3\)

Fixed (clamped, built-in) support, or three struts. No movement and no turning. Reactions: a force of any direction (\(A_H\), \(A_V\)) and a couple \(M_A\). A beam welded to a wall plate is also a fixed support.

Supports in 3D

What is this? (supports in 3D)

Off the page, a body can also slide towards you and turn about two more axes: six movements in all. A ball-and-socket joint, like your hip, stops all three slides but none of the turns, so it gives three forces and no moments.

In space a body has six degrees of freedom: three translations (\(x, y, z\)) and three rotations (about \(x, y, z\)). A strut gives \(R=1\); a ball-and-socket hinge gives \(R=3\) (three force components, no moments). A fixed support (clamping) gives \(R=6\) (three forces and three moments); a sliding sleeve gives \(R=4\) (it allows sliding along and turning about the bar's axis, so 2 forces and 2 moments remain).

How to find the reactions of any support:

  1. Do: List the three movements in the plane: slide in \(x\), slide in \(y\), turn. These are the three degrees of freedom a support can take away.
  2. Do: For each one, ask whether the support stops it. A support can only push back in a direction it blocks; where the body is free to move, the support gives no force.
  3. Do: Draw one reaction per stopped movement: a force for a blocked slide, a moment for a blocked turn, each in an assumed positive direction. The number of arrows is \(r\), ready for Section 11, and equilibrium will tell you, by the sign, which way each one really acts.

11Statically determinate, indeterminate, or movable? ~5 min

What is this?

Lean a ladder against a wall. If the wall and the floor were both perfectly smooth, the wall could only push sideways and the floor only up: two reactions, and the ladder slides down. Nail a block to the floor at its foot and you add a third reaction: now the three equations give all three reactions, and the ladder is statically determinate. Let the wall grip the ladder as well and there are four unknown reactions but still only three equations: the ladder stands, but statics alone cannot say how the load is shared. That is statically indeterminate.

Definition · statically determinate

A structure is statically determinate if its support reactions can be found from the three equilibrium conditions alone. This depends only on the supports, not on the loading.

A plane structure is supported statically and kinematically determinate if it cannot move and exactly three support reactions appear. These may be:

  1. three forces whose lines of action are not all parallel and not all through one point ("not central"), or
  2. two non-parallel forces and one moment.

Three determinate examples: (a) a beam with a pin at \(A\) and a roller at \(B\) (\(A_H, A_V, B\)); (b) a cantilever fixed at \(A\) (\(A_H, A_V, M_A\)), here with a triangular load and an end couple \(M_0\); (c) a rigid block held by three struts \(A\), \(B\) (vertical) and \(C\) (inclined): three forces, not all parallel, not concurrent.

Definition · kinematically indeterminate (movable)

A structure that can undergo finite or infinitesimal displacements is kinematically indeterminate. It is a mechanism, not a structure. It happens when there are too few reactions, or when three reactions are badly arranged:

  • three forces whose lines all pass through one point \(P\): the body can turn about \(P\), since \(\sum M^{(P)}\ne0\) cannot be balanced;
  • three parallel forces: nothing resists movement across them, since \(\sum F_{iH}\ne0\) cannot be balanced.
Definition · statically indeterminate, degree \(x\)

If extra supports are added to a determinate structure, there are more than three reactions and equilibrium alone cannot find them all: the structure is statically indeterminate. The degree of static indeterminacy \(x\) is the number by which the unknown reactions exceed the available equilibrium equations:

\[x=r-3\qquad\text{(one body in a plane)}.\]

In words: count the reactions, subtract the three equations you have; what is left over is how many unknowns statics alone cannot find. (S5 finds them with deflection conditions.)

Reading the formula
\(r\)
the unknown reactions: for the gripped ladder, wall push, wall grip, floor push and block, so \(r=4\).
\(3\)
the equations one body in a plane gives (Section 6).
\(x\)
the unknowns statics cannot find: \(4-3=1\) for the gripped ladder.

For example, a cantilever fixed at \(A\) with an extra roller at \(B\) has \(r=4\): one degree of indeterminacy. A beam on a pin and three rollers has \(r=5\): two degrees.

How to classify a structure:

  1. Do: Count the support reactions \(r\) (for several bodies, also the joint reactions \(v\) and the bodies \(n\), Section 12). These are all the unknown forces and moments.
  2. Do: Work out \(x=r-3\) (or \(x=r+v-3n\)). Each body gives only three equations, so \(x\) compares unknowns with equations.
  3. Do: If \(x\lt0\), the structure is movable. If \(x\ge0\), check the arrangement: reactions all parallel, or all through one point, still let it move. The count only compares numbers; a badly placed set of reactions leaves a movement nothing resists, like the ladder on a smooth floor and wall.
  4. Do: If it cannot move: \(x=0\) means statically determinate, \(x\gt0\) means statically indeterminate of degree \(x\). The leftover unknowns need extra information about how the structure bends (S5).

Try it · count and classify

\(r\) = support reactions, \(v\) = connection reactions, \(n\) = rigid bodies; \(x=r+v-3n\). The count is necessary but not enough: the arrangement must also be checked (the parallel-struts case has \(x=0\) but still moves).

You can now: decide whether a plane structure is movable, statically determinate or statically indeterminate, checking both the count and the arrangement of its reactions; and give the degree of indeterminacy \(x=r-3\).

12Multi-part structures ~4 min

What is this?

A stepladder is two bodies, the front legs and the back legs, joined by a hinge at the top. The hinge passes forces between them but no twist, which is why, without the chain or strap between them, the legs would splay apart on a slippery floor. In a multi-part structure each joint is described by what it passes, and each body gives its own three equations.

Many structures consist of several rigid bodies joined together. The connecting members pass forces and moments between the bodies. There are three kinds:

(a) Strut \(S\)A bar pinned at both ends. Passes one force along the strut: \(v=1\).
(b) Hinge \(G\)A pin joining the two bodies. Passes a force of any direction, \(G_H\) and \(G_V\), but no moment: \(v=2\).
(c) Parallel motion \(P\)Lets the bodies slide relative to each other across the members, but not turn or pull apart. Passes a force \(N\) along the members and a moment \(M\): \(v=2\).

When you cut each connection, the two bodies ① and ② receive equal and opposite forces (action and reaction, Section 3), so a strut gives \(S\) on ① and \(-S\) on ②, a hinge gives \(G_H, G_V\) on one side and the opposites on the other, and the parallel motion gives \(N\) and \(M\) on one side and their opposites on the other.

Going further · the counting rule for several bodies

Let \(v\) be the number of connection (joint) reactions. For \(n\) rigid bodies in a plane, each body gives 3 equilibrium equations, so there are \(3n\) equations for \(r+v\) unknowns:

\[x=r+v-3n\]

\(x\lt0\): movable (a mechanism); \(x=0\): statically determinate, if the arrangement does not allow motion; \(x\gt0\): statically indeterminate of degree \(x\).

In words: all the unknown forces (at supports and at joints) against all the equations (three per body).

Reading the formula
\(r\)
reactions at the supports, such as the floor under each leg of the stepladder.
\(v\)
forces and moments passed at the joints: the stepladder's top hinge passes 2.
\(3n\)
three equations for each of the \(n\) bodies.
\(x\)
as in Section 11: negative means it moves, zero means determinate (if it cannot move), positive means indeterminate.

Statically determinate or indeterminate? Two structures to try are solved as group work in Section 16.

You can now: say what a strut, a hinge and a parallel guide pass between two bodies; draw equal and opposite joint forces when you cut a structure into parts; and count \(x=r+v-3n\) for a multi-part structure.

14Common mistakes ~2 min

✗Putting both forces of an action–reaction pair on one free-body diagram. ✓Each force of the pair acts on a different body; draw it only on the body it acts on. ✗Leaving out a reaction because "the load is vertical" (e.g. no \(A_H\) at a pin). ✓Draw every reaction the support can give; equilibrium will show it is zero. ✗Forgetting the moment \(M_A\) at a fixed support. ✓A wall that stops the beam turning must apply a couple. ✗Using the perpendicular distance from the wrong point, or the slant distance, in \(M=hF\). ✓\(h\) is measured perpendicular to the line of action, from the moment point. ✗Placing the resultant of a triangular load at \(l/3\) from the zero end. ✓It is \(2l/3\) from the zero end (\(l/3\) from the high end). ✗Calling a structure determinate because \(r=3\) without checking the arrangement. ✓Three parallel or three concurrent reactions leave it movable. ✗Averaging centroid coordinates without weighting by area. ✓\(x_c=\sum A_ix_i/\sum A_i\), not \(\frac1n\sum x_i\).

Q1 · two bars, a parallel guide and a roller

Bar \(AB\) is horizontal; its left end \(A\) is a plate on rollers against a vertical wall (vertical sliding on wall). \(AB\) is joined at \(B\) by a hinge to an inclined bar \(BC\), whose lower end \(C\) sits on a roller support on the ground.

the support at \(C\) is drawn as a pin on a base with two small rollers, read as a roller (one vertical reaction). If your copy shows a fixed pin at \(C\), use the second answer below.

Hint

Count with \(x=r+v-3n\): the support at \(A\) allows vertical sliding only (which reactions?), the hinge at \(B\) passes 2 forces, there are 2 bodies.

Answer

\(A\): horizontal force + moment, \(r_A=2\). \(C\) (roller): \(r_C=1\). Hinge \(B\): \(v=2\). Bodies \(n=2\).

\[x=(2+1)+2-3(2)=-1\]

As drawn, \(x\lt0\): the structure is movable (kinematically indeterminate, one degree of freedom): \(AB\) can slide up the wall while \(C\) rolls along the ground, like a ladder sliding down.

Q2 · three bars between two walls

Three bars \(AB\), \(BC\), \(CD\) in a horizontal line, joined by hinges at \(B\) and \(C\), and built into walls at \(A\) and \(D\).

Two fixed supports (3 each), two hinges (2 each), three bodies.

Answer
\[x=(3+3)+(2+2)-3(3)=1\]

17Concept checks ~1 min

1 · Sliding a force

On a rigid body, may a force be moved to a different point on its line of action? To a parallel line?

Answer

Along its own line: yes, the effect on equilibrium is unchanged. To a parallel line: only if you add a couple equal to the force times the distance moved (Section 6, step 1).

2 · A couple on a beam

A simply supported beam (pin \(A\), roller \(B\), span \(l\)) carries only a clockwise couple \(M_0\) at mid-span. What are the reactions?

Hint

Moments about \(A\). The reactions must form a couple themselves.

Answer

\(\sum M^{(A)}=lB_y-M_0=0\Rightarrow B_y=M_0/l\) up, \(A_y=-M_0/l\) (i.e. \(M_0/l\) down), \(A_x=0\). The answer does not depend on where the couple sits.

3 · Which support?

A beam end must be free to slide horizontally but must not move vertically or rotate. Name the support and its reactions.

Answer

A sliding sleeve (clamped on wall with horizontal sliding): a vertical force and a moment, \(r=2\).

4 · Three equal moments

Why must \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) not lie on one line when using three moment conditions?

Answer

A single force along that line has zero moment about all three points, so all three equations would hold even though the body is not in equilibrium.

5 · Uniform pressure

A plate carries a uniform pressure \(p\). Where is the centre of force?

Answer

At the centroid of the loaded area, because \(p\) cancels from \(\int xp\,\mathrm dA/\int p\,\mathrm dA\).

6 · Degree of indeterminacy

A beam fixed at both ends, no hinges. What is \(x\)?

Answer

\(r=6\), \(n=1\): \(x=6-3=3\).
