  • Why C1 has so few of them: both courses go straight to transfer functions, so their questions are mostly the maths-heavy design of handout C2. Only three written parts need nothing beyond C1 ideas: Aug 2010 Q4(e) under C1.4 and Dec 2011 Q3(a)(iv), Q4(v) under C1.6. The maths-heavy questions are on guide C2a (models and responses) and guide C2b (stability and design); related-course topics beyond MECH2413 are on guide Cx.
  • What the handout's worked examples suggest (the source of the practice questions): the handout's aims (slide 1), its one numerical worked example (slide 30: compute the pump command), its one equilibrium calculation (slide 25: why P stops below target), the gain table (slide 29), the response measures (slide 15), the four "check your understanding" questions (slide 34) and the review list (slide 35). Those are the things a lecturer can test without new material, so treat the types below as likely styles, not a pattern.
  • How to spot each style: loop vocabulary and a story about a system → C1.1; numbers for target, level, rate and gains → C1.2; "steady state" with P only → C1.3; "what happens if \(K\) is increased" or a disturbance test → C1.4; a plotted response → C1.5; "explain", "comment", "advantages and disadvantages" → C1.6.
  • MECH2413 papers 2014–2024 (statics and dynamics only): 3 h · any five of six 20-mark questions · at least two from each section (A statics, B dynamics) · about 36 min per question. 2026/27 changes: orbital dynamics and re-entry are removed (D1 handout, slide 7) and control is added.
  • Formula sheet: the 2021–2024 MECH2413 sheet covered dynamics only and nothing about control; MECH2004 printed none. Do not expect the PID law to be printed: learn \(u=K_{\rm p}e+K_{\rm i}\int_0^t e\,\mathrm d\tau+K_{\rm d}\,\mathrm de/\mathrm dt\), \(e=r-y_{\rm m}\), and the overshoot formula by heart.
  • Time budget: at MECH2004's rate (20 marks in 40 min) a 4-mark written part is about 8 minutes: half a page, four or five marker points.
Sign convention (same as the notes)

Error \(e=r-y_{\rm m}\) (target minus measured output), so \(e\gt0\) means the output is below target. With a fixed target, \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt=-\mathrm dy_{\rm m}/\mathrm dt\). Tank: level \(h\) in cm, time in s, pump command \(u\) a fraction of full effort from 0 to 1 (the pump cannot run backwards). Gains carry units so every term of \(u\) is dimensionless: \(K_{\rm p}\) in cm⁻¹, \(K_{\rm i}\) in (cm·s)⁻¹, \(K_{\rm d}\) in s·cm⁻¹. Rise time 10% → 90% of the change; settling band ±2% of the change (slide 15). Rotation (Aug 2010 Q4): angle \(\theta\) and torques positive anticlockwise.

Free-body diagrams: the tank questions have no force systems to resolve, so block diagrams play that role (one for every solved question that has a loop in it). The one mechanical system here, the antenna of Aug 2010 Q4, gets its free-body diagram.

C1.1Loop roles; open vs closed loop

0 related-course parts · practice only · handout slides 5–14, 18, 35 · Learn it · note §7

Answer skeleton · likely 3–6 marks · no related-course part
  1. Diagram to draw (the figure below): \(r\) → Σ (+\(r\), −\(y_{\rm m}\)) → \(e\) → controller → \(u\) → actuator → plant → \(y\) → sensor → \(y_{\rm m}\) back to Σ. Disturbance arrow into the plant. One mark is usually for a labelled diagram: every arrow named.
  2. Name each role with this system's real object (1 mark per 2 or so): target (quantity and value, with units), output (the thing actually controlled, not the device), input/command, controller, actuator, plant, sensor.
  3. Open or closed? Apply the one test: does a measurement of the controlled output change the input? Say yes/no and give the reason in one line. Automatic ≠ closed; logging/displaying ≠ feedback.
  4. Disturbance / uncertainty / measurement problem if asked: one concrete example each, saying which way it pushes the output.
  5. Sign of the error if a reading is given: \(e=r-y_{\rm m}\), its sign, and which way the command must move.

Learn it: Notes §2 · target, input, output, §3 · four roles, §5 · feedback, §7 · open vs closed.

Ready answer · open or closed?

Closed loop if and only if the measured output is used to change the input. "Who decides" (person or device) is a separate question: manual closed loop (driver watching the speedometer) and automatic open loop (timer) both exist (slide 14).

Practice · written for these notes

Practice C1-1.1 · written for these notesabout 6 marksstyle of slides 6, 11, 18

An electric heater keeps the air in a greenhouse at 22 °C. A thermometer in the greenhouse reports the air temperature every 10 s, and a small computer sets the heater power from 0 to 100%.

(a) Draw the block diagram of this system, labelling the target, error, command, output, measured output, controller, actuator, plant and sensor. (b) Is it open or closed loop? Explain. (c) Give two disturbances and one measurement problem. (d) On a sunny afternoon the thermometer reads 25 °C. Find the error and say what the controller should do to the heater command. What can it not do?

Hint

Style C1-1. First step: decide what the output is (a temperature, not "the heater"), then place the heater and the greenhouse in the actuator and plant boxes.

Solution
  1. (a) Target \(r=22\) °C; error \(e=r-T_{\rm m}\); command \(u\) = heater power (0–1); controller = the computer program; actuator = the electric heater; plant = the greenhouse air; output \(y\) = actual air temperature \(T\); sensor = thermometer, giving the measured \(T_{\rm m}\) back to Σ.
  2. (b) Closed loop: the measured temperature changes the heater command every 10 s.
  3. (c) Disturbances: sunshine, a door left open, a cold night outside. Measurement problem: the thermometer sits in direct sun (bias, reads high) or near the heater; electrical noise on the reading.
  4. (d) \(e=22-25=-3\) °C: the air is 3 °C too warm, so the controller should reduce the heater command, down to 0. It cannot cool: the actuator is a heater, so with \(u=0\) the temperature falls only as fast as heat is lost (compare the pump that cannot pump water out, slide 16).

"Output = heater" scores nothing: the output is the quantity being controlled (air temperature), the heater is the actuator (slide 2).

\(\text{closed loop};\ e=-3\ ^\circ\text{C}\ \Rightarrow\ u\downarrow\ \text{(to 0 at most)}\)
Practice C1-1.2 · written for these notesabout 4 marksstyle of slides 14, 34 Q1

For each garden-watering arrangement say whether the control of soil moisture is open or closed loop, and whether it is manual or automatic. Give a one-line reason for each.

(i) A sprinkler runs for 20 minutes every morning on a timer. (ii) As (i), but a soil-moisture sensor now sends readings to a phone app, which stores them in a chart; the timer is unchanged. (iii) A gardener feels the soil each morning and decides how long to water. (iv) A valve opens whenever the soil-moisture sensor reads below a set value and closes when it reads above it.

Hint

Style C1-1. For each case ask only: does the moisture measurement change how long the water runs?

Solution
  1. (i) Open loop, automatic: a preset plan; moisture never affects watering (slide 8).
  2. (ii) Open loop, automatic: the readings are only monitored (stored), not used to change the input (slide 10).
  3. (iii) Closed loop, manual: the gardener measures, compares and adjusts; the person is the controller and the sensor.
  4. (iv) Closed loop, automatic: the measured moisture switches the valve. Beyond this on/off rule is called bang-bang control; it is a control law, but not PID.
\(\text{(i) open, auto · (ii) open, auto · (iii) closed, manual · (iv) closed, auto}\)

C1.2Compute a PID command

0 related-course parts · practice only · handout slide 30 · Learn it · note §17

Predictable · Pump command from given readings
Looks like
target, measured level, "rising/falling at … cm/s", a given integral contribution \(I\), and \(K_{\rm p}\), \(K_{\rm d}\); "find the pump command".
Do
\(e=r-h_{\rm m}\) → \(u_{\rm P}=K_{\rm p}e\) → \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt=-\mathrm dh_{\rm m}/\mathrm dt\) (fixed target) → \(u_{\rm D}=K_{\rm d}\,\mathrm de/\mathrm dt\) → \(u=u_{\rm P}+I+u_{\rm D}\) → clamp to 0–1 → say what each term is doing.
Watch
the D sign: a rising level gives a negative D term. Show units on each term (all dimensionless).

Learn it: Notes §11 · PID law, §14 · D term, §17 · worked example (slide 30).

Ready answer · the four lines \[\begin{aligned}e&=r-h_{\rm m}\\ u_{\rm P}&=K_{\rm p}\,e\\ u_{\rm D}&=-K_{\rm d}\,\frac{\mathrm dh_{\rm m}}{\mathrm dt}\\ u&=u_{\rm P}+I+u_{\rm D}\end{aligned}\]

In words: command = gain × how far below target now + the stored integral + gain × how fast the error is growing; then keep it between 0 and 1.

Practice · written for these notes

Practice C1-2.1 · written for these notesabout 6 marksstyle of slide 30

A tank level is held by a PID controller driving a pump, with the command \(u\) a fraction of full pump effort (0 to 1).

(a) Target \(r=45\u{cm}\) (fixed), measured level \(h_{\rm m}=41\u{cm}\), level rising at \(0.30\u{cm/s}\), integral contribution \(I=0.30\), \(K_{\rm p}=0.05\u{cm^{-1}}\), \(K_{\rm d}=0.40\u{s\,cm^{-1}}\). Find the pump command and say what each term is doing.

(b) Later the level has overshot: \(r=40\u{cm}\), \(h_{\rm m}=42\u{cm}\), level falling at \(0.15\u{cm/s}\), \(I=0.55\), \(K_{\rm p}=0.04\u{cm^{-1}}\), \(K_{\rm d}=0.50\u{s\,cm^{-1}}\). Find the pump command. Why is the pump still running although the level is above target?

Hint

Style C1-2. First step: the error, then convert "rising/falling" into the sign of \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt\).

Solution
  1. (a) \(e=45-41=4\u{cm}\); \(u_{\rm P}=0.05\u{cm^{-1}}\times4\u{cm}=0.20\).
  2. Rising level ⇒ \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt=-0.30\u{cm/s}\); \(u_{\rm D}=0.40\u{s\,cm^{-1}}\times(-0.30\u{cm\,s^{-1}})=-0.12\).
  3. \(u=0.20+0.30-0.12=0.38\) (38%). P pushes because the level is 4 cm low; I supplies the stored steady correction; D eases off because the level is already rising towards the target.
  4. (b) \(e=40-42=-2\u{cm}\) ⇒ \(u_{\rm P}=-0.08\). Falling level ⇒ \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt=+0.15\u{cm/s}\) ⇒ \(u_{\rm D}=+0.075\).
  5. \(u=-0.08+0.55+0.075=0.545\) (54.5%). The stored integral still holds most of the command needed to replace the outflow; P trims it because the level is high; D adds a little because the level is already falling and would otherwise drop below target.
\(\text{(a)}\ u=0.38\qquad\text{(b)}\ u=0.545\)
Practice C1-2.2 · written for these notesabout 6 marksBeyond discrete update, notes §11

A computer updates the tank's PID command every \(\Delta t=2\u{s}\) using \(I_k=I_{k-1}+K_{\rm i}e_k\Delta t\) and \(u_k=K_{\rm p}e_k+I_k+K_{\rm d}(e_k-e_{k-1})/\Delta t\), then limits \(u\) to 0–1. Gains: \(K_{\rm p}=0.04\u{cm^{-1}}\), \(K_{\rm i}=0.0005\u{(cm\,s)^{-1}}\), \(K_{\rm d}=0.6\u{s\,cm^{-1}}\). Target 40 cm. At the previous update \(e_{k-1}=3.0\u{cm}\) and \(I_{k-1}=0.42\).

(a) The level now reads 36.5 cm. Find \(I_k\), each term and the command sent. (b) The outlet is suddenly opened wider and at the next update the level reads 33 cm. Repeat. What does the pump actually receive, and what problem could the integral cause if this continues?

Hint

Style C1-2. First step: \(e_k=40-h\); update \(I\) before using it in \(u_k\).

Solution
  1. (a) \(e_k=3.5\u{cm}\). \(I_k=0.42+0.0005\times3.5\times2=0.4235\) (units (cm·s)⁻¹·cm·s = 1).
  2. \(u_{\rm P}=0.04\times3.5=0.14\); \(u_{\rm D}=0.6\times(3.5-3.0)/2=0.15\); \(u=0.14+0.4235+0.15=0.7135\), inside 0–1, so it is sent as it is.
  3. (b) \(e=7\u{cm}\). \(I=0.4235+0.0005\times7\times2=0.4305\); \(u_{\rm P}=0.28\); \(u_{\rm D}=0.6\times(7-3.5)/2=1.05\); sum \(=1.7605\).
  4. The pump cannot exceed full effort, so it receives \(u=1\) (saturated). If the level stays low for long, the integral keeps growing although the pump can do no more (integral windup); when the level finally recovers, the oversized integral causes a large overshoot (Notes §13, Beyond box).

The D term jumps to 1.05 because the error jumped in one step: a sudden change makes D give a large "kick" (notes §14, Beyond box).

\(\text{(a)}\ u=0.7135\qquad\text{(b)}\ 1.7605\to u=1\ \text{(saturated)}\)

C1.3Steady state under P only; what I must store

0 related-course parts · practice only · handout slide 25 · Learn it · note §12

Predictable · P-only equilibrium and offset
Looks like
"the plant holds \(h=(80\u{cm})\,u\) at steady state … P control with \(K_{\rm p}=\)… Find the final level / error / pump command"; "what gain keeps the error below …"; "what does I hold at the target?"
Do
write two steady-state relations, the plant's \(y_\infty=Gu_\infty\) (plus any offset such as outside temperature) and the controller's \(u_\infty=K_{\rm p}(r-y_\infty)\); solve the pair; check \(K_{\rm p}e_\infty=u_\infty\) and \(0\le u\le1\).
Watch
"at equilibrium \(e=0\)" is the classic wrong start: under P, \(e=0\) gives \(u=0\). Only I reaches zero error; it stores \(u_\infty=y_\infty/G\) (if that needs \(u\gt1\) the target is unreachable).

Learn it: Notes §12 · why P stops below the target, §13 · I term, §9 · flow balance.

Ready answer · P-only steady state \[\begin{aligned}y_\infty&=\frac{GK_{\rm p}}{1+GK_{\rm p}}\,r\\ e_{\rm ss}&=\frac{r}{1+GK_{\rm p}},\quad u_\infty=\frac{y_\infty}{G}\end{aligned}\]

In words: the final output is the fraction \(GK_{\rm p}/(1+GK_{\rm p})\) of the target; the offset shrinks as the loop gain \(GK_{\rm p}\) grows but never reaches zero. Checks: \(K_{\rm p}\to\infty\) gives \(y_\infty\to r\); \(K_{\rm p}\to0\) gives \(y_\infty\to0\). With PI the stored integral at the target is \(u_\infty=r/G\). (General formula: Beyond, notes §12; the handout does the 0.04 case only.)

Practice C1-3.1 · written for these notesabout 6 marksstyle of slide 25

The handout's tank holds a level \(h_\infty=(80\u{cm})\,u_\infty\) at steady state. It is run with P control only, target 50 cm, \(K_{\rm p}=0.05\u{cm^{-1}}\).

(a) Find the steady level, pump command and steady-state error. (b) What is the smallest \(K_{\rm p}\) that keeps the steady-state error at or below 2 cm? Why might you not want to use it? (c) A PI controller is used instead. What value does the integral contribution hold once the level has settled at 50 cm?

Hint

Style C1-3. First step: \(h_\infty=80\,u_\infty\) and \(u_\infty=0.05(50-h_\infty)\); eliminate \(u_\infty\).

Solution
plant: \(u=h/80\)controller: \(u=0.05(50-h)\)

The steady state is where both relations hold: the crossing at \(h=40\u{cm}\), \(u=0.5\).

  1. (a) \(h_\infty=80\times0.05\,(50-h_\infty)=4(50-h_\infty)\) ⇒ \(5h_\infty=200\) ⇒ \(h_\infty=40\u{cm}\).
  2. \(u_\infty=40/80=0.5\); check \(K_{\rm p}e=0.05\times10=0.5\) ✓. \(e_{\rm ss}=50-40=10\u{cm}\).
  3. (b) \(e_{\rm ss}=50/(1+80K_{\rm p})\le2\) ⇒ \(1+80K_{\rm p}\ge25\) ⇒ \(K_{\rm p}\ge0.3\u{cm^{-1}}\), six times the original gain. A gain that large typically gives much more overshoot or oscillation and saturates the pump (slide 29); it still leaves a 2 cm offset.
  4. (c) At 50 cm, \(e=0\) so P is zero, and the level is constant so D is zero. The integral alone must supply the command: \(I=50/80=0.625\).
\(h_\infty=40\u{cm},\ u_\infty=0.5,\ e_{\rm ss}=10\u{cm};\ K_{\rm p}\ge0.3\u{cm^{-1}};\ I=0.625\)
Practice C1-3.2 · written for these notesabout 7 markssteady state with a disturbance

A room heater has a steady-state model \(T_\infty=T_{\rm out}+(20\ ^\circ\text{C})\,u_\infty\): full power (\(u=1\)) holds the room 20 °C above the outside temperature \(T_{\rm out}\). The target is 22 °C. A P controller uses \(K_{\rm p}=0.25\ ^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\), with \(u\) limited to 0–1.

(a) With \(T_{\rm out}=10\) °C, find the steady room temperature, heater command and error. (b) The night cools to \(T_{\rm out}=4\) °C. Repeat, and comment on what the disturbance did to the offset. (c) A PI controller replaces P. Find the steady command it needs for \(T_{\rm out}=4\) °C, and explain what happens if \(T_{\rm out}\) falls to −2 °C.

Hint

Style C1-3. First step: \(T=T_{\rm out}+20\times0.25\,(22-T)\); solve for \(T\).

Solution
  1. (a) \(T=10+5(22-T)\) ⇒ \(6T=120\) ⇒ \(T=20\) °C; \(u=0.25\times2=0.5\); \(e_{\rm ss}=2\) °C. Check: \(10+20\times0.5=20\) ✓.
  2. (b) \(T=4+5(22-T)\) ⇒ \(6T=114\) ⇒ \(T=19\) °C; \(u=0.25\times3=0.75\); \(e_{\rm ss}=3\) °C. The colder night needs more heat; under P that extra command can only come from a larger error, so the offset grows from 2 to 3 °C.
  3. (c) PI holds \(T=22\) °C with \(u=(22-4)/20=0.9\), stored in the integral; fine since \(0.9\le1\).
  4. At \(T_{\rm out}=-2\) °C it would need \(u=(22+2)/20=1.2\gt1\): the target is unreachable. The heater saturates at full power, the room settles at \(-2+20=18\) °C, and the integral keeps growing (windup) while the error stays at 4 °C (slide 33, test B).
\(\text{(a) }20\ ^\circ\text{C},\ u=0.5\ \ \text{(b) }19\ ^\circ\text{C},\ u=0.75\ \ \text{(c) }u=0.9;\ 18\ ^\circ\text{C (saturated)}\)

C1.4What happens when a gain changes

1 related-course part · 4 marks · MECH2004 Aug 2010 Q4(e) · plus practice · handout slides 29, 31–33 · Learn it · note §16

Decision procedure · symptom → which gain, which way
You see / are askedDoTrade-off to state

too slow to reactraise \(K_{\rm p}\)more overshoot or oscillation; actuator saturates

Cue: change one gain at a time and compare the whole response and the command (slide 29). Traps: writing "always" (they are tendencies); claiming D or a bigger \(K_{\rm p}\) removes the offset; forgetting that the actuator has limits.

Learn it: Notes §16 · changing a gain (slide 29), §15 · four controllers + live tuner, §18 · two tests.

Ready answer · one line per gain (slide 29)

Raising \(K_{\rm p}\): stronger, faster correction and a smaller offset, but tends to more overshoot/oscillation. Raising \(K_{\rm i}\): offset removed sooner, but tends to more overshoot and longer settling. Raising \(K_{\rm d}\): tends to add damping and cut overshoot, but may slow the response and amplify measurement noise. Only I removes a constant steady-state error.

Ready answer · one gain, two specs (second-order loop) \[\begin{aligned}\omega_n&=\sqrt{K/J}\ \uparrow\ \text{with }K\\ \zeta&=\frac{B}{2\sqrt{KJ}}\ \downarrow\ \text{with }K\end{aligned}\]

In words: for a plant \(Js^2+Bs\) under P control, raising the gain makes the loop stiffer (higher natural frequency, so a shorter rise time) but leaves the damping force unchanged (lower damping ratio, so more overshoot). Rise time and overshoot trade off; \(\zeta\omega_n=B/2J\) does not move, so the settling time does not improve either. Derivative action is the second knob that adds damping. Beyond formulas: note C2 §9.

Past-paper parts (related course)

MECH2004 Aug 2010 (special exam) Q4(e)4 marksRelated courseBeyond the slides

Consider a control system for the elevation of a satellite-tracking antenna. The antenna and drive parts have a moment of inertia \(J\) and a damping \(B\); these arise to some extent from bearing and aerodynamic friction, but mostly from the back emf of the DC drive motor. The equations of motion are

\[J\ddot\theta+B\dot\theta=T_c,\]

where \(T_c\) is the torque from the drive motor. Assume that \(J=600{,}000\u{kg\,m^2}\) and \(B=20{,}000\u{N\,m\,s}\).

Parts (a)–(d), 16 marks, are solved on guide C2a: (a) the transfer function from \(T_c\) to \(\theta\) (C2.1); (b) with the feedback law \(T_c=K(\theta_r-\theta)\), the transfer function from \(\theta_r\) to \(\theta\) (C2.2); (c) the largest \(K\) for an overshoot \(M_p\lt10\%\) and (d) the values of \(K\) giving a rise time under 100 s while keeping the \(M_p\) limit (C2.4). Their results, used below: \(\dfrac{\Theta}{\Theta_r}=\dfrac{K}{Js^2+Bs+K}\); \(K\lt477\u{N\,m/rad}\) from (c); \(K\gt194\u{N\,m/rad}\) from (d).

(e) Comment on the relation between rise time and overshoot. (4 marks)

Hint

Style C1.4 (one gain, two effects). Write \(\omega_n\) and \(\zeta\) of the closed loop in terms of \(K\), then say which way each moves as \(K\) rises and what that does to the rise time and to the overshoot. The rise-time and overshoot formulas are in the orange Beyond box of note C2 §9; the P loop worked the same way is in note C2 §13.

Solution
\(K=194\u{N\,m/rad}\)\(K=477\u{N\,m/rad}\)\(K=1500\u{N\,m/rad}\)

Response of \(\theta/\theta_r\) to a unit step in \(\theta_r\). Raising \(K\) makes it rise sooner (65 s at \(K=477\), 27 s at 1500) but overshoot more (0.05%, 10%, 33%). The decay envelope \(e^{-t/60}\) is the same for all three.

  1. Free-body diagram and model (figure): torques on the antenna about its pivot are the drive torque \(T_c\) and the damping torque \(B\dot\theta\) opposing the motion, so \(J\ddot\theta=T_c-B\dot\theta\). With \(T_c=K(\theta_r-\theta)\) the closed loop is \(Js^2+Bs+K\), a second-order system. Every comment below comes from this one polynomial.
  2. Name the two parameters by matching \(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2\): \[\begin{aligned}\omega_n&=\sqrt{K/J}\\ \zeta&=\frac{B}{2\sqrt{KJ}}\end{aligned}\] Marker point 1: both depend on the same gain \(K\).
  3. Rise time: \(t_r\approx1.8/\omega_n\propto1/\sqrt K\), so raising \(K\) shortens the rise time (stiffer loop, faster response). Marker point 2.
  4. Overshoot: \(\zeta\propto1/\sqrt K\) and \(M_p=e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\) grows as \(\zeta\) falls, so raising \(K\) increases the overshoot. Marker point 3.
  5. The trade-off, with numbers: a shorter rise time and a smaller overshoot pull \(K\) in opposite directions. At \(K=477\u{N\,m/rad}\): \(\zeta=0.591\), \(M_p=10\%\), \(t_r\approx1.8/0.0282=64\u{s}\). At \(K=194\u{N\,m/rad}\): \(\zeta=0.93\), \(M_p\approx0.05\%\), \(t_r\approx100\u{s}\). Both specs hold only in the window \(194\lt K\lt477\u{N\,m/rad}\); no \(K\) gives \(t_r\lt64\u{s}\) with \(M_p\lt10\%\). Marker point 4.
  6. Why, and the way out: \(\zeta\omega_n=B/(2J)=1/60\u{s^{-1}}\) is fixed by the plant, so \(K\) cannot add damping; it only trades speed for oscillation (settling time \(\approx4.6/(\zeta\omega_n)\approx276\u{s}\) for every underdamped \(K\)). To get both, a second adjustable term is needed: derivative (velocity) feedback adds damping and raises \(\zeta\) without changing \(\omega_n\) (C1 slide 29; note C2 §14; compare Dec 2011 Q4(v)).

Model answer (exam length): From (b), \(\omega_n=\sqrt{K/J}\) and \(\zeta=B/(2\sqrt{KJ})\), both set by \(K\). Increasing \(K\) increases \(\omega_n\), so the rise time \(t_r\approx1.8/\omega_n\) falls, but it decreases \(\zeta\), so the overshoot \(M_p\) rises. Rise time and overshoot therefore conflict: a fast rise means a large overshoot. Here the 10% overshoot limit caps \(K\) at 477 N·m/rad, i.e. \(t_r\ge64\) s, and \(t_r\lt100\) s needs \(K\gt194\) N·m/rad. Because \(\zeta\omega_n=B/2J\) is fixed, proportional gain cannot improve both; derivative (rate) feedback would add damping so that \(K\) could be raised without extra overshoot.

"Comment" needs the direction of both effects and the reason (one gain sets \(\omega_n\) and \(\zeta\) together); quoting the \(K\) window from (c) and (d) shows the marker you have linked the parts.

\(\begin{aligned}&K\uparrow:\ \omega_n\uparrow\Rightarrow t_r\downarrow;\ \ \zeta\downarrow\Rightarrow M_p\uparrow\\&\text{trade-off: }194\lt K\lt477\u{N\,m/rad}\\&\text{both together: add derivative action}\end{aligned}\)

Reading used: the paper intends \(t_r\approx1.8/\omega_n\), which is poor at \(\zeta\approx0.93\). The exact 10–90% rise time is 167 s at \(K=194\u{N\,m/rad}\) and equals 100 s at \(K\approx305\u{N\,m/rad}\), so the true window is about \(305\lt K\lt477\u{N\,m/rad}\). The comment in (e) is unchanged.

C1.5Read a response curve

0 related-course parts · practice only · handout slide 15 · Learn it · note §8

Predictable · Rise time, overshoot, settling, steady-state error
Looks like
a step response plotted (or peak/final values given) and "determine the rise time, percentage overshoot, settling time and steady-state error", often for two controllers to compare.
Do
read start \(y_0\), final \(y_\infty\) and target \(r\) first → change \(=y_\infty-y_0\) → 10% and 90% levels → peak → ±2% band → last time outside the band → \(e_{\rm ss}=r-y_\infty\). State both conventions.
Watch
overshoot is measured from the final value, not the target, and divided by the change, not by the final value. With an offset these differ.

Learn it: Notes §8 · reading a response curve, §15 · comparing four controllers.

Ready answer · the four measures \[\begin{aligned}\text{OS}&=\frac{y_{\rm peak}-y_\infty}{y_\infty-y_0}\times100\%\\ e_{\rm ss}&=r-y_\infty\\ \text{band}&=y_\infty\pm0.02\,(y_\infty-y_0)\end{aligned}\]

In words: overshoot = how far past the end value it went, as a share of the distance it had to travel; steady-state error = target minus final output; settling time = when it enters the band and stays in it. Rise time = time from 10% to 90% of the change.

C1.6"Explain why" written answers

2 related-course parts · 7 marks · MECH2004 Dec 2011 Q3(a)(iv), Q4(v) · plus practice · handout slides 12, 25–28, 34 · Learn it · note §12–14

Answer skeleton · likely 2–5 marks each · related-course parts 3–4 marks
  1. State the term's rule in one line (1 mark): P ∝ present error, I = accumulated error, D ∝ rate of change of error.
  2. Apply it at the moment the question is about (1 mark): at steady state (\(e\) constant, \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt=0\)), at the target (\(e=0\)), or while the output is rising/falling.
  3. Link to the physics (1 mark): the tank needs inflow = outflow to hold a level, so a non-zero command is needed even at the target.
  4. Consequence (1 mark): offset, overshoot, zero D contribution, etc. A number (e.g. 30.48 cm, \(u=0.381\)) earns credibility.
  5. Caveat where a tendency is involved: "typically / for these gains / depends on the plant" (slide 29).

Model answer (exam length, 4 marks): "Why does a P controller leave the tank level below its 40 cm target?" A P controller sends a command proportional to the present error, \(u=K_{\rm p}e\). Water is continuously leaving through the outlet, so to hold any constant level the pump must keep supplying the same flow, i.e. the command must stay above zero. If the level reached 40 cm the error and hence the command would be zero, the pump would stop and the level would fall. The level therefore settles where the remaining error is just large enough to make \(K_{\rm p}e\) equal to the command that balances the outflow: for \(K_{\rm p}=0.04\u{cm^{-1}}\), 30.48 cm with \(u=0.381\). A larger \(K_{\rm p}\) reduces this offset but cannot remove it; integral action can, because its stored value holds the command at zero error.

Learn it: Notes §12–14 · P, I, D, §6 · negative feedback, §19 · slide 34 questions, §22 · common mistakes.

Ready answer · one-sentence reasons

P offset: zero error would mean zero command, but the outflow needs a non-zero command. D no help with offset: at steady state \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt=0\), so D contributes nothing. I removes offset: its stored value holds the command at zero error. I overshoots: it is still growing as the output reaches the target and must unwind through negative error. Negative feedback ≠ stable: right direction, but too much gain or delay over-corrects.

Ready answer · advantages and disadvantages
AdvantagesDisadvantages
Psimple; stronger, faster correction as \(K_P\) risesleaves an offset (needs \(e\ne0\) to hold a command); high \(K_P\) → overshoot, oscillation, saturation
PIremoves the steady-state error to steps and constant loads (stores the command); two gainspole at \(s=0\): order +1, more lag → more overshoot, slower settling; large \(K_I\) can destabilise; wind-up when saturated
PDadds damping (\(\zeta\uparrow\), \(\omega_n\) same) → less overshoot; lets \(K_P\) rise for speed; anticipates; locus moves leftno effect on steady-state error (D = 0 at steady state); amplifies noise; derivative kick; needs a filter
PIDzero offset (I) with damping (D) and speed (P)three gains to tune; inherits noise (D) and wind-up (I)

In words: I fixes the end of the response, D fixes the middle (the swing), P sets the speed; each fix has its price. In an exam answer give every point with its one-line reason.

MECH2004 Dec 2011 Q3(a)(iv)3 marksRelated course

3. (a) A cruise control system for a car is shown in Figure Q3(a), where \(G(s)\) and \(D(s)\) are, respectively, the transfer function of the car and the controller, \(R(s)\) is the desired speed and \(Y(s)\) is the actual speed of the car.

Figure Q3(a), redrawn: unity negative feedback.

Let \(G(s)=\dfrac{b}{s+a}\) and \(D(s)=K_P+\dfrac{K_I}{s}\).

Other parts of this question: (i) closed-loop transfer function, \(\omega_n\) and \(\zeta\), and (ii) PI design for a 10 s settling time and under 20% overshoot (C2.7); (iii) extending second-order results to higher order (C2.4); (v) effect of PI on root loci (C2.8); (b) Ziegler–Nichols open-loop tuning (Cx.5, beyond MECH2413).

(iv) Explain the advantages and disadvantages of PI controllers. (3 marks)

Hint

Style C1.6 (pros and cons). One advantage that only I can give, then what the integrator costs: think about what it stores, what it adds to the loop's order, and what happens when the throttle is already at its limit. Use the PI/PD ready answer.

Solution
  1. Advantage (the one marker point you must have): integral action removes the steady-state error to a constant demand or a constant disturbance (step in desired speed, a hill, a headwind). The integrator keeps growing while any error remains, and its stored value holds the throttle needed at zero error (C1 slides 26, 33; note C1 §13; note C2 §15). For this car, P alone leaves the fraction \(a/(a+bK_P)\) of a speed step as error; PI leaves none.
  2. Advantage (second): simple, only two gains, and \(K_P\) still gives the fast proportional response; that is why PI is the most widely used industrial controller.
  3. Disadvantage 1: the integrator adds a pole at \(s=0\): the loop's order rises by one (the first-order car becomes the second-order \(s^2+(a+bK_P)s+bK_I\)), adding lag, so the response typically overshoots more and settles more slowly (C1 slide 26).
  4. Disadvantage 2: too large a \(K_I\) lowers the damping and, in higher-order plants, can make the loop unstable; the PI zero at \(-K_I/K_P\) adds further overshoot beyond the \(\zeta\) prediction (see part (ii) on C2.7).
  5. Disadvantage 3: integrator wind-up: when the throttle saturates, the integral keeps growing and produces a large overshoot once the error changes sign (note C1 §13, Beyond box).

Model answer (exam length): Advantages: the integral term accumulates the error, so it eliminates the steady-state error to a step command or a constant load such as a hill, and it does so with only two gains to tune. Disadvantages: it adds a pole at the origin, raising the system order and the phase lag, so the response tends to overshoot more and settle more slowly; a large \(K_I\) reduces damping and may destabilise the loop; and if the actuator saturates the integrator winds up, causing a large overshoot.

3 marks: about one mark per correct point with its reason. "Removes steady-state error" is expected; "more overshoot / slower / less stable" and "wind-up" are the usual disadvantages. A bare list without reasons scores about half.

\(\begin{aligned}&\text{+ zero steady-state error to steps and constant loads}\\&\text{− extra pole at }s=0\text{: more overshoot, slower, can destabilise}\\&\text{− wind-up when the actuator saturates}\end{aligned}\)
MECH2004 Dec 2011 Q4(v)4 marksRelated course

4. The position control of a DC motor is modeled as a second-order system as below

\[G_p(s)=\frac{\omega_o^2}{s(s+2\zeta_o\omega_o)}.\]

We assume negative unity feedback for the closed loop.

Other parts of this question: (i) and (ii) how \(\omega_n\) and \(\zeta\) change under P and PD control (C2.4); (iii) whether PD can give zero steady-state error to a unit ramp (C2.5); (iv) PD design for \(\zeta=0.8\) and a 1 s peak time (C2.7); (vi) effect of PI on root loci (C2.8); (vii) Ziegler–Nichols closed-loop tuning (Cx.5, beyond MECH2413).

(v) Comment on the advantages and disadvantages of the proportional-plus-derivative control. (4 marks)

Hint

Style C1.6 (pros and cons). D acts on the rate of change of error: what does that do to the swing, what does it do at steady state, and what does it do to a fast-changing (noisy) signal? Use the PI/PD ready answer; part (ii)'s result is the evidence.

Solution
P: \(K_P=4\)PD: \(K_P=4,\ K_D=0.2\u{s}\)

Illustration (not part of the question), with the plant of part (iv), \(G_p=16/[s(s+8)]\): P alone gives \(\zeta=0.5\) and 16.3% overshoot; adding \(K_D=0.2\u{s}\) raises \(\zeta\) to 0.7 with the same \(\omega_n=8\u{rad/s}\) and cuts the overshoot to 5.2%.

  1. Advantage 1, more damping: with \(K_P+sK_D\) the closed-loop polynomial is \(s^2+(2\zeta_o\omega_o+K_D\omega_o^2)s+K_P\omega_o^2\): \(\omega_n=\omega_o\sqrt{K_P}\) is unchanged but \(\zeta=(2\zeta_o+K_D\omega_o)/(2\sqrt{K_P})\) rises, so overshoot and oscillation fall (C1 slide 27; note C2 §14).
  2. Advantage 2, speed without overshoot: because D supplies the damping, \(K_P\) can be raised for a faster response (and a smaller ramp error) while the overshoot stays acceptable: it breaks the P-only trade-off of Aug 2010 Q4(e). D also anticipates: it brakes as the error closes quickly, and its zero pulls the root locus left (better stability margin).
  3. Disadvantage 1, no help at steady state: at steady state \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt=0\), so D contributes nothing; PD does not change the system type and cannot remove a steady-state error (the ramp error of part (iii) stays finite, \(2\zeta_o/(\omega_oK_P)\)).
  4. Disadvantage 2, noise: the derivative's gain grows with frequency (\(|j\omega K_D|\)), so high-frequency measurement noise is amplified into a jumpy actuator command; in practice the derivative needs a filter (pure \(sK_D\) cannot be built exactly).
  5. Disadvantage 3, derivative kick: a step in the reference makes \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt\) very large for an instant, demanding a huge, saturating actuator effort; the added zero can also give some extra overshoot beyond the \(\zeta\) prediction (5.2% vs 4.6% for \(\zeta=0.7\) in the illustration).

Model answer (exam length): Advantages: the derivative term adds damping: \(\omega_n=\omega_o\sqrt{K_P}\) is unchanged while \(\zeta\) increases by \(K_D\omega_o/(2\sqrt{K_P})\), so overshoot and settling time are reduced and the stability margin improves. This allows a larger \(K_P\) for a faster response. Disadvantages: D is zero at steady state, so PD does not reduce the steady-state error (the system type is unchanged); it amplifies high-frequency noise, so it needs filtering; and a sudden change in the reference gives a large derivative kick that can saturate the actuator.

4 marks: two advantages and two disadvantages, each with its reason. Citing the \(\zeta\) formula from part (ii) turns "adds damping" from a claim into a shown result.

\(\begin{aligned}&\text{+ }\zeta\uparrow\text{ at fixed }\omega_n\text{: less overshoot, allows larger }K_P\\&\text{− no effect on steady-state error}\\&\text{− amplifies noise; derivative kick, needs a filter}\end{aligned}\)

Practice C1-6.2 · written for these notesabout 3 marksstyle of slide 12

"Because negative feedback always acts to reduce the error, a closed-loop system with negative feedback is always stable." Discuss.

Hint

Style C1-6. Negative feedback fixes the direction of the correction. What about its size and timing?

Solution
  1. Negative feedback subtracts the measurement from the target, so the action is in the direction that reduces the error: that part is true (slide 12).
  2. It says nothing about how hard or how late the correction is. With too much gain, a lag in the actuator or plant, or a delayed/noisy measurement, the correction overshoots, the error changes sign, the next correction overshoots the other way, and the swings can grow: unstable.
  3. So the statement is false: stability depends on the plant and the gains together (e.g. an over-eager shower user swinging between too hot and too cold).
\(\text{False: right direction, but gain and delay can still cause growing oscillation}\)
