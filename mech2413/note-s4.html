MECH2413 · Engineering Mechanics · Statics chapter 4

Bending of Beams: Normal Stress in a Beam Section

0Where this chapter sits

Bend a plastic ruler between your hands into a smile. The top face gets squashed, the bottom face gets stretched, and one layer in the middle keeps its length. This chapter (the handout's 27 slides) turns that picture into numbers: if the bending moment at a cut is \(M\), how hard is each layer pushed or pulled, and which property of the cross-section's shape controls it? That stress is what decides whether the beam breaks or yields.

Definition · normal stress

A normal stress \(\sigma\) is a force per unit area acting perpendicular to a surface. Positive \(\sigma\) is tension (pulling the surface outwards), negative is compression (pushing it in). Unit: pascal, \(1\u{Pa}=1\u{N/m^2}\); engineers usually use \(1\u{MPa}=10^6\u{Pa}=1\u{N/mm^2}\).

Sign convention for this chapter
  • \(x\) runs along the beam axis, through the centroid of every cross-section (the centroid is the "balance point" of the section shape, S1 Section 8).
  • In a cross-section: \(z\) points down, \(y\) points to the left (seen looking at the section from the front), so that \(x, y, z\) are right-handed. In a side view of the beam, \(x\) is to the right, \(z\) down, and \(y\) points out of the page (⊙). This is the handout's choice (slides 12, 15); past papers use it too.
  • \(\sigma\gt0\) is tension, \(\sigma\lt0\) compression.
  • Bending moment \(M\) (about the \(y\)-axis) is positive when it sags the beam (bottom stretched, top squashed), exactly as in S2. On the right-hand face of a piece in a side view, positive \(M\) is drawn anticlockwise.
  • With these choices \(\sigma=\dfrac{M}{I}z\): positive \(M\) gives tension where \(z\gt0\) (below the centroid).

1Bending and bending theory (slides 2–3) ~2 min

What is this?

Pull a plastic ruler by its two ends and it gets very slightly longer: loaded along its length, it is a bar. Hold it at the ends and press down on the middle and it curves: loaded across its length, it is a beam, and it bends. Bending theory asks what happens inside the ruler while it curves: which layers are pushed, which are pulled, and how hard.

The chapter's aim (slide 2) is the normal stress distribution across the section of a beam: how the stress varies from the top of a cross-section to the bottom.

Slide 2 recalls the difference between the two slender members of S1 Section 9: the same straight bar is a bar or rod if it is loaded along its axis, and a beam if the loads act across it.

Definition · bending, bending theory (slide 3)

If a beam is loaded by forces perpendicular to its axis, it deforms (curves). This is called bending of the beam. The study of the stresses and deformations this causes is called bending theory.

Slide 3 shows a simply supported beam (pin and roller) loaded by a point force \(F\) and a triangular distributed load \(q\). The undeformed beam is straight; the deformed beam (green) sags into a curve. This chapter finds the stresses; S5 finds the shape of the curve.

You can now: tell a bar (loaded along its axis) from a beam (loaded across it); define bending and bending theory.

2Assumptions 1–3: a straight beam with a plane of symmetry (slides 4–5) ~3 min

What is this?

Press down on the middle of a ruler, centred across its width, and it curves straight down in one plane. Press on one edge only, or bend a strip with an L-shaped section, and it also twists or swings sideways. Assumptions 1–3 keep us in the simple case: a straight ruler, the same on the left as on the right, loaded in its middle plane. Then every slice just tilts and nothing twists.

Bending theory as taught here rests on four assumptions. The first three are on slide 4:

  1. The beam is initially straight.
  2. There is a vertical plane of symmetry, and the bending moment \(M\) acts in that plane (or symmetrically about it). The cross-section looks the same in a mirror held along this vertical plane.
  3. The material properties are symmetric about that plane (e.g. the same steel on both sides).

Slide 4 draws two examples: a rectangular bar and an I-shaped beam, each with the moment \(M\) applied at both ends in the vertical plane of symmetry.

Consequence (slide 5): with assumptions 2 and 3 the deformation is also symmetric about the plane of symmetry. A cross-section that was symmetric stays symmetric after bending; it may tilt forwards or backwards, but it does not twist sideways. Slide 5 contrasts a symmetric deformation of a transverse section with an asymmetric one (the section warps and twists), which these assumptions rule out.

Definition · transverse section

A transverse section (cross-section) is a slice through the beam perpendicular to its axis, i.e. what you would see if you sawed the beam straight across.

Beyond the handouts · sections without a plane of symmetry

An L-section or Z-section has no vertical plane of symmetry. Bent about a horizontal axis, it also bends sideways (and may twist). That is unsymmetric bending; it is handled by first finding the section's principal axes (Section 13). It is not in this course's handout or past papers; just recognise that \(\sigma=Mz/I\) needs a symmetric section (or principal axes).

You can now: state assumptions 1–3; explain why a symmetric section loaded in its plane of symmetry bends without twisting; recognise an L- or Z-section as outside this theory.

3The neutral surface, and plane sections stay plane (slides 6–7) ~5 min

What is this?

Bend the ruler into a smile between your hands. The top face gets shorter (squashed) and the bottom face gets longer (stretched). Going down from top to bottom, the change in length passes smoothly from shortening to lengthening, so one layer in between keeps exactly its old length. That layer is the neutral surface. Where it meets a cut straight across the ruler is the neutral axis.

Bend a thick rubber eraser with your fingers. One face gets longer, the opposite face gets shorter. Somewhere in between there must be a layer that does neither.

Definition · neutral surface (slide 6)

In a bent beam, the material on one side of the axis is stretched (tension) and on the other side squashed (compression). In between there is a surface that is neither stretched nor squashed, so it carries zero stress. Before bending it is a flat horizontal plane, the neutral plane; after bending it is curved, the neutral surface. Where it cuts a cross-section is a line called the neutral axis.

Slide 6's moment bends the block so that it arches upward (a frown, hogging), so the upper part is in tension and the lower part in compression. For a sagging moment (a smile) it is the other way round. Either way, the neutral surface separates the two.

Assumption 4 (slide 7)

What is this? (plane sections)

Think of the ruler as a sliced loaf: a stack of thin slices. Bending tilts each slice a little against its neighbour, so the slices press closer together at the top and open apart at the bottom, like a fan. Each slice stays flat; it only tilts. That is all assumption 4 says, and it is what makes the stretch grow evenly with depth.

Assumption 4 · plane sections remain plane

Transverse sections (the vertical planes perpendicular to the plane of symmetry) remain planar and perpendicular to the neutral surface after bending.

In words: draw straight vertical lines on the side of a beam. After bending they are still straight, they just tilt so that they all point towards the centre of the curve, like the spokes of a wheel.

Slide 7 shows a block with end moments \(M\): before bending, the end faces are vertical and the dash-dot centre line is straight; after bending, the centre line is an arc and the end faces are still flat, now tilted to stay perpendicular to it.

Beyond the handouts · why the strain is linear in \(z\)

Assumption 4 is what makes the stress linear (slide 12 just states it). Let the neutral surface bend into a circular arc of radius \(\rho\) (the radius of curvature), and take two neighbouring sections an angle \(\mathrm d\theta\) apart. The fibre on the neutral surface keeps its length \(\rho\,\mathrm d\theta\). A fibre a distance \(z\) on the outside of the curve (for sagging, below the neutral surface) has length \((\rho+z)\,\mathrm d\theta\). Its strain (change in length ÷ original length, S3) is

\[\varepsilon=\frac{(\rho+z)\,\mathrm d\theta-\rho\,\mathrm d\theta}{\rho\,\mathrm d\theta}=\frac{z}{\rho}.\]
Reading the formula
\(\rho\,\mathrm d\theta\)
the length of the slice gap on the neutral layer. It does not change, so it is also every fibre's original length.
\((\rho+z)\,\mathrm d\theta\)
the length of a fibre a distance \(z\) further out on the curve: longer, because it goes round a bigger circle.
difference ÷ original
the strain: how much that fibre was stretched per unit length.
\(z/\rho\)
twice as far from the neutral layer, twice the stretch; a tighter bend (smaller \(\rho\)) stretches every fibre more.

With Hooke's law \(\sigma=E\varepsilon\) (S3, \(E\) = Young's modulus):

\[\sigma=\frac{E}{\rho}\,z.\]

In words: the further a fibre is from the neutral surface, the more it is stretched or squashed, in direct proportion. So the "unknown factor" \(c\) of slide 12 is \(E/\rho\). (Gere; Hibbeler.)

You can now: explain the neutral plane and neutral surface before and after bending, with a sketch (Dec 2016 A2(a)); state assumption 4; show that the strain is \(z/\rho\), so it grows linearly with distance from the neutral surface.

4Pure bending (slides 8–11) ~2 min

What is this?

Grip the ruler near each end and turn your wrists to bend it into a smile. Between your hands nothing pushes the ruler up or down; it only carries the turning effect of your wrists. That stretch is in pure bending: a bending moment and nothing else. It is the cleanest case, so the theory is built on it first.

Definition · pure bending (slide 8)

A part of a beam is in pure bending if the loads cause only a bending moment \(M\) there: no shear force \(V\) and no normal force \(N\). The loads must also act through the section centroid so that they cause no twisting (torsion, S6).

The handout's example (slides 8–11) is four-point bending: a simply supported beam with two equal downward forces \(F\), each a distance \(a\) from its support.

System (slide 8)

FBD: by symmetry each reaction is \(F\)

By symmetry each support carries \(F\). Using S2 (left piece: \(V\) = upward forces, \(M\) = clockwise moments about the cut):In the middle stretch the downward force's moment \(F(x-a)\) grows exactly as fast as the reaction's \(Fx\), so the growth cancels and \(M\) stays at \(Fa\).

\[\begin{aligned}0\lt x\lt a:&\quad V=F,\ \ M=Fx,\\ a\lt x\lt L-a:&\quad V=F-F=0,\\ &\quad M=Fx-F(x-a)=Fa,\\ L-a\lt x\lt L:&\quad V=-F,\ \ M=F(L-x).\end{aligned}\]

Four-point bending · SFD (slide 10) and BMD (slide 11), drawn for \(L=4a\)

Between the two forces \(V=0\) and \(M=Fa\) is constant: that stretch of the beam is in pure bending. Outside it there is also shear, so it is not.

In words: where the shear force is zero, the bending moment is flat (since \(\mathrm dM/\mathrm dx=V\), S2). The middle part of a four-point-bent beam carries a constant moment and nothing else, which is why materials labs use this test to measure bending strength.

You can now: define pure bending; find the pure-bending stretch of a beam from its SFD (where \(V=0\)); explain why four-point bending gives a constant moment \(Fa\) between the loads.

5Normal stress in a beam section: \(\sigma=cz\) (slides 12–13) ~5 min

What is this?

Back to the ruler bent into a smile. The further a layer is below the neutral layer, the more it is stretched; the further above, the more it is squashed, in proportion. Stress follows stretch, so a graph of stress against depth is a straight line through zero at the neutral axis. Points side by side at the same depth are stretched the same, so the stress does not change across the width.

Take a beam in pure bending (slide 12). The \(z\)-axis is an axis of symmetry of the cross-section, and the \(x\)-axis coincides with the beam axis and passes through the centroid \(C\) of the cross-sectional area \(A\). A tiny patch of area \(\mathrm dA\) on the section carries a normal stress \(\sigma\) along \(x\).

The handout states three facts about this stress (slide 12):

  • Only normal stresses \(\sigma\), along \(x\), act on the cross-section (no shear stress, because there is no shear force in pure bending).
  • They are independent of \(y\): every point at the same height carries the same stress.
  • They vary linearly with \(z\):
\[\sigma(z)=cz\]

In words: the stress is zero at the centroid height and grows in proportion to the distance above or below it; \(c\) is a constant of proportionality, still unknown.

Slide 12: \(\sigma(z)=cz\) on the cut face (\(c\gt0\))

Slide 13: the stresses add up to the moment \(M\)

With \(c\gt0\) (slide 12), points above the axis (\(z\lt0\)) are in compression, arrows pointing into the face; points below (\(z\gt0\)) are in tension, arrows pointing out.

Linking \(\sigma\) to \(M\) (slide 13)

What is this? (statically equivalent moment)

Imagine cutting the bent ruler. On the cut face the top layers push against the other half and the bottom layers pull on it. A push at the top and a pull at the bottom add up to no net force but a turning effect, a couple. That couple is the bending moment \(M\) at the cut.

The bending moment \(M\) at the cut is statically equivalent to the stresses: the stresses on the whole face, added up, must have the same moment about the \(y\)-axis as \(M\). (This is S2's idea that \(M\) is the "resultant couple" of the spread forces on a cut.) For one patch:

\[\begin{aligned}\mathrm dF&=\sigma\,\mathrm dA,\\ \mathrm dM&=z\,\mathrm dF=z\sigma\,\mathrm dA,\\ M&=\int_A z\sigma\,\mathrm dA=c\int_A z^2\,\mathrm dA.\end{aligned}\]

In words: each patch's force is stress × area; its moment about the neutral axis is that force × its lever arm \(z\); \(M\) is the sum over the whole section.

Reading the formula
\(\sigma\,\mathrm dA\)
the push or pull on one tiny patch of the cut face.
\(z\,\mathrm dF\)
its turning effect about the neutral axis: force × lever arm.
\(\int_A\)
add up every patch on the face.
\(c\int_A z^2\,\mathrm dA\)
since \(\sigma=cz\), the distance \(z\) enters twice: once in how hard the patch is pulled, once in its lever arm.
Definition · statically equivalent

Two sets of forces are statically equivalent if they have the same resultant force and the same resultant moment, so they would have the same effect on a rigid body.

Beyond the handouts · why the neutral axis passes through the centroid

In pure bending the normal force is zero, so the stresses must also add up to no net force:

\[N=\int_A\sigma\,\mathrm dA=c\int_A z\,\mathrm dA=cS_y=0.\]

Since \(c\ne0\), the first moment \(S_y=\int z\,\mathrm dA\) (Section 7) must vanish, which happens exactly when \(z\) is measured from the centroid. So the neutral axis passes through the centroid of the section. This is why the slide puts the \(x\)-axis through \(C\), and why you must find the centroid of a T- or U-section first (S1 Section 8). Dec 2016 A2(a) and Dec 2021 A2(c) test this.

You can now: state the three facts about \(\sigma\) on a section in pure bending; derive \(M=\int_A z\sigma\,\mathrm dA=cI\) (Dec 2021 A2(c)); explain why the neutral axis passes through the centroid.

6The bending (flexural) formula (slide 14) ~6 min

What is this?

The bending formula answers one question: for a given bending moment, how hard are the layers of the ruler pushed and pulled? Bend harder and every layer works harder. Use a shape that resists bending better (the ruler on edge instead of flat) and the same moment causes much less stress. The top and bottom faces always work hardest, because they are furthest from the neutral layer.

Definition · moment of inertia of the section (slide 14) \[I=\int_A z^2\,\mathrm dA\qquad(\text{unit m}^4)\]

is the moment of inertia (more precisely, the second moment of area) of the cross-section about the \(y\)-axis. It measures the ability of a structure to resist bending in a plane (the \(xz\)-plane here, the plane of the loads).

In words: add up every bit of area times the square of its distance from the neutral axis. Area far from the axis counts a lot (squared distance); area near the axis hardly counts.

slide 14 says "bending in a plane (xy plane in this case)". With the handout's axes the loads and the deflection lie in the \(xz\)-plane (the plane of symmetry); bending is about the \(y\)-axis. Both mean the same thing.

From \(M=c\int z^2\,\mathrm dA=cI\):

\[M=cI\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c=\frac MI\]
Result · the bending formula (flexural formula) \[\sigma=cz=\frac{M}{I}\,z\]

In words: bending stress = bending moment × distance from the neutral axis ÷ second moment of area. Double the moment → double the stress; double \(I\) → half the stress.

Reading the formula
\(M\)
how hard the beam is bent at this cut; positive when it sags (smiles).
\(z\)
how far the layer is from the neutral axis, measured down: positive below, negative above.
\(I\)
how well the shape of the section resists bending; area far from the axis counts most.
sign of \(\sigma\)
positive \(M\) and positive \(z\) give \(\sigma\gt0\): tension below the axis, the stretched bottom of the smiling ruler.

These stresses are called flexural or bending stresses. The largest values are at the fibres furthest from the neutral axis, the top and bottom surfaces.

Beyond the handouts · section modulus and curvature

Maximum stress. If the extreme fibre is at distance \(z_{\max}\), then \(|\sigma|_{\max}=\dfrac{|M|}{W}\) with the section modulus \(W=I/z_{\max}\) (for a rectangle \(W=bh^2/6\)). A section with the neutral axis off-centre (a T) has two values, one for the top and one for the bottom.

Curvature. Comparing \(\sigma=Ez/\rho\) (Section 3) with \(\sigma=Mz/I\):

\[\frac1\rho=\frac{M}{EI}\]

In words: the beam curves more where the moment is larger, and less where it is stiffer. The product \(EI\) is the flexural rigidity; S5 turns this into deflections.

Worked example 1 · written for these notes · rectangular beam in four-point bending

A simply supported beam, span \(L=2\u{m}\), carries two downward forces \(F=10\u{kN}\), each \(a=0.5\u{m}\) from a support. Its cross-section is a rectangle \(b=50\u{mm}\) wide and \(h=100\u{mm}\) deep. Find the bending stress at the top and bottom in the middle part, and draw the stress distribution.

System and section

FBD of the left part, cut in the middle region

  1. Do: Moment. Reactions \(F=10\u{kN}\) each. In the middle region (Section 4), \(M=Fa=10\u{kN}\times0.5\u{m}=5\u{kN\,m}\), sagging (positive). The stress at a cut depends only on the moment there, so find \(M\) where you want \(\sigma\) first.
  2. Do: Second moment of area (rectangle about its centroid, derived in Section 11): \[\begin{aligned}I&=\frac{bh^3}{12}=\frac{50\times100^3}{12}\\ &=4.167\times10^6\u{mm^4}.\end{aligned}\] \(I\) is how well this shape shares out the moment; the same \(M\) on a deeper section gives less stress.
  3. Do: Distances. Neutral axis at mid-depth: top \(z=-50\u{mm}\), bottom \(z=+50\u{mm}\). \(z\) is measured from the neutral axis, which sits at the centroid (the middle of a rectangle), and \(z\) points down, so the top is negative.
  4. Do: Stress (use N and mm, so the answer is in N/mm² = MPa): \[\begin{aligned}\sigma_{\rm bottom}&=\frac{5\times10^6\u{N\,mm}\times50\u{mm}}{4.167\times10^6\u{mm^4}}\\ &=+60\u{MPa},\\ \sigma_{\rm top}&=-60\u{MPa}.\end{aligned}\] Equal distances above and below give equal and opposite stresses: the top is squashed exactly as hard as the bottom is stretched.
  5. Do: Dimension check: (N·mm)(mm)/mm⁴ = N/mm² = MPa ✓. Sense check: a sagging beam is stretched underneath ✓. A sign slip in \(z\) swaps tension and compression; the picture of the smiling ruler catches it.

\(\boxed{\sigma_{\rm bottom}=60\u{MPa}\ \text{(tension)},\quad\sigma_{\rm top}=60\u{MPa}\ \text{(compression)}}\), varying linearly through zero at mid-depth.

With steel (\(E=200\u{GPa}\)), \(\rho=EI/M=200\times10^9\times4.167\times10^{-6}/5000=167\u{m}\): a very gentle curve.

Try it · stress across a section

Left: the section with its centroid (neutral axis, dashed). Right: \(\sigma(z)=Mz/I\), tension (+) to the right. Turn \(M\) negative (hogging) and the picture flips. For the T-section the neutral axis is nearer the wide flange, so the stress is largest at the bottom of the web.

You can now: use \(\sigma=Mz/I\) with this chapter's signs (\(z\) down, \(M\) positive when sagging) to find the stress at any depth; find the largest tension and compression on a section; work in N and mm to get MPa.

7Area properties: first moment of area (slides 15–16) ~3 min

What is this?

Cut the cross-section out of card and rest it on a knife edge along an axis. Area on one side tries to tip it one way, area on the other side the other way, and each bit tips harder the further it is from the edge. The first moment adds up these tipping effects. Put the knife edge through the centroid and the card balances: the first moment is zero.

The bending formula needs \(I\), a property of the shape of the cross-section only. Slide 15 lists the family of such shape properties:

  • First moment of area: a measure of how the area of a shape is distributed in relation to an axis.
  • Second moments of area: (a) rectangular moments of inertia, (b) the polar moment of inertia, (c) the product of inertia.
  • Radii of gyration.

Slide 16 sets up an area \(A\) in the \(y,z\)-plane, with \(z\) downward and \(y\) to the left, a reference point \(0\), and a small element \(\mathrm dA\) at \((y,z)\), a distance \(r\) from \(0\).

Definitions · centroid and first moments of area (slide 16)

The centroid \(C\) of the area has coordinates

\[y_c=\frac1A\int_A y\,\mathrm dA,\qquad z_c=\frac1A\int_A z\,\mathrm dA.\]

The integrals

\[S_y=\int_A z\,\mathrm dA,\qquad S_z=\int_A y\,\mathrm dA\]

are the first moments of area about the \(y\)-axis and about the \(z\)-axis. Unit: m³.

In words: a first moment is "area × distance", added up. \(S_y\) uses distances \(z\) measured from the \(y\)-axis. So \(S_y=Az_c\) and \(S_z=Ay_c\): the first moment equals the whole area times the centroid's distance from the axis.

Consequence

About an axis through the centroid, the first moment is zero (\(z_c=0\Rightarrow S_y=0\)). This is the fact used in Section 5 to put the neutral axis through the centroid.

The naming trap: \(S_y\) contains \(z\), and \(I_y\) (next section) contains \(z^2\). The subscript names the axis the distances are measured from, not the coordinate in the integral.

You can now: define the centroid and the first moments \(S_y\), \(S_z\); use \(S_y=Az_c\); explain why the first moment about a centroidal axis is zero; avoid the "\(S_y\) contains \(z\)" naming trap.

8Second moments of area (slides 17–19) ~4 min

What is this?

Bend the ruler lying flat and it flops easily. Turn it on edge and you can hardly bend it with your hands. Same material, same area: what changed is how far the material sits from the axis it bends about. The second moment of area scores a shape on exactly that, counting each bit of area times its distance squared. That is why steel beams are I-shaped: the flanges put most of the metal far from the neutral axis, where it is stretched and squashed most and so does the most work, and the thin web just holds them apart.

Integrals that contain the square of the distances of \(\mathrm dA\), or the product of the two distances, are called second moments of area, also called moments of inertia of the area (slide 17). (The name borrows from dynamics, D2, where the moment of inertia \(\int r^2\,\mathrm dm\) uses mass instead of area.)

Definitions · the three second moments (slides 17–18)

Rectangular moments of inertia: a measure of the ability of a structure to resist bending in a plane.

\[I_y=\int_A z^2\,\mathrm dA,\qquad I_z=\int_A y^2\,\mathrm dA.\]
What is this? (product of inertia)

Split the section into four quarters by the two axes. Area in the quarters where \(y\) and \(z\) have the same sign counts positive; area in the other two quarters counts negative. If the shape is a mirror image across either axis, every bit has a twin of the opposite sign and the total is zero. A non-zero value says the shape is lopsided across both axes at once.

Product of inertia:

\[I_{yz}=I_{zy}=\int_A yz\,\mathrm dA.\]
What is this? (polar moment)

Now twist the ruler about its long axis instead of bending it. What matters is how far each bit of area sits from the centre point of the section, in any direction. The polar moment adds up area × (distance from that point)². By Pythagoras that distance squared is \(y^2+z^2\), so it is just the two bending values added.

Polar moment of inertia (slide 18): a measure of a beam's ability to resist torsion (twisting, S6). With \(r^2=y^2+z^2\):

\[\begin{aligned}I_p&=\int_A r^2\,\mathrm dA\\ &=\int_A(z^2+y^2)\,\mathrm dA=I_y+I_z.\end{aligned}\]

In words: \(I_y\) adds area × (distance from the \(y\)-axis)², \(I_z\) adds area × (distance from the \(z\)-axis)², \(I_{yz}\) adds area × (one distance × the other), and \(I_p\) adds area × (distance from the origin point)², which is the sum of the first two. Units: m⁴ (or mm⁴, cm⁴).

Reading the formula
\(\mathrm dA\)
one tiny piece of the section.
\(z^2\)
its distance from the bending axis, squared: a piece twice as far out counts four times as much.
\(\int_A\)
add up every piece of the section.
unit m⁴
area (m²) × distance² (m²); a shape property only, nothing to do with the material.

The \(I\) of slide 14 is \(I_y\): bending by a moment about \(y\) stretches fibres in proportion to \(z\), so \(z^2\) appears.

Characteristics (slides 18–19)
  1. The value of a moment of inertia depends on the origin and on the orientation of the axes. (Always say which axes; usually axes through the centroid.)
  2. \(I_y\), \(I_z\) and \(I_p\) are always positive (squares of distances). The product of inertia \(I_{yz}\) may be positive, negative or zero.
  3. \(I_{yz}=0\) if one of the axes is an axis of symmetry of the area (slide 19).

Why (iii) holds (slide 19): if \(z\) is an axis of symmetry, every element \(\mathrm dA\) at \((+y,z)\) has a mirror twin at \((-y,z)\). Their contributions \(yz\,\mathrm dA\) and \((-y)z\,\mathrm dA\) cancel in pairs, so the whole integral is zero.

Analogy: \(I\) is like the "stiffness value" of a shape. A plank laid flat bends easily; the same plank on edge is much stiffer, because on edge its material sits far from the neutral axis. This is why steel beams are I-shaped: most of the metal is in the flanges, far from the axis, where it does the most good.

You can now: write the definitions and units of \(I_y\), \(I_z\), \(I_{yz}\) and \(I_p\); say which are always positive; show that \(I_{yz}=0\) when either axis is an axis of symmetry; explain why I-beams put their material far from the axis.

9Radii of gyration (slide 20) ~2 min

What is this?

Imagine packing the whole area of a shape into thin strips all at one distance from the axis. How far out would they have to sit to give the same \(I\)? That distance is the radius of gyration. It turns \(I\) into a length you can picture: large for an I-beam, whose flanges sit far out, and small for a ruler bent flat.

Definition · radius of gyration (slide 20) \[\begin{aligned}r_{gy}&=\sqrt{\frac{I_y}{A}},\qquad r_{gz}=\sqrt{\frac{I_z}{A}},\\ r_{gp}&=\sqrt{\frac{I_p}{A}}.\end{aligned}\]

These have the dimension of a length. Equivalently \(I_y=Ar_{gy}^2\): the radius of gyration is the distance at which the whole area would have to be concentrated to give the same second moment.

In words: the "effective distance" of the area from the axis. Slide 20's picture: it is the radius of a uniform thin hoop (ring) having the same moment of inertia, about an axis through its centre, as the given body about the specified axis. In a thin hoop every bit of material sits at the same distance \(r=k\) from the centre, so \(I=Ak^2\) exactly.

It is sometimes more practical to use \(r_g\) than \(I\), for example to compare how efficiently different shapes use their area (a larger \(r_g\) for the same area means a stiffer shape).

Beyond the handouts · where you meet it again

The radius of gyration reappears in column buckling (slenderness ratio \(L/r_g\)) and, with mass instead of area, in D2 (\(I=mk^2\) for flywheels).

You can now: find \(r_g=\sqrt{I/A}\) about any axis; explain it as the distance at which the whole area gives the same \(I\) (the thin-hoop picture); use it to compare how well shapes use their area.

10Composite areas (slide 21) ~1 min

What is this?

Build a section out of simple pieces, like gluing three strips of ruler together into an I. Each strip adds its own share of \(I\), and the shares simply add up, as long as every share is measured about the same axis: the one the whole section bends about.

An area \(A\) is often made of several simple parts \(A_i\) whose moments of inertia are known (slide 21 draws a section built from four rectangles \(A_1\) to \(A_4\)). Because an integral over the whole area is the sum of the integrals over the parts:

\[\begin{aligned}I_y&=\int_A z^2\,\mathrm dA\\ &=\int_{A_1}z^2\,\mathrm dA+\int_{A_2}z^2\,\mathrm dA+\cdots=\sum I_{y_i},\end{aligned}\]

and similarly

\[I_z=\sum I_{z_i},\qquad I_{yz}=\sum I_{yz_i}.\]

In words: the second moment of a built-up shape is the sum of the second moments of its pieces, provided every piece's value is taken about the same axes \(y, z\) (here through point 0).

Watch

Tables give each piece's \(I\) about its own centroid. Before adding, move each one to the common axes with the parallel-axis theorem (Section 13). Adding centroidal values directly is the most common mistake in this topic.

You can now: split a built-up section into simple pieces and add their second moments and products of inertia about common axes; say why table values about each piece's own centroid must be moved first.

11Example 1: the rectangle (slides 22–24) ~3 min

What is this?

The rectangle is the ruler's own cross-section. Slice it into thin horizontal strips: each strip sits at one depth, so its share is easy, area × depth². Adding the strips from top to bottom gives \(bh^3/12\). The cube on the depth is the "ruler on edge" effect written as a formula.

A rectangle of width \(b\) and height \(h\), centroid \(C\), axes \(y\) (left) and \(z\) (down) through \(C\). Find all the second moments and radii of gyration.

\(I_y\) (slide 22). Slice the rectangle into thin horizontal strips of thickness \(\mathrm dz\) at depth \(z\). Every point of a strip has the same \(z\), and its area is \(\mathrm dA=b\,\mathrm dz\):

\[\begin{aligned}I_y&=\int_A z^2\,\mathrm dA=\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}z^2\,b\,\mathrm dz\\ &=\frac{bz^3}{3}\Big|_{-h/2}^{h/2}=\frac{bh^3}{12}.\end{aligned}\]

\(I_z\) (slide 23). Exchanging \(b\) and \(h\) (vertical strips instead) givesAbout the vertical axis the width \(b\) is now the dimension across the axis, so it is the one cubed.

\[I_z=\frac{hb^3}{12}.\]

\(I_{yz}\). The \(z\)-axis is an axis of symmetry, so \(I_{yz}=0\).

Polar moment.

\[\begin{aligned}I_p&=I_y+I_z=\frac{bh^3}{12}+\frac{hb^3}{12}\\ &=\frac{bh}{12}\left(h^2+b^2\right).\end{aligned}\]

Radii of gyration (slide 24). With \(A=bh\) and the diagonal \(d=\sqrt{b^2+h^2}\):

\[\begin{aligned}r_{gy}&=\sqrt{\frac{bh^3/12}{bh}}=\frac{h}{2\sqrt3},\\ r_{gz}&=\frac{b}{2\sqrt3},\quad r_{gp}=\frac{d}{2\sqrt3}.\end{aligned}\]
Result · rectangle about its centroid \[I_y=\frac{bh^3}{12}\]
Reading the formula
\(b\)
the width, along the bending axis: double it and \(I\) doubles.
\(h^3\)
the depth, across the bending axis: double it and \(I\) grows 8 times.
\(12\)
what integrating \(z^2\) from \(-h/2\) to \(h/2\) leaves: \(2\cdot(h/2)^3/3=h^3/12\).

In words: width × height³ ÷ 12, where "height" is the side perpendicular to the bending axis. The cube is why depth matters so much: doubling the depth makes \(I\) 8 times larger, and for the same \(M\) the largest bending stress drops to a quarter (since \(\sigma_{\max}=M/W\) with \(W=bh^2/6\)).

Example · written for these notes · plank flat or on edge

A 200 mm × 50 mm plank. On edge (\(b=50\), \(h=200\)): \(I=50\cdot200^3/12=33.3\times10^6\u{mm^4}\). Flat (\(b=200\), \(h=50\)): \(I=200\cdot50^3/12=2.08\times10^6\u{mm^4}\). Same wood, 16 times stiffer on edge (ratio \((200/50)^2=16\)).

You can now: derive \(I_y=bh^3/12\) by horizontal strips (Dec 2021 A1(b)); write \(I_z\), \(I_p\) and the radii of gyration of a rectangle; explain why depth matters far more than width.

12Example 2: the circle (slides 25–27) ~2 min

What is this?

A round bar looks the same from every direction, so it resists bending equally about every axis through its centre. So it is easier to first measure how far the area sits from the centre point, using thin rings like the growth rings of a tree trunk, and then share that equally between the two bending axes.

A circular area of radius \(R\), centroid \(C\). Find \(I_y\), \(I_z\), \(I_{yz}\), \(I_p\) and the radii of gyration (slide 25).

Symmetry first (slide 26). Every diameter is an axis of symmetry, so the moments of inertia about every axis through \(C\) are equal: \(I_y=I_z\), and \(I_{yz}=0\). Then \(I_p=I_y+I_z=2I_y\), i.e.

\[I_y=I_z=\tfrac12I_p.\]

Polar moment (slide 27). \(I_p\) is easy because \(r\) is the natural variable: slice the circle into thin rings of radius \(r\) and width \(\mathrm dr\), area \(\mathrm dA=2\pi r\,\mathrm dr\):Every point of a thin ring is the same distance \(r\) from the centre, so its share is just \(r^2\) × its area.

\[I_p=\int_A r^2\,\mathrm dA=\int_0^R r^2\,2\pi r\,\mathrm dr=\frac\pi2R^4.\]

Hence

\[I_y=I_z=\tfrac12I_p=\frac\pi4R^4.\]

Radii of gyration. With \(A=\pi R^2\):

\[\begin{aligned}r_{gy}&=r_{gz}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi R^4/4}{\pi R^2}}=\frac R2,\\ r_{gp}&=\sqrt{\frac{\pi R^4/2}{\pi R^2}}=\frac{R}{\sqrt2}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: for bending a round bar, \(I=\pi R^4/4\) (or \(\pi D^4/64\) with diameter \(D\)); for twisting it (S6), \(I_p=\pi R^4/2\) (\(\pi D^4/32\)). Finding the polar value first and halving it is the slick route.

13Parallel axes and built-up sections Beyond ~6 min

What is this?

Take one flange of an I-beam. About its own middle line it is a thin plate and hardly resists bending. Sitting far from the beam's neutral axis, it counts a lot. The parallel-axis theorem adds exactly that extra: the piece's area times the distance it has been moved, squared.

The handout's composite rule (Section 10) needs every piece's moment of inertia about the same axes, but its examples only give values about each piece's own centroid. The bridge is the parallel-axis theorem (also called Steiner's theorem). Past papers (Dec 2022 A1(c), Dec 2017 A1(d)) cannot be done without it.

Beyond the handouts · parallel-axis theorem (Gere; Hibbeler; Gross et al.)

Let \(\bar y,\bar z\) be axes through the centroid \(C\) of an area \(A\), and let \(y,z\) be parallel axes such that \(C\) sits at \((y_c,z_c)\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}I_y&=I_{\bar y}+z_c^2A,\\ I_z&=I_{\bar z}+y_c^2A,\\ I_{yz}&=I_{\bar y\bar z}+y_cz_cA.\end{aligned}\]
Reading the formula
\(I_{\bar y}\)
the piece's own second moment about its own centroid (the table value).
\(z_c^2A\)
the extra for sitting a distance \(z_c\) away from the new axis; never negative.
\(y_cz_cA\)
for the product of inertia: both offsets, with their signs, so this term can be negative.

In words: moment of inertia about any axis = moment about the parallel axis through the centroid + area × (distance between the axes)². The product of inertia gains area × (both offsets multiplied), with their signs.

Proof for \(I_y\): with \(z=\bar z+z_c\),

\[\begin{aligned}I_y&=\int(\bar z+z_c)^2\,\mathrm dA\\ &=\int\bar z^2\,\mathrm dA+2z_c\int\bar z\,\mathrm dA+z_c^2\int\mathrm dA,\end{aligned}\]

and the middle integral is the first moment about a centroidal axis, which is zero (Section 7).

Consequence: the centroidal axis gives the smallest moment of inertia of all parallel axes, since \(z_c^2A\ge0\).

Worked example 2 · written for these notes · T-section

A T-section: flange 100 mm wide × 20 mm thick on top of a web 20 mm wide × 100 mm deep. It carries a sagging moment \(M=4\u{kN\,m}\). Find the neutral axis, \(I\), and the largest tensile and compressive stresses.

Section (dimensions in mm)

Stress distribution

  1. Do: Centroid (S1 Section 8), measured up from the bottom: flange area 2000 mm² at 110 mm, web 2000 mm² at 50 mm: \[\begin{aligned}\bar z_{\rm bottom}&=\frac{2000(110)+2000(50)}{4000}\\ &=80\u{mm}.\end{aligned}\] The neutral axis is 80 mm above the bottom, 40 mm below the top. The neutral axis passes through the centroid (Section 5), so every \(z\) must be measured from here, not from the bottom.
  2. Do: \(I\) about the neutral axis, each piece by the parallel-axis theorem (both centroids are 30 mm from the neutral axis): \[\begin{aligned}I&=\Big[\tfrac{100\cdot20^3}{12}+2000\cdot30^2\Big]\\ &\quad+\Big[\tfrac{20\cdot100^3}{12}+2000\cdot30^2\Big]\\ &=(0.0667+1.8)\times10^6\\ &\quad+(1.6667+1.8)\times10^6\\ &=5.333\times10^6\u{mm^4}.\end{aligned}\] Each \(bh^3/12\) is about the piece's own centroid; the \(Ad^2\) term moves it to the axis the whole T bends about.
  3. Do: Stresses with \(M=4\times10^6\u{N\,mm}\): top \(z=-40\u{mm}\), bottom \(z=+80\u{mm}\): \[\begin{aligned}\sigma_{\rm top}&=\frac{4\times10^6(-40)}{5.333\times10^6}=-30\u{MPa},\\ \sigma_{\rm bottom}&=\frac{4\times10^6(80)}{5.333\times10^6}=+60\u{MPa}.\end{aligned}\] Using the signed \(z\) (down positive) gives the sign directly: negative above the axis means compression, as on top of a sagging beam.

\(\boxed{\text{N.A. 80 mm above the bottom},\ I=5.33\times10^6\u{mm^4},\ \sigma_{\max}=60\u{MPa}\text{ (tension, bottom)},\ 30\u{MPa}\text{ (compression, top)}}\)

In words: the stress is still linear and zero at the centroid; since the bottom is twice as far from the neutral axis as the top, it carries twice the stress. Turn the T upside down (or apply a hogging moment) and the 60 MPa becomes compression.

The L of S1 slide 39: a bottom bar 5 × 1 and an upright 2 × 3 standing on its left end (units of length). Axes \(x\) right, \(y\) up (the axis names used in Dec 2022 A1(c)). Find the product of inertia about axes through the centroid parallel to the edges.

Beyond the handouts · principal axes

Rotating the axes changes \(I_y\), \(I_z\) and \(I_{yz}\) by exactly the same transformation rules as the stresses \(\sigma_x\), \(\sigma_y\), \(\tau_{xy}\) in S3, so a Mohr's circle can be drawn for them. At one orientation the product of inertia is zero: these are the principal axes of the area, with the largest and smallest second moments. An axis of symmetry is always a principal axis, which is why the handout's symmetric sections need none of this. Not examined in MECH2413.

15Common mistakes

✗Measuring \(z\) from the bottom (or top) of the section in \(\sigma=Mz/I\). ✓\(z\) is measured from the neutral axis, which passes through the centroid. Find the centroid first. ✗Using \(hb^3/12\) when bending is about the horizontal axis. ✓The cubed dimension is the one perpendicular to the bending axis (the depth, for ordinary bending). ✗Adding each piece's centroidal \(I\) to get a composite \(I\). ✓Add \(I_C+Ad^2\) for every piece, with \(d\) measured to the composite neutral axis. ✗Assuming a product of inertia must be positive. ✓\(I_{yz}\) takes the sign of \(yz\) where the area mostly sits; it is zero if either axis is a symmetry axis. ✗Mixing units: kN·m with mm⁴. ✓Work in N and mm throughout: N·mm × mm / mm⁴ = N/mm² = MPa. ✗Giving only one maximum stress for a T-section. ✓Top and bottom are at different distances, so tension and compression maxima differ. ✗Confusing \(S_y\) (first moment, m³) with \(I_y\) (second moment, m⁴). ✓Check the units: one power of distance per moment order plus the area's two.

17Concept checks

Written for these notes. (This handout has no group-work slides.)

1 · Where is the stress zero?

A beam with a U-shaped (channel) cross-section, opening upwards, is bent. Where is the bending stress zero?

Answer

On the neutral axis, which passes through the centroid of the U. That is not at mid-height: the centroid is pulled towards the heavy bottom bar.

2 · Doubling

A rectangular beam's width is doubled and its depth is halved. For the same moment, how does the largest bending stress change?

Answer

\(\sigma_{\max}=M/W\), \(W=bh^2/6\). New \(W=(2b)(h/2)^2/6=W/2\), so the stress doubles.

3 · Pure bending

On a cantilever with a single point load at its free end, is any part in pure bending?

Answer

No. The shear force is the same non-zero value everywhere. A cantilever loaded only by a couple at its free end would be in pure bending along its whole length.

4 · Product of inertia

Which of these has \(I_{yz}=0\) about centroidal axes parallel to its edges: a rectangle, an I-section, a T-section, an L-section?

Answer

Rectangle, I and T (each has at least one axis of symmetry). The L has none, so \(I_{yz}\ne0\) (worked example 3).

5 · Radius of gyration

A hollow circular tube and a solid bar have the same area. Which has the larger radius of gyration, and why does that matter?

Answer

The tube: its material is further from the centre, so \(I\) and \(r_g=\sqrt{I/A}\) are larger. For the same mass of material it resists bending (and twisting) better.

6 · Units

What are the units of \(S_y\), \(I_y\), \(r_g\) and \(\sigma\)?

Answer

m³, m⁴, m, and Pa (N/m²).

Given the normal stress distribution in the cross-sectional area, \(\sigma(z)=cz\), how to calculate a statically equivalent bending moment?

Dec 2016 A2(a)4 marksS4.2 neutral surface (written)

Explain the neutral plane or surface in a beam before and after bending. Use sketches if necessary.

Hint

Slides 6–7: which side is stretched, which squashed, what lies between, where is it?

Answer
  • When a beam bends, fibres on one side lengthen (tension) and on the other shorten (compression).
  • Between them is a surface whose fibres keep their length, so it carries zero bending stress: the neutral surface.
  • Before bending it is a flat horizontal plane (the neutral plane); after bending it is curved (an arc of radius \(\rho\)), and transverse sections stay plane and perpendicular to it.
  • It passes through the centroid of each cross-section (since \(\int\sigma\,\mathrm dA=0\)); its trace on a section is the neutral axis, where \(\sigma=Mz/I=0\).
