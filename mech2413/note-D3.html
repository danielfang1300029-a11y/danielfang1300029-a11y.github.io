Dynamics of Rockets

0Where this chapter sits

A rocket is a person on a skateboard throwing bricks backwards: each throw pushes them forward, and the pile on board shrinks as they go. This chapter turns that picture into Newton's law for a body that loses mass, and into the rocket equation.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 Follow all the mass, bricks included. Momentum of you plus the bricks is what Newton's law tracks.
  2. §2 Speed gained = throw speed × ln(mass ratio). Later throws help more because you are lighter.
  3. §3 Derive it from one throw: momentum after − momentum before = gravity's impulse. Asked almost every paper
  4. §4 Thrust = throw speed × mass thrown per second. Integrate for the burnout speed, minus the gravity loss \(gt\).
  5. §5 Three true-or-false traps on burn time, payload and exhaust speed.
  6. §6 The Saturn V, worked in full.
  7. §7 Anything that throws mass away is pushed; mass that just drops off gives no push.
  8. §8 Better fuel means harder throws: hot, light exhaust.
  9. §9 Stages: drop the empty crate so later throws push less dead weight. Formula not examined 2026/27
  10. §10 Orbits and satellites. Out of 2026 syllabus Background only

You need: Newton's second law, momentum and impulse from D1 (where mass never changed), and \(\int\mathrm dM/M=\ln M\). In the exam: one Section B rocket question: derive the rocket equation, then thrust, burnout time, burnout speed or fuel needed. See the D3 exam guide.

Some context first. Rockets such as China's Long March and SpaceX's Falcon 9 climb on their own exhaust plumes. U.S. rockets have evolved through many families since the 1950s, and the cost of putting 1 kg into low Earth orbit has fallen steeply: on a logarithmic scale it goes from tens of thousands of dollars per kilogram (Space Shuttle era) towards a few hundred (Falcon Heavy, Starship), and it is projected to keep falling. Orbital launch attempts per year since 1957 show a space age coming back: a peak of about 120–140 a year in the Soviet era, a dip to about 50–60 around 2000–2005, and a sharp rise after 2015 driven by the USA and China, back to more than 140 a year by the early 2020s.

Syllabus change for 2026/27

Orbital dynamics (circular, elliptic and escape orbits, orbit transfer) and atmospheric re-entry were removed from the course from 2026/27. Only a short look at satellites remains (Section 10), as background. Past-paper questions on orbits and re-entry are listed in the exam guide as "no longer examined" and are not used on this page.

Formula sheet

The Dec 2021–Dec 2024 papers print a Section B formula list (relative velocity, \(I=\int r^2\,\mathrm dm\), orbit formulas), but it does not give the rocket equation or the thrust formula. Older papers (Dec 2016–May 2019) printed \(\Delta V=V_{ex}\ln(M_i/M_f)\) (with \(-gt\) in 2018/19). Learn the equation and its derivation: the derivation alone is a 4–10 mark question on almost every paper.

Sign convention for this chapter
  • The rocket moves along a straight line; upward (direction of flight) is positive.
  • \(M(t)\) is the instantaneous mass of the rocket (structure + payload + fuel still on board). It decreases, so \(\dfrac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dt}\lt 0\).
  • \(\dot m=-\dfrac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dt}\gt0\) is the burn rate (fuel mass leaving per second, kg/s). It is often written \(\mathrm dm/\mathrm dt\), where \(\mathrm dm/\mathrm dt=-\mathrm dM/\mathrm dt\).
  • \(v_{ex}\gt0\) is the exhaust speed relative to the rocket, pointing backwards (down). Seen from the ground the exhaust moves at \(v-v_{ex}\).
  • \(g\) is taken constant (9.81 m/s² unless a question says otherwise); air drag is ignored unless stated.
Definitions · words used throughout
  • Exhaust (jet) velocity \(v_{ex}\): how fast the burnt gas leaves the nozzle, measured by someone riding on the rocket.
  • Thrust \(F_{th}\): the forward push on the rocket produced by throwing the exhaust backwards (Section 4).
  • Payload: what the rocket is built to carry (satellite, capsule, crew). Structural mass: tanks, engines, shell.
  • Burnout: the instant the fuel runs out. The time from ignition to burnout is the burnout time.
  • Mass ratio: initial mass ÷ final mass, \(M_{ini}/M_{fi}\) (some questions give its inverse, final ÷ initial).
  • Stage: a rocket section with its own tanks and engines that is thrown away once empty (Section 9).

1Newton's second law for an open system ~3 min

What is this?

You sit on a skateboard holding a pile of bricks and throw them backwards, one at a time. Each brick is pushed back by your hand, and your hand is pushed forward by the brick, so you roll forward. Nobody outside pushes you, so the total momentum of you plus all the bricks stays the same. A rocket does exactly this with its exhaust gas: millions of tiny bricks every second.

Newton's second law is usually remembered as \(F=ma\), but Newton stated it in terms of momentum \(m\vb v\) (mass × velocity):

\[\vb F=\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(m\vb v),\] \[\vb I=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\vb F(t)\,\mathrm dt=(m\vb v)_2-(m\vb v)_1\quad\text{Eq (1)}.\]
Reading the formula
\(\vb F\)
the net push from outside the system (gravity, drag). The push between you and a brick is inside, so it is not here.
\(\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(m\vb v)\)
how fast the momentum of the whole system (you and every brick) changes.
\(\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\vb F\,\mathrm dt\)
the impulse: the outside push added up over the time it acts.
\((m\vb v)_2-(m\vb v)_1\)
momentum after minus momentum before, counting the bricks already thrown too.

In words: the net external force equals the rate of change of momentum; integrated over a time interval, the impulse \(\vb I\) of the force equals the change of momentum. \(F=ma\) is the special case where \(m\) is constant.

What is this? (open system)

Draw a box round yourself only and bricks keep leaving it, so mass simply vanishes from your sums. Draw the box round you and the brick in your hand instead, and nothing leaves during the throw. That moving box is what following all the particles means.

Definition · open system

An open system is one where part of the mass leaves (or enters) across a boundary, such as exhaust gas leaving a rocket nozzle. Newton's law must then be applied so that it follows all the particles in question: draw the boundary around the rocket and the bit of gas that is about to leave, so that no mass crosses the boundary during the short time you look at (a "control volume" that moves with the mass).

Analogy: sit on a skateboard holding a pile of bricks and throw them backwards one by one. Each throw pushes you forward. If you only "watched" yourself, mass would vanish from your sums; if you include each brick in the system until it has left, momentum book-keeping works.

In chemical rocket engineering, two external forces act: gravity, which varies with time and distance from Earth, and atmospheric drag. When both are ignored, the momentum of rocket + exhaust is conserved: the push between rocket and gas is internal (action and reaction). Interplanetary or interstellar flight will probably need propulsion beyond chemical rockets: nuclear or other ideas (tethers, electrical propulsion, anti-matter).

You can now: state Newton's second law in momentum form and the impulse–momentum equation; explain why the system must include the gas about to leave; and say when the momentum of rocket plus exhaust is conserved.

2The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation at a glance ~3 min

What is this?

Throw the first brick and you gain a little speed. By the last brick you are much lighter, so the same throw gives you a bigger kick. Each kick is in proportion to the brick's mass divided by your mass at that moment, so what counts is the fraction of your mass you throw away, not the kilograms. Adding up equal fractions gives a logarithm: halving your mass always buys the same speed, whether you go from 4 tonnes to 2 or from 2 tonnes to 1.

Result · Tsiolkovsky rocket equation \[\Delta v=v_{ex}\ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}\]

\(\Delta v\): change in velocity; \(v_{ex}\) (often written \(v_e\)): exhaust velocity; \(m_0\): initial mass; \(m_f\): final mass. Valid with no gravity and no drag.

In words: the speed a rocket can gain equals its exhaust speed times the natural logarithm of how many times heavier it was at the start than at the end.

Reading the formula
\(\Delta v\)
the total speed gained once all the throws are done.
\(v_{ex}\)
how hard each brick is thrown, measured from the skateboard. Throw twice as hard and every kick doubles.
\(\dfrac{m_0}{m_f}\)
how many times heavier you were with the full pile than with none left.
\(\ln\)
adds up the kicks: every halving of your mass gives the same gain, \(0.69\,v_{ex}\).

The equation is named after the Russian scientist Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, who derived it independently and published it in 1903. Turned round, it gives the mass ratio needed for a given \(\Delta v\) is

\[\frac{m_0}{m_f}=e^{\Delta v/v_{ex}},\]

In words: the required mass ratio grows exponentially with the speed you want. \(\Delta v=v_{ex}\) needs a ratio of \(e=2.72\); \(2v_{ex}\) needs 7.39; \(3v_{ex}\) needs 20.1; \(4v_{ex}\) needs about 55.

Try it · the price of speed

Curve: \(\Delta v/v_{ex}=\ln(m_0/m_f)\) (the mass-ratio formula above is the same curve with the axes swapped). Going from ratio 4 to 8 adds only \(0.69\,v_{ex}\); raising \(v_{ex}\) scales every speed directly. Low Earth orbit needs roughly 9–10 km/s once gravity and drag losses are included, which a single stage with \(v_{ex}\approx 3\)–4 km/s cannot reach with a useful payload.

You can now: quote the Tsiolkovsky equation and when it holds (no gravity, no drag); turn it round to find the mass ratio for a given \(\Delta v\); and explain why extra fuel gives only a logarithmic gain.

3Deriving the rocket equation: one small puff of exhaust ~4 min

What is this?

Zoom in on one throw. Before it, you and the brick roll together. After it, you roll a little faster and the brick moves at your speed minus the throw speed. Momentum after minus momentum before must equal the impulse from outside, which here is only gravity pulling on everything for that short moment. Write that down, shrink the brick to a tiny puff, and you have the rocket's equation of motion.

Consider a rocket of total instantaneous mass \(M(t)\) at time \(t\), moving at speed \(v(t)\) upward against gravity \(g\). Over a small time interval \(\Delta t\), a chunk of exhaust of mass \(\Delta m\) is expelled at velocity \(v_{ex}\) relative to the ascending rocket. Seen by a stationary observer the exhaust moves at \(v-v_{ex}\) upward (a negative number if \(v_{ex}\gt v\), i.e. downward). The remaining rocket, mass \(M-\Delta m\), now moves at \(v+\Delta v\). Both the remaining rocket and the exhaust feel gravity. Air drag is ignored for the moment, and \(g\) is constant over the short \(\Delta t\). Find \(\Delta v\) with the momentum law Eq (1), upward positive.

Time \(t\): the system is the whole rocket, mass \(M\), speed \(v\).

Time \(t+\Delta t\): the same mass, now in two parts (dashed boundary).

Colour key in this chapter: red = force, grey = velocity.

  1. Do: External force and impulse. The only external force on the system (rocket + chunk) is its weight: \(F=-Mg\), so \(I=\int_0^{\Delta t}F\,\mathrm dt=-Mg\,\Delta t\). The push between you and the brick is inside the system, so only gravity counts as an outside push.
  2. Do: Change of momentum. After minus before: \[\begin{aligned}\Delta(mv)&=(M-\Delta m)(v+\Delta v)\\&\quad+\Delta m\,(v-v_{ex})-Mv\\&=(M-\Delta m)\Delta v-\Delta m\,v_{ex}.\end{aligned}\] (Expand: the \(Mv\), \(-\Delta m\,v\) and \(+\Delta m\,v\) terms cancel.) Momentum is measured by someone standing still, so the brick is counted at its ground speed \(v-v_{ex}\), not at the throw speed.
  3. Do: Impulse = change of momentum, then divide by \(\Delta t\): \[\begin{aligned}-Mg\,\Delta t&=(M-\Delta m)\Delta v\\&\quad-\Delta m\,v_{ex}\\ \Rightarrow\ (M-\Delta m)\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}&=\frac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}v_{ex}\\&\quad-Mg.\end{aligned}\] This is Eq (1) for a single throw; dividing by \(\Delta t\) turns "per throw" into "per second".
  4. Do: Let \(\Delta t\to0\). Every quantity with "\(\Delta\)" tends to zero, so \(M-\Delta m\to M\); but the ratios become derivatives, \(\Delta v/\Delta t\to\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt\) and \(\Delta m/\Delta t\to\mathrm dm/\mathrm dt\). The differential equation of rocket flight is \[M\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=v_{ex}\frac{\mathrm dm}{\mathrm dt}-Mg.\] Real exhaust is a smooth stream, not separate bricks, so we make each brick vanishingly small and count how much leaves per second.
Reading the formula
\(M\dfrac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}\)
your mass right now × your acceleration, the left side of \(F=ma\).
\(v_{ex}\dfrac{\mathrm dm}{\mathrm dt}\)
throw speed × mass thrown per second: the push you get back from throwing (the thrust).
\(-Mg\)
the weight of everything still on board, pulling back.

In words: mass × acceleration = (exhaust speed × rate of throwing out mass) − weight. The first term on the right behaves like a forward force, even though nothing outside the rocket is pushing it.

Watch

The exhaust's velocity in the momentum sum is the ground-frame velocity \(v-v_{ex}\), not \(-v_{ex}\). Using \(-v_{ex}\) alone gives an extra wrong \(\Delta m\,v\) term. Also note the term \(\Delta m\,\Delta v\) inside \((M-\Delta m)\Delta v\) is a product of two small quantities and drops out in the limit.

You can now: derive the rocket differential equation from momentum before and after one small puff, with gravity; use the exhaust's ground velocity \(v-v_{ex}\); and explain why \(\Delta m\,\Delta v\) drops out.

4Thrust, and integrating the equation ~5 min

What is this?

Throw bricks at a steady rate and the separate kicks blur into one steady push. Its size is how fast you throw each brick times how many kilograms you throw per second. That steady push is the thrust. With it the rocket obeys an ordinary \(F=ma\), except that the mass \(M\) keeps falling.

\(\Delta m\) is the reduction of the rocket's mass, so \(\mathrm dm/\mathrm dt=-\mathrm dM/\mathrm dt\). The equation becomes an ODE (ordinary differential equation) in \(M\) and \(v\) only:

\[M\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=-v_{ex}\frac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dt}-Mg.\]
Result · thrust force, Eq (2) \[F_{th}=-v_{ex}\frac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dt}=v_{ex}\,\dot m\]

In words: thrust = exhaust speed × mass of gas thrown out per second. Unit check: (m/s)(kg/s) = kg·m/s² = N. With it the rocket equation reads like \(F=ma\): \(M\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt=F_{th}-Mg\).

Reading the formula
\(v_{ex}\)
the speed of each brick relative to you.
\(\dot m\)
kilograms of bricks thrown per second.
\(v_{ex}\,\dot m\)
momentum handed to the bricks every second, which by action and reaction is the push you get back.
\(-v_{ex}\dfrac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dt}\)
the same thing written with your own mass loss; the minus makes it positive, because \(M\) is falling.

Free-body diagram of the rocket at any instant: thrust up, weight down, acceleration \(a=\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt\).

Integrating (Eq 3 and Eq 4)

What is this? (burnout speed)

Now add up all the kicks from the first brick to the last. The throwing part gives the logarithm of Section 2. Gravity pulls the whole time you are throwing and takes away speed \(g\) every second, however heavy you are. So a long, slow burn wastes speed.

Divide by \(M\):

\[\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=-v_{ex}\frac{\mathrm dM}{M\,\mathrm dt}-g\qquad\text{Eq (3)}.\]

Integrate from \(0\) to \(t\) (with limits: at \(t=0\), \(v=v_i\), \(M=M_i\); at time \(t\), \(v=v_f\), \(M=M_f\)), using \(\mathrm dM/M=\mathrm d\ln M\): Each small mass loss gives a kick in proportion to \(\mathrm dM/M\), the fraction of your current mass thrown away, and adding up fractions gives a logarithm.

\[\int_{v_i}^{v_f}\mathrm dv=-v_{ex}\int_{M_i}^{M_f}\frac{\mathrm dM}{M}-g\int_0^t\mathrm dt,\] \[v(t)=v_{initial}+v_{ex}\ln\frac{M_{initial}}{M(t)}-gt.\]
Reading the formula
\(v_{initial}\)
your speed before this burn started.
\(v_{ex}\ln\dfrac{M_{initial}}{M(t)}\)
speed gained from all the throws so far.
\(-gt\)
the gravity loss: speed gravity has taken during \(t\) seconds of burning. It does not depend on the mass.

In words: speed now = starting speed + (exhaust speed × log of how much lighter the rocket has become) − (gravity × time spent burning). The last term is the gravity loss.

When the effect of \(g\) is ignored (in space, or for a very short burn):

Result · Eq (4) \[\frac{v_{final}-v_{initial}}{v_{ex}}=\ln\frac{M_{initial}}{M_{final}}\]

In words: the velocity gain measured in "exhaust speeds" is the log of the mass ratio. This is the Tsiolkovsky equation of Section 2.

Hereafter subscripts "initial" and "final" are shortened to "ini" and "fi". Do not confuse \(v_{ini}\) with \(v_0\), the satellite's speed at perigee in orbit problems; orbits are no longer examined, so simply keep \(v_{ini}\) as "the speed before this burn".

Ready answer · rocket formulas to write from memory \[\begin{aligned}&M\dot v=-v_{ex}\dot M-Mg,\quad F_{th}=-v_{ex}\dot M,\\ &v_{fi}=v_{ini}+v_{ex}\ln\frac{M_{ini}}{M_{fi}}-g\,t_b,\\ &t_b=\frac{\text{fuel mass}}{\dot m},\quad M_{fi}=M_{ini}e^{-\Delta v/v_{ex}}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the ODE, the thrust, the burnout speed with the gravity loss \(g\,t_b\), the burnout time, and the final mass for a required \(\Delta v\) (fuel needed = \(M_{ini}-M_{fi}\)).

Design guidelines

To get a high burnout speed \(v_{fi}\):

  1. Do: Make the exhaust speed \(v_{ex}\) as high as possible. \(v_{fi}\) is proportional to \(v_{ex}\). It is not very effective to push up the logarithmic term instead: doubling the mass ratio adds only \(v_{ex}\ln2=0.69\,v_{ex}\). Throwing every brick harder makes every kick bigger in proportion; carrying more bricks only helps through the slow-growing logarithm.
  2. Do: Make the final mass as small as possible. So the payload is severely limited: for a high orbit velocity it may be only about \(0.02\,M_{ini}\) (2% of the launch mass). Check: \(\ln(1/0.02)=3.91\), so even then \(\Delta v\approx3.9\,v_{ex}\) at most. Whatever is left at the end is dead weight that every throw had to push; less of it makes the mass ratio, and the logarithm, bigger.
  3. Do: Burn all the fuel quickly, so that the gravity loss \(gt\) is small. The total burning time is the burnout time. Gravity takes speed \(g\) for every second you spend throwing, so finishing sooner loses less.

You can now: write the thrust as \(v_{ex}\dot m\) and check its units; integrate the rocket equation to get the burnout speed with the gravity loss \(gt\); find the final mass or fuel for a required \(\Delta v\); and state the three design guidelines with reasons.

5True or false? ~1 min

What is this?

Each statement can be settled on the skateboard. Ask three things: does gravity get time to act while you throw? How much is still on the board at the end? And how fast is each brick thrown, compared with how fast you are already rolling?

Settle the three questions below with the reference formula \(v_{fi}=v_{ini}+v_{ex}\ln(M_{ini}/M_{fi})-gt\) and the shape of \(y=\ln x\), which passes through \((1,0)\) and \((e,1)\) and rises ever more slowly.

C1 · Burnout time in outer space

True or false: in outer space, the shorter the burnout time, the higher the maximum speed attainable by a rocket.

Hint

Which term in the formula contains \(t\)? What is \(g\) far out in space?

Answer

False. Far from any planet \(g\approx0\), so \(v_{fi}=v_{ini}+v_{ex}\ln(M_{ini}/M_{fi})\) does not depend on how fast the fuel is burnt. Burnout time only matters near a planet, where a short burn reduces the gravity loss \(gt\) (design guideline 3).

C2 · Payload

True or false: the smaller the payload, the higher the speed that can be attained by a rocket.

Answer

True. A smaller payload makes \(M_{fi}\) smaller and the mass ratio larger, so \(\ln(M_{ini}/M_{fi})\) is larger. The gain is only logarithmic, though, and \(M_{fi}\) can never go below the empty structure.

C3 · Faster than the exhaust?

True or false: the speed of a rocket is always greater than the exhaust gas speed \(v_{ex}\).

Answer

False. Starting from rest with no gravity, \(v=v_{ex}\ln(M_{ini}/M)\), which is less than \(v_{ex}\) until the mass ratio passes \(e=2.72\) (from the \((e,1)\) point on the graph). With ratio 2, \(v=0.69\,v_{ex}\). A rocket can go faster than its exhaust (ratio above \(e\)); then the exhaust moves forward relative to the ground, but still backward relative to the rocket, so it still pushes.

You can now: decide true-or-false statements on burnout time, payload and exhaust speed by pointing to the term of the burnout-speed formula that settles each one.

6Worked example: the Saturn V ~3 min

What is this?

The Saturn V is the skateboard at full size: 3 million kg on the pad, throwing 14 tonnes of gas backwards every second. The four questions follow the picture in order: how hard each throw is, how long the bricks last, how hard the first push is against gravity, and how fast the rocket is going when the last brick leaves.

Worked example

The Saturn V (土星 5, "Saturn five", not "v") rocket was used in NASA's Apollo Moon programme. Use the approximate data: \(M_{ini}=3\times10^6\u{kg}\), \(\mathrm dm/\mathrm dt=14\times10^3\u{kg/s}\), thrust \(F_{th}=36\times10^6\u{N}\), mass ratio (final ÷ initial) \(=0.25\). (1) Determine the exhaust speed of the burned fuel. (2) Determine the time of burnout. (3) Calculate the acceleration at take-off. (4) Calculate the speed at burnout.

System: rocket on the pad, \(M_{ini}=3\times10^6\) kg.

FBD at take-off: \(F_{th}\) up, \(Mg\) down, \(Ma\) up.

  1. Do: Exhaust speed from Eq (2), \(F_{th}=v_{ex}\,\dot m\): \[v_{ex}=\frac{36\times10^6\u{N}}{14\times10^3\u{kg/s}}=2571\u{m/s}.\] Thrust is throw speed × mass per second, so dividing the push by the mass rate leaves the throw speed.
  2. Do: Burnout time. Final mass \(=0.25\times3\times10^6=0.75\times10^6\u{kg}\), so the fuel is \(2.25\times10^6\u{kg}\): \[t_b=\frac{2.25\times10^6\u{kg}}{14\times10^3\u{kg/s}}=160.7\u{s}.\] The bricks run out when all the fuel has gone at the steady rate; the 25% that is left (structure and payload) is never thrown.
  3. Do: Acceleration at take-off (\(M=M_{ini}\)): \[\begin{aligned}a&=\frac{F_{th}-Mg}{M}=\frac{36\times10^6}{3\times10^6}-9.81\\&=12-9.81=2.19\u{m/s^2}.\end{aligned}\] (Thrust-to-weight ratio \(12/9.81=1.22\): the rocket barely lifts off at first. Just before burnout, \(M=0.75\times10^6\u{kg}\) and \(a=48-9.81=38.2\u{m/s^2}\), about 4\(g\): the same thrust on a much lighter rocket.) On the pad the rocket is at its heaviest, so the same thrust gives the smallest acceleration, and the weight must be beaten first.
  4. Do: Speed at burnout, from rest: \[\begin{aligned}v_b&=v_{ex}\ln\frac{M_{ini}}{M_{fi}}-g\,t_b\\&=2571\ln4-9.81(160.7)\\&=3565-1577=1988\u{m/s}.\end{aligned}\] The logarithm counts the kicks from all the fuel; \(g\,t_b\) is the speed gravity took during the 160.7 s burn.

\(\boxed{v_{ex}=2571\u{m/s},\ t_b=160.7\u{s},\ a_0=2.19\u{m/s^2},\ v_b\approx1990\u{m/s}}\)

Sense check: the \(gt\) term → 0 if the burnout time is very short. Here it is not short: gravity eats 1577 m/s, about 44% of the ideal 3565 m/s. (Drag, ignored here, takes more.)

Saturn V first stage · speed and acceleration during the burn

Top: \(v(t)=v_{ex}\ln\frac{M_{ini}}{M_{ini}-\dot mt}-gt\) (accent) and the no-gravity speed (dashed). Bottom: \(a(t)=F_{th}/M(t)-g\) rises as the rocket gets lighter. Neither curve is a straight line.

You can now: find the exhaust speed from thrust and burn rate, the burnout time from the fuel mass, the acceleration at take-off and burnout, and the burnout speed with the gravity loss.

7Varying mass beyond rockets ~5 min

What is this?

The skateboard rule works for anything that sheds mass while it moves. Throw a brick backwards and you are pushed forwards. But let a brick simply slip off the back of the board and it keeps moving at your speed beside you: there was no throw, so there is no push. You are just lighter.

The thrust expression Eq (2), \(F_{th}=-v_{ex}\,\mathrm dM/\mathrm dt\), came from momentum alone, so it applies to any system that gains or loses mass while moving. Two examples: a fire-fighting helicopter releasing water, and a truck unloading sand from its back.

Rule · when does losing mass push?

Mass leaving with relative velocity \(\vb u\) (relative to the body) gives the body a force \(-\dot m\,\vb u\): opposite to the way the mass leaves, of size (relative speed) × (mass rate). If the mass simply drops off with zero relative velocity, there is no thrust: the body only gets lighter.

In words: it is throwing mass away fast that pushes you, not merely losing it.

Example · helicopter

A hovering fire-fighting helicopter pumps water out of a nozzle pointing straight down at \(10\u{m/s}\) relative to the helicopter, at \(200\u{kg/s}\). (a) What extra force does this give? (b) What if the water is just released through an open hatch?

FBD: rotor lift \(L\), weight \(Mg\), jet thrust \(F_{th}\).

(a) \(F_{th}=u\,\dot m=10\u{m/s}\times200\u{kg/s}=2000\u{N}\), upward (opposite to the jet). The rotor can produce 2000 N less lift and the helicopter still hovers. (b) Released water leaves with zero relative velocity: no thrust. But the helicopter's weight falls by \(200\times9.81=1962\u{N}\) every second, so the pilot must reduce lift steadily or the helicopter climbs.

Example · truck

A truck drives forward while a conveyor throws sand out of the back at \(2\u{m/s}\) relative to the truck, at \(50\u{kg/s}\). Thrust on the truck: \(2\times50=100\u{N}\) forward. If the sand just slides off the tailgate with the truck's own speed, the thrust is zero.

Past-paper worked example · Dec 2017 B3(a) · 12 marks · type D3.3

The rocket has an initial mass \(m_0\) including the fuel. For practical reasons desired for the crew, it is required that it maintain a constant upward acceleration \(a_0\). If the fuel is expelled from the rocket at a constant relative speed \(v_{e/r}\), prove that the rate at which the fuel should be consumed \(\mathrm dm/\mathrm dt\) to maintain the desired motion is \(\dfrac{\mathrm dm}{\mathrm dt}=\dfrac{m_0(a_0+g)}{v_{e/r}}e^{(a_0+g)t/v_{e/r}}\). Neglect air resistance, and assume that the gravitational acceleration \(g\) is constant.

FBD: thrust \(v_{e/r}\dot m\) up, weight \(Mg\) down, \(\dot v=a_0\).

  1. Do: Rocket ODE with \(\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt=a_0\) and \(M(t)\) the rocket mass: \[Ma_0=-v_{e/r}\frac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dt}-Mg.\] The acceleration is fixed by the crew's comfort, so the equation now tells us how fast the mass must fall instead.
  2. Do: Separate variables: \[\begin{aligned}\frac{\mathrm dM}{M}&=-\frac{a_0+g}{v_{e/r}}\,\mathrm dt\\ \Rightarrow\ M&=m_0e^{-(a_0+g)t/v_{e/r}}.\end{aligned}\] The mass must drop by the same fraction every second, and a steady fractional drop is an exponential decay.
  3. Do: Burn rate (mass leaving per second): \[\dot m=-\frac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{m_0(a_0+g)}{v_{e/r}}e^{-(a_0+g)t/v_{e/r}}.\] The question asks for fuel leaving per second, which is minus the rocket's rate of mass change.

\(\boxed{\dot m=\dfrac{m_0(a_0+g)}{v_{e/r}}\,e^{-(a_0+g)t/v_{e/r}}}\)

the printed result has \(+\) in the exponent. A rocket that gets lighter needs a falling burn rate for constant acceleration (constant thrust on a lighter rocket would accelerate it more), so the exponent must be negative. Prove the correct form and point out the sign in one line.

In words: to keep the crew's acceleration steady, burn fuel in proportion to the rocket's current mass, so the burn rate decays exponentially.

You can now: apply thrust = relative speed × mass rate to helicopters, trucks and other bodies; tell a throw (thrust) from a drop (no thrust); and find the burn rate for constant acceleration by separating variables.

8Liquid fuels: maximising the exhaust speed ~3 min

What is this?

On the skateboard, the best way to go faster is to throw harder. For a rocket, throwing harder means making the gas hotter and its molecules lighter, so it leaves the nozzle faster. The fuel table below is a list of how hard each fuel can throw.

Design guideline 1 says: maximise \(v_{ex}\). It is set mainly by the chemistry: how hot the combustion gas gets and how light its molecules are (light, hot gas leaves the nozzle fastest). The table lists fuel/oxidiser pairs (the oxidiser supplies the oxygen, since there is no air in space):

Fuel / oxidiserCombustion temperature (K)Max exhaust speed \(v_{ex}\) (km/s)
Ethyl alcohol / O₂30003.9 (earlier rockets)
Kerosene / O₂33004 (Saturn V, stage 1)
Unsymmetrical dimethyl hydrazine / O₂33004.1 (Long March, Ariane)

H₂ / fluorine36005.5 (polluting, not good)

Different propellants burn with different flame colours: RP-1 (refined kerosene) burns bright orange; methane (liquefied natural gas) blue-purple; hydrogen clear to pale blue; solid propellant cloudy and bright white; hypergolic propellants (N₂O₄ with UDMH, which ignite on contact) blue with orange fumes.

The table values are maximum (ideal) exhaust speeds. The Saturn V example in Section 6 gives an effective \(v_{ex}=2.57\) km/s for stage 1, lower than the table's 4 km/s, because real engines and sea-level operation lose performance and the example's data are approximate.

In words: hydrogen with oxygen gives the highest practical exhaust speed, which is why upper stages (which need the most efficiency) use it; denser kerosene suits first stages, where compact tanks and high thrust matter more.

You can now: name the main fuel/oxidiser pairs and their maximum exhaust speeds; explain why H₂/O₂ suits upper stages and kerosene first stages; and say why the Saturn V example's \(v_{ex}\) is below the table value.

9Multi-stage rockets ~4 min

What is this?

Say your bricks come in a big crate. Halfway through, half the crate is empty, but you are still pushing all of it along. Better: carry two crates, and when the first is empty, just let it drop off the board and carry on with the second. Dropping it gives no push (Section 7), but every later throw now moves less dead weight. That is staging.

Compare a single-stage rocket (payload on one booster) with a two-stage rocket (payload on stage 2, on stage 1). Stage 1 burns with initial and final masses \(M_{ini}^{(1)}, M_{fi}^{(1)}\). When it is empty its shell is discarded, and stage 2 then burns from \(M_{ini}^{(2)}\) to \(M_{fi}^{(2)}\).

Result · two stages (no gravity) \[\begin{aligned}\Delta V&=v_{ex}\ln\frac{M_{ini}^{(1)}}{M_{fi}^{(1)}}+v_{ex}\ln\frac{M_{ini}^{(2)}}{M_{fi}^{(2)}}\\&=v_{ex}\ln\left(\frac{M_{ini}^{(1)}}{M_{fi}^{(1)}}\times\frac{M_{ini}^{(2)}}{M_{fi}^{(2)}}\right),\end{aligned}\]

with \(M_{fi}^{(1)}\gt M_{ini}^{(2)}\) (the difference is the discarded shell).

Reading the formula
\(v_{ex}\ln\dfrac{M_{ini}^{(1)}}{M_{fi}^{(1)}}\)
speed from stage 1's fuel, pushing everything including both crates.
\(v_{ex}\ln\dfrac{M_{ini}^{(2)}}{M_{fi}^{(2)}}\)
speed from stage 2's fuel, after the empty first crate has been dropped.
\(\ln(\cdots\times\cdots)\)
the two gains add, so the mass ratios multiply.
\(M_{fi}^{(1)}\gt M_{ini}^{(2)}\)
the gap is the dropped crate (the stage-1 shell).

In words: apply the rocket equation once per stage and add the speed gains; the overall effect is as if the mass ratios were multiplied. Throwing away the shell makes the second ratio bigger than it would be if the shell were carried.

The two-stage formula is not examined this year. Past papers (Dec 2018 B3, Dec 2015 B2, May 2014 B2) did ask staging calculations; they need nothing beyond applying the rocket equation once per stage, so the practice question in the exam taster is kept, labelled lower priority.

Two points:

  • Multi-stage rockets are more energy efficient than single stage, because you are not wasting fuel to propel empty fuel containers.
  • Infinitely many stages would in theory be most efficient, but in reality structural complication and cost prevent the design of more than 3 stages typically.

Try it · one stage or two? (data of Dec 2018 B3: 500 kN at launch, 420 kN fuel, 40 kN payload, 40 kN structure, burn 3 kN/s, \(v_{ex}=3\) km/s, \(g=9.6\) m/s²)

single stagetwo stages

Stage 2 gets the rest: \(420-F_1\) fuel and \(40-S_1\) structure. Both rockets burn for 140 s in total. The two-stage curve jumps nowhere, but after separation it climbs faster because it no longer carries stage 1's shell. Start values reproduce the past paper (single 4154 m/s, two-stage 5076 m/s).

Going further · why "about 3 stages"

Each extra stage saves less than the one before (the dead mass you avoid carrying gets smaller), while every stage adds engines, separation hardware, and a chance of failure at separation. For typical Earth-to-orbit missions the benefit flattens after 2–3 stages; most modern launchers have 2 (Falcon 9) or 3 (Saturn V, Long March 3B), sometimes with strap-on boosters as a "stage 0".

You can now: apply the rocket equation once per stage, dropping the empty shell between stages; explain why staging is more energy efficient; and say why designs stop at about 3 stages.

10Orbits and satellites: background only ~2 min

What is this?

Once the last brick is thrown, the rocket's job is done and the satellite coasts round the Earth. This section is about where satellites live and how crowded those places are getting, not about rocket maths.

No longer examined

Orbital dynamics was removed from the 2026/27 syllabus. This section is qualitative background, and orbits are not part of the chapter summary. No formulas are needed.

  • Man-made satellites. Since about 1960 satellites have enabled trans-continental TV broadcast, GPS navigation, meteorology (weather) and defence. Since 2015 SpaceX's Starlink connects the whole world through a network of low-orbit satellites (over 10,000 Starlink satellites were in orbit by June 2026). The orbit bands: LEO (low Earth orbit) about 500–2000 km up, MEO (medium) about 8000–20,000 km, GEO (geostationary) at 35,786 km.
  • Low-Earth-orbit traffic. The number of objects in orbit has surged in recent years (the count of tracked objects rises steeply after 2019, mainly from Starlink), so orbital space is a limited resource. Discussion: what strategies can engineers develop to prevent satellite crashes?
  • Plenty of space up there, but… Geosynchronous satellites (orbit period 24 hours, so they stay over the same point on Earth) are very useful, but they all orbit at the same height, 35,786 km above sea level, so there is a risk of collision, especially between satellites run by different agencies and companies.
Discussion · some answers
  • Track every object from the ground and share the data between operators; plan collision-avoidance burns when a close approach is predicted (with a small thruster, i.e. the rocket equation again).
  • Allocate orbital "slots" (for GEO, international coordination of positions).
  • At end of life, de-orbit LEO satellites so they burn up in the atmosphere, and move GEO satellites to a higher "graveyard" orbit.
  • Design satellites not to shed debris; passivate (empty) old tanks and batteries so they cannot explode.

You can now: name LEO, MEO and GEO with their rough heights, and explain why crowded orbits need tracking and collision avoidance (background only, not examined).

12Common mistakes

✗Writing the exhaust's momentum as \(\Delta m(-v_{ex})\). ✓Its ground velocity is \(v-v_{ex}\): momentum \(\Delta m(v-v_{ex})\). ✗Using \(F=Ma\) with \(F=F_{th}\) and forgetting the weight on the launch pad. ✓\(Ma=F_{th}-Mg\); a rocket lifts off only if \(F_{th}\gt Mg\). ✗Putting the mass ratio upside down: \(\ln(M_{fi}/M_{ini})\), giving a negative speed. ✓Heavier (initial) on top: \(\ln(M_{ini}/M_{fi})\gt0\). Watch questions that quote "final/initial = 0.25". ✗Leaving out \(-gt_b\) when the question says the rocket is launched from Earth's surface with gravity. ✓Include \(g t_b\) unless told to ignore gravity; state which you did. ✗Mixing weights (kN) and masses (kg). ✓Mass ratios can be taken from weights directly (\(g\) cancels), but the burn rate in kN/s must be divided by \(g\) before \(F_{th}=v_{ex}\dot m\). ✗In staging, starting stage 2 from the stage-1 burnout mass. ✓Subtract the discarded shell first: \(M_{ini}^{(2)}=M_{fi}^{(1)}-\text{shell}\). ✗Dividing by a common logarithm (\(\log_{10}\)). ✓The rocket equation uses the natural log \(\ln\).

13Find the mistake

1 · Saturn V burnout speed

"Mass ratio final/initial = 0.25, so \(v_b=2571\ln(0.25)-9.81(160.7)=-3565-1577=-5142\u{m/s}\). The minus sign means the rocket is going down."

Hint

Which mass goes on top of the logarithm?

Answer

The ratio must be initial/final \(=4\): \(v_b=2571\ln4-1577=1988\u{m/s}\) upward.

2 · Thrust from a weight rate

"Dec 2018 B3: burn rate 3 kN/s, \(v_{ex}=3\u{km/s}\), so \(F_{th}=3000\times3000=9\times10^6\u{N}\)."

Hint

Is 3 kN/s a mass rate?

Answer

3 kN/s is a weight rate. Mass rate \(=3000/9.6=312.5\u{kg/s}\), so \(F_{th}=312.5\times3000=937.5\u{kN}\).

3 · Two stages

"Stage 1 burns 500 → 200 kN, stage 2 burns 200 → 80 kN, so \(\Delta v=3000[\ln2.5+\ln2.5]\)."

Hint

What happens to the 30 kN stage-1 shell?

Answer

The shell is discarded before stage 2 fires: stage 2 runs from \(170\) to \(50\u{kN}\) (120 kN fuel burnt; 40 kN payload + 10 kN stage-2 shell remain), ratio 3.4, not 2.5.

14Concept checks

1 · Pushing on nothing

In space there is no air to push against. What does the rocket push on?

Answer

On its own exhaust gas. The engine pushes the gas backwards; by action and reaction the gas pushes the rocket forwards. Momentum of rocket + gas is conserved.

2 · Double the fuel

A rocket in space has mass ratio 3. Its tanks are enlarged so that the ratio becomes 6 (same \(v_{ex}\)). By what factor does \(\Delta v\) grow?

Answer

\(\ln6/\ln3=1.79/1.10=1.63\): about 63% more, not double. Doubling \(v_{ex}\) instead would double \(\Delta v\) (design guideline 1).

3 · Dropping an empty shell

After its booster has finished firing, a satellite releases the empty booster shell (it just lets go). Does the satellite's speed change? (Dec 2016 B2(d) asked this.)

Answer

No. The shell leaves with zero relative velocity, so no impulse acts between them (Section 7 rule): both keep moving at the same speed, and the satellite's path is unchanged. Releasing it only reduces the mass for later burns.

4 · Lift-off

A 50 t rocket has \(v_{ex}=3\u{km/s}\). What minimum burn rate lets it leave the pad (\(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\))?

Answer

Need \(v_{ex}\dot m\gt Mg\): \(\dot m\gt50{,}000\times9.81/3000=163.5\u{kg/s}\).

5 · Shape of \(v(t)\)

With constant burn rate and no gravity, is \(v(t)\) a straight line?

Answer

No. \(v=v_{ex}\ln\frac{M_{ini}}{M_{ini}-\dot mt}\) curves upward: the same thrust acts on a lighter and lighter rocket, so the acceleration \(v_{ex}\dot m/M\) keeps rising.

Dec 2024 B3(a)4 marksalso Dec 2023 B3(a), Dec 2022 B3(a), Dec 2021 B2(a), Dec 2015 B2(a), Dec 2014 B2(a)D3.1 derive the rocket equation

In a space mission, a single stage rocket containing a space shuttle is launched into space from Earth's surface. During the launch from Earth, a chunk of mass \(\Delta m\) is ejected at a speed of \(v_{ex}\) relative to the rocket, causing the rest of the rocket to gain a velocity increment \(\Delta v\). Derive the differential equation for the rocket flight by ignoring gravity and air resistance. Show by integration that the total velocity increase from mass \(M_0\) to \(M_1\) is \(\Delta v=v_{ex}\ln(M_0/M_1)\).

Hint

Momentum before \(=Mv\); after \(=(M-\Delta m)(v+\Delta v)+\Delta m(v-v_{ex})\). No external force, so they are equal. Then \(\Delta m=-\Delta M\).

Answer
  1. No external force: \((M-\Delta m)(v+\Delta v)+\Delta m(v-v_{ex})=Mv\Rightarrow(M-\Delta m)\Delta v=\Delta m\,v_{ex}\).

Dec 2023 B3(d)4 marksD3.5 launch practicalities

In reality, gravity and air resistance cannot be neglected. Describe what engineering design strategies are used to minimise these effects in a real space shuttle launch.

Hint

Gravity loss is \(g t_b\); drag grows with air density and speed squared.

Answer
  • Gravity: high thrust-to-weight ratio so the fuel burns quickly (short \(t_b\), small \(gt_b\), design guideline 3); staging to shed dead mass so the rocket accelerates harder later.
  • Drag: slender, streamlined body with a pointed nose/fairing; climb vertically first to leave the dense lower atmosphere quickly, then tilt over gradually ("gravity turn"); throttle down through the region of maximum aerodynamic pressure.
  • Going further Launch eastward near the equator to gain Earth's rotation speed (about 0.46 km/s).
Dec 2015 B2(c)4 marksalso May 2014 B2(c), May 2013 B3(d)D3.4 why multi-stage low priority

Explain why a multiple-stage rocket is more energy efficient than a single-stage one, and why rocket design is usually limited to 3 stages.

Answer
  • A single stage must accelerate its empty tanks and engines all the way to burnout; a multi-stage rocket discards each empty stage, so later fuel accelerates less dead mass and each stage achieves a better mass ratio (\(\Delta V=v_{ex}\ln\prod M_{ini}/M_{fi}\)).
  • Infinitely many stages would be ideal, but each extra stage adds structural complication, separation hardware, failure risk and cost, while the gain shrinks; so typically no more than 3.
