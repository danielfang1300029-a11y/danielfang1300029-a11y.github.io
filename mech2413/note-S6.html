Torsion of Circular Shafts

0Where this chapter sits

Wring out a wet towel: your two hands turn opposite ways about its length, and every slice in between is twisted. A car's drive shaft does the same job in steel. This chapter is about round shafts twisted about their own axis: the torque they carry, the shear stress it causes, and how far they twist.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 Torque is a twist about the shaft's own axis. Every slice of a wrung towel carries the same internal torque.
  2. §2 A round shaft twists like a stack of coins fanned round. Lines on the surface become helices; the strain grows with the radius.
  3. §3 Shear stress grows from zero on the axis to a maximum at the surface: \(\tau=T\rho/J\).
  4. §4 \(J\) measures how much material sits far from the axis. It goes with \(d^4\).
  5. §5 Twisting stretches the material along one \(45^\circ\) helix. That is why chalk breaks on a spiral.
  6. §6 A shaft is a twisting spring: \(\varphi=TL/(GJ)\), with signed twists added along the shaft.
  7. §7 Torque diagram, then twist from the wall outwards. Dec 2023 and Dec 2024
  8. §8 Power = torque × speed: a slow shaft carries a big torque.
  9. §9 Two walls share the torque; the total twist between them is zero. 3 past papers
  10. §10 Gears are levers made of teeth: big gear, big torque, small rotation. 3 past papers
  11. §11 Compound shafts: end to end the twists add; bonded together they share the torque by \(GJ\).

You need: free-body diagrams, the method of sections and equilibrium (S1, and the internal forces of beams in S2); shear stress, shear strain and Mohr's circle (S3); second moments of area (S4). In the exam: a whole Section A question (usually A3, 15–20 marks) in seven of the twelve papers, with no formula sheet. Every worked example here is solved in full with a free-body diagram. See the S6 exam guide.

Notation

Every result below is standard. Books differ in their letters; this note uses the letters of the MECH2413 past papers: \(T\) or \(M\) for torque, \(G\) for shear modulus, \(J\) for polar moment of inertia, \(\varphi\) for angle of twist.

Sign convention for this chapter
  • The \(x\)-axis runs along the shaft axis, from the fixed end (or the left end) to the right.
  • A torque (a couple about the axis) is drawn as a double-headed arrow along the axis, using the right-hand rule: curl the fingers of your right hand in the turning direction, and your thumb points along the arrow. An applied torque is positive if its arrow points in \(+x\).
  • Internal torque \(T\) on a cut is positive if its arrow points away from the cut face (out of the piece), on both pieces. This is the same idea as tension being positive for the normal force in S2.
  • Angle of twist \(\varphi\) (the angle one cross-section turns) is positive by the right-hand rule about \(+x\). The twist of end \(B\) relative to end \(A\) is \(\varphi_{B/A}=\varphi_B-\varphi_A\).
  • Unknown support torques are drawn positive (arrow in \(+x\)). A negative answer means the real torque points the other way; say so in words at the end.

In words: with this rule a positive internal torque gives a positive twist, so \(\varphi=TL/(GJ)\) (Section 6) carries the sign automatically.

1Torque and shafts ~3 min

What is this?

Wring out a wet towel. Your two hands turn in opposite directions about the towel's length, and the towel between them twists. Your hands apply the torques; every slice of towel passes the twist on to the next. A car's drive shaft is the same thing in steel: the gearbox turns one end and the wheels resist at the other.

Definitions · shaft, torque, torsion
  • A shaft is a long member, usually of circular cross-section, that transmits turning effect: a car drive shaft, a motor spindle, a screwdriver, a turbine shaft.
  • A torque (twisting moment) is a couple whose moment vector points along the shaft axis (S1 Section 5: a couple turns a body without pushing it). Unit: N·m.
  • Torsion is the twisting of a member by torques.

A torque can be applied by a pair of equal and opposite forces (two hands on a steering wheel), by a gear or pulley (a force at the rim times the rim radius), or by a motor. In drawings it appears either as a curved arrow round the shaft (seen from the side) or as a double-headed arrow along the axis, as in the past papers.

Analogy: wring out a wet towel. Your two hands apply equal and opposite torques; every slice of towel between them is twisted by the same internal torque.

Internal torque by the method of sections

What is this? (internal torque)

Imagine cutting the towel in the middle while you are still wringing it. To keep the left half twisted, someone would have to grip the cut end and turn it as hard as your right hand was turning. That grip is the internal torque: the twist each part of the shaft passes to the other across a section.

Cut the shaft at any section (S1 Section 2). For the piece on one side to stay in equilibrium, the other piece must apply a couple about the axis on the cut face: the internal torque \(T\). Moment equilibrium about the \(x\)-axis of either piece gives it:

\[\textstyle\sum M_x=0\quad\text{for the piece you keep.}\]

In words: the internal torque at a cut equals the sum of all the applied torques on one side of the cut (taken with the sign rule of Section 0, as Section 7 shows).

You can now: describe a torque as a couple about the shaft axis and draw it as a double-headed arrow by the right-hand rule; and find the internal torque at a cut from \(\sum M_x=0\) on one piece.

2How a round shaft twists ~4 min

What is this?

Draw lines along a rubber rod and rings round it, so its surface is covered in small squares, then twist it. The rings stay flat rings: each one simply turns a little more than its neighbour, like a stack of coins fanned round. The straight lines tilt into gentle spirals (helices), and every small square leans over into a diamond. That leaning is the whole story: everything else in this chapter follows from it.

Draw a square grid on the surface of a rubber rod and twist it. Experiments (and symmetry) show that for a circular shaft, solid or hollow:

  1. Each cross-section stays plane and circular; it just rotates as a rigid disc about the axis.
  2. Radii stay straight.
  3. Lengthwise lines on the surface become gentle helices; the small squares of the grid become rhombuses (they are sheared).
  4. For small twists, the length and the radius do not change.
What is this? (shear strain)

Look at one small square on the twisted rod. Its corners are no longer right angles: it has leaned over, like a rubber eraser pushed sideways at the top. The angle by which a corner closes is the shear strain. A square near the axis hardly leans, because points there barely move when the section turns; a square on the outer surface leans the most.

Definition · shear strain

Shear strain \(\gamma\) is the change, in radians, of an angle that was originally a right angle (S3). On the twisted shaft, a lengthwise line tilts by \(\gamma\), so a right-angled corner of the surface grid closes by \(\gamma\).

Take a slice of length \(\mathrm dx\). One face turns by \(\mathrm d\varphi\) more than the other. A point at radius \(\rho\) (distance from the axis) moves round by the arc \(\rho\,\mathrm d\varphi\), so a lengthwise line at that radius tilts by

\[\gamma=\rho\,\frac{\mathrm d\varphi}{\mathrm dx}.\]
Reading the formula
\(\rho\)
how far the line is from the axis: the further out, the further a point travels when its section turns.
\(\mathrm d\varphi\)
how much more one face of the thin slice turns than the other.
\(\rho\,\mathrm d\varphi\)
the arc that a point at radius \(\rho\) slides round, relative to the matching point on the other face.
\(\dfrac{\cdots}{\mathrm dx}\)
that sideways slide divided by the slice length is how far the line leans, in radians: the shear strain \(\gamma\).

When the torque is the same along a length \(L\) of uniform shaft, \(\mathrm d\varphi/\mathrm dx=\varphi/L\) is constant, and with outer radius \(c\):

\[\gamma=\frac{\rho\varphi}{L},\qquad \gamma_{\max}=\frac{c\,\varphi}{L},\qquad \gamma=\frac{\rho}{c}\gamma_{\max}.\]

In words: shear strain is zero on the axis and grows in proportion to the distance from it, largest at the outer surface. This comes from geometry alone; it holds for any material.

Watch

All of this needs a circular section. A square or rectangular bar warps (its sections do not stay plane) and the formulas of this chapter do not apply to it. Exam shafts are always circular, solid or hollow.

You can now: state what happens to sections, radii and surface lines when a circular shaft twists; derive \(\gamma=\rho\,\mathrm d\varphi/\mathrm dx\) from a thin slice; and say why the strain is zero on the axis and largest at the surface.

3Shear stress: the torsion formula ~5 min

What is this?

Back to the rubber rod. Each small square that has leaned into a diamond pushes back, as an eraser does when you push its top sideways. That push, per unit area and acting along the face, is shear stress. Squares on the outside lean most, so they push back hardest; material near the axis barely works. Add up every push times its distance from the axis and you get the torque the shaft carries.

Definitions · shear stress, shear modulus

Shear stress \(\tau\) is force per unit area acting along (parallel to) a surface (S3). In the elastic range it is proportional to shear strain, Hooke's law in shear:

\[\tau=G\gamma.\]

\(G\) is the shear modulus (modulus of rigidity), in Pa; steel \(G\approx 77\) to \(80\u{GPa}\), aluminium about \(26\) to \(28\u{GPa}\). For an isotropic material it is linked to Young's modulus \(E\) and Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) (S3) by

\[G=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}.\]

In words: stiffness in shear is a bit less than half the stiffness in tension. Steel: \(200/(2\times1.3)=76.9\u{GPa}\).

Combining \(\tau=G\gamma\) with Section 2, the shear stress on a cross-section also grows linearly with radius, and acts in the direction of rotation (perpendicular to the radius):

\[\tau=\frac{\rho}{c}\,\tau_{\max}.\]

From stress back to torque

A small ring of area \(\mathrm dA\) at radius \(\rho\) carries a force \(\tau\,\mathrm dA\), whose moment about the axis is \(\rho\,\tau\,\mathrm dA\). All these moments together must equal the internal torque:

\[\begin{aligned}T&=\int_A\rho\,\tau\,\mathrm dA=\frac{\tau_{\max}}{c}\int_A\rho^2\,\mathrm dA\\ &=\frac{\tau_{\max}}{c}\,J,\qquad J=\int_A\rho^2\,\mathrm dA.\end{aligned}\]

Each piece's stress already carries one factor \(\rho\) (outer material leans more); its lever arm brings a second. That is where \(\rho^2\), and so \(J\), comes from.

Result · the torsion formula \[\tau=\frac{T\rho}{J},\qquad \tau_{\max}=\frac{Tc}{J}.\]

In words: shear stress = torque × distance from the axis ÷ polar moment of inertia. The biggest stress is at the outer surface. \(J\) (Section 4) measures how much material sits far from the axis.

Reading the formula
\(\tau\)
the sideways push per unit area on the cross-section, at the point you look at.
\(T\)
the internal torque at that section: wring harder and every push grows in proportion.
\(\rho\)
distance from the axis: outer material leans more, so it pushes harder. Zero on the axis.
\(J\)
how much material sits far from the axis. More of it shares the torque, so each piece pushes less.
\(\tau_{\max}=Tc/J\)
put \(\rho=c\): the outer surface, where the stress is largest and failure starts.
Ready result · solid shaft of diameter \(d\) \[\tau_{\max}=\frac{16T}{\pi d^3}.\]

In words: double the diameter and the peak stress falls eightfold.

Try it · shear stress across a solid or hollow section

Same torque, same outer diameter. Stress is zero on the axis and largest on the outside. Making the shaft hollow removes lightly stressed material near the centre, so \(J\) (and the strength) falls far less than the weight.

Worked example · stress in a solid shaft

A solid steel shaft of diameter \(d=50\u{mm}\) carries \(T=2\u{kN\,m}\). Find the shear stress at the surface and at \(\rho=15\u{mm}\). Then compare a hollow shaft of the same cross-sectional area with outer diameter 60 mm.

System

FBD of the piece to the right of a cut: internal torque \(T\) (green)

  1. \(J=\dfrac{\pi(0.05)^4}{32}=6.136\times10^{-7}\u{m^4}\).
  2. \(\tau_{\max}=\dfrac{2000\times0.025}{6.136\times10^{-7}}=81.5\u{MPa}\) (check: \(16(2000)/(\pi\,0.05^3)\) gives the same).
  3. \(\tau(15\u{mm})=\dfrac{15}{25}\times81.5=48.9\u{MPa}\).
  4. Hollow, same area: \(\tfrac\pi4(60^2-d_i^2)=\tfrac\pi4 50^2\Rightarrow d_i=33.2\u{mm}\). Then \(J=\tfrac{\pi}{32}(0.06^4-0.0332^4)=1.154\times10^{-6}\u{m^4}\) and \(\tau_{\max}=2000(0.03)/J=52.0\u{MPa}\). The same amount of steel moved further out leans more and has a longer lever arm, so \(J\) rises and the stress falls.
  5. Units: N·m × m ÷ m⁴ = N/m² = Pa ✓.

\(\boxed{\tau_{\max}=81.5\u{MPa},\ \tau(15\u{mm})=48.9\u{MPa};\ \text{hollow: }52.0\u{MPa}}\)

The hollow shaft uses the same steel but is about 36% less stressed.

You can now: use \(\tau=G\gamma\) and \(G=E/(2(1+\nu))\); find the shear stress at any radius with \(\tau=T\rho/J\) and at the surface with \(\tau_{\max}=Tc/J=16T/(\pi d^3)\); and explain why a hollow shaft is more efficient.

4Polar moment of inertia ~3 min

What is this?

\(J\) measures how hard a cross-section is to twist, from its shape alone. Think of wringing a thick bath towel and then a thin tea towel: the thick one fights back far more. Material far from the axis counts twice over: it leans more, so it pushes harder, and it has a longer lever arm, so each push gives more torque. That is why every bit of area is weighted by its distance squared.

Definition · polar moment of inertia \(J\)

\(J=\int_A\rho^2\,\mathrm dA\), the sum of every small area times the square of its distance from the axis. Unit: m⁴ (or mm⁴; \(1\u{mm^4}=10^{-12}\u{m^4}\)). The past papers call it the "polar moment of inertia" or the "torsion constant". Since \(\rho^2=y^2+z^2\), \(J=I_y+I_z\), the sum of the two second moments of area of S4.

For a solid circle of radius \(c\), use rings of radius \(\rho\), width \(\mathrm d\rho\), area \(2\pi\rho\,\mathrm d\rho\):

\[J=\int_0^c\rho^2\,2\pi\rho\,\mathrm d\rho=\frac{\pi c^4}{2}=\frac{\pi d^4}{32}.\]
Reading the formula
\(2\pi\rho\,\mathrm d\rho\)
the area of a thin ring: its length round (\(2\pi\rho\)) times its width.
\(\rho^2\)
the double weighting: one \(\rho\) for the bigger push, one for the longer lever arm.
\(\int_0^c\)
add every ring from the axis out to the surface.
\(\dfrac{\pi d^4}{32}\)
fourth power of the diameter: a slightly fatter shaft is much harder to twist.
Ready results · \(J\) for circular sections
Section\(J\)\(\tau_{\max}\)
solid, diameter \(d\) (radius \(c\))\(\dfrac{\pi d^4}{32}=\dfrac{\pi c^4}{2}\)\(\dfrac{16T}{\pi d^3}\)
hollow, diameters \(d_o\), \(d_i\)\(\dfrac{\pi(d_o^4-d_i^4)}{32}\)\(\dfrac{T\,d_o/2}{J}\)
thin tube, mean radius \(r\), wall \(t\)\(\approx2\pi r^3t\)\(\approx\dfrac{T}{2\pi r^2t}\)

In words: \(J\) goes with the fourth power of the diameter, so a small increase in diameter gives a big increase in strength and stiffness. A hollow \(J\) is the big circle's \(J\) minus the hole's.

Numbers you will meet · from the past papers

\(d=60\u{mm}\): \(J=1.272\times10^{6}\u{mm^4}\). \(d=30\u{mm}\): \(J=7.952\times10^{4}\u{mm^4}\). \(d=50\u{mm}\): \(J=6.136\times10^{5}\u{mm^4}\). \(d=72\u{mm}\): \(J=2.638\times10^{6}\u{mm^4}\).

Watch

Use the diameter in \(\pi d^4/32\) and the radius \(c=d/2\) in \(\tau=Tc/J\). Mixing them up gives answers out by a factor of 16 or 2. And convert mm to m before raising to the fourth power, or keep everything in N·mm and mm (then \(\tau\) comes out in N/mm² = MPa).

You can now: compute \(J\) for solid, hollow and thin-tube sections in m⁴ or mm⁴; link \(J=I_y+I_z\) to S4; and avoid the radius/diameter and mm/m slips.

5Pure shear, inclined planes and failure ~2 min

What is this?

Look once more at a small square on the twisted rubber rod, now leaned into a diamond. One diagonal of the diamond has got longer and the other shorter. So along one \(45^\circ\) direction the material is being stretched, and along the other it is squashed, even though the shaft only carries shear. Twist a stick of chalk and it snaps along that stretched spiral.

Cut a tiny square element from the surface of a twisted shaft, with sides along and across the axis. Its four faces carry only shear stress \(\tau\), no normal stress: a state of pure shear. (Shear stresses on perpendicular faces are always equal in size, because the element must not spin.)

Using the stress transformation of S3 (Mohr's circle centred at the origin with radius \(\tau\)), the same state seen on planes at \(45^\circ\) to the axis is

\[\sigma_1=+\tau,\qquad \sigma_2=-\tau\]

(on planes at \(\pm45^\circ\) to the axis)

In words: twisting a shaft pulls the material in tension along one \(45^\circ\) helix and squashes it along the other, each with stress equal to the shear stress.

Ductile material (steel)Weaker in shear: breaks on a plane square to the axis, a flat break.
Brittle material (chalk, cast iron)Weaker in tension: breaks along a \(45^\circ\) helix, where \(\sigma_1=\tau\) pulls it apart. Twist a stick of chalk to see it.

You can now: describe the pure-shear state on the surface of a twisted shaft; state that it equals tension \(+\tau\) and compression \(-\tau\) on \(45^\circ\) planes; and predict a flat break for ductile and a helical break for brittle materials.

6Angle of twist ~3 min

What is this?

A shaft is a twisting spring. Hold one end of the rubber rod and turn the other: a long thin rod turns a long way for a small effort, while a short fat one hardly moves. The angle of twist says how far one section of a shaft turns relative to another. A car's drive shaft winds up by a small angle when you accelerate hard and unwinds when you ease off.

Combine the three links: \(\gamma_{\max}=c\varphi/L\) (geometry), \(\tau_{\max}=G\gamma_{\max}\) (material), \(\tau_{\max}=Tc/J\) (equilibrium). Eliminating \(\tau_{\max}\) and \(\gamma_{\max}\):

Result · angle of twist of a uniform segment \[\varphi=\frac{TL}{GJ}.\]

In words: twist = torque × length ÷ (shear modulus × polar moment). In radians. \(GJ\) is the torsional rigidity: the stiffer the material and the fatter the shaft, the less it twists.

Reading the formula
\(\varphi\)
how far one end turns relative to the other, in radians.
\(T\)
twist harder and it turns further, in proportion, like a spring.
\(L\)
every slice adds its own small turn, so a longer shaft turns further.
\(G\)
the material's stiffness in shear: aluminium turns about three times as far as steel.
\(J\)
the shape of the section: a fatter shaft turns much less (fourth power of \(d\)).

This is the torsion version of the spring law: the torsional stiffness of a segment is

\[k_t=\frac{T}{\varphi}=\frac{GJ}{L}.\]

Several segments, or torque that changes along the shaft

What is this? (several segments)

Think of the drive shaft as several shorter shafts joined end to end. Each piece twists by its own amount, and the far end turns by the total, just as the end of a chain of springs moves by the sum of their stretches. A piece whose torque acts the other way twists back and undoes part of the rest.

If the torque, the diameter or the material changes along the shaft:

  1. Do: split it into segments in each of which \(T\), \(G\) and \(J\) are constant. Inside one segment the shaft is a single uniform spring, so \(\varphi=TL/(GJ)\) holds there.
  2. Do: find the internal torque \(T_i\) of each segment, with its sign (Section 7). The sign says which way that piece twists.
  3. Do: add the twists, keeping the signs of the internal torques: A piece twisted backwards turns its far end back, so its twist subtracts.
\[\varphi_{B/A}=\varphi_B-\varphi_A=\sum_i\frac{T_iL_i}{G_iJ_i}.\]

In words: the rotation of one section relative to another is the sum of the twists of the pieces between them, like adding the stretches of springs in series.

If \(T\) or \(J\) varies smoothly (a tapered shaft, a distributed torque \(t(x)\) in N·m/m):

\[\varphi=\int_0^L\frac{T(x)}{G\,J(x)}\,\mathrm dx.\]
Watch

\(\varphi\) comes out in radians. Multiply by \(180/\pi\) for degrees. A limit such as "rotation must not exceed 0.5 rad" or "1° per metre" is a stiffness requirement; a stress limit is a strength requirement. A design must satisfy both (Section 8).

You can now: derive \(\varphi=TL/(GJ)\) from geometry, material and equilibrium; use the torsional stiffness \(k_t=GJ/L\); add the signed twists of several segments; and convert radians to degrees.

7Torque diagrams and stepped shafts (fixed at one end) ~5 min

What is this?

Picture a drive shaft held in a wall at one end, with spanners turning it at several points along its length. Start at the free end and walk towards the wall: each time you pass a spanner, the torque the shaft carries jumps by that spanner's torque. The torque diagram records those jumps. The twist diagram then adds up, from the wall outwards, how far each piece turns.

This is the most common exam form (Dec 2024 A3, Dec 2023 A3): a shaft built into a wall at \(A\), made of segments of different diameters (a stepped shaft), with several torques applied along it. Find \(J\), the wall reaction, the internal torque in each segment, the twists and the largest stress.

Recipe · shaft fixed at one end
  1. Do: \(J\) for each diameter (Section 4). Each diameter is a different spring with its own strength and stiffness, and every later step needs them.
  2. Do: Wall reaction from equilibrium of the whole shaft: \(\sum M_x=0\Rightarrow M_A=-\sum(\text{applied torques})\). The wall must hold back the net twist of all the spanners, or the shaft would spin.
  3. Do: Internal torque in each segment: cut, keep the piece away from the wall (no unknown on it). With the sign rule of Section 0, equilibrium of that piece gives \[\begin{aligned}T=\textstyle\sum&(\text{applied torques}\\&\text{beyond the cut}).\end{aligned}\] That piece has only known torques on it, so one equation gives \(T\) with no unknown: the jumps you pass walking in from the free end.
  4. Do: Twists from the wall outward: \(\varphi_A=0\), then \(\varphi_{\text{next}}=\varphi_{\text{prev}}+T_iL_i/(GJ_i)\). The wall cannot turn, so it is the fixed starting point; each segment adds its own twist on top of the one before.
  5. Do: Stresses \(\tau_{\max}=|T_i|c_i/J_i\) in every segment; the largest wins. A thin segment with a small torque can beat a fat one with a big torque. Stress goes with \(T/d^3\), not with \(T\) alone, so every segment must be checked.

Why keep the outer piece? For the piece beyond the cut, its cut face looks back towards the wall, so its outward normal is \(-x\). A positive internal torque points in \(-x\) there, and \(\sum M_x=0\) gives \(-T+\sum(\text{beyond})=0\).

Worked example · stepped shaft

A steel shaft (\(G=80\u{GPa}\)) is fixed at \(A\). Segment \(AB\): diameter 40 mm, length 0.6 m. Segment \(BC\): diameter 25 mm, length 0.4 m. A torque of \(900\u{N\,m}\) acts at \(B\) with its arrow pointing towards the wall (\(-x\)), and \(300\u{N\,m}\) at the free end \(C\) pointing away from the wall (\(+x\)). Find the wall torque, the internal torques, \(\varphi_B\), \(\varphi_C\) and the maximum shear stress.

System

FBD: wall torque \(M_A\) (teal) drawn positive

  1. \(J\): \(J_{AB}=\pi(0.04)^4/32=2.513\times10^{-7}\u{m^4}\); \(J_{BC}=\pi(0.025)^4/32=3.835\times10^{-8}\u{m^4}\).
  2. Wall: \(\sum M_x=0:\ M_A-900+300=0\Rightarrow M_A=+600\u{N\,m}\) (arrow in \(+x\)).
  3. Internal torques (keep the outer piece):
    \[\begin{aligned}BC:&\ T_{BC}=+300\u{N\,m},\\ AB:&\ T_{AB}=-900+300=-600\u{N\,m}.\end{aligned}\] Check with the wall side for \(AB\): the left piece has \(M_A\) and \(T_{AB}\) pointing \(+x\) (outward), so \(M_A+T_{AB}=0\Rightarrow T_{AB}=-600\) ✓.
  4. Twists: \[\begin{aligned}\varphi_B&=\frac{(-600)(0.6)}{(80\times10^9)(2.513\times10^{-7})}\\ &=-0.0179\u{rad}=-1.03^\circ,\\ \varphi_{C/B}&=\frac{300(0.4)}{(80\times10^9)(3.835\times10^{-8})}\\ &=0.0391\u{rad},\\ \varphi_C&=-0.0179+0.0391\\ &=0.0212\u{rad}=1.22^\circ.\end{aligned}\] \(AB\) carries a negative torque, so \(B\) turns backwards; \(BC\) carries a positive one and turns \(C\) forwards again. Keep the signs when adding.
  5. Stresses: \(\tau_{AB}=600(0.02)/J_{AB}=47.7\u{MPa}\); \(\tau_{BC}=300(0.0125)/J_{BC}=97.8\u{MPa}\).

\(\boxed{\begin{aligned}&M_A=600\u{N\,m}\ (+x),\ \varphi_B=-0.0179\u{rad},\\ &\varphi_C=+0.0212\u{rad},\ \tau_{\max}=97.8\u{MPa}\text{ on the surface of }BC\end{aligned}}\)

The thinner segment is the critical one even though it carries half the torque, because \(\tau\propto T/d^3\).

Try it · torque and twist along a stepped shaft (the example above)

Top: internal torque \(T(x)\), a staircase that steps at each applied torque (like the SFD of S2 for point forces). Bottom: angle of twist \(\varphi(x)\) in degrees, straight lines whose slope is \(T/(GJ)\); it starts at 0 at the wall. Thin segments twist faster.

Ready answer · "maximum shear stress and its location"

State the value, the segment and "at the outer surface" (\(\rho=c\)), e.g. "\(\tau_{\max}=97.8\u{MPa}\), on the outer surface of segment \(BC\), all along \(BC\)."

You can now: find the wall torque, the internal torque in each segment (keeping the outer piece) and the torque diagram of a shaft fixed at one end; build the twist from the wall outwards; and locate the maximum shear stress by checking \(|T|c/J\) in every segment.

8Power transmission and shaft design ~2 min

What is this?

A car's drive shaft carries the engine's power to the wheels. Power is how fast work is done: torque times how fast the shaft spins. In first gear the drive shaft turns slowly, so to pass on the same power it must carry a big torque; in top gear it spins fast with a smaller torque. A shaft must be thick enough not to break under its torque, and stiff enough not to twist too far.

A shaft that turns at angular speed \(\omega\) while carrying torque \(T\) transmits power

\[P=T\omega=2\pi fT=\frac{2\pi n}{60}T,\]
Reading the formula
\(T\)
how hard the shaft is being twisted.
\(\omega\)
how fast it spins, in rad/s.
\(T\omega\)
work done per second: torque times the angle turned per second.
\(2\pi f\), \(\dfrac{2\pi n}{60}\)

with \(P\) in W, \(T\) in N·m, \(\omega\) in rad/s, \(f\) in rev/s (Hz) and \(n\) in rev/min.

In words: power = torque × angular speed. For the same power, a slow shaft carries a big torque and needs to be thicker; that is why gearboxes put the reduction (slow, high-torque) shafts at the output.

Design rules
  1. Do: Strength: \(\tau_{\max}\le\tau_{\text{allow}}\Rightarrow d\ge\left(\dfrac{16T}{\pi\tau_{\text{allow}}}\right)^{1/3}\) for a solid shaft. The outer surface carries the highest stress, so the shaft must be thick enough that even there the stress stays below the allowed value.
  2. Do: Stiffness: \(\varphi=TL/(GJ)\le\varphi_{\text{allow}}\Rightarrow J\ge\dfrac{TL}{G\varphi_{\text{allow}}}\). A shaft can be strong enough and still wind up too far, so the driven end lags behind the driving end.
  3. Do: Take the larger diameter of the two, then round up to a standard size. Only the larger diameter meets both limits; rounding down would break one of them.

9Shafts fixed at both ends (statically indeterminate) ~5 min

What is this?

Two people each hold one end of a towel firmly, and a third twists it somewhere in between. Both ends resist. Because neither end can turn, whatever twist one part of the towel takes, the other part must take back. Equilibrium alone cannot say how the two ends share the load; the extra fact that the total twist is zero can. The shorter, stiffer side takes more.

If a shaft is built into walls at both ends, there are two unknown wall torques \(T_A\), \(T_E\) but only one equilibrium equation (\(\sum M_x=0\)). The shaft is statically indeterminate to degree 1 (S1 Section 11). The missing equation is compatibility: both walls are fixed, so the total twist from one end to the other is zero.

Recipe · both ends fixed
  1. Do: Draw the FBD with both wall torques \(T_A\), \(T_E\) pointing \(+x\). Equilibrium: \(T_A+T_E+\sum(\text{applied})=0\). Drawing both positive lets the signs of the answers give the real directions. This is one equation for two unknowns, so it is not enough on its own.
  2. Do: Write every internal torque in terms of \(T_A\) alone (keep the left piece: \(T=-T_A-\sum(\text{applied torques left of the cut})\)). With only one unknown in every segment, the twist condition becomes one equation in \(T_A\).
  3. Do: Compatibility: \(\varphi_{E/A}=\sum_iT_iL_i/(G_iJ_i)=0\). Solve for \(T_A\). Neither wall turns, so whatever twist one part puts in, the rest must take out.
  4. Do: Back-substitute: \(T_E\), the internal torques, the torque diagram, stresses and twists. Once \(T_A\) is known, the shaft is just like one fixed at a single end with all torques known.
Worked example · one torque between two walls

10Geared shafts ~5 min

What is this?

Two meshing gears are a pair of levers that touch at the teeth. The teeth push on each other with one force. On the big gear that force acts at a long radius, so it makes a big torque, but the big gear turns through a small angle. A car's gearbox uses this trade to give a big torque at low speed in first gear.

Two parallel shafts can be linked by a pair of meshing gears. The pitch circle of a gear is the circle on which the teeth effectively roll; its diameter is the pitch diameter, and its radius \(r\) is the lever arm of the tooth force.

Results · a meshing pair \(B\) (radius \(r_B\)) and \(C\) (radius \(r_C\))
  • Force: the teeth push on each other with equal and opposite forces \(F\) (action and reaction, S1 Section 3), tangent to the pitch circles.
  • Torques: \(T_B=Fr_B\), \(T_C=Fr_C\Rightarrow\dfrac{T_C}{T_B}=\dfrac{r_C}{r_B}\).
  • Rotations: the pitch circles roll without slipping, so equal arcs: \(\varphi_Br_B=\varphi_Cr_C\), in opposite senses. The same holds for speeds: \(\omega_Br_B=\omega_Cr_C\).
  • Power is unchanged (ignoring friction): \(T_B\omega_B=T_C\omega_C\).

In words: the bigger gear gets the bigger torque and the smaller rotation, in the ratio of the radii (or the numbers of teeth). It is a lever made of teeth.

Reading the formula
\(F\)
the one push between the teeth, the same on both gears (action and reaction).
\(T=Fr\)
the same push on a longer lever arm gives a bigger torque.
\(\varphi r\)
the arc each pitch circle rolls through; equal on both, because the teeth do not slip.
\(T\omega\)
torque up, speed down by the same ratio: the power passes through unchanged.
Recipe · twist at the loaded end of a gear train
  1. Do: Torque in the input shaft = applied torque \(T\). Tooth force \(F=T/r_B\). The torque reaches gear \(B\) and is handed on as one push at the teeth, with lever arm \(r_B\).
  2. Do: Torque in the other shaft \(T'=Fr_C=T\,r_C/r_B\). The same push acts on gear \(C\), but at radius \(r_C\).
  3. Do: Twist of the shaft fixed at the far end: \(\varphi_C=T'L'/(GJ')\). The far end cannot turn, so gear \(C\) turns by exactly the twist of its shaft.
  4. Do: Gear on the input shaft turns by \(\varphi_B=\varphi_C\,r_C/r_B\) (a rigid-body rotation passed through the teeth). The pitch circles roll through equal arcs, so the smaller gear turns through the bigger angle.
  5. Do: Add the input shaft's own twist: \(\varphi_A=\varphi_B+TL/(GJ)\). (Magnitudes add: both rotations are in the sense of \(T\).) End \(A\) turns by as much as gear \(B\), plus however much the input shaft winds up between \(B\) and \(A\).

11Compound shafts ~4 min

What is this?

Glue a pencil inside a cardboard tube, or bond a steel core inside an aluminium sleeve, and twist the end. Because they are joined, both parts turn by the same angle, so they share the torque, the stiffer part taking more. Put two shafts end to end instead and the opposite happens: the same torque goes through both, and their twists add.

A compound (composite) shaft is made of two materials. There are two ways to join them:

In series (end to end)The same torque passes through both; the twists add: \(\varphi=\sum T_iL_i/(G_iJ_i)\). Nothing new: Section 7.
In parallel (one inside the other, joined)Both parts twist by the same angle; the torques add: \(T=T_1+T_2\). Statically indeterminate: equilibrium + compatibility.
Result · two parts in parallel over length \(L\) \[\begin{aligned}&\frac{T_1L}{G_1J_1}=\frac{T_2L}{G_2J_2}=\varphi,\\ &T_i=T\,\frac{G_iJ_i}{G_1J_1+G_2J_2},\\ &\varphi=\frac{TL}{G_1J_1+G_2J_2}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: each part takes a share of the torque in proportion to its torsional rigidity \(GJ\); together they behave like one shaft of rigidity \(G_1J_1+G_2J_2\).

Reading the formula
\(\dfrac{T_1L}{G_1J_1}=\dfrac{T_2L}{G_2J_2}\)
joined, so both parts turn by the same angle \(\varphi\): the compatibility condition.
\(G_iJ_i\)
each part's torsional rigidity: how hard it is to twist.
\(T_i=T\,\dfrac{G_iJ_i}{G_1J_1+G_2J_2}\)
each part takes a share of the torque in proportion to its rigidity.
\(\varphi=\dfrac{TL}{G_1J_1+G_2J_2}\)
together they twist like one shaft with the two rigidities added, like two springs side by side.

Parts joined by a pin

What is this? (pin joint)

Push a skewer through a cardboard tube and the pencil inside it. Turn the pencil and the skewer drags the tube round. The skewer is pushed at the two places where it crosses from pencil into tube, one push on each side of the pencil, in opposite directions. Those two pushes form a couple, and that couple is the torque handed from the inner shaft to the tube.

Sometimes the inner shaft and the outer tube are joined only by a pin through both. The pin then carries the torque between them as a pair of shear forces \(V\) at the interface, one on each side of the shaft, a distance \(d\) apart (the shaft diameter):

\[T_{\text{pin}}=V\,d,\qquad V=\tau_{\text{pin}}\,\frac{\pi d_{\text{pin}}^2}{4}.\]

In words: the pin is sheared at the two places where it crosses from shaft into tube; the two shear forces form a couple, and that couple is the torque handed from the shaft to the tube. A limit on the pin's shear stress therefore limits the torque the pin can pass. (This is the set-up of Dec 2014 A3(b), in the exam taster.)

15Concept checks

1 · Doubling the diameter

A solid shaft's diameter is doubled; the torque stays the same. By what factors do \(\tau_{\max}\) and \(\varphi\) change?

Answer

\(\tau_{\max}\propto1/d^3\): falls 8 times. \(\varphi\propto1/J\propto1/d^4\): falls 16 times.

2 · Where is the stress zero?

Where in a twisted solid shaft is the shear stress zero, and why can a hollow shaft be more efficient?

Answer

On the axis (\(\rho=0\)). Material near the axis carries little stress and adds little to \(J\), so removing it saves much weight for little loss of strength (Section 3, "Try it").

3 · Same power, different speed

A motor's output is passed through a gearbox that halves the speed. Ignoring losses, what happens to the torque in the output shaft?

Answer

\(P=T\omega\) is unchanged, so the torque doubles; the output shaft needs \(2^{1/3}=1.26\) times the diameter for the same stress.

4 · Both ends fixed

A uniform shaft between two walls carries one torque \(T_0\) at its middle. What does each wall carry, and what is the rotation at the middle?

Answer

By symmetry \(T_0/2\) each, opposite to \(T_0\). Each half carries \(T_0/2\) over \(L/2\): \(\varphi=T_0L/(4GJ)\).

5 · Chalk

Why does a stick of chalk twisted to failure break on a \(45^\circ\) helix, but a steel bar break flat?

Answer

Pure shear \(\tau\) is equivalent to tension \(\sigma_1=\tau\) on \(45^\circ\) planes (Section 5). Chalk is weakest in tension, so it fails there; steel is weakest in shear, so it fails on the cross-section where \(\tau\) is largest.
