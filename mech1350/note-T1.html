Sign conventions for this chapter (and T2–T4)
  • Work \(W\) is positive when done by the system on its surroundings (a gas expanding and pushing a piston out) and negative when done on the system (a piston compressing the gas). Section 4 shows this, and T4 uses the same rule.
  • Heat \(Q\) is positive when it flows into the system and negative when it flows out (the standard convention, used in T4).

1Thermal science, thermodynamics and heat transfer ~4 min

What is this?

Think of energy as money. Thermodynamics is the accountant: it says how much energy has to move for a change to happen, for example how much it takes to melt a block of ice. Heat transfer is the payment system: it says how fast that energy can actually be delivered, and by which route. Fluid mechanics describes the moving air and water that often carry the energy around.

Picture thermal science as a triangle. At the centre is thermal systems engineering: designing engines, power plants, air-conditioners, boilers and the like. Three subjects feed it:

  • Thermodynamics: conservation of mass, conservation of energy, the laws of thermodynamics (chapters T2, T4);
  • Heat transfer: conduction, convection, radiation (chapter T3);
  • Fluid mechanics: hydrostatics and fluid dynamics (Themes 2 and 3 of this course).

The three subjects that make up thermal science, linked by arrows around the central box.

Definition · thermodynamics

Thermodynamics is the study of energy, energy transformations (changes from one form to another) and how energy relates to matter. It describes how systems change when their energy content changes. The name comes from the Greek therme (heat) and dynamis (power).

What do we measure to see that a system has changed? Three linked properties: temperature, pressure and volume. Heat a sealed can of gas: its temperature rises and so does its pressure. Let the gas push a piston out: its volume grows, and its pressure and temperature fall. Changing one usually changes the others, which is why they are drawn on a circle. T2 makes this exact (the ideal-gas law \(pV=mRT\) and steam tables).

What is this? (heat transfer)

Heat transfer has three routes, like three ways to deliver a payment. Conduction passes the energy hand to hand along a line of people: each molecule nudges its neighbour. Convection puts it in a delivery van: a moving fluid carries the energy away. Radiation is like a wireless transfer: electromagnetic waves need no material at all, which is how sunlight crosses empty space.

Definition · heat transfer

Heat transfer is the study of energy in transit (energy on the move because of a temperature difference), including how energy, matter, space and time are related: how fast the energy moves and where it goes. Its three principal modes are

  • conduction: passed from molecule to neighbouring molecule through a material;
  • convection: carried away by a moving fluid;
  • radiation: carried by electromagnetic waves (infrared light), needing no material.

A pot of water on a gas flame shows all three modes at once.

The difference between the two subjects: thermodynamics says how much energy a change needs (for example, 334 kJ to melt 1 kg of ice); heat transfer says how fast it can be delivered (how long the ice takes to melt in a given room). Chapter T3 is all about heat transfer.

You can now: name the three subjects that make up thermal science; define thermodynamics and heat transfer; say which one asks "how much" and which asks "how fast"; and name the three modes of heat transfer.

2Why energy matters: from fire to electricity ~8 min

What is this?

Every machine in this story is an exchange booth for energy. Fire turns the chemical energy in fuel into heat; Watt's engine turns heat into motion; a generator turns motion into electricity. Each booth changes the form of the energy, not the amount, although part of it always ends up as unwanted heat on the way, like a fee charged at each booth.

Here is the story of how people learned to turn one form of energy into another. You will not be asked dates in the exam, but the story explains why engineers care about thermodynamics: every machine in it is an energy converter.

Fire: heat and light

The oldest use of burned fossil fuels (and wood) is for heat and energy directly. Four uses: light (an oil lamp), cooking (a brick stove), warmth (a heated brick bed, the Chinese kang) and industrial heat (kilns and furnaces, and today the boilers of power stations).

Machines for motion

To get kinetic (mechanical) energy, energy of motion, people first used animals (a donkey turning a millstone, horses pulling a plough), then wind (windmills) and water (water wheels). None of these used heat.

The industrial revolution: heat into motion

In the 18th–19th century, steam power changed everything. James Watt's (1736–1819, UK) improved steam engine, patented in 1775, was first used to pump water out of mines; from the 1780s it powered other machines. Factories could now be built anywhere, not only beside rivers that could turn a water wheel. In short:

\[\begin{aligned}&\text{burn fossil fuels}\to\text{thermal energy}\\&\xrightarrow{\text{J. Watt}}\text{kinetic energy}\end{aligned}\]

Before Watt there was the Newcomen atmospheric engine. Watt's engine added a separate condenser: steam pushes a piston, the steam is condensed (cooled back to water) in a separate vessel so the cylinder stays hot, and a flywheel turns the to-and-fro piston motion into rotation. Steam then moved vehicles: the steam locomotive, and before it Cugnot's three-wheeled steam carriage.

The internal combustion engine

In 1896 Karl Benz was granted a patent for the first combustion engine with horizontally opposed pistons. Instead of burning fuel outside to make steam, an internal combustion engine burns it inside the cylinder. The four-stroke cycle is the one used in car engines today:

  1. Intake: the intake valve opens and the piston moves down, drawing in the air–fuel mixture.
  2. Compression: both valves closed; the piston moves up and squeezes the mixture into the small combustion chamber.
  3. Power: the spark plug fires; the burning gas at high pressure and temperature forces the piston down. This is the only stroke that does work on the crankshaft (through the connecting rod).
  4. Exhaust: the exhaust valve opens and the piston moves up, pushing the burned gases out.

Electricity

  • Benjamin Franklin (1706–1790, Boston, USA) showed with his kite experiment that lightning is electricity. Nature makes electricity; how could we make it ourselves?
  • James Clerk Maxwell (1831–1879, Edinburgh, UK) showed that electricity and magnetism, once thought of as two separate forces, are one. His four equations (Gauss's law for the electric field, Gauss's law for the magnetic field, Faraday's law, and Ampère's law with Maxwell's correction) describe all electromagnetic effects: \[\begin{aligned}\nabla\cdot\mathbf D&=\rho,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\\ \nabla\times\mathbf E&=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\\ \nabla\times\mathbf H&=\mathbf J+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}.\end{aligned}\] You do not need these equations in MECH1350. The one that matters for engineering is Faraday's law: a changing magnetic field makes a voltage, so spinning a magnet inside a coil (an AC generator) turns kinetic energy into electrical energy.
  • Thomas Edison (1847–1931, USA; light bulb, the alkaline Ni–Fe battery, the company that became GE) and Alexander Graham Bell (1847–1922, USA; the telephone, AT&T Bell Laboratories) turned electricity into everyday products: bulbs, gramophones, radios, televisions, refrigerators.

The whole chain together:

\[\begin{aligned}&\text{fossil fuels}\to\text{thermal}\xrightarrow{\text{Watt}}\text{kinetic}\\&\xrightarrow{\text{Maxwell}}\text{electrical}\end{aligned}\]

and lists what the electricity is used for: bulbs, telephone, TV, refrigerator, … "What else?" (a class question; see Group work, Section 19). Two kinds of thermal power station do exactly this chain:

  • Coal-fired plant: pulverised coal burns with air in a boiler; the heat turns water into high-pressure steam; the steam spins a steam turbine, which turns a generator to make electricity; the used steam is cooled back into water in a condenser and pumped back to the boiler; combustion gases leave through the stack.
  • Nuclear plant (pressurised water reactor): heat from the reactor core (controlled by control rods) heats pressurised water, which makes steam in a separate steam generator; from there on it is the same turbine–generator–condenser loop. The large cooling towers reject the condenser's heat to the air.

Thermal science in every branch of engineering

Engineering branches and thermal problems they solve:

  • mechanical engineers: engines and turbines;
  • electrical engineers: power plants (the nuclear plant schematic: reactor, heat exchangers, turbine, generator, condenser);
  • civil engineers: temperature and heat flow through tall buildings;
  • industrial engineers: heat in production lines and machinery;
  • biomedical engineers: wearable sensors (a smartwatch) and body heat during exercise;
  • computing engineers: cooling air flow through racks in a data centre (the coloured streamlines).

You can now: trace the chain fossil fuels → thermal → kinetic → electrical; describe the four strokes of a car engine; and explain how a coal-fired or nuclear power station makes electricity.

3Where our energy comes from ~2 min

What is this?

If energy is money, the Sun is the salary that fills almost every account. Coal and oil are old savings: sunlight stored by plants long ago. Wind and rain are this year's sunshine, passed on by the air and the water cycle. Only nuclear, geothermal and tidal energy are paid from other sources.

Understanding energy means understanding how the Universe works. Apart from nuclear, geothermal (heat from inside the Earth) and other terrestrial energy (such as the tides), almost all our energy comes from the Sun, through heat and radiation. Fossil fuels are ancient plants that stored sunlight; wind is driven by uneven solar heating; rain that fills hydroelectric dams was evaporated by the Sun.

Not to scale: Sun diameter \(1.39\times10^9\u{m}\), Earth diameter \(1.274\times10^7\u{m}\), Sun–Earth distance \(1.496\times10^{11}\u{m}\) (±1.7% over the year); solar radiation arriving on the atmosphere (11 km above the ground) 1377 W/m².

Worked estimate · how much solar power does each person get?

The Earth intercepts sunlight over its shadow area: a disc of the Earth's radius, not the whole sphere (sunlight arrives from one direction, so the Earth "blocks" a circle of area \(\pi r^2\)).

  1. Solar power per square metre at the top of the atmosphere: about \(1.4\times10^3\u{W/m^2}\).
  2. Disc area, with Earth diameter about \(1.3\times10^7\u{m}\): \[\pi\Big(\frac{1.3\times10^7}{2}\Big)^2\approx1.33\times10^{14}\u{m^2}\]
  3. Total power received: \[\begin{aligned}P&\approx1.4\times10^3\times1.33\times10^{14}\\&\approx1.9\times10^{17}\u{W}\approx2\times10^{17}\u{W}\end{aligned}\]
  4. Number of people: about \(7.8\times10^9\), rounded here to \(10^{10}\).
  5. Per person: \(2\times10^{17}/10^{10}=\boxed{\approx2\times10^7\u{W}}\), that is 20 MW each.

For comparison, the appliance table in Section 7 shows a whole household using a few kilowatts: the Sun delivers thousands of times more than we use. The engineering problem is collecting and converting it.

the same estimate without the rounding

Disc area with the Earth diameter \(1.274\times10^7\u{m}\): \(\pi(6.37\times10^6)^2=1.275\times10^{14}\u{m^2}\). \(\pi r^2\), radius = half the diameter.

With 1377 W/m²: \[\begin{aligned}P&=1377\times1.275\times10^{14}\\&=1.76\times10^{17}\u{W}\end{aligned}\] Still "about \(2\times10^{17}\)" to one significant figure.

Shared by \(7.8\times10^9\) people: \(2.25\times10^7\u{W}\) each, again about \(2\times10^7\u{W}\). An order-of-magnitude estimate does not depend on the rounding.

You can now: name where our energy comes from; and estimate the solar power reaching the Earth, and per person, using the shadow disc \(\pi r^2\).

4Energy and work ~5 min

What is this?

Energy is the balance in an account; work is one way of moving it. Push a box along the floor and energy leaves you and arrives in the box: the push is the transfer. The harder the push and the further the box moves along it, the more energy is moved.

Definition · energy and work

Science defines energy as the ability to do work (Greek energeia). Work measures what happens as a result of transferring energy from one place to another: when a force pushes something along, energy passes from whatever pushes to whatever is pushed.

The SI unit of energy is the joule (J), after James Prescott Joule (1818–1889, UK). 1 J is the energy needed for a force of 1 newton (N) to push an object through a distance of 1 m.

Result · work done by a constant force \[W=F\,s\]

In words: work = force × displacement in the direction of the force.

\[\begin{aligned}1\u{N}\times1\u{m}&=1\u{N\,m}=1\u{J}\\1\u{N}&=1\u{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}\end{aligned}\]

The newton itself comes from Newton's second law \(F=ma\): 1 N gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s².

As a free-body diagram: a constant 1 N push moves a ball 1 m along the push, so the work done on it is 1 J. (Gravity and the floor's support act at right angles to the motion and do no work.)

Going further · force at an angle

Only the part of the force along the displacement does work: if the force makes an angle \(\theta\) with the displacement, \(W=Fs\cos\theta\). A force at right angles to the motion (\(\theta=90^\circ\)) does no work, which is why the ball's weight does no work while it rolls along a level floor.

Positive and negative work: gas in a cylinder

What is this? (positive and negative work)

Watch the gas's account. When the gas pushes the piston out, it pays energy to the piston: energy leaves the gas, and we call that positive work by the gas. When the piston is pushed in, the piston pays the gas: energy arrives, and the work by the gas is negative. The sign is always written from the point of view of the account you chose.

Take air in a cylinder closed by a piston. Take the air as the thing we study (the "system", Section 8).

  • Expansion: the air pushes the piston up with force \(F\) and the piston moves up a distance \(s\), in the same direction as the push. The air does work on the piston: positive work (energy leaves the air as work).
  • Contraction (compression): the piston is pushed down; the air still pushes up on the piston, but the piston moves down, against that push. Work is done on the air: negative work by the air (energy enters the air as work).
Example · work by an expanding gas

Gas in a cylinder pushes a piston with a steady force of 500 N while the piston rises 0.10 m. A second time, an outside force pushes the piston back down the same 0.10 m against the gas's 500 N push. Find the work done by the gas each time.

Free-body diagram of the piston: the gas pushes up (500 N), and the outside (atmosphere, weights or a hand) pushes down; if the piston moves slowly, these balance.

  1. Expansion: force and displacement both upward, \(W=Fs=500(0.10)=\boxed{+50\u{J}}\) (done by the gas).
  2. Compression: the gas's force is up, the displacement is down, so the gas does \(\boxed{-50\u{J}}\); equivalently, 50 J of work is done on the gas. The gas pushes up while the piston moves down, so the gas pays nothing out: the piston pays 50 J into the gas's account.
Watch

"Positive" or "negative" work always depends on which body you are talking about. In the compression above, the outside does +50 J on the gas, and the gas does −50 J. State the system first, then the sign (Section 0 convention: positive = done by the system).

You can now: define energy, work and the joule; calculate \(W=Fs\); and give the sign of the work done by a gas that expands or is compressed.

5Other units of energy ~4 min

What is this?

Joules, kilowatt-hours, calories, Btu and electronvolts are different currencies for the same money. A kilowatt-hour is a large banknote (3.6 million joules); an electronvolt is a tiny coin. Converting is changing money at a fixed exchange rate: multiply by how many joules one unit is worth, or divide to go back.

The joule is the SI unit, but four other units are common:

Definitions · energy units
  • kilowatt-hour (kWh): the energy used by a 1 kW appliance running for 1 hour; the unit on electricity bills (the "unit" or 度 of a Hong Kong bill). \[\begin{aligned}1\u{kWh}&=1000\u{W}\times3600\u{s}\\&=3.6\times10^6\u{J}=3.6\u{MJ}\end{aligned}\]
  • calorie (cal): the heat needed to raise the temperature of one gram of water by 1 °C. 1 cal ≈ 4.19 J (the conversion table in Section 15 uses 1 kcal = 4.1868 kJ).
  • British thermal unit (BTU or Btu): used mainly in heating, ventilation and air-conditioning (HVAC); air-conditioner capacities are often quoted in BTU per hour. Its exact value varies slightly between definitions, all close to 1055 J (1 Btu = 1.0551 kJ).
  • electronvolt (eV): used in atomic, particle and high-energy physics. 1 eV is the work done in moving one electron through a potential difference of 1 V: \(1\u{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}\u{J}\) (more precisely \(1.60217653\times10^{-19}\u{J}\)).

On food labels "Calorie" (capital C) means the kilocalorie, 1 kcal = 1000 cal ≈ 4.19 kJ: the heat to warm 1 kg of water by 1 °C. This note uses "cal" for the small unit and "kcal" for the food unit.

You may also see the calorie defined as the heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 mL of water by 1 °C; 1 mL of water has a mass of 1 g, so this is the same definition.

Result · conversions to memorise \[\begin{aligned}1\u{kWh}&=3.6\u{MJ}\\1\u{kcal}&\approx4.19\u{kJ}\\1\u{Btu}&\approx1.055\u{kJ}\\1\u{eV}&\approx1.602\times10^{-19}\u{J}\end{aligned}\]

In words: to change any energy to joules, multiply by "how many joules one of that unit is"; to go back, divide.

Example · a 1.5 kW air-conditioner

An air-conditioner draws 1500 W (Section 7's table) for 8 hours a night. Find the electrical energy used in kWh, MJ, kcal and Btu.

  1. kWh: \(1.5\u{kW}\times8\u{h}=\boxed{12\u{kWh}}\).
  2. Joules: \(12\times3.6\u{MJ}=\boxed{43.2\u{MJ}}\) (\(4.32\times10^{7}\u{J}\)).
  3. kcal: \(4.32\times10^7/4186.8=\boxed{1.03\times10^4\u{kcal}}\), about five days of food energy for an adult. Dividing by the joules in one kcal counts how many kcal "notes" the energy is worth.
  4. Btu: \(4.32\times10^7/1055.1=\boxed{4.09\times10^4\u{Btu}}\).

6Conservation, conversion and transfer of energy ~5 min

What is this?

Energy is money that can never be printed or burned. It can move from one account to another (a transfer) or be changed into another currency (a conversion), but the total over all accounts stays the same. When some seems to vanish, look for the account you forgot: usually a little warming of the surroundings.

Law · conservation of energy

"Energy cannot be created or destroyed." It may appear in different forms, but the total amount of energy present does not change.

In words: energy in − energy out = change of energy stored. Nothing is lost; it only changes form or place.

Two pictures show it:

  • A book falling from a hand: gravitational potential energy turns into kinetic energy as it falls (and, when it hits the floor, into sound and a little heating of the book and floor).
  • A hair dryer: electrical energy goes in; out come kinetic energy (mechanical energy of the moving air, driven by the fan) and thermal energy (the hot air, from the heating element). Electrical in = kinetic out + thermal out.
Example · a falling book

A 0.8 kg book falls 1.5 m from a shelf. Ignoring air resistance, how fast is it moving just before it lands?

Energy conserved: potential energy lost = kinetic energy gained. With no air resistance nothing leaves the book's accounts: energy only moves from the height account to the motion account.

\[mgh=\tfrac12 mV^2\ \Rightarrow\ V=\sqrt{2gh}\] \[V=\sqrt{2(9.81)(1.5)}=\boxed{5.42\u{m/s}}\]

The mass cancels; the energy involved is \(mgh=0.8(9.81)(1.5)=11.8\u{J}\).

Energy conversion: from sources to electricity

What is this? (conversion)

Converting is changing currency, not spending. Chemical energy in coal becomes heat in steam, then rotation in a turbine, then electricity. Most routes to electricity pass through the same booth: heat.

Here is a flow chart from energy sources to electrical energy:

  • Energy sources are non-renewable (nuclear, fossil fuel: they run out) or renewable (biomass, geothermal, solar, wind, hydraulic: they are replaced naturally).
  • Nuclear gives atomic energy; fossil fuel and biomass give chemical energy (released by burning).
  • Atomic energy, chemical energy and geothermal energy (and solar heat) all pass through a box marked "?" before becoming mechanical energy. The missing box is thermal energy (heat): the reactor or the fire heats water into steam, and a steam turbine turns the heat into rotation (Section 2's power plants).
  • Wind and hydraulic (falling or flowing water) energy are already mechanical: they turn a turbine directly, with no heat step.
  • Solar energy can also become electricity directly, by solar (photovoltaic) cells, skipping both heat and mechanical energy.
  • Mechanical energy turns a generator to give electrical energy.

This is why thermal science matters so much: most of the world's electricity passes through the "thermal energy" box.

Energy transfers

What is this? (transfer)

There are three ways to pay. Work is handing it over with a push. Heating is a payment that only ever flows from the hotter body to the colder one. Waves carry it across a distance, like a transfer sent down a wire.

An analogy: energy is like money in the bank. You can use it to do many things, and you can move it in different ways (writing a cheque or handing over cash), but moving it does not create or destroy any: what leaves one account arrives in another.

Three ways to transfer energy
  1. By a force acting on an object (work, Section 4): pushing a piston, turning a shaft, lifting a load.
  2. By heating, caused by a temperature difference: from a hot object to a cold one (heat, \(Q\); chapter T3).
  3. By waves, oscillations of particles or fields: water waves, sound waves, electromagnetic waves (light, radio, the radiation of T3).

In thermodynamics (T4) the first two are the important ones: energy crosses the boundary of a closed system only as work or as heat.

You can now: state the conservation of energy and use it (falling book); follow the flow chart from energy sources to electricity, including the missing "thermal" box; and list the three ways energy is transferred.

7Power ~4 min

What is this?

Energy is how much money; power is how fast it is spent. A wage of HK$100 per hour is a rate, not an amount; work 8 hours and you have an amount. In the same way a watt is a joule per second, and power × time gives energy. A 1250 W hair dryer for 6 minutes and a 15 W LED for 8 hours use almost the same energy (Concept check 5): one spends fast, the other slowly.

Work plus the effect of time gives power. Energy cannot move from one place to another instantaneously; it takes time. So besides "how much energy" we ask "how fast".

Definition · power

Power is the rate at which energy is transferred from one place to another (the rate at which work is done). Unit: the watt, \(1\u{W}=1\u{J/s}\).

\[P=\frac{W}{t}\]

Power is also written \(\dot W\) ("W-dot": work per second).

In words: power = energy transferred ÷ time taken. Equivalently, energy = power × time.

This is where the kilowatt-hour comes from: power (kW) × time (h) is an energy.

Example · replacing a bulb

A 100 W incandescent bulb is replaced by an equally bright LED (15 W). It is on 5 hours a day. How much energy is saved in a year?

  1. Power saved: \(100-15=85\u{W}=0.085\u{kW}\). Both bulbs give the same light, so only the difference in their spending rate is saved.
  2. Time: \(5\times365=1825\u{h}\).
  3. Energy saved: \(0.085\times1825=\boxed{155\u{kWh}}\), which is \(155.1\times3.6=558\u{MJ}\).
Example · power of a lift

A lift raises 800 kg (car plus passengers) through 30 m in 20 s at steady speed. What is the minimum power of its motor?

Free-body diagram of the car: cable tension \(F\) up, weight \(mg\) down; at steady speed they balance, so \(F=mg=800(9.81)=7848\u{N}\).

  1. Work done by the cable: \(W=Fs=7848(30)=2.354\times10^5\u{J}\). At steady speed the car never speeds up, so all this work goes into the height account, \(mgz\).
  2. Power: \[P=\frac{W}{t}=\frac{2.354\times10^5}{20}=\boxed{11.8\u{kW}}\]

"Minimum" because a real motor also loses energy to friction and heating.

8Thermodynamic systems ~7 min

What is this?

A system is a fence you draw on purpose around the thing you want to study. Everything inside the fence is the system, the fence line is the boundary, and everything outside is the surroundings. Then you stand at the fence and watch what crosses it: that is all thermodynamic bookkeeping is. Move the fence and the same problem gives a different answer, so always draw it first.

Definitions · system, boundary, surroundings
  • The thermodynamic system is the region of space, or the quantity of matter, that you choose to analyse: the item chosen for thermodynamic analysis. (It is also called a control volume; see the box below.)
  • The surface that encloses it is the system boundary. It can be real (the walls of a tank, the inside face of a piston) or imaginary (a dashed line drawn across the inlet of a pipe). It may be fixed or move (a piston face moves as the gas expands).
  • Everything outside the boundary is the surroundings. System + surroundings together form the universe (in thermodynamics, "universe" just means "everything that matters here").

A system inside a dashed boundary; energy crosses the boundary as heat in and work out.

Choosing the system is the first step of every thermodynamics problem: the boundary decides what counts as "in" and "out". Draw it as a dashed line, then look at what crosses it.

Three types of system

What is this? (open, closed, isolated)

Ask what can get through the fence. An open system has a gate: matter walks in and out (air through a jet engine). A closed system has no gate, but energy can still be passed over the fence (heat through a pot lid, work through a moving piston). An isolated system is a sealed wall: no gate, and nothing passed over.

What can cross the boundary? Two things matter: mass (matter flowing in or out) and energy (as heat or work). A pot on a stove shows the three possibilities:

Definitions · isolated, closed and open systems
  • Isolated system: neither mass nor energy can cross the boundary. Picture (a): the whole pot and stove inside a sealed, perfectly insulated box. Nothing gets in or out; its total energy can never change.
  • Closed system (also called a control mass): mass cannot cross the boundary, but energy can. Picture (b): a pot with a tight lid on a stove: heat goes in from the flame, but no water or steam escapes. The amount of matter inside is fixed.
  • Open system (also called a control volume): both mass and energy can cross the boundary. Picture (c): an open pot under a running tap: water flows in, and heat (and steam) cross the boundary too.
Result · the one-question test (exam type T1.1)

Does mass cross the boundary? Yes → open. No → ask: does energy (heat or work) cross? Yes → closed. No → isolated.

Going further · "control volume" and "control mass"

The system is often loosely called a control volume. Strictly, engineers keep control volume for an open system (a fixed region that matter flows through, such as a turbine or nozzle) and use control mass for a closed system (a fixed lump of matter, such as the gas in a sealed cylinder). The wind-turbine example in Section 9 uses the words this way too: the open wind turbine is a control volume, the battery a closed system. In an exam answer, "open system (control volume)" and "closed system (control mass)" are always safe.

Going further · is anything truly isolated?

No real system is perfectly isolated: some heat always leaks. A sealed, well-insulated vacuum flask over a short time is close. The "universe" (system + surroundings) is isolated by definition, which is why T4 states the second law for the universe.

Other examples

  • Piston–cylinder device: a gas trapped under a piston loaded with weights. The boundary is the inner walls of the cylinder and the underside of the piston. The gas cannot escape, so no mass crosses: closed system. Energy can cross: heat through the walls, and work when the piston moves. The boundary itself moves with the piston; a moving boundary is allowed in a closed system.
  • Jet engine: air flows in at the front (through the fan), fuel is injected and burned in the engine core, and hot gas and bypass air flow out at the back. The dashed boundary is drawn around the engine, with air crossing it at the inlet and the exits: open system (control volume).
Watch

"Energy crosses" does not make a system open. A sealed battery being charged, a closed pressure cooker on a stove, a piston being pushed: energy crosses, mass does not, so they are all closed. Only a flow of matter across the boundary makes a system open.

You can now: define system, boundary and surroundings; classify a device as isolated, closed or open with the one-question test; and explain why a moving piston or a charging battery is still closed.

9Worked example: a wind turbine and its battery ~5 min

What is this?

Now use the fence on a real machine. Draw it round the turbine and generator: air walks through, so the system is open. Draw it round the battery instead: only electrical energy is passed in along the wires, so it is closed. Same machine, two fences, two answers.

Worked example · a wind turbine and its battery

A wind turbine–electric generator is mounted on top of a tower. As wind blows steadily across the turbine blades, electricity is generated. The electric output of the generator is fed to a storage battery.

(a) Considering only the wind turbine–electric generator as the system, identify locations on the system boundary where the system interacts with the surroundings. Describe changes occurring within the system with time.

(b) Repeat for a system that includes only the storage battery.

Engineering model: 1. in part (a) the system is the control volume shown by the dashed line around the turbine–generator; 2. in part (b) the system is the closed system shown by the dashed line around the battery; 3. the wind is steady.

Solution.

  1. (a) Turbine–generator: an open system. Air flows in across the boundary on the upwind side and out on the downwind side: mass crosses, so it is open. The other main interaction is the electric current in the wires crossing the boundary at the bottom of the tower. Seen at the large (macroscopic) scale, a current is not a mass transfer (the electrons stay in the wire loop; what crosses is electrical energy, a form of work). With a steady wind, the turbine–generator is likely to reach steady-state operation: the blades turn at constant speed and a steady current is generated, so nothing inside changes with time.
  2. (b) Battery: a closed system. No matter crosses its boundary. The main interaction is the electric current passing into the battery through the wires, again not a mass transfer. As the battery charges, chemical reactions occur inside it; its surface may warm up a little, so a small thermal interaction (heat loss to the surroundings) may occur, of secondary importance. As it charges, the state inside changes with time (more chemical energy stored): the battery is not at steady state.
What is this? (steady state)

Picture a shop with customers coming in and going out at the same rate: people keep flowing through, but the crowd inside stays the same size. That is steady state. A piggy bank that only receives coins is not steady: what is inside keeps growing, like the charging battery.

Definition · steady state

A system is at steady state when nothing at any point inside it changes with time, even though mass or energy keeps flowing through. The turbine in a steady wind is steady (air and energy flow through at constant rates); the battery being charged is not (its stored energy keeps rising).

Ready answer · "is it open, closed or isolated?" (exam type T1.1)
  1. Do: Draw (or imagine) the boundary around the device as the question describes it. The answer depends on where the fence is, so fix it before asking what crosses.
  2. Do: Mass crossing? Look for an inlet and an outlet: air, fuel, water or exhaust flowing through. If so: open. A gate in the fence is the only thing that makes a system open, so checking it first settles most questions at once.
  3. Do: No flow? Heat through the walls, a moving piston, a turning shaft or an electric current means energy crosses: closed. Energy can be passed over a fence that has no gate, so energy crossing only rules out "isolated".
  4. Do: Nothing crosses (sealed and insulated): isolated. A sealed, insulated wall lets neither matter nor energy through, so the total energy inside can never change.
  5. Do: Electric current and heat are energy, never mass. A moving piston changes the volume, not the mass. In a current the electrons go round the wire loop and come back, so no matter collects inside the fence; only energy is delivered.

Usual answers: open: jet engine, wind turbine, car engine (running), turbine, compressor, pump, nozzle, boiler with feed water, kettle with the lid off while boiling, tap filling a pot, human body. Closed: piston–cylinder with valves shut, sealed rigid tank, liquid-in-glass thermometer, battery, sealed pressure cooker (before it vents). Isolated: an ideal sealed and insulated flask, the universe.

You can now: say where a system interacts with its surroundings and what crosses (mass, work, heat, electricity); and decide whether a system is at steady state.

10Total energy: internal, kinetic and potential ~5 min

What is this?

Back to money: a system keeps its energy in three accounts. Kinetic energy is what it has because the whole thing moves; potential energy is what it has because it sits high up; internal energy is hidden inside, in the jiggling and pulling of its molecules and in their chemical bonds. Total energy is just the sum of the three balances. In thermal problems the hidden account usually changes far more than the other two.

A system can store energy in many forms. Neglecting magnetic and electric energy and surface tension (all tiny in the engines and tanks of this course), the total energy has three parts.

Result · total energy of a system \[E=U+KE+PE=U+\frac{mV^2}{2}+mgz\]

In words: total energy = internal energy + kinetic energy (half mass × speed squared) + potential energy (mass × \(g\) × height).

Per unit mass (divide by \(m\)), written with small letters:

\[e=u+ke+pe=u+\frac{V^2}{2}+gz\]

In words: energy per kilogram = internal energy per kg + speed²/2 + \(g\) × height. Unit: J/kg.

Reading the formula
\(E\)
the system's total balance over all three accounts.
\(U\)
the hidden account: molecules jiggling and pulling on each other, and chemical bonds.
\(\dfrac{mV^2}{2}\)
the motion account: the whole system moving at speed \(V\). Double the speed and it grows four times.
\(mgz\)
the height account, measured from the \(z=0\) level you chose. Only its changes matter.
small letters \(e,u,ke,pe\)
the same balances per kilogram: divide by \(m\).
Definitions · the three parts
  • Kinetic energy \(KE=\tfrac12mV^2\): energy of the system's motion as a whole, \(V\) being its velocity relative to a fixed reference frame.
  • Potential energy \(PE=mgz\): energy due to the system's height in a gravity field, \(z\) being the elevation of its centre of gravity above a reference plane you choose and \(g\) the gravitational acceleration.
  • Internal energy \(U\): energy stored inside the matter at the molecular scale: the kinetic energy of the molecules (their random jiggling, which we sense as temperature), the potential energies between molecules (the forces holding a liquid or solid together, which change when it melts or boils) and the chemical energy of the particles (bonds broken or made in burning or in a battery).

Left: a box moving at speed \(V\) (kinetic energy, "K.E.") at height \(h\) above the ground (potential energy, "P.E."). Right: inside the box, molecules move at random (intermolecular K.E.) and pull on each other (intermolecular P.E.): together these are the internal energy.

KE and PE are macroscopic (they belong to the system moving or sitting as a whole); \(U\) is microscopic (it belongs to the molecules). A cup of tea on a still table has \(KE=0\) but plenty of \(U\).

Example · how big is each term?

1 kg of water flows at 10 m/s at a height of 50 m above the reference level. Find \(ke\) and \(pe\), and compare them with the internal energy needed to warm the water by just 1 °C (about 4.18 kJ/kg, the specific heat of water).

  1. \(ke=V^2/2=10^2/2=\boxed{50\u{J/kg}}\).
  2. \(pe=gz=9.81(50)=\boxed{490.5\u{J/kg}}\).
  3. Warming by 1 °C: \(\Delta u\approx4180\u{J/kg}\), eight times the PE of a 50 m height and over 80 times the KE at 10 m/s. Warming raises the hidden account: each kilogram of water needs about 4.18 kJ for every degree.

This is why many thermodynamics problems (T2, T4) ignore KE and PE: thermal and phase changes involve far more energy per kilogram. (The specific heat \(c\) of water comes from HKDSE Physics and is used again in T2.)

Watch

Here \(V\) is velocity. In Section 14 \(V\) is volume. Units tell you which: m/s or m³. Also, \(mgz\) must use \(m\) in kg and \(z\) in m to give joules; \(gz\) on its own is in J/kg (m²/s² = J/kg, see Section 15).

You can now: write \(E=U+KE+PE\) and its per-kilogram form; explain what internal energy is made of; and compare the sizes of \(ke\), \(pe\) and \(\Delta u\).

11Pressure ~10 min

What is this?

Pressure is how hard something pushes on each square metre, not the total push. Stand on snow in ordinary shoes and you sink; in snowshoes you don't: the same weight spread over more area gives less pressure. A gas presses like this on every wall of its container, at right angles to the wall.

Definition · pressure

Pressure is the force per unit area that a fluid (gas or liquid) exerts on a surface, acting at right angles to it. SI unit: the pascal, \(1\u{Pa}=1\u{N/m^2}\).

\[p=\frac{F}{A}\]

In words: pressure = force ÷ area it acts on.

A pascal is tiny (a sheet of paper lying on a table presses with about 1 Pa), so engineering uses multiples. Common units:

Result · pressure units \[\begin{aligned}1\u{kPa}&=10^3\u{Pa}\\1\u{MPa}&=10^6\u{Pa}\\1\u{bar}&=100\u{kPa}=10^5\u{Pa}\\1\u{atm}&=101.325\u{kPa}\\1\u{psi}&=6894.76\u{Pa}\end{aligned}\]

In words: a bar is 100 kPa, roughly one atmosphere; a standard atmosphere is 101.325 kPa; a psi ("pound per square inch", lbf/in²) is about 6.9 kPa.

Steam tables (T2) list pressure in kPa, MPa or bar; a tyre gauge reads psi or bar. Also \[\begin{aligned}1\u{atm}&=1.01325\u{bar}=14.696\u{psi}\\&=760\u{mmHg}\end{aligned}\]

Absolute, gauge and vacuum pressure

What is this? (absolute, gauge, vacuum)

Think of heights in a building. Absolute pressure is like height measured from the ground: it starts from a perfect vacuum, and nothing can be below it. Gauge and vacuum pressure are like height measured from the floor you stand on, the local atmosphere: gauge is how far above that floor, vacuum how far below. Climb a mountain (a lower atmosphere, a lower floor) and readings from the floor change, but the height above the ground does not.

Definitions · three ways to quote a pressure
  • Absolute pressure: measured from absolute zero pressure (a perfect vacuum). It can never be negative. All thermodynamic calculations use absolute pressure.
  • Gauge pressure: how far the pressure is above the local atmospheric pressure. Most pressure gauges read this: they read zero when open to the air. \[p_\text{gauge}=p_\text{abs}-p_\text{atm}\]
  • Vacuum pressure: how far the pressure is below atmospheric, quoted as a positive number. A vacuum meter reads this. \[p_\text{vac}=p_\text{atm}-p_\text{abs}\]

In words: gauge = absolute − atmosphere (when above); vacuum = atmosphere − absolute (when below). Both are measured from the local atmosphere; absolute is measured from zero.

Reading the formula
\(p_\text{abs}\)
height above the ground: pressure measured from a perfect vacuum. The one the gas really has.
\(p_\text{atm}\)
the floor you stand on: the local air pressure, which changes with weather and altitude.
\(p_\text{gauge}\)
how far above the floor: what a tyre gauge shows.
\(p_\text{vac}\)
how far below the floor, written as a positive number.

A pressure gauge reads above atmospheric and a vacuum meter reads below. The bar chart shows the three on one scale; try it:

Try it · absolute, gauge and vacuum pressure

All bars stand on absolute zero pressure. Above the atmospheric line the difference is the gauge pressure; below it, the vacuum pressure. Lower \(p_\text{atm}\) (as on a mountain) and the same absolute pressure reads a higher gauge pressure.

Example · a tyre and a vacuum pump

Local atmospheric pressure is 101.3 kPa. (a) A tyre gauge reads 220 kPa. (b) A vacuum meter on a vacuum chamber reads 30 kPa. Find each absolute pressure, and give (a) in bar and psi.

  1. (a) \[\begin{aligned}p_\text{abs}&=p_\text{gauge}+p_\text{atm}\\&=220+101.3=\boxed{321.3\u{kPa}}\end{aligned}\] That is 3.213 bar, or \(321\,300/6894.76=\) 46.6 psi absolute. (The gauge itself reads \(220\,000/6894.76=31.9\) psi, the familiar "32 psi" tyre pressure.) A gauge measures from the atmosphere, so add the atmosphere back to measure from zero.
  2. (b) \[\begin{aligned}p_\text{abs}&=p_\text{atm}-p_\text{vac}\\&=101.3-30=\boxed{71.3\u{kPa}}\end{aligned}\] A vacuum reading says how far below the atmosphere, so subtract it.

Measuring pressure with a liquid column

What is this? (liquid column)

Picture a stack of books: the bottom book carries the weight of all the books above it. In a liquid at rest each layer carries the weight of the liquid above it, so the deeper you go, the harder the liquid presses. Every extra metre of depth adds the same amount, because it adds the same weight of liquid above each square metre.

A column of liquid can measure pressure. Take a mercury barometer: a glass tube, closed at the top, filled with mercury and turned upside down in a dish of mercury. The mercury falls until the column is a height \(L\) above the dish; the space above it holds only a little mercury vapour (pressure \(p_\text{vapor}\), almost zero). Point a on the open surface feels the atmosphere, \(p_\text{atm}\); point b inside the tube is at the same level in the same liquid, so it has the same pressure.

Result · liquid-column (manometer) equation \[p=p_\text{atm}+\rho gL\]

In words: pressure at the bottom of a liquid column = pressure on top + (density × \(g\) × height of the column). Going down a liquid by \(L\), pressure rises by \(\rho gL\).

Here \(p_\text{atm}\) is the local atmospheric pressure, \(\rho\) the density of the manometer liquid, \(g\) the acceleration of gravity and \(L\) the difference in liquid levels.

Reading the formula
\(p\)
pressure at the deeper point: the bottom of the stack.
\(p_\text{atm}\)
what presses on the top surface of the liquid.
\(\rho g\)
the weight of one cubic metre of the liquid: a heavier liquid (mercury) gains pressure faster with depth.
\(L\)
how far below the top surface: the difference in levels.
\(\rho gL\)
the weight of liquid sitting on each square metre: the books on top.
where \(\rho gL\) comes from (free-body diagram of a liquid column)

1. Take a vertical column of the liquid, cross-section area \(A\), height \(L\), as a free body. Any shape works; a straight column is simplest.

2. Its volume is \(AL\), so its mass is \(\rho AL\) and its weight is \(\rho gAL\), acting down. mass = density × volume.

3. The fluid above pushes down on its top face with force \(p_\text{top}A\); the fluid below pushes up on its bottom face with \(p_\text{bottom}A\). Side forces are horizontal and cancel. Pressure acts at right angles to each face.

4. The liquid is at rest, so the vertical forces balance: \(p_\text{bottom}A=p_\text{top}A+\rho gAL\). Newton's first law.

5. Divide by \(A\): \(p_\text{bottom}=p_\text{top}+\rho gL\). For a manometer open to the air at the top, \(p_\text{top}=p_\text{atm}\). Area cancels: the result is the same for a thin or thick tube.

In the barometer of the picture the roles are swapped: the top of the column is (almost) a vacuum and the bottom is at atmospheric pressure, so \(p_\text{atm}\approx\rho_\text{m}gL\). That is why atmospheric pressure is quoted as "760 mmHg". Chapter H2 (hydrostatics) develops this fully.

Example · barometer and water manometer

(a) Mercury (\(\rho=13\,595\u{kg/m^3}\)) stands 760 mm high in a barometer, \(g=9.80665\u{m/s^2}\). Find \(p_\text{atm}\). (b) A water manometer (\(\rho=1000\u{kg/m^3}\), \(g=9.81\)) connected to a gas tank shows a level difference \(L=25\u{cm}\), the gas side lower. With \(p_\text{atm}=101.325\u{kPa}\), find the gas pressure, absolute and gauge.

  1. (a) \[\begin{aligned}p_\text{atm}&=\rho gL=13\,595(9.80665)(0.760)\\&=\boxed{101.3\u{kPa}}\end{aligned}\] This is one standard atmosphere.
  2. (b) \(\rho gL=1000(9.81)(0.25)=2452.5\u{Pa}\), so the gauge pressure is \(\boxed{2.45\u{kPa}}\). The gas side is lower, so the gas pushes the water down harder than the air does: its pressure is above atmospheric by \(\rho gL\).
  3. Absolute: \(p=101.325+2.453=\boxed{103.78\u{kPa}}\).

Water is 13.6 times less dense than mercury, so a water column is 13.6 times taller for the same pressure: a water barometer would be about 10.3 m high.

Pressure in a piston–cylinder device

Example · gas under a loaded piston

A vertical piston–cylinder device (as in Section 8) holds gas under a frictionless piston. The piston and the weights on it have a total mass of 50 kg; the piston area is 0.01 m²; the atmosphere outside is at 100 kPa. Find the gas pressure, absolute and gauge (\(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\)).

Free-body diagram of the piston (with its weights): atmosphere pushes down, \(p_\text{atm}A\); weight acts down, \(mg\); the gas pushes up, \(pA\). The piston is at rest, so up = down.

\[pA=p_\text{atm}A+mg\] \[p=p_\text{atm}+\frac{mg}{A}\]
Reading the formula
\(pA\)

\(p_\text{atm}A\)
the air outside pushing down on the top face.
\(mg\)

  1. \[\begin{aligned}\frac{mg}{A}&=\frac{50(9.81)}{0.01}\\&=49\,050\u{Pa}=49.05\u{kPa}\end{aligned}\]

12Measuring temperature ~5 min

What is this?

Temperature measures how hard the molecules jiggle on average. Put two bodies in contact and heat flows from the hotter to the colder until they are equally hot, rather like two water tanks joined by a pipe that flow until their levels match. A thermometer joins the body, waits until the two match, and then reports its own temperature through something that changes with it: a length, a voltage, a resistance.

Definition · temperature and thermometers

Temperature says how hot or cold something is; at the molecular scale it measures how fast the molecules jiggle on average. Two bodies in contact exchange heat until their temperatures are equal (thermal equilibrium); a thermometer works by reaching equilibrium with the body and showing its own temperature.

Temperature can be measured with any physical property that changes with temperature in a reproducible way, called a thermometric property.

Five such properties, with the instruments that use them:

  1. Length: the length of a column of alcohol (or mercury) in a liquid-in-glass thermometer; the liquid expands as it warms. Many have °F and °C scales side by side.
  2. Voltage: a thermocouple, two different metal wires joined at a tip, makes a small voltage that depends on the tip temperature (often a probe on a braided cable).
  3. Electrical resistance: a platinum wire (resistance thermometer) or a semiconductor bead (thermistor, often a small black disc; an NTC thermistor's resistance falls as temperature rises).
  4. Pressure: a gas thermometer; a fixed mass of gas at constant volume has a pressure proportional to its absolute temperature (read on a dial gauge at the end of a long capillary tube).
  5. Thermal radiation: a pyrometer (such as a hand-held infrared thermometer gun) measures the infrared light a hot object gives off, without touching it.

13Temperature scales ~4 min

What is this?

A temperature scale is a ladder against one wall. Kelvin puts its zero at absolute zero, the true floor. Celsius uses rungs of the same size but starts its numbering higher up, at the freezing point of water. Fahrenheit uses smaller rungs (1.8 for each Celsius rung) and yet another starting number. Changing scale renumbers the rungs; the height on the wall does not change. A difference, how many rungs you climbed, depends only on rung size, not on where the numbering starts.

There are three common scales:

  • Kelvin (K): the absolute temperature scale. Its zero is absolute zero, the lowest possible temperature, where molecular motion is at its minimum. No negative kelvin temperatures exist.
  • Celsius (°C): part of the metric system; water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C (at 1 atm). One kelvin is the same size as one Celsius degree: the two scales have the same graduation.
  • Fahrenheit (°F): used in the English (US customary) system; water freezes at 32 °F and boils at 212 °F, so 180 Fahrenheit degrees span what 100 Celsius degrees span: 1 °C step = 1.8 °F steps.
Result · converting temperatures \[\begin{aligned}T(^\circ\mathrm C)&=T(\mathrm K)-273.15\\T(^\circ\mathrm F)&=1.8\,T(\mathrm K)-459.67\\T(^\circ\mathrm F)&=1.8\,T(^\circ\mathrm C)+32\end{aligned}\]

In words: kelvin = Celsius + 273.15; Fahrenheit = 1.8 × Celsius + 32. The third line follows from the first two.

Reading the formula
\(273.15\)
how far above the true floor Celsius starts its numbering: 0 °C is 273.15 K.
\(1.8\)
Fahrenheit rungs per Celsius (or kelvin) rung.
\(+32\)
the Fahrenheit number at the freezing point of water, where Celsius reads 0.
\(-459.67\)
the Fahrenheit number at absolute zero, where kelvin reads 0.
getting \(T(^\circ\mathrm F)=1.8\,T(^\circ\mathrm C)+32\) from the first two formulas

1. From the first line, \(T(\mathrm K)=T(^\circ\mathrm C)+273.15\). Rearrange.

2. Substitute into the second: \(T(^\circ\mathrm F)=1.8[T(^\circ\mathrm C)+273.15]-459.67\).

3. \(1.8\times273.15=491.67\), and \(491.67-459.67=32\). So \(T(^\circ\mathrm F)=1.8\,T(^\circ\mathrm C)+32\).

Check with the figure: water freezes at 273 K = 0 °C = 32 °F, boils at 373 K = 100 °C = 212 °F; absolute zero is 0 K = −273 °C = −460 °F (rounding −273.15 and −459.67).

Try it · one temperature on three scales

Three thermometers, with water's freezing and boiling points and absolute zero marked. Drag to −40: the Celsius and Fahrenheit readings are equal there (233.15 K).

Definition · Rankine scale

The Rankine scale (°R) is the absolute scale of the English system: it starts at absolute zero like kelvin but uses Fahrenheit-sized degrees. \(T(^\circ\mathrm R)=1.8\,T(\mathrm K)\) and \(T(^\circ\mathrm F)=T(^\circ\mathrm R)-459.67\).

Example · body temperature and a temperature rise

(a) Convert 37 °C to K, °F and °R. (b) Water warms from 20 °C to 80 °C. What is the temperature rise in K and in °F?

  1. (a) \(T=37+273.15=\boxed{310.15\u{K}}\); \(1.8(37)+32=\boxed{98.6\ ^\circ\mathrm F}\); \(1.8(310.15)=\boxed{558.27\ ^\circ\mathrm R}\).
  2. (b) The rise is \(60\) °C, which is \(\boxed{60\u{K}}\) (same size degrees; the 273.15 cancels in a difference) and \(1.8\times60=\boxed{108\ ^\circ\mathrm F}\) (no +32 in a difference).
Watch

A temperature converts with the offset (+273.15, +32); a temperature difference does not (\(\Delta T\) in K = \(\Delta T\) in °C; \(\Delta T\) in °F = 1.8 × \(\Delta T\) in °C). And any formula using \(T\) itself, not a difference (ideal-gas law, radiation \(T^4\)), must use kelvin.

You can now: convert a temperature between K, °C, °F and °R; convert a temperature difference without the offsets; and say when a formula needs kelvin.

14Mass, volume, density and specific volume ~3 min

What is this?

Density is how many kilograms are packed into each cubic metre, like how crowded an MTR carriage is at rush hour. Specific volume turns the question round: how much room each kilogram gets, like the space per passenger on a late-night train. They carry the same information, one is 1 divided by the other. Steam has a large specific volume: each kilogram spreads out over a lot of space.

Definitions · density and specific volume \[\rho=\frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}}=\frac{m}{V}\qquad \upsilon=\frac{1}{\rho}=\frac{V}{m}\]

In words: density = mass per unit volume (kilograms in each cubic metre); specific volume = volume per unit mass (cubic metres taken up by each kilogram), the reciprocal of density.

SI units: \(\rho\) in kg/m³ (or g/cm³), \(\upsilon\) in m³/kg (or cm³/g). \(1\u{g/cm^3}=1000\u{kg/m^3}\).

Why two quantities that carry the same information? Solids and liquids are usually described by density. Gases and steam, which spread out, are described by specific volume: the steam tables of T2 list \(\upsilon\) (written v) for water and steam at every state. The symbol \(\upsilon\) (Greek upsilon, a "curly v") keeps it apart from velocity \(V\) and volume \(V\).

Example · water, air and steam
  1. Liquid water: \(\rho\approx1000\u{kg/m^3}\), so \(\upsilon=1/1000=\boxed{0.001\u{m^3/kg}}\) (1 litre per kilogram).
  2. Air at room conditions: \(\rho\approx1.2\u{kg/m^3}\), so \(\upsilon\approx\boxed{0.83\u{m^3/kg}}\).
  3. A 0.5 m³ tank holds 2 kg of gas: \(\rho=2/0.5=\boxed{4\u{kg/m^3}}\), \(\upsilon=0.5/2=\boxed{0.25\u{m^3/kg}}\).
  4. Steam boiling off water at 100 °C and 1 atm has \(\upsilon\approx1.67\u{m^3/kg}\) (steam tables, T2): each kilogram of water takes up about 1600 times as much room as steam as it did as liquid (\(1.672/0.001043\); liquid water at 100 °C is slightly less dense than 1000 kg/m³).
Going further · intensive and extensive properties (used in T2)

Mass \(m\), volume \(V\) and energy \(E\) double if you double the amount of substance: they are extensive. Density, specific volume, pressure and temperature do not change if you take half of a uniform sample: they are intensive. Dividing an extensive property by mass gives a "specific" (per-kg) intensive one: \(\upsilon=V/m\), \(e=E/m\), \(u=U/m\).

You can now: calculate density and specific volume from mass and volume and convert one into the other; and say why steam tables list \(\upsilon\).

15Units and dimensions ~8 min

What is this?

A dimension is what kind of thing you measure: a length, a mass, a time. A unit is which ruler you use for it: metres or feet. Changing units is changing currency again: the amount stays the same and only the number changes, and you change it by multiplying by an exchange rate equal to 1, such as 1000 Pa per kPa.

Definitions · dimension and unit

A dimension is the kind of quantity: mass [M], length [L], time [T], temperature [Θ]. A unit is the agreed amount used to measure it: kilogram, metre, second, kelvin (SI); pound mass, foot, second, rankine (English). Every other quantity's dimension is built from these: force is [M L T⁻²], energy [M L² T⁻²], pressure [M L⁻¹ T⁻²].

Ready answer · converting units without mistakes
  1. Do: Write the quantity with its unit. The unit is part of the amount, like the currency sign on a price: without it the number means nothing.
  2. Do: Multiply by fractions equal to 1, with the unit you want to remove on the bottom, e.g. \(\dfrac{1000\u{Pa}}{1\u{kPa}}\) or \(\dfrac{3.6\u{MJ}}{1\u{kWh}}\). Each fraction is an exchange rate worth exactly 1, so the amount does not change; only its currency does.
  3. Do: Cancel the units like algebra; only the wanted unit should remain. A leftover unwanted unit means a fraction was upside down: this is your check.
  4. Do: Put every quantity in SI (kg, m, s, K, Pa, J) before substituting into a formula. A formula such as \(p=\rho gL\) gives pascals only when every input is in kg, m and s; mixed units give a wrong number without warning, as in the story below.
Example · units as a check

Show that \(\rho gL\) is a pressure and \(gz\) is an energy per kilogram.

\[\begin{aligned}[\rho gL]&=\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm m^3}\cdot\frac{\mathrm m}{\mathrm s^2}\cdot\mathrm m\\&=\frac{\mathrm{kg\,m/s^2}}{\mathrm m^2}=\frac{\mathrm N}{\mathrm m^2}=\mathrm{Pa}\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}[gz]&=\frac{\mathrm m}{\mathrm s^2}\cdot\mathrm m=\frac{\mathrm m^2}{\mathrm s^2}\\&=\frac{\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s^2}}{\mathrm{kg}}=\frac{\mathrm J}{\mathrm{kg}}\end{aligned}\]

If the units of an answer come out wrong, the formula or a substitution is wrong: a free check on every calculation.

Example · English-unit conversions

(a) A car engine is rated 150 hp. Power in kW? (b) A tank holds 50 gal. Volume in m³? (c) 1 lbf in newtons, from \(F=ma\)?

  1. (a) \(150\times0.7457=\boxed{111.9\u{kW}}\).
  2. (b) \(50\times3.7854\times10^{-3}=\boxed{0.189\u{m^3}}\).
  3. (c) \[\begin{aligned}1\u{lbf}&=1\u{lb}\times32.174\u{ft/s^2}\\&=0.4536\u{kg}\times9.8066\u{m/s^2}\\&=\boxed{4.448\u{N}}\end{aligned}\] (32.174 ft/s² × 0.3048 = 9.8066 m/s²).

How NASA lost a spacecraft because of units

In September 1999, after almost 10 months travelling to Mars, NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter (cost US$125 million) burned up and broke apart in the Martian atmosphere instead of going into orbit. One team's software reported the thrusters' impulse in English units (pound-force seconds); the navigation team's software expected SI units (newton seconds). Every small correction was therefore off by a factor of 4.45 (1 lbf = 4.448 N), and the orbiter flew far too low, all because the right units were not used. The lesson for exams: write the unit next to every number, and convert everything to one system before calculating.

You can now: tell a dimension from a unit; convert between SI and English units with factors equal to 1; check a formula with its units; and use the constants above.

18Find the mistake

1 · A student's answer to "which are open systems?"

"(a) Jet engine: open, air flows through. (b) Piston–cylinder: open, because work is done when the piston moves and heat crosses the walls. (c) Wind turbine: open. (d) Liquid-in-glass thermometer: open, because heat flows in from the surroundings."

Hint

What exactly has to cross the boundary for a system to be open?

Answer

(b) and (d) are wrong. Work and heat are energy; no mass crosses the boundary of a sealed piston–cylinder or of the sealed glass of a thermometer, so both are closed. Only (a) and (c) are open (air flows through them).

2 · A student's tank pressure

"A gauge on a gas tank reads 2.5 bar; the atmosphere is 1 atm. The absolute pressure is \(2.5-1.01325=1.49\u{bar}\)."

Hint

Is a gauge reading above or below atmospheric? Which way does that move the absolute value?

Answer

Gauge pressure is measured above the atmosphere, so add: \[\begin{aligned}p_\text{abs}&=2.5+1.01325\\&=3.51\u{bar}=351\u{kPa}\end{aligned}\] Subtracting is only right for a vacuum reading.

3 · A student's temperature conversion

"An oven is heated from 20 °C to 220 °C. In kelvin that is a rise of \((220+273.15)-(20)=473.15\u{K}\), and in Fahrenheit \(1.8(200+32)=417.6\) °F."

Hint

Two different mistakes. Does a difference need an offset at all?

Answer

The rise is \(200\) °C. In kelvin it is \(\boxed{200\u{K}}\): if you convert, convert both ends (493.15 − 293.15). In Fahrenheit a difference is \(1.8\times200=\boxed{360\ ^\circ\mathrm F}\), with no +32 (and +32 would go outside the bracket for a temperature anyway).

4 · A student's loaded piston

"A piston of mass 20 kg and area 0.005 m² floats on gas; atmosphere 100 kPa. Gas pressure \(=mg/A=20(9.81)/0.005=39.2\u{kPa}\), so the gas is below atmospheric pressure."

Hint

Draw every force on the piston, including the air above it.

Answer

The atmosphere also pushes down on the piston: \(pA=p_\text{atm}A+mg\), so \(p=100+39.24=\boxed{139.2\u{kPa}}\) absolute. The 39.2 kPa is the gauge pressure; the gas is above atmospheric, as it must be to hold the piston up against both the air and its weight.

19Group work

Two questions for class discussion. Try them before opening the answers.

1 · "What else?"

The chain fossil fuels → thermal → kinetic → electrical made possible bulbs, the telephone, TV and the refrigerator. What else? For each item you name, say which energy conversion it performs.

Hint

Walk through a day: what in your home, phone, transport and hospital runs on electricity? Is the electricity turned into light, motion, heat, sound or information?

Answer

Many good answers, for example: electric motors (fans, lifts, MTR trains, washing machines: electrical → kinetic); air-conditioners and heat pumps (electrical → work that moves heat from cold to hot); electric cookers, kettles, irons, hair dryers (electrical → thermal); computers and phones (electrical → information processing, ending as heat); radio, loudspeakers (electrical → sound); X-ray and MRI machines (electrical → electromagnetic waves and magnetic fields); battery charging, electrolysis (electrical → chemical); lasers, LED screens (electrical → light). In every case energy is conserved: whatever is not useful output ends up as heat.

2 · The missing box

In the energy-conversion chart, atomic energy, chemical energy and geothermal energy flow into a box marked "?" before becoming mechanical energy, while wind and hydraulic energy go straight to mechanical energy. What is the "?" and why do wind and water skip it?

Hint

Think of what happens inside a coal-fired or nuclear power station (Section 2) before the turbine spins.

Answer

The "?" is thermal energy (heat): fission or burning releases heat, which boils water into high-pressure steam (geothermal sources supply the heat directly), and a steam turbine converts the heat into rotation. Wind and moving water are already kinetic energy, so they can turn a turbine directly. Solar energy can go either way: via heat (solar-thermal plants) or straight to electricity (photovoltaic cells), as the chart's two lines from "Solar" show.

20Concept checks

1 · The kettle

An electric kettle is switched on with its lid closed and no steam escaping yet. Is the water (plus the kettle) an open, closed or isolated system? What about once it boils with steam pouring out of the spout?

Answer

Before boiling: closed (electrical energy enters, some heat leaks out, no mass crosses). Once steam leaves through the spout: open, because mass now crosses the boundary.

2 · Same reading, different place

A sealed tank's gauge reads 150 kPa in Hong Kong (\(p_\text{atm}=101\u{kPa}\)). The tank is taken unchanged up a mountain where \(p_\text{atm}=80\u{kPa}\). What does the gauge read now? Has the gas changed?

Hint

Which pressure belongs to the gas, and which depends on the air outside?

Answer

The gas's absolute pressure is unchanged, \(150+101=251\u{kPa}\). The gauge now reads \(251-80=\boxed{171\u{kPa}}\). Nothing happened to the gas; only the reference (the atmosphere) changed. That is why thermodynamics uses absolute pressure.

3 · Where is the energy?

A sealed bottle of water is carried up 10 floors (30 m) by a lift, then warmed by 2 °C in the sun. Per kilogram of water, which change of energy is larger, and by roughly how much?

Answer

\(\Delta pe=gz=9.81\times30\approx294\u{J/kg}\). \(\Delta u\approx4180\times2\approx8360\u{J/kg}\). The internal-energy change is about 28 times larger: small temperature changes involve a lot of energy.

4 · The thermometer that agrees

At what temperature do the Celsius and Fahrenheit scales read the same number? Is there a temperature where Kelvin and Fahrenheit agree?

Answer

Set \(x=1.8x+32\): \(x=\boxed{-40}\) (−40 °C = −40 °F). Kelvin and Fahrenheit: \(x=1.8x-459.67\), so \(x=\boxed{574.59}\) (574.59 K = 574.59 °F, about 301 °C).

5 · Power or energy?

Which uses more energy: a 1250 W hair dryer used for 6 minutes, or a 15 W LED left on for 8 hours?

Answer

Hair dryer: \(1.25\u{kW}\times0.1\u{h}=0.125\u{kWh}\). LED: \(0.015\times8=0.12\u{kWh}\). Almost the same: a high power for a short time can equal a low power for a long time.

6 · Choosing a thermometer

Which thermometer from the Section 12 comparison would you choose to measure (a) molten steel at about 1800 K, (b) the temperature inside a running engine's cylinder head, read on a car's computer, (c) the boiling point of liquid nitrogen (77 K) as an absolute reference?

Answer

(a) optical pyrometer (above 1250 K, no contact); (b) thermocouple (fast, wide range, read remotely; a thermistor would also feed a computer but is less suited to the high temperature); (c) constant-volume gas thermometer (3–500 K, gives the absolute scale) or a platinum resistance thermometer (15–900 K).
