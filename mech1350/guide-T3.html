Decision procedure · the composite-wall question (T3.2 + T3.3)
  1. Identify layers and films. List every solid layer (\(k\), \(L\)) and every fluid face (\(h\), \(T_\infty\)). Note faces with radiation (\(h_\text{rad}\)). Cue: "air temperature … kept at" means a convection film; "the wall surface is kept at" means no film.
  2. Series or parallel? Layers one after another along the heat path: series. A column split into top and bottom pieces (3a/3b): those pieces are parallel. Convection and radiation from the same face to the same temperature: parallel.
  3. Draw the circuit from the hot fluid to the cold fluid (or left to right), labelling every \(R\), the end temperatures and the asked interface nodes; add the arrow \(q\).
  4. Compute each \(R\) with that layer's own area: \(L/(kA)\) for layers (cm → m), \(1/(hA)\) for films, \(1/[(h_\text{conv}+h_\text{rad})A]\) for a face with both.
  5. Total: add series values; combine each parallel pair first, \(R_aR_b/(R_a+R_b)\).
  6. Heat rate \(q=(T_\text{hot}-T_\text{cold})/R_\text{total}\), in W, with its direction.
  7. Step temperatures: start at a known end and subtract \(qR\) for each resistance crossed with the arrow (add it walking against the arrow). Check that you land on the other end temperature.
Answer skeleton · "explain" parts (T3.1, e.g. May 2019 Q2, 8 marks)

Diagram to draw: the pot on the element, with one numbered arrow per step: radiation/contact into the base, conduction through the base, convection loops in the soup, bubbles at the base, losses from the sides and lid.

Marker points (about one mark each): (1) element heated electrically; (2) heat reaches the pot base by conduction at contact points and by radiation; (3) conduction through the metal base (\(q=kA\Delta T/L\), metal = high \(k\)); (4) conduction into the layer of soup touching the base; (5) warm soup expands, becomes less dense and rises, cool soup sinks: natural convection mixes the pot; (6) temperature rises to the boiling point; vapour bubbles form at the hot base, rise and burst; (7) while boiling, the heat supplied becomes latent heat and the temperature stays constant; (8) losses: sides and lid lose heat to the room by convection and radiation, steam carries energy away. Name the mode at every step.

Model answer (exam length) is in the May 2019 Q2 card.

2T3.1 · Conduction & heat-transfer-mode concepts

Quote \(q=kA\Delta T/L\); same \(q\) through layers in series; essays: mode by mode with a sketch. Learn it: note-T3 §1 three modes · §4 conductivity · §6 composite slab

May 2023 Q12 marksMC

Which of the following is NOT necessary for determining the heat transfer due to conduction?

  • (a) Temperature gradient
  • (b) Thermal resistance or thermal conductivity
  • (c) Cross section area of the conduction material in the direction of heat transfer
  • (d) Mass of the conduction material
Hint

Type T3.1. Write the conduction law and tick off each quantity that appears in it.

Solution
  1. Conduction law: \(q=kA\,\dfrac{\Delta T}{L}\) (or \(q=\Delta T/R\) with \(R=L/(kA)\)).
  2. It contains the temperature gradient \(\Delta T/L\) (a), the conductivity or resistance (b) and the area (c). Mass appears nowhere: a heavier slab of the same \(k\), \(A\), \(L\) and \(\Delta T\) conducts at the same steady rate.

\(\boxed{\text{(d) mass}}\)

May 2019 Q28 marksexplainolder format

Explain the process of heating soup on the stove. Start with the heat given off by the stove's element and proceed from there, step-by-step, until the soup is boiling. Use a precise written description and include diagrams to illustrate your explanation.

Hint

Type T3.1 (written). Follow the energy from the element to the bubbles; name the mode (conduction, convection, radiation) at each step and draw one labelled sketch. Skeleton in At a glance.

Solution

Draw the sketch of the answer skeleton (pot on element, numbered arrows), then write:

  1. Element. Electric current through the element's resistance wire heats it (it may glow red).
  2. Element → pot. Where the pot base touches the element, heat passes by conduction; across the small air gaps the hot element also radiates infrared energy, absorbed by the base (on a gas stove, hot combustion gases also heat the base by convection).
  3. Through the base. Heat conducts through the metal base from its hot outer face to its inner face, \(q=kA\Delta T/L\); metals have high \(k\), so the base passes heat quickly with a small temperature drop.
  4. Base → soup. The thin layer of soup touching the base is heated by conduction.
  5. Through the soup. Warmed soup expands, becomes less dense and rises (buoyancy); cooler, denser soup sinks to take its place. These natural convection currents carry heat through the whole pot (stirring adds forced convection).
  6. Boiling. The soup temperature rises until it reaches its boiling point (about 100 °C at 1 atm, slightly higher with dissolved salt). Vapour bubbles form at the hottest spot, the base, rise and burst at the surface. While it boils, the heat supplied is used as latent heat of vaporisation, so the temperature stays constant.
  7. Losses. Throughout, the pot's sides and the soup surface lose heat to the room by convection and radiation, and evaporation and steam carry energy away; a lid reduces these losses and the soup boils sooner.

Marker note: marks go for naming the correct mode at each step in the right order and for a labelled diagram; a list of the three modes without the soup story earns little.

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{radiation + contact}\\&\to\text{conduction (base)}\\&\to\text{convection (soup)}\\&\to\text{boiling}\end{aligned}}\]
Dec 2018 Q1(a)(b)2 + 10 marksexplainolder format

The wall of a container is made of two layers of thickness 3 mm and 10 mm, aluminum alloy on the inside and wood on the outside as shown in Figure A1 respectively. The thermal conductivity of the aluminum alloy and wood are given by 54 W m⁻¹K⁻¹ and 0.147 W m⁻¹K⁻¹ respectively. In the steady state, with energy flowing steadily from inside to outside, the inside surface temperature of the aluminum alloy is 5 °C and the outside surface temperature of the wood is 24 °C.

(a) Will the rate of energy transfer be greatest through the wood layer, the aluminum alloy layer, or the same for both? Explain your reasoning. (2 marks)

Hint

Type T3.1. Steady state: can energy pile up at the interface?

Solution
  1. The layers are in series: all the energy that crosses one layer must cross the other.
  2. In steady state nothing changes with time, so no energy can accumulate at the interface: rate in = rate out.

\(\boxed{\text{the same for both layers}}\)

What differs is the temperature drop: almost all of it is across the wood (its \(L/k\) is about 1200 times the aluminium's), see part (c) in T3.3.

(b) Explain why the process of thermal conduction in metals is faster than that in wood. (10 marks) Beyond the handouts

Hint

Type T3.1 (written). Two carriers of heat in solids: lattice vibrations and free electrons. Which solid has both? Then use the numbers in the stem.

Solution
  1. Conduction is energy passed from faster-vibrating particles to slower neighbours, without bulk motion of the material.
  2. In every solid, atoms are bonded to neighbours, so vibrations travel through as lattice vibrations.
  3. Metals also contain free (delocalised) electrons. In the hot region they gain kinetic energy, move fast through the whole lattice and deliver energy to distant atoms in collisions: a second, much faster carrier. (The same free electrons make metals good electrical conductors.)
  4. Metal atoms sit in a regular, closely packed crystal lattice, so vibrations pass on efficiently.
  5. Wood has no free electrons: only vibrations, passed along long, loosely bonded organic fibres in a disordered structure.
  6. Wood is also porous: it contains many small pockets of air, and still air is a very poor conductor (about 0.026 W/(m·K)).
  7. Result: \(k\) of the aluminium alloy is 54 W/(m·K), of wood 0.147 W/(m·K), about 370 times smaller; for the same \(A\), \(\Delta T\) and \(L\), the conduction rate \(q=kA\Delta T/L\) through aluminium is about 370 times larger.

Marker note: the key marks are "free electrons carry energy in metals" and "wood: only lattice vibrations, porous with trapped air"; finish with the \(k\) comparison and the law.

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{metal: free electrons}\\&\quad\text{+ ordered lattice}\\&\text{wood: vibrations only,}\\&\quad\text{porous}\end{aligned}}\]

3T3.2 · Drawing the thermal resistance circuit

Hot fluid → film → layers (split column = parallel branches) → film → cold fluid; convection ∥ radiation on one face. Learn it: note-T3 §7 series & parallel · §10 drawing circuits · §16 convection + radiation

Ready answer · what a full-marks circuit shows
  • Both end temperatures written at the ends (\(T_{\infty,i}\), \(T_{\infty,o}\), or the given surface temperatures).
  • One labelled resistor per layer, \(R_n=L_n/(k_nA_n)\), in the order of the wall; one per film, \(R=1/(hA)\).
  • Split columns as two branches between the same two nodes: \(R_{3a}\parallel R_{3b}\).
  • Convection and radiation on the same face: \(R_\text{conv}\parallel R_\text{rad}\).
  • The heat-flow arrow \(q\), and any interface temperatures the question asks for, as labelled nodes.

Consider the composite wall consists of multiple layers given in the diagram below. The wall has an area of 12 m². The layer 3a occupies 8 m² and the layer 3b occupies 4 m². The heat flows from right to left (from layer 5 to layer 1). The air temperature on the left and right of the composite wall are kept constant at −10 °C and 20 °C. Heat conduction occurs inside the composite wall. Heat convection occurs between the outer air and the wall surface. The heat convection coefficients between the air and the wall on the left and the right are given by 70 W/m²·°C and 40 W/m²·°C, respectively. Moreover, the right side of the composite wall is subject to thermal radiation with a radiant heat transfer coefficient of 10 W/m²·°C. The thermal conductivity and the length of the different layers of the composite wall are given by the table below.

Layer123a3b45
Thermal conductivity (W/m·°C)10.4310.51
Length (cm)523321

(a) Draw the thermal resistance circuit for the problem.

Hint

Type T3.2. Start at the hot air (right, 20 °C). Two things act on the right face at once; one column is split.

Solution
  1. Right face: convection \(R_\text{conv,i}=1/(h_\text{conv,i}A)\) and radiation \(R_\text{rad,i}=1/(h_\text{rad,i}A)\) both connect the surface node \(T_{s,i}\) to 20 °C: parallel.
  2. Layers 5, 4 in series; 3a and 3b side by side (areas 8 and 4 m²): parallel; then 2, 1 in series.
  3. Left film \(R_\text{conv,o}=1/(h_\text{conv,o}A)\) to −10 °C; arrow \(q\) right to left.
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}R_\text{tot}&=R_\text{conv,o}+R_1+R_2\\&\quad+(R_{3a}\parallel R_{3b})+R_4+R_5\\&\quad+(R_\text{conv,i}\parallel R_\text{rad,i})\end{aligned}}\]

no surroundings temperature is given for the radiation, so it is taken to exchange with surroundings at the right-hand air temperature (20 °C): both right-face paths then join the same two nodes and are in parallel (as in handout slide 48).

4T3.3 · Solving the circuit: heat rate & temperatures

Each \(R\) with its own area → series add, parallel by reciprocals → \(q=\Delta T/R_\text{total}\) → \(T_\text{next}=T-qR\). Learn it: note-T3 §7 · §12 convection + conduction · §17 full combined wall

Ready answer · composite wall with films \[\begin{aligned}R_\text{tot}&=\frac{1}{h_iA}+\sum\frac{L_n}{k_nA_n}+\frac{1}{h_oA}\\q&=\frac{T_{\infty,i}-T_{\infty,o}}{R_\text{tot}}\end{aligned}\]

Split column: \(R_{ab}=\dfrac{R_aR_b}{R_a+R_b}\). Face with convection and radiation: \(\dfrac{1}{(h_\text{conv}+h_\text{rad})A}\). Interface temperature: \(T_\text{next}=T-qR\), walking with the heat.

In words: heat rate = overall temperature difference ÷ total resistance; each resistance then "uses up" \(q\times R\) degrees.

5T3.4 · Inverse design: insulation thickness or conductivity

Same \(\Delta T\): \(R_\text{new}=R_\text{old}/(1-f)\); the extra \(R\) is the new layer's \(L/(kA)\); thicken the largest \(R\). Learn it: note-T3 §9 insulation design

Ready answer · insulation for an f % cut \[\begin{aligned}q_\text{new}&=(1-f)\,q_\text{old}\\R_\text{new}&=\frac{R_\text{old}}{1-f}\\R_\text{add}&=R_\text{old}\,\frac{f}{1-f}=\frac{L}{kA}\end{aligned}\]

40% cut: \(R_\text{add}=\tfrac23R_\text{old}\). 50% cut: \(R_\text{add}=R_\text{old}\).

In words: with the same temperature difference, the heat rate is inversely proportional to the total resistance, so cutting heat by a fraction \(f\) needs the total resistance divided by \(1-f\).

6T3.5 · Convection & radiation concepts

\(h\): free gas < forced gas < liquids < boiling/condensation; \(\varepsilon\): shiny metal low, non-metals high, water highest. Learn it: note-T3 §13 typical h · §15 emissivity

Ready answer · the two lists

\(h\) (smallest → largest): free convection in gas (5–37) < forced convection in gas (10–200; faster air → larger \(h\)) < liquids (50–10 000) < boiling and condensation (3000–100 000) W/(m²·K).

\(\varepsilon\) (smallest → largest): polished/unoxidised aluminium 0.02 < oxidised aluminium 0.11–0.31 < cast iron 0.6–0.7 < cotton 0.77 < brick, asphalt 0.93 < water 0.95–0.963.

Convection depends on: fluid speed, fluid type and temperature, surface area and shape. Not on emissivity or fan efficiency.

A column of 10 m² surface area with a surface temperature of 550 K is kept within a large vessel whose walls are at 300 K. Assume the column surface to be black body. The sum of heat transfer coefficient including the radiation and convection is 40 W/m²·°C. The Stefan-Boltzmann constant σ is given as \(5.6703\times10^{-8}\u{W/(m^2\cdot K^4)}\). The radiation energy per unit time is given by the formula \(q=\sigma(T_1^4-T_2^4)A\), where \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) are the temperature in Kelvin scale for body 1 and body 2.

(a) Calculate the coefficient of radiant heat transfer. (2 marks)

Hint

Type T3.6. Temperatures are already in kelvin; find \(q\), then divide by \(A\,\Delta T\).

Solution
  1. \(550^4=9.151\times10^{10}\), \(300^4=8.1\times10^{9}\).
  2. Net radiation:
\[\begin{aligned}q&=5.6703\times10^{-8}(550^4-300^4)(10)\\&=4.729\times10^{4}\u{W}\end{aligned}\] \[h_\text{rad}=\frac{47\,294}{10(550-300)}\] \[\boxed{h_\text{rad}\approx18.9\u{W/(m^2\,{}^\circ C)}}\]

(b) Determine the convective heat transfer coefficient. (2 marks)

Hint

Type T3.6. Convection and radiation from one surface are in parallel: their \(h\) values add.

Solution
  1. \(h_\text{total}=h_\text{rad}+h_\text{conv}\), so \(h_\text{conv}=40-18.92\).
\[\boxed{h_\text{conv}\approx21.1\u{W/(m^2\,{}^\circ C)}}\]

Hint

Type T3.6. Subtract from the total.

Solution
  1. \(h_\text{conv}=60-22.96\).
\[\boxed{h_\text{conv}\approx37.0\u{W/(m^2\,{}^\circ C)}}\]

8Traps across T3

Traps
  • °C inside \(T^4\). Radiation needs kelvin: with °C, Dec 2024 Q5 would give \(h_\text{rad}\approx1.3\) instead of 18.9.
  • Wrong area. A split layer uses its own area (8 m², not 12 m²); films and full layers use the whole wall.
  • Adding parallel resistances. 3a and 3b, or convection and radiation on one face, combine by reciprocals (or add the \(h\) values), never \(R_a+R_b\).
  • Forgetting a film. "Air at 25 °C with \(h\)" adds \(1/(hA)\); "surface kept at 400 °C" adds nothing.
  • cm left in cm. \(L=3\) cm is 0.03 m in \(L/(kA)\).
  • "Reduced by 40%". \(q_\text{new}=0.6\,q_\text{old}\), so \(R_\text{new}=R_\text{old}/0.6\), not \(/0.4\).
  • Walking the wrong way. Temperatures fall in the direction of \(q\); a node outside the two end temperatures means a sign slip.
  • Rounding too early. Resistances such as \(1.071\times10^{-3}\) need 4 significant figures until \(q\).
  • Mass in conduction / emissivity in convection. Neither appears in the law (May 2023 Q1, May 2024 Q2).
