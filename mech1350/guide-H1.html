Decision procedure · the oil-film question (H1.5)
  1. Draw the free-body diagram of the moving body: the driving force (applied \(F\), or \(W\sin\alpha\) on a slope), the viscous force opposite the motion, and (on a slope) \(W\cos\alpha\) and the normal reaction, which cancel.
  2. Gap and speed. \(h\) = oil thickness (the radial clearance for a shaft), in metres; \(U\) = speed of the body relative to the fixed wall.
  3. Shear stress \(\tau=\mu U/h\) (linear velocity profile across a thin film).
  4. Wetted area \(A\): bottom face of a block (\(a\times b\)); side of a shaft inside the sleeve, \(\pi DL\) with \(L\) the sleeve length.
  5. Balance. Cue "constant speed", "terminal speed", "steady": the acceleration is zero, so driving force \(=\tau A=\mu UA/h\). Solve for the unknown (\(F\) or \(U\)).
  6. Sense check. Terminal speeds of oiled blocks come out around 0.1–10 m/s; a force in kN for a small shaft means a mm left in mm.
Answer skeleton · "distinguish Newtonian and non-Newtonian fluids" (H1.1, 2–4 marks)

Diagram to draw: shear stress \(\tau\) against shear rate \(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\) with four labelled curves.

Marker points: (1) Newtonian: \(\tau=\mu\,\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\), straight line through the origin, constant viscosity (water, air, oil); (2) non-Newtonian: non-linear, the effective viscosity changes with the stress; (3) shear thinning: viscosity falls as stress rises (curve bends down; ketchup, paint); (4) shear thickening: viscosity rises (bends up; corn starch in water); (5) Bingham plastic: no flow below a yield stress (toothpaste).

Model answer in the May 2019 Q1(b) card.

Answer skeleton · contact angle and capillarity sketches (H1.2, 2–4 marks)

Diagram to draw: a drop on a solid with the tangent at the contact line and \(\theta\) marked through the liquid, once wetting and once non-wetting; for capillarity, a glass tube in water (rise, concave meniscus) and in mercury (depression, convex meniscus).

Marker points: (1) \(\theta\) measured from the solid surface to the tangent of the liquid surface, through the liquid; (2) wetting if \(\theta\lt90^\circ\), non-wetting if \(\theta\gt90^\circ\); (3) examples: water on glass, mercury on glass; (4) for tubes: water rises (\(h\gt0\)), mercury is depressed (\(h\lt0\)).

Model answers in the Dec 2023 Q1(d) and ENGG1205 2012–13 Q1(d) cards.

Answer skeleton · "cause and effect of cavitation" (H1.2, 3–6 marks)

Marker points (about one mark each): (1) where the liquid moves fast, its pressure falls (Bernoulli); (2) when it falls to the vapour pressure at that temperature the liquid boils locally; (3) a large number of tiny vapour bubbles form; (4) carried to a higher-pressure region they collapse (implode); (5) the collapse sends out intense shock waves; (6) effects: noise, vibration, lower efficiency, pitting/erosion of pump and propeller surfaces.

Model answer in the ENGG1205 2016–17 Sem 1 Q1(b) card.

2H1.1 · Fluid behaviour & viscosity concepts

Fluid: no shear strength, deforms continuously; viscosity = stress ÷ shear rate; heated liquid thins, gas thickens; Newtonian vs thinning / thickening / yield stress. Learn it: note-H1 §1 fluid vs solid · §5 viscosity · §7 temperature · §8 non-Newtonian · §9 falling sphere

Ready answer · the lecturer's one-liners (from the ENGG1205 printed solutions)
  • Fluid vs solid: under a constant shear stress a fluid deforms continuously, a solid only to a finite extent; a fluid has no shear strength, and a fluid at rest carries no shear stress.
  • Viscosity: the coefficient relating shear stress and shear rate, \(\tau=\mu\,\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\).
  • Newtonian: \(\tau\) linear in shear rate, viscosity independent of stress. Shear thinning / thickening: effective viscosity decreases / increases with stress.
  • Temperature: as temperature rises, viscosity decreases for a liquid but increases for a gas.

In words: viscosity is a fluid's resistance to being sheared quickly; a solid resists being sheared at all.

Dec 2024 Q1(a)–(f)6 × 1 mark(g)–(l): H1.2; (m)–(p): guides H2–H4

Data line: density of water = 1,000 kg/m³; acceleration due to gravity = 9.81 m/s²; pressure means gauge pressure.

State true (T) or false (F) for each of the following statements. (16 marks for (a)–(p); 1 mark each assumed)

(a) Pure fluid does not have the strength to resist shear.

Hint

Type H1.1. What happens to a fluid under a small steady shear?

Solution
  1. A fluid deforms continuously under any shear stress, however small: it has no shear strength.

\(\boxed{\text{True}}\)

(b) Viscosity is a coefficient relating stress and strain.

Hint

Type H1.1. Write Newton's law of viscosity and read off what multiplies \(\mu\).

Solution
  1. \(\tau=\mu\,\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\): viscosity relates stress to the shear rate (rate of strain), not to strain. Stress ∝ strain describes an elastic solid (shear modulus).

\(\boxed{\text{False}}\)

(c) Oil flows more readily when it is heated up.

Hint

Type H1.1. Oil is a liquid: which way does a liquid's viscosity go with temperature?

Solution
  1. A liquid's viscosity falls as its temperature rises (molecules break free of their neighbours more easily), so hot oil flows more readily.

\(\boxed{\text{True}}\)

(d) A bubble in water rises up more quickly than a bubble of the same size in oil does.

Hint

Type H1.1. Terminal speed of a sphere: what resists the motion?

Solution
  1. The bubble rises at the terminal speed where upthrust − weight = viscous drag (\(3\pi\mu DV\) for a small sphere), so the speed is roughly inversely proportional to \(\mu\).
  2. Oil is far more viscous than water, so the bubble rises more slowly in oil.

\(\boxed{\text{True}}\)

(e) An ideal fluid has zero viscosity.

Hint

Type H1.1. Definition.

Solution
  1. An ideal (inviscid) fluid is defined as one with no viscosity, so no shear stress and no energy dissipation.

\(\boxed{\text{True}}\)

(f) Ketchup may remain static even when it is sheared.

Hint

Type H1.1. Which non-Newtonian behaviour has a threshold stress?

Solution
  1. Ketchup has a yield stress (Bingham-plastic behaviour): below it, it holds its shape like a solid; only above it does it flow.
  2. That is why it sits in an upturned bottle until the bottle is shaken.

\(\boxed{\text{True}}\)

Marker note: one mark each with no working asked, so decide fast; a wrong guess costs only that mark (no negative marking is stated).

Data line: take density of water = 1,000 kg/m³, acceleration due to gravity = 9.81 m/s².

(a) Briefly describe how fluid is distinguished from solid in terms of their behaviours in response to shear action. (2 marks)

Hint

Type H1.1. Apply a constant shear stress to each: what does the deformation do over time?

Solution
  1. Solid: under a constant shear stress it deforms only to a finite extent and then stops (it has shear strength; it springs back if the stress is small).
  2. Fluid: under any shear stress, however small, it deforms continuously for as long as the stress acts; it has no strength to resist shear, so a fluid at rest carries no shear stress.
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{fluid: deforms continuously}\\&\text{solid: finite deformation}\end{aligned}}\]

Marker note: the printed ENGG1205 answers (2012–13, 2016–17 Sem 2) give exactly these two points; the 4-mark version adds "a solid has shear strength, a fluid does not".

(b) How does the viscosity or effective viscosity of a fluid depend on the stress when the fluid is Newtonian, shear thinning, or shear thickening? (2 marks)

Hint

Type H1.1. Three short statements, one per fluid; a sketch helps.

Solution
  1. Newtonian: viscosity is constant, independent of the stress.
  2. Shear thinning: effective viscosity decreases as the stress increases.
  3. Shear thickening: effective viscosity increases as the stress increases.

\(\boxed{\text{constant; decreases; increases}}\)

"effective viscosity" read as \(\tau\) divided by the shear rate at that point (as the note's interactive figure uses); the handout's local slope gives the same trends.

(c) State the effect of temperature on the viscosity of a liquid, and on that of a gas. (2 marks)

Hint

Type H1.1. Opposite trends: think hot syrup and hot air.

Solution
  1. Liquid: viscosity decreases as temperature increases (cohesive forces between molecules weaken).
  2. Gas: viscosity increases as temperature increases (faster molecules carry more momentum between layers).

\(\boxed{\text{liquid}\downarrow,\ \text{gas}\uparrow\ \text{as }T\uparrow}\)

May 2019 Q1(b)4 markspart (a): H1.3older format

What is the difference between a Newtonian fluid and a non-Newtonian fluid?

Hint

Type H1.1 (written). 4 marks: the definition, the \(\tau\)–\(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\) sketch, the kinds of non-Newtonian fluid with examples. Skeleton in At a glance.

Solution
  1. Newtonian fluid: shear stress is directly proportional to shear rate, \(\tau=\mu\,\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\), with a constant viscosity \(\mu\) (at a given temperature): a straight line through the origin. Examples: water, air, most oils.
  2. Non-Newtonian fluid: the relation between \(\tau\) and \(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\) is non-linear (or has a yield stress), so the effective viscosity changes with the stress or shear rate.
  3. Kinds: shear thinning (effective viscosity falls with stress; ketchup, paint, blood); shear thickening (rises with stress; corn starch in water); Bingham plastic (no flow until a yield stress is exceeded; toothpaste).
  4. Draw the sketch:
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{Newtonian: }\tau\propto\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\\&\text{non-Newtonian: non-linear}\end{aligned}}\]

3H1.2 · Surface tension, cavitation & compressibility concepts

σ = pull per unit length of a cut line; droplet pressure higher inside; θ < 90° wetting, θ > 90° non-wetting; cavitation: \(p\to p_v\) in fast flow; compressible only for Ma > 0.3. Learn it: note-H1 §10 surface tension · §11 contact angle · §12 cavitation · §13 compressibility

Ready answer · the lecturer's one-liners (from the ENGG1205 printed solutions)
  • Contact angle \(\theta\): measured from the solid surface to the tangent of the liquid surface, through the liquid; wetting if \(\theta\lt90^\circ\), non-wetting if \(\theta\gt90^\circ\).
  • Surface-tension phenomena: larger pressure inside a droplet than outside; capillary rise of water in a glass tube.
  • Cavitation: velocity so high that the pressure falls to the vapour pressure at that temperature; bubbles form, then collapse in higher pressure, with shock waves and erosion of the surface.
  • Compressibility: the Mach number, flow velocity ÷ speed of sound, decides it; significant only above about 0.3.

In words: a liquid surface acts like a stretched membrane; a liquid boils when its pressure drops to the vapour pressure, whether by heating or by speeding up.

Dec 2024 Q1(g)–(l)6 × 1 mark(a)–(f): H1.1

Data line: density of water = 1,000 kg/m³; acceleration due to gravity = 9.81 m/s²; pressure means gauge pressure.

State true (T) or false (F) for each of the following statements. (1 mark each assumed)

(g) Surface tension is force per length.

Hint

Type H1.2. Unit of \(\sigma\)?

Solution
  1. Surface tension is the pulling force per unit length of a line cut in the liquid surface, unit N/m.

\(\boxed{\text{True}}\)

(h) Pressure inside a droplet is greater than pressure outside the droplet.

Hint

Type H1.2. Force balance on half a droplet.

Solution
  1. Half a droplet: \(\pi R^2\Delta p=2\pi R\sigma\), so \(\Delta p=p_\text{in}-p_\text{out}=2\sigma/R\gt0\).

\(\boxed{\text{True}}\)

(i) A liquid wets a solid if the contact angle is greater than 90 degrees.

Hint

Type H1.2. Water on glass has \(\theta\approx0^\circ\).

Solution
  1. Wetting means \(\theta\lt90^\circ\) (the liquid spreads); \(\theta\gt90^\circ\) is non-wetting (it beads up, like mercury).

\(\boxed{\text{False}}\)

(j) Mercury is drawn by surface tension to rise up in a capillary tube.

Hint

Type H1.2. Is mercury wetting or non-wetting on glass?

Solution
  1. Mercury is non-wetting on glass (\(\theta\approx135^\circ\gt90^\circ\)): its level in the tube is pushed down (capillary depression), with a convex meniscus.

\(\boxed{\text{False}}\)

(k) Cavitation is the formation of bubbles in a liquid when the liquid moves at a high speed.

Hint

Type H1.2. Compare with the review sheet's wording of item (v).

Solution
  1. In fast flow the pressure drops; where it reaches the vapour pressure, vapour bubbles form: cavitation. The Dec 2026 review sheet words it as "formation of bubbles in a fast moving liquid".

\(\boxed{\text{True}}\)

answered True, following the review sheet's own wording; strictly the cause is the low pressure (down to the vapour pressure) that the high speed produces.

(l) The effect of compressibility is always important in air flow.

Hint

Type H1.2. Which number, and what threshold?

Solution
  1. Compressibility matters only when the Mach number exceeds about 0.3 (about 100 m/s in air); slower air flows (fans, ventilation, most wind) are treated as incompressible.

\(\boxed{\text{False}}\)

(d) Draw a sketch to show the contact angle at a liquid-gas-solid interface. At what contact angles is a liquid called wetting or non-wetting? (2 marks)

Hint

Type H1.2. Mark the tangent to the liquid surface at the contact line; the angle sits inside the liquid.

Solution
  1. The contact angle \(\theta\) is the angle between the solid surface and the tangent to the liquid–gas interface at the contact line, measured through the liquid.
  2. Wetting if \(\theta\lt90^\circ\) (water on glass); non-wetting if \(\theta\gt90^\circ\) (mercury on glass).
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{wetting: }\theta\lt90^\circ\\&\text{non-wetting: }\theta\gt90^\circ\end{aligned}}\]

Marker note: the sketch must show where \(\theta\) is measured (through the liquid); a drop with an unlabelled angle loses the sketch mark.

(e) Name and define the number that determines the significance of compressibility in fluid flow. (2 marks)

Hint

Type H1.2. A ratio of two speeds.

Solution
  1. The Mach number: \(\text{Ma}=V/c\), the flow velocity divided by the speed of sound in the fluid, \(c=\sqrt{E/\rho}\).
  2. Compressibility is significant only when Ma is larger than about 0.3.

\(\boxed{\text{Ma}=V/c}\)

ENGG1205 2016–17 Sem 1 Q1(b)6 marksAlso: ENGG1205 2013–14 Sem 1 Q1(d) (4 marks)older course

Data line: take density of water = 1,000 kg/m³, acceleration due to gravity = 9.81 m/s².

Briefly describe the cause and effect of cavitation.

Hint

Type H1.2 (written). Cause: speed → pressure → vapour pressure. Effect: what the bubbles do when they reach higher pressure. Skeleton in At a glance.

Solution
  1. Cause. Where a liquid moves very fast (pump impeller, propeller tip, valve throat), its pressure falls. If it falls to the vapour pressure of the liquid at that temperature, the liquid boils locally.
  2. A very large number of very tiny vapour bubbles form in the flow.
  3. Effect. Carried into a region of higher pressure, the bubbles implode (collapse), which can generate intense shock waves.
  4. Repeated collapses next to a solid surface badly erode and pit (corrode) the material in contact with the high-speed flow; they also cause noise, vibration and loss of efficiency.
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&p\to p_v\Rightarrow\text{bubbles}\\&\Rightarrow\text{collapse, erosion}\end{aligned}}\]

Printed solutions (both sittings): the same points; the 4-mark version stops at "bursting of these bubbles can badly corrode the surface" — agrees.

ENGG1205 2015–16 Sem 1 Q1(b)(c)2 + 3 marksolder course

(b) Name two phenomena that are due to effect of surface tension. (2 marks)

Hint

Type H1.2. One about pressure, one about a thin tube.

Solution
  1. The pressure inside a droplet (or bubble) is larger than outside, \(\Delta p=2\sigma/R\).
  2. Capillary rise of water in a thin glass tube (and depression of mercury).

(c) Briefly explain the cause of cavitation. (3 marks)

Hint

Type H1.2. Only the cause: the first half of the 2016–17 answer.

Solution
  1. The velocity is so high that the fluid pressure becomes very low.
  2. When the pressure equals the vapour pressure of the liquid at that temperature, the liquid vaporises: bubbles form in the fluid.

\(\boxed{p\text{ falls to }p_v\Rightarrow\text{bubbles}}\)

ENGG1205 2012–13 Q1(d)(e)3 + 1 marks(a)–(c): H1.1older course

Data line: take density of water = 1,000 kg/m³, acceleration due to gravity = 9.81 m/s².

(d) Draw diagrams to show the effect of surface tension when a glass capillary tube is placed through the free surface in a pool of water, and of mercury. (3 marks)

Hint

Type H1.2. Wetting liquid rises, non-wetting liquid is pushed down; draw the meniscus shape.

Solution
  1. Water wets glass (\(\theta\approx0^\circ\)): it climbs the tube above the pool level (\(h\gt0\)) with a concave meniscus (dipping in the middle).
  2. Mercury does not wet glass (\(\theta\approx135^\circ\)): its level in the tube stays below the pool level (\(h\lt0\)) with a convex meniscus (domed).

(e) What happens to the boiling point of water when water is under a pressure higher than the standard atmospheric pressure? (1 mark)

Hint

Type H1.2. Pressure cooker.

Solution
  1. Water boils when its vapour pressure equals the surrounding pressure; a higher surrounding pressure needs a higher vapour pressure, reached only at a higher temperature.

\(\boxed{\text{boiling point rises above }100^\circ\text{C}}\)

4H1.3 · Density, specific weight & relative density

\(\rho=\text{SG}\,\rho_w\), \(\gamma=\rho g\), \(W=\gamma V\); backwards \(\rho=\gamma/g\), SG \(=\gamma/\gamma_w\). Learn it: note-H1 §3 density & specific weight · §4 water at 4 °C

Ready answer · the conversion chain \[\text{SG}\ \xrightarrow{\times\rho_w}\ \rho\ \xrightarrow{\times g}\ \gamma\ \xrightarrow{\times V}\ W\]

In words: relative density times water's density gives the density; times \(g\) gives the weight per cubic metre; times the volume gives the weight in newtons.

Dec 2021 Q14 marksAlso: May 2019 Q1(a) (4 marks, same numbers)

Data line: acceleration due to gravity = 9.81 m/s², density of water = 1000 kg/m³. (May 2019: "Assume the density of water at 4 °C is 1,000 kg/m³ and gravitational acceleration to be 9.81 m/s²".)

A tank of 0.9 m³ is full of gasoline with a specific gravity of 0.73. Determine the weight of the gasoline.

Hint

Type H1.3. SG → density → specific weight → weight; the answer is a force.

Solution
  1. Density: \(\rho=0.73\times1000=730\u{kg/m^3}\).
  2. Specific weight: \(\gamma=\rho g=730\times9.81\) \(=7161.3\u{N/m^3}\).
  3. Weight: \(W=\gamma V=7161.3\times0.9\) \(=6445\u{N}\).
\[\boxed{W\approx6.45\u{kN}}\]

Marker note: giving the mass (657 kg) instead of the weight loses the final mark; state the unit.

May 2021 Q1(a)4 markspart (b): H1.4

Section B gives specific weights in each question (\(\gamma_w=9.81\u{kN/m^3}\)); \(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\).

Assume the specific weight of seawater at the ocean surface is 10.0 kN/m³, and the bulk modulus of elasticity is \(2.3\times10^9\u{N/m^2}\).

(a) What is the density of seawater at the ocean surface? (4 marks)

Hint

Type H1.3. \(\rho=\gamma/g\), with \(\gamma\) in N/m³. The bulk modulus is for part (b).

Solution
  1. \(\gamma=10.0\u{kN/m^3}=10\,000\u{N/m^3}\).
  2. \(\rho=\gamma/g=10\,000/9.81\).
\[\boxed{\rho\approx1019\u{kg/m^3}}\]

Sense check: sea water is about 2% denser than fresh water (SG 1.02).

Dec 2018 Q1(a)4 markspart (b): H1.4older format

The specific weights of water and mercury are 9.81 kN/m³ and 133.0 kN/m³, respectively. Determine the density of water and the specific gravity of mercury. Assume gravitational acceleration to be 9.81 m/s².

Hint

Type H1.3. Divide by \(g\) for density; specific gravity is a ratio of specific weights (no \(g\) needed).

Solution
  1. \(\rho_w=\gamma_w/g=9810/9.81\) \(=1000\u{kg/m^3}\).
  2. \(\text{SG}_\text{Hg}=\gamma_\text{Hg}/\gamma_w=133.0/9.81\) \(=13.56\).
\[\boxed{\rho_w=1000\u{kg/m^3},\ \text{SG}_\text{Hg}\approx13.6}\]

SG has no unit; the usual table value for mercury is 13.6.

5H1.4 · Bulk modulus: volume or density change under pressure

\(E=\Delta p/(-\Delta V/V)\): volume falls by \(V\Delta p/E\); mass is conserved, so density rises by the same fraction. Learn it: note-H1 §13 compressibility & bulk modulus

Ready answer · small pressure change \[\begin{aligned}\frac{\Delta V}{V}&=-\frac{\Delta p}{E}\\V_2&=V\Big(1-\frac{\Delta p}{E}\Big)\\\rho_2&=\rho\Big(1+\frac{\Delta p}{E}\Big)\end{aligned}\]

In words: bulk modulus = pressure rise ÷ fractional volume decrease; a stiff liquid (large \(E\)) shrinks very little.

May 2021 Q1(b)6 markspart (a): H1.3

Assume the specific weight of seawater at the ocean surface is 10.0 kN/m³, and the bulk modulus of elasticity is \(2.3\times10^9\u{N/m^2}\).

(b) The pressure has increased by 82 MPa at a depth of 8 km in the ocean. What is the density of seawater at such depth? (6 marks)

Hint

Type H1.4. Start from the surface density of part (a), 1019 kg/m³; find the fractional change \(\Delta p/E\).

Solution
  1. Surface density (part (a)): \(\rho_1=10\,000/9.81=1019.4\u{kg/m^3}\).
  2. Fractional change: \(\dfrac{\Delta p}{E}=\dfrac{82\times10^6}{2.3\times10^9}\) \(=0.03565\).
  3. Mass is conserved, so the density rises by that fraction (\(\Delta\rho/\rho=\Delta p/E\)):
\[\begin{aligned}\rho_2&=\rho_1\Big(1+\frac{\Delta p}{E}\Big)\\&=1019.4(1.03565)\\&=1055.7\u{kg/m^3}\end{aligned}\] \[\boxed{\rho_2\approx1056\u{kg/m^3}}\]

Marker note: MPa is \(10^6\) and the modulus \(10^9\): a slip here gives a density 1000 times off.

density route used. The volume route, \(V_2=V_1(1-\Delta p/E)\) then \(\rho_2=\rho_1V_1/V_2\), gives 1057 kg/m³; the 0.1% gap is within the small-change model, and either working should earn the marks.

Dec 2018 Q1(b)4 markspart (a): H1.3older format

Water has an original volume of 3,000 cm³. Estimate the new volume of water after a pressure of \(2\times10^6\u{N/m^2}\) is applied. The bulk modulus of water is \(2.15\times10^9\u{Pa}\).

Hint

Type H1.4. \(\Delta V=-V\Delta p/E\); cm³ can stay in cm³ because only a ratio is used.

Solution
  1. \(\dfrac{\Delta p}{E}=\dfrac{2\times10^6}{2.15\times10^9}\) \(=9.302\times10^{-4}\).
  2. \(\Delta V=-3000\times9.302\times10^{-4}\) \(=-2.79\u{cm^3}\).
  3. \(V_2=3000-2.79\).
\[\boxed{V_2\approx2997.2\u{cm^3}}\]

A 20-atmosphere squeeze shrinks water by under 0.1%: why liquids are treated as incompressible.

6H1.5 · Viscous shear force in a thin film

Thin film, linear profile: \(\tau=\mu U/h\), \(F=\tau A\); constant speed: driving force = viscous force. Learn it: note-H1 §5 Newton's law of viscosity · §6 simple shear flow & oil films

Ready answer · body sliding on an oil film at constant speed \[\begin{aligned}F_\text{drive}&=F_\text{visc}=\mu\,\frac{U}{h}\,A\\U&=\frac{F_\text{drive}\,h}{\mu A}\end{aligned}\]

Shaft in a sleeve: \(A=\pi DL\), \(h\) = radial clearance. Block on a slope at angle \(\alpha\): \(F_\text{drive}=W\sin\alpha\), \(A\) = bottom face.

In words: viscous force = viscosity × (speed ÷ film thickness) × wetted area; at constant speed it exactly balances whatever drives the body.

May 2025 Q26 markssame template: Dec 2022 Q1

Data line: acceleration due to gravity = 9.81 m/s², density of water = 1000 kg/m³.

A shaft of diameter 100 mm is made to move through a sleeve at a speed of 0.08 m/s under the action of an applied force \(F\). The sleeve is 500 mm long and there is a clearance of 0.5 mm (assume uniform all around) between the shaft and the sleeve. The clearance is filled with oil of viscosity of 0.35 Ns/m². Determine the value of the applied force \(F\)? (There is no axial load on the shaft).

Hint

Type H1.5. Constant speed: \(F\) equals the viscous force on the part of the shaft inside the sleeve.

Solution
  1. FBD of the shaft: \(F\) forward, viscous drag backward (no axial load); constant speed, so \(F=F_\text{visc}\).
  2. Shear stress: \(\tau=\mu U/h=0.35\times0.08/0.0005\) \(=56\u{Pa}\).
  3. Wetted area: \(A=\pi DL=\pi(0.1)(0.5)\) \(=0.1571\u{m^2}\).
  4. \(F=\tau A=56\times0.1571\).
\[\boxed{F\approx8.80\u{N}}\]

Marker note: the area is the curved side \(\pi DL\) over the sleeve length only, not the shaft's end face.

shaft diameter used for the area (the surface being dragged). The sleeve bore (101 mm) or the mean diameter (100.5 mm) give 8.88 N or 8.84 N.

Dec 2022 Q110 markssame template: May 2025 Q2

Data line: acceleration due to gravity = 9.81 m/s², density of water = 1000 kg/m³.

A rectangular block slides down a 30° inclining plane on a film of oil of thickness 0.5 mm. The block weighs 200 N and has a bottom face of size 0.2 m by 0.3 m. The oil has a viscosity of 0.5 Ns/m². Calculate the terminal speed of the block.

Hint

Type H1.5. Resolve the weight along the slope; at terminal speed that component equals \(\mu UA/h\).

Solution
  1. FBD: weight \(W=200\u{N}\) down; normal reaction \(N\) perpendicular to the slope; viscous force \(F_\text{visc}\) up the slope.
  2. Perpendicular to the slope: \(N=W\cos30^\circ\) \(=173.2\u{N}\) (it plays no part in the oil force).
  3. Along the slope, driving component: \(W\sin30^\circ=200\times0.5\) \(=100\u{N}\).
  4. Terminal speed: acceleration zero, so \(W\sin30^\circ=\mu UA/h\), with \(A=0.2\times0.3=0.06\u{m^2}\) and \(h=0.0005\u{m}\).
\[\begin{aligned}U&=\frac{W\sin30^\circ\,h}{\mu A}\\&=\frac{100(0.0005)}{0.5(0.06)}\end{aligned}\] \[\boxed{U\approx1.67\u{m/s}}\]

Marker note: most marks go to the FBD and the force balance along the slope; using the full 200 N instead of \(W\sin30^\circ\) doubles the answer.

ENGG1205 2014–15 Sem 2 Q1(a)6 markspart (b): timber, guide H4older course

Data line: take density of water = 1,000 kg/m³, acceleration due to gravity g = 9.81 m/s².

Briefly describe how the viscosity of a Newtonian fluid is measured by a simple shear experiment.

Hint

Type H1.5 (written). Two parallel plates, one fixed, one pulled: what do you measure, and how do you turn it into \(\mu\)?

Solution
  1. Set-up. Place the fluid between two parallel plates a small distance \(b\) apart. The lower plate is fixed; the upper plate, of wetted area \(A\), is pulled at a constant speed \(U\) by a constant force \(F\).
  2. Flow. The fluid sticks to both plates (no slip), so its velocity varies linearly from 0 to \(U\) across the gap: the velocity gradient is \(U/b\).
  3. Measure \(F\) for several speeds \(U\). The fluid is Newtonian if \(F\) is directly proportional to \(U\) (a straight line through the origin).
  4. Compute the viscosity as stress ÷ velocity gradient:
\[\boxed{\mu=\frac{F/A}{U/b}}\]

Printed solution: the same set-up and \(\mu=(F/A)/(U/b)\) — agrees.

7H1.6 · Surface-tension force along a contact line

\(F=\sigma L_\text{contact}\cos\theta\); a thin-walled tube touches the liquid inside and outside, \(L=2\pi D\). Learn it: note-H1 §10 surface tension (contact-line box) · §11 contact angle

May 2021 Q210 marksBeyond the handouts

Section B gives specific weights in each question (\(\gamma_w=9.81\u{kN/m^3}\)).

A hollow cylinder with a diameter of 130 mm is kept floating vertically in a liquid with a surface tension of 0.50 N/m². The contact angle is 60°. Its weight is equal to the buoyant force. What is the additional force required due to surface tension?

Hint

Type H1.6. Weight and buoyancy cancel, so only surface tension is left. List every line where the liquid surface touches the cylinder.

Solution
  1. Weight = buoyancy, so the only unbalanced force is the surface-tension pull. A wetting liquid (\(\theta=60^\circ\lt90^\circ\)) clings to the wall and pulls it down along every contact line.
  2. A thin-walled hollow cylinder meets the surface on its outside and its inside: \(L=2\pi D=2\pi(0.13)\) \(=0.8168\u{m}\).
  3. Vertical component of the pull: \(F=\sigma L\cos\theta\).
\[\begin{aligned}F&=0.50(0.8168)\cos60^\circ\\&=0.204\u{N}\end{aligned}\] \[\boxed{F\approx0.204\u{N}\text{ (upward, to hold it)}}\]

Marker note: the ten marks reward the FBD and the two contact lines; one perimeter only gives half the answer.

surface tension printed "0.50 N/m²", read as 0.50 N/m. The wall is taken as thin, so the inside and outside contact lines both have diameter 130 mm. If the cylinder were read as closed at the bottom (liquid only outside), \(F=\sigma\pi D\cos\theta\approx0.102\) N.

8Traps across H1

Traps
  • Strain vs strain rate. Viscosity relates stress to shear rate; "stress and strain" is false (Dec 2024 Q1(b)).
  • Heating a gas. Liquids thin when heated, gases thicken: one word each, both needed for 2 marks.
  • Mercury climbing. Mercury is non-wetting (\(\theta\gt90^\circ\)): depressed in a glass tube, convex meniscus.
  • Wetting backwards. Wetting is \(\theta\lt90^\circ\), measured through the liquid.
  • "Always" compressible. Air is compressible in practice only above Ma ≈ 0.3.
  • Weight vs mass. \(W=\rho gV\) in N; 657 kg is not "the weight" of the gasoline.
  • kN and N. \(\gamma=10.0\u{kN/m^3}\) is 10 000 N/m³ before dividing by \(g\).
  • Film in mm. \(h=0.5\) mm is 0.0005 m in \(\mu U/h\); leaving mm makes forces 1000 times too small.
  • Whole weight down the slope. Only \(W\sin\alpha\) drives a block; \(W\cos\alpha\) is carried by the film's pressure.
  • One perimeter. A thin-walled tube has an inside and an outside contact line: \(2\pi D\), times \(\cos\theta\).
