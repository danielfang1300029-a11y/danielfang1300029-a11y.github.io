Pressure and manometry

0Where this chapter sits

Sign convention for this chapter
  • "Pressure" means gauge pressure (measured above the local atmospheric pressure) unless the question says "absolute". This holds on most papers. Open surfaces in contact with the air are at \(p=0\) gauge.
  • \(z\) is the elevation, measured upwards; \(h\) is the depth below a free surface, measured downwards (\(h=-z\) when \(z=0\) at the surface).
  • Pressure is positive when it compresses (pushes on a surface). A negative gauge pressure means "below atmospheric" (a partial vacuum, or suction).
  • Walking through a still fluid: going down by \(\Delta z\) adds \(\rho g\,\Delta z\); going up subtracts it. Moving sideways within the same continuous fluid changes nothing.
  • Unless a question gives other values: \(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\), \(\rho_\text{water}=1000\u{kg/m^3}\). Mercury: relative density 13.6, so \(\rho_\text{Hg}=13\,600\u{kg/m^3}\).

1A fluid at rest ~3 min

What is this?

Picture a swimming pool on a calm day. No layer of water slides past another, so the water cannot rub along your skin; it can only press on it. Dive down and the only thing that changes is how much water is above you. That is why a still fluid needs only its density, the depth and gravity.

Definition · fluid statics

Fluid statics (or hydrostatics for water) is the study of fluids that are at rest: no part of the fluid moves relative to any other part. A lake on a calm day, the water in a closed tank and the air in a sealed room are all static.

What makes a fluid at rest so much simpler than a moving fluid:

  • Velocity is zero. Nothing moves.
  • Shear stress is zero (for a Newtonian fluid). H1 showed that a fluid deforms continuously under any shear stress (a sideways, "sliding" force per area). A fluid that is not deforming must therefore carry no shear stress at all. The only stress left is the push straight onto surfaces: pressure.
  • Viscosity has no effect. Viscosity relates shear stress to the rate of deformation; with no deformation it plays no part.
  • Only one important property: density \(\rho\). (Surface tension matters only in bubbles, droplets and thin capillary tubes, as in H1.)
  • Only one "dynamics" variable: pressure \(p\), the quantity we solve for.
  • Only one independent variable: the vertical coordinate (elevation \(z\), or depth \(h\) below the free surface). Pressure changes only as you go up or down, never sideways.
  • Only one forcing: gravity (the body force, i.e. the weight of the fluid).

In short: in a still fluid, pressure depends only on depth and density, and it is produced only by the weight of fluid above. The rest of this chapter turns that sentence into formulas.

You can now: list what makes a fluid at rest simple (no velocity, no shear, viscosity irrelevant); and say which single property, variable and force control the pressure in it.

2Pressure is a normal compressive stress ~5 min

What is this?

Underwater, the water presses on your skin everywhere, and each push goes straight into the skin, never along it. Pressure measures how hard that push is on each square metre. The same push on a small patch is a high pressure; spread over a big patch it is a low one.

Definition · pressure

Pressure is the normal compressive force per unit area: the force with which a fluid pushes straight onto (perpendicular to) a surface, divided by the area it pushes on. Formally,

\[p=\lim_{\Delta A\to0}\frac{\Delta F_n}{\Delta A}\]

where \(\Delta F_n\) is the normal compressive force on a tiny area \(\Delta A\). Taking the limit \(\Delta A\to0\) makes \(p\) the pressure at a point.

In words: pressure = push (perpendicular to the surface) ÷ area, measured on a very small patch.

Reading the formula
\(\Delta F_n\)
the push of the water straight into a small patch of skin.
\(\Delta A\)
the area of that patch.
\(\dfrac{\Delta F_n}{\Delta A}\)
push per square metre on the patch.
\(\Delta A\to0\)
shrink the patch to a point, so the answer belongs to one spot at one depth.

Each word in "normal compressive stress" means something:

  • Stress: a force per area (like the stress in a stretched rod in mechanics).
  • Normal: perpendicular to the surface. The other kind of stress, shear, acts along the surface; a fluid at rest has none (Section 1).
  • Compressive: it pushes, it never pulls. Pressure always acts inward on any surface, whatever its orientation: down on the top of a submerged box, up on its bottom, sideways on its walls.

A block held in still water. The pressure forces on every face point into the face and are perpendicular to it. They grow with depth (Section 5), so the push up on the bottom is larger than the push down on the top.

Units and dimensions

Pressure has the dimension of force per area, \([\mathrm{ML^{-1}T^{-2}}]\) (mass × length⁻¹ × time⁻²), since a newton is \(\mathrm{kg\,m/s^2}\) and dividing by \(\mathrm{m^2}\) gives \(\mathrm{kg/(m\,s^2)}\). The SI unit is the pascal: \(1\u{Pa}=1\u{N/m^2}\). A pascal is tiny (a sheet of paper lying flat presses on a table with about 1 Pa), so engineers use kPa and MPa.

Example · force from pressure

(a) A 600 N person balances on the heels of one shoe, contact area \(0.002\u{m^2}\) (20 cm²). Average pressure on the floor: \(p=F/A=600/0.002=300\u{kPa}\). (b) Standing on two flat shoes, total area \(0.04\u{m^2}\): \(p=600/0.04=15\u{kPa}\). Same force, 20 times the area, 1/20 of the pressure. (c) Atmospheric pressure (101.3 kPa) on 1 cm² (\(10^{-4}\u{m^2}\)) gives a force of \(101.3\times10^3\times10^{-4}=10.13\u{N}\): the weight of about 1 kg on every square centimetre of your body. You do not feel it because the fluids inside your body push back with the same pressure.

One more property, that pressure at a point has the same value in all directions, is the subject of the next section.

You can now: define pressure as normal compressive force per area; convert between Pa, kPa, bar, hPa and mmHg; and find a force from a pressure and an area.

3Pascal's law: pressure is a scalar ~4 min

What is this?

Stay at one depth in the pool and turn your head: your ear feels the same squeeze whether it faces up, down or sideways. The water at a point does not prefer a direction. So pressure is one number at each point, not one number per direction.

Pressure is defined as a force divided by an area. Force has a direction, so you might expect pressure to have one too: perhaps water pushes harder downward than sideways? It does not. A small piece of fluid is cut out as a wedge (a triangular prism); because it is at rest, the forces on it must balance; letting the wedge shrink to a point shows the pressures on all its faces are equal, whatever the angle of the slanted face.

Free-body diagram of the wedge. It has sides \(\delta y\) (horizontal), \(\delta z\) (vertical) and slanted face \(\delta s\) at angle \(\theta\), and length \(\delta x\) into the page. Forces: \(p_y\) on the vertical face, \(p_z\) on the bottom face, \(p_s\) on the slanted face (each = pressure × area, inward and perpendicular), and the weight \(\gamma\,\delta x\,\delta y\,\delta z/2\). There are no shear forces because the fluid is still.

Law · Pascal's law (pressure at a point) \[p_y=p_z=p_s=p\quad\text{for any angle }\theta\]

In words: the pressure at a point in a fluid at rest is the same in every direction. Pressure is a point property (it has one value at each point), it is independent of orientation, and so it is a scalar (a number with no direction), also called an isotropic stress ("isotropic" = the same in all directions).

Reading the formula
\(p_y\)
the push on the face that looks sideways.
\(p_z\)
the push on the face that looks down.
\(p_s\)
the push on the slanted face, which can look any way you like.
any angle \(\theta\)
however you turn the face (your ear), the push per square metre is the same.
the wedge proof, step by step

1. Forces on each face = pressure × area of that face. Vertical face: area \(\delta x\,\delta z\). Bottom face: \(\delta x\,\delta y\). Slanted face: \(\delta x\,\delta s\). The element is so small that the pressure on each face can be taken as constant over it.

2. Weight of the wedge = specific weight × volume. The volume is the triangle area \(\tfrac12\delta y\,\delta z\) times the length \(\delta x\): \(W=\gamma\,\delta x\,\delta y\,\delta z/2\), acting down.

3. The slanted face pushes inward, perpendicular to itself. Its horizontal part is \(p_s\,\delta x\,\delta s\,\sin\theta\) (pointing towards \(-y\)) and its vertical part \(p_s\,\delta x\,\delta s\,\cos\theta\) (pointing down). \(\theta\) is the angle of the slanted face's normal from the vertical, as in the figure.

4. Equilibrium in \(y\) (horizontal):

\[\sum F_y=p_y\,\delta x\,\delta z-p_s\,\delta x\,\delta s\sin\theta=0\]

5. Equilibrium in \(z\) (vertical):

\[\begin{aligned}\sum F_z&=p_z\,\delta x\,\delta y-p_s\,\delta x\,\delta s\cos\theta\\&\quad-\gamma\,\frac{\delta x\,\delta y\,\delta z}{2}=0\end{aligned}\]

6. Geometry of the triangle: \(\delta s\sin\theta=\delta z\) and \(\delta s\cos\theta=\delta y\). Substitute and divide step 4 by \(\delta x\,\delta z\), step 5 by \(\delta x\,\delta y\):

\[p_y-p_s=0,\qquad p_z-p_s-\gamma\,\frac{\delta z}{2}=0\]

7. Shrink the wedge to a point: \(\delta x,\delta y,\delta z\to0\). The weight term \(\gamma\,\delta z/2\to0\) (weight scales with volume, size³; surface forces with area, size², so weight becomes negligible for a tiny element), leaving \(p_y=p_s=p_z\).

8. \(\theta\) was arbitrary, so the pressure is the same on a surface facing any direction. ∎

The result holds as long as there are no shearing forces, which is true in any fluid at rest. In fact pressure is a scalar, isotropic, normal and compressive stress in general, whether the fluid is at rest or in motion (in a moving fluid, the "pressure" is defined as the isotropic part of the normal stress).

At depth \(h\) below a free surface (\(p=0\) gauge at the surface, \(z=-h\) at the point), the pressure \(p\) pushes equally in all directions, like the arrows of a star.

Watch · pressure vs pressure force

Pressure is a scalar: no direction. The pressure force on a surface, \(p\,\mathrm dA\), is a vector: its direction is set by the surface (perpendicular to it, pointing in). "Pressure acts downward" is wrong; "the pressure force on the top face acts downward" is right. Dec 2024 Q1(m) asked "Pressure is an isotropic quantity" (true).

You can now: state Pascal's law and outline the wedge proof; and tell pressure (a scalar) apart from the pressure force (a vector).

4Transmission of pressure: the hydraulic lift ~4 min

What is this?

Fill a sealed bag with water and press one spot: the whole bag goes firm, not just the spot. The extra pressure you add reaches every point of the liquid in full. A hydraulic lift uses this: the same extra pressure acting on a bigger piston gives a bigger force.

There is a second statement, also called Pascal's law (or Pascal's principle, or the principle of transmission of fluid pressure). It follows from the first.

Law · Pascal's principle (transmission of pressure)

Any change in pressure at any point in a confined incompressible fluid is transmitted throughout the fluid equally and undiminished to all points, in all directions.

In words: squeeze a sealed liquid somewhere, and every point in it feels the same extra pressure.

Why it is true. Section 5 shows the pressure difference between two points in a still liquid is fixed by their height difference alone: \(p_\text{below}-p_\text{above}=\rho g\,\Delta z\). If the pressure at one point rises by \(\Delta p\), the differences cannot change (the heights and density have not changed: the liquid is incompressible), so every pressure must rise by the same \(\Delta p\).

This is how a hydraulic jack or hydraulic lift works. Two cylinders are joined by a pipe, all filled with oil. A small piston (area \(A_1\)) is pushed down with force \(F_1\); a large piston (area \(A_2\)) carries the load \(F_2\), e.g. a car.

Result · hydraulic lift (pistons at the same level) \[p_1=p_2\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{F_1}{A_1}=\frac{F_2}{A_2}\]\[\frac{F_1}{F_2}=\frac{A_1}{A_2}\ll1\]

In words: the same pressure under both pistons, so the force on each is in proportion to its area. A small force on a small piston holds up a large force on a large piston.

Reading the formula
\(F_1/A_1\)
the pressure you create by pushing on the small piston.
\(F_2/A_2\)
the pressure the oil puts on the large piston.
\(=\)
same oil, same level, so the same pressure everywhere under both pistons.
\(A_1/A_2\ll1\)
the large piston has many times the area, so it gets many times the force.
Example

A garage lift has a small piston of diameter 2 cm and a large piston of diameter 20 cm, at the same level. What force on the small piston holds a 1500 kg car? (Ignore the piston weights.)

Load: \(F_2=mg=1500(9.81)=14\,715\u{N}\). Area ratio \(A_1/A_2=(d_1/d_2)^2=(2/20)^2=0.01\). So

\[F_1=F_2\,\frac{A_1}{A_2}=14\,715(0.01)=\boxed{147\u{N}}\]

The oil pressure under both pistons is

\[\frac{F_2}{A_2}=\frac{14\,715}{\pi(0.2)^2/4}\approx468\u{kPa}\]
Going further · no free lunch

The lift multiplies force, not energy. The oil is incompressible, so the volume pushed out of the small cylinder, \(A_1d_1\), equals the volume entering the large one, \(A_2d_2\). To raise the car \(d_2=5\u{cm}\), the small piston must travel \(d_1=d_2A_2/A_1=5\u{m}\) (in many short pumping strokes). Work in \(=F_1d_1=147\times5\) = work out \(=F_2d_2=14\,715\times0.05\), both about 736 J. If the pistons are at different levels, add \(\rho g\,\Delta z\) between them (Section 5).

You can now: state Pascal's principle; and use \(F_1/A_1=F_2/A_2\) to find the force or piston size of a hydraulic lift.

5The hydrostatic law: pressure variation with depth ~3 min

What is this?

Dive one metre deeper and one more metre of water now sits on top of you. Each square metre above you carries the weight of that extra cubic metre, about 9.81 kN, so the pressure grows by about 9.81 kPa. Swim back up and you shed that weight: the pressure falls.

Why is the pressure higher at the bottom of a swimming pool? Because the water there carries the weight of all the water above it. A free-body diagram of a small vertical cylinder of fluid makes this precise.

Set-up. Take \(z\) pointing vertically upward, with \(z=0\) at the free surface. Cut out a small vertical cylinder of fluid of cross-section area \(A\) and height \(\Delta z\), at depth \(h\). The pressure on its bottom face is \(p\); on its top face (higher up by \(\Delta z\)) it is \(p+\Delta p\). Its weight is \(W=\rho g A\,\Delta z\).

Free-body diagram of the fluid cylinder. The pressure forces on the curved side are horizontal and cancel in pairs, so only the vertical forces matter.

Law · hydrostatic equation \[\frac{\mathrm dp}{\mathrm dz}=-\rho g=-\gamma\]

In words: going up, pressure falls at a rate equal to the specific weight of the fluid; going down, it rises at that rate. Every metre deeper in water adds \(9.81\u{kPa}\).

Reading the formula
\(\mathrm dz\)
a small step upward (\(z\) points up).
\(\mathrm dp\)
the change in pressure you feel over that step.
\(\rho g=\gamma\)
the weight of one cubic metre of the fluid: what each metre of depth adds per square metre.
minus sign
stepping up leaves fluid below you, so less is on top and the pressure falls.
the force balance on the cylinder

1. Forces up: pressure on the bottom face pushes up, \(pA\). Pressure pushes into the face; the bottom face is pushed upward.

2. Forces down: pressure on the top face, \((p+\Delta p)A\), and the weight \(W=\rho gA\,\Delta z\).

3. At rest, up = down:

\[(p+\Delta p)A+W=pA\]

4. Cancel \(pA\) and put in \(W\): \(\Delta p\,A+\rho gA\,\Delta z=0\), so \(\Delta p=-\rho g\,\Delta z\).

5. Divide by \(\Delta z\) and let \(\Delta z\to0\): \(\mathrm dp/\mathrm dz=-\rho g\). The minus sign: higher up (\(\Delta z\gt0\)) means lower pressure (\(\Delta p\lt0\)).

Conclusion: the hydrostatic pressure increases with depth, at a rate equal to the specific weight \(\gamma\equiv\rho g\) of the fluid. \(\gamma\) and \(\rho g\) are used interchangeably; several exam papers give \(\gamma\) directly (e.g. "specific weight of mercury 133.7 kN/m³").

Definition · specific weight (from H1)

The specific weight \(\gamma=\rho g\) is the weight of one cubic metre of the fluid, in N/m³. Water: \(1000\times9.81=9810\u{N/m^3}=9.81\u{kN/m^3}\). Mercury: \(13.6\times9.81=133.4\u{kN/m^3}\).

You can now: derive \(\mathrm dp/\mathrm dz=-\rho g\) from a force balance on a fluid cylinder; explain the minus sign; and work out a specific weight from a density or SG.

6Constant specific weight: absolute and gauge pressure ~4 min

What is this?

At the pool surface the air already presses on the water. Dive down and the weight of the water above you is added to that. Absolute pressure counts both, starting from empty space. Gauge pressure counts only the part above the atmosphere, because the air presses on you, and on most gauges, all the time anyway.

For a liquid, \(\rho\) (and so \(\gamma\)) hardly changes with depth: liquids are nearly incompressible. Then the hydrostatic equation can be integrated in one line.

integrating \(\mathrm dp=-\rho g\,\mathrm dz\)

1. Separate: \(\int\mathrm dp=-\int\rho g\,\mathrm dz\). \(\rho g\) is constant, so it comes out of the integral.

2. Integrate: \(p=-\rho gz+C\), where \(C\) is an integration constant.

3. Fix \(C\) at the free surface: there \(z=0\) and the pressure is the atmospheric pressure \(p_0\). So \(p_0=C\).

4. Result: \(p=-\rho gz+p_0\). Below the surface \(z\) is negative; writing the depth \(h=-z\) gives \(p=\rho gh+p_0\).

Result · pressure in a liquid of constant density \[p=-\rho gz+p_0=\rho gh+p_0\quad\text{(absolute)}\] \[p=-\rho gz=\rho gh\quad\text{(gauge, }p_0=0\text{)}\]

In words: pressure = pressure at the surface + (density × g × depth). Pressure grows linearly with depth.

Reading the formula
\(p_0\)
the air pressing on the pool surface.
\(h\)
how deep you are below that surface.
\(\rho gh\)
the weight of the water column above one square metre of you.
\(p_0=0\)
gauge pressure: count only the water, not the air.
Definitions · absolute and gauge pressure
  • Absolute pressure: measured above a perfect vacuum (zero absolute pressure, no molecules at all). It can never be negative.
  • Gauge pressure (also spelled "gage"): measured above the local atmospheric pressure. Setting \(p_0=0\) gives gauge pressure. Most pressure gauges (tyre gauges, the dial gauges on tanks) read gauge pressure, because the atmosphere pushes on their outside too.
\[p_\text{abs}=p_\text{gauge}+p_\text{atm}\]

In words: absolute = gauge + atmospheric.

Point A is above the local atmosphere: positive gauge pressure. Point B is below it: negative gauge pressure, also called a vacuum or "positive vacuum" reading (a vacuum gauge reads how far below atmospheric). The standard atmosphere, 101.3 kPa = 14.7 psi = 30.0 in Hg = 760 mm Hg = 34 ft H₂O = 1.013 bar, sits above zero absolute; the local atmosphere varies with weather and altitude.

Example

(a) A diver is 10 m below the surface of fresh water. Gauge pressure: \(p=\rho gh=1000(9.81)(10)=98.1\u{kPa}\). Absolute pressure: \(98.1+101.3=199.4\u{kPa}\): about twice atmospheric. (b) A tank of air has a gauge pressure of \(-16\u{kPa}\). Its absolute pressure is \(101.3-16=85.3\u{kPa}\): a partial vacuum. (c) The pressure on the floor of a 2.5 m deep pool is \(9.81\times2.5=24.5\u{kPa}\) gauge, whatever the size of the pool. Only the depth of water above a point counts, not how far the pool stretches sideways.

Watch

Section B papers state "pressure means gauge pressure". Only add 101.3 kPa if the question asks for absolute pressure. Negative gauge answers are normal (e.g. air in a tank under suction, May 2024 Q1(a)); do not "fix" the sign.

You can now: use \(p=\rho gh+p_0\); convert between gauge and absolute pressure; and read a negative gauge pressure as a partial vacuum.

7Pressure in a liquid: "same level, same fluid" ~5 min

What is this?

Swim sideways at one depth and your ears feel no change, even if you swim under a ledge or into a side alcove. In one connected body of still water only the depth matters, not the shape of the pool. But the rule needs one continuous fluid: a separate tank, or a pocket of a different liquid, does not count.

The hydrostatic law turns into the two rules used in every manometer problem.

Rule 1 · below vs above \[p_\text{below}=p_\text{above}+\rho g\,|\Delta z|\]

In words: the pressure at a lower point is higher than at a higher point by (specific weight) × (difference in depth). It does not matter where the two points are sideways, as long as one continuous fluid joins them.

Rule 2 · same level, same fluid

Pressures at two points are equal when the two points are at the same elevation (on the same horizontal level) and can be joined by a line drawn through the same fluid.

A third consequence: the shape of the container does not matter in hydrostatics. Picture a container of water with odd-shaped compartments that all open into one body of water above them, and a narrow compartment on the right, walled off from the others, holding mercury at its base.

Points a, b, c, d are at equal depth (depth 1) in water: same pressure, whatever shape of compartment they are in. Points A, B, C are at the same deeper level (depth 2) in water and have equal pressures, higher than at a–d. Point D is at the same level but in the mercury: a water path from A, B or C cannot reach it, so \(p_D\ne p_A\) (here \(p_D\) is larger: the column above D includes heavy mercury).

Older-course question · ENGG1205 2012–13 Q1(h) · 1 mark

"Is it always true that the hydrostatic pressure at one point is the same as that at another point if the two points are at the same level?"

Answer

No. The two points must also be connected by the same continuous fluid. Point D in the figure above is at the same level as A but under mercury, so its pressure is different. (Two separate tanks with different free-surface heights are another example.)

Concept question: two containers

Try a short sequence of questions about two containers with the same bottom area and the same water depth: a wide jar, and a "T-shaped" container (a thin flat box at the bottom, with a narrow tube rising from its centre to the same height).

  1. What is the force on the bottom of the wide container? The weight of fluid in the container. True for straight vertical walls: the water column above the bottom is all the water.
  2. What is the force on the bottom of the T-shaped container? Tempting to say the weight of fluid again, but this time it is not the weight.
  3. If the bottom area is the same, which container has the larger force on the bottom? Neither: the force is the same.
  4. Why the same force on the bottom, despite the different volumes of fluid?
why the bottom forces are equal (with numbers)

1. Bottom force = pressure at the bottom × bottom area: \(F=\rho gH\,A_b\). The bottom is horizontal, so the pressure is the same all over it.

2. The pressure depends only on the depth \(H\) (Section 6), not on the shape, so both containers have the same bottom pressure and the same \(F\). With \(A_b=0.01\u{m^2}\) and \(H=0.30\u{m}\): \(F=9810(0.30)(0.01)=29.4\u{N}\) for both.

3. Wide jar: water volume \(A_bH=0.003\u{m^3}\), weight 29.4 N = bottom force. ✓

4. T-shape: say the flat part is 1 cm tall and the tube has area 1 cm² (\(10^{-4}\u{m^2}\)) for the remaining 29 cm. Volume:

\[\begin{aligned}V&=0.01(0.01)+10^{-4}(0.29)\\&=1.29\times10^{-4}\u{m^3}\end{aligned}\]

so the weight is only 1.27 N.

5. Where does the other 28.2 N come from? Look at the water in the T-shape as a free body (figure, right). The ceiling of the flat part is at depth 0.29 m, where the pressure is \(9810(0.29)=2845\u{Pa}\). The water pushes up on that ceiling, so the ceiling pushes down on the water with \(2845\times(0.01-0.0001)=28.2\u{N}\). Pressure force on a surface is perpendicular to it and inward into the fluid.

6. Vertical balance of the water: bottom push up = weight + ceiling push down \(=1.27+28.2=29.4\u{N}\). ✓ The container's ceiling, not the water's weight, supplies most of the force on the bottom.

In words: pressure depends on depth, not on how much fluid there is. The bottom force equals the weight of the fluid only when the walls are vertical. This "hydrostatic paradox" is why you can write \(p=\rho gh\) for any container shape.

Watch

The container itself still weighs what it weighs: on a scale, the T-shape container with its water reads the true total weight (container + 1.27 N of water). The extra 28.2 N on the bottom is cancelled by the 28.2 N upward push on the ceiling, which is part of the same container.

You can now: apply "below = above + \(\rho g|\Delta z|\)" and "same level, same fluid"; spot when two points at one level do not share a pressure; and explain why the bottom force depends on depth, not on the amount of fluid.

8Pressure in a static gas ~2 min

What is this?

Diving 10 m into a pool adds almost a whole atmosphere of pressure. Climbing 10 m up a ladder above the pool takes away only about 118 Pa, about a tenth of a percent, because air is so light. So in a tank or pipe full of gas, a few metres of height make no difference worth counting.

The hydrostatic equation \(\mathrm dp/\mathrm dz=-\rho g\) applies to gases too, but a gas is very light: air at room conditions has \(\rho\approx1.2\u{kg/m^3}\), about \(1/830\) of water.

Example

Across 10 m of height, air changes pressure by \(\rho g\,\Delta z=1.2(9.81)(10)\approx118\u{Pa}\), while 10 m of water changes it by 98 100 Pa. Even over 100 m of air the change is only about 1.2 kPa, around 1% of atmospheric pressure.

Rule · pressure in a gas

Unless a huge elevation difference is involved, the pressure in a static gas may be taken as constant everywhere in the system, regardless of height.

In words: in a manometer or tank problem, gas columns add nothing. The air at the top of a tank has the same pressure as the air in the pipe leading to the manometer, even if the pipe goes up or down.

This is why exam questions say "the air pressure in this vessel can be assumed to be the same everywhere" (May 2023) and why, in a U-tube manometer measuring a gas, the gas term is dropped (Section 13). "Huge" means kilometres: across the whole atmosphere, the pressure falls from 101 kPa at sea level to about half at 5.5 km, and there the density change also matters (air is compressible).

You can now: explain why a gas column adds nothing in a manometer or tank problem; and say when that is no longer true (kilometres of height).

9Pressure distributions and pressure force ~5 min

What is this?

Think of the pool wall. The water presses on it gently near the surface and harder near the floor, always straight into the wall. A pressure diagram draws this as a row of arrows: each one perpendicular to the wall, and longer the deeper it sits. It is a picture of a spread-out load, like snow lying on a roof.

This section leads from pressure at a point to the force on a whole surface, which is the subject of H3. It is summarised here because they rest on the ideas above.

Recall:

  • Pressure increases linearly with vertical depth.
  • In unit depth (1 m), pressure increases by an amount equal to the specific weight of the fluid.
  • Pressure is a scalar, independent of direction.
  • The force due to pressure acting on a submerged surface (the pressure force) is a distributed load: it is spread over the surface, like snow on a roof, rather than acting at one point.
  • The pressure force is always directed inward and perpendicular to the surface on which it acts.
Definition · pressure distribution diagram

A pressure distribution diagram shows the distributed load on a submerged surface (plane or curved, fully or partly submerged) by a row of arrows:

  • each arrow is the local pressure force, drawn inward and perpendicular to the surface;
  • its length is proportional to the local pressure, so it grows linearly with vertical depth: zero at the free surface, largest at the bottom;
  • the line joining the arrow tails (the far ends) is the pressure envelope.

Reminder: pressure is a scalar, not associated with any direction, and increases linearly with vertical depth. The pressure force is a local force on an infinitesimal part of a surface, therefore a vector; its direction is set by the direction of the surface.

Shapes of pressure diagrams

Left: a vertical wall that pierces the free surface carries a triangular distribution (zero at the surface, \(\rho gh\) at the bottom). Middle: a vertical plate whose top is at depth \(h_1\) and bottom at \(h_2\) carries a trapezoidal distribution, from \(\gamma h_1\) to \(\gamma h_2\). Right: the same plate inclined: the arrows tilt to stay perpendicular to the plate, but their lengths still go from \(\gamma h_1\) to \(\gamma h_2\). The inclination changes only the direction of the pressure force, not the pressure: pressure depends only on vertical depth.

Left: on a circular surface, every pressure arrow is perpendicular to the circle, so its line of action passes through the centre \(O\); therefore the resultant force \(F_R\) also passes through \(O\) (used in H3). Right: a spherical buoy held under seawater by a cable. The pressure envelope around it is larger on the lower half (deeper) than on the upper half, so the pressure forces add up to a net upward force: the buoyant force \(F_B\), which a free-body sketch balances by the cable tension \(T\) pulling down (with the buoy's own weight, if not negligible; H4).

H3 shows how to replace a distributed pressure load by a single resultant force (magnitude and point of action). Here the main point is how to draw the diagrams: perpendicular, inward, length ∝ vertical depth.

You can now: draw triangular, trapezoidal, inclined and curved pressure diagrams; and explain why the pressure force on a circular surface passes through its centre.

10Layered liquids ~6 min

What is this?

Picture a layer of oil floating on the pool. Dive through it: the oil above you adds weight, then the water adds more, and each metre of water adds more than each metre of oil, because water is heavier. At the boundary nothing jumps: the pressure at the bottom of the oil is where the water layer starts.

Liquids that do not mix (oil and water, water and mercury) settle in layers, lightest on top. Inside each layer the hydrostatic law holds with that layer's own density.

A bilinear pressure diagram

Take a vertical wall holding back a layer of oil (thickness \(b_1\)) floating on water (thickness \(b_2\)). The pressure diagram is bilinear: two straight pieces with a kink at the oil–water interface. It gives two resultant forces \(F_{R_1}\) (oil part, triangle) and \(F_{R_2}\) (water part, trapezoid), which H3 computes.

Oil over water against a vertical wall. With pressure drawn horizontally and depth vertically, the envelope's slope (depth per unit of pressure) is \(1/\gamma\): the heavier water layer has the smaller slope, so its envelope leans outward faster.

In words: the slope of the pressure envelope is scaled by the inverse of the specific weight of the fluid. The greater the specific weight, the smaller the slope (and the faster the pressure grows with depth).

The interface carries the pressure across

Take a column of water (height \(\Delta z_1\)) on top of mercury (height \(\Delta z_2\)), open to the air (\(p_0=0\)). Call 1 the water–mercury interface and 2 the bottom.

Result · two layers \[p_1=\rho_\text{water}g\,\Delta z_1\] \[p_2=p_1+\rho_\text{mercury}g\,\Delta z_2\]

In words: the pressure is continuous at an interface. The pressure at the bottom of the water, \(p_1\), is the starting pressure at the top of the mercury. It acts on the mercury like a surcharge: a uniform extra pressure transmitted to, and felt at, every depth of the mercury below.

Example

0.5 m of water on 0.1 m of mercury, open top. Interface: \(p_1=9810(0.5)=4905\u{Pa}\). Bottom:

\[\begin{aligned}p_2&=4905+13\,600(9.81)(0.1)\\&=4905+13\,342\\&=18\,247\u{Pa}\approx18.2\u{kPa}\end{aligned}\]

The 10 cm of mercury adds almost three times as much as the 50 cm of water.

A column of many liquids

Now stack oil (\(\rho_o\)), water (\(\rho_w\)), glycerin (\(\rho_G\)) and mercury (\(\rho_M\)) with a known pressure \(p_1\) at the top (\(z=z_1\)) and interfaces at \(z_2,z_3,z_4\), bottom at \(z_5\). Across each layer, \(p_{i+1}-p_i=-\rho_i g(z_{i+1}-z_i)\); summing all four:

\[\begin{aligned}p_5&=p_1+\gamma_o|\Delta z_{12}|+\gamma_w|\Delta z_{23}|\\&\quad+\gamma_G|\Delta z_{34}|+\gamma_M|\Delta z_{45}|\end{aligned}\]

In words: start from the known pressure and add (specific weight × thickness) for every layer you go down through. This "add as you go down" walk is exactly the manometer method of Sections 13–15.

Reading the formula
\(p_1\)
whatever presses on the top layer (0 gauge if it is open to the air).
\(\gamma_o|\Delta z_{12}|\)
the weight of the oil layer resting on each square metre below it.
\(+\gamma_w|\Delta z_{23}|+\dots\)
each deeper layer adds its own weight, with its own specific weight.
\(p_5\)
the total pressing on the bottom: everything stacked above it.
Example · four layers with numbers

Open tank (\(p_1=0\)): oil 1.0 m (\(\rho=850\)), water 1.5 m, glycerin 0.5 m (\(\rho=1260\)), mercury 0.2 m. Find the pressure at the bottom.

layer by layer

1. Oil: \(p_2=0+850(9.81)(1.0)=8339\u{Pa}\).

2. Water:

\[\begin{aligned}p_3&=8339+1000(9.81)(1.5)\\&=23\,054\u{Pa}\end{aligned}\]

3. Glycerin:

\[\begin{aligned}p_4&=23\,054+1260(9.81)(0.5)\\&=29\,234\u{Pa}\end{aligned}\]

4. Mercury:

\[\begin{aligned}p_5&=29\,234+13\,600(9.81)(0.2)\\&=55\,917\u{Pa}\end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{p_5\approx55.9\u{kPa}\ \text{(gauge)}}\]

Try it · pressure down a layered column

Oil (SG 0.85) on water on mercury (SG 13.6). Depth is drawn downward and pressure (gauge, kPa) across. Each layer is a straight line; the heavier the liquid, the flatter the line (more kPa per metre). Raising the top pressure shifts the whole line sideways: Pascal's principle (Section 4) in a picture.

You can now: draw a bilinear pressure diagram; carry the pressure across an interface; and find the pressure at the bottom of a stack of layers.

11Measuring pressure; the mercury barometer ~4 min

What is this?

We live at the bottom of an ocean of air, like a diver on the floor of a pool. A barometer weighs that air. The air pushes on the open mercury in the dish and holds up a column of mercury in a tube with nothing above it. The column stops rising when its weight per square metre equals the push of the air.

The principle of hydrostatics is widely applied to the measurement of pressure or of a pressure difference. Every instrument in Sections 11–15 uses only the two rules of Section 7:

  1. Pressure "below" is higher than pressure "above" by (specific weight of the fluid) × (difference in depth).
  2. Two points on the same horizontal level have the same pressure as long as they are connected by the same fluid.

The mercury barometer

Definition · barometer

A barometer measures the atmospheric pressure. The mercury barometer is a glass tube, closed at the top, filled with mercury and turned upside down into a dish of mercury. The mercury falls until its column is about 760 mm high, leaving an almost perfect vacuum at the top of the tube.

The atmosphere pushes on the open mercury surface in the dish; the column is held up by that push.

Free-body diagram of the mercury column (area \(A\)): vacuum above (no push), weight \(\rho_\text{Hg}gAh\) down, \(p_\text{atm}A\) up at the dish level.

Result · barometer \[p_\text{atm}=\rho_\text{Hg}\,g\,h\]

In words: atmospheric pressure = specific weight of mercury × height of the mercury column. A point inside the tube at the level of the dish surface is joined to the dish surface through mercury, so it is at \(p_\text{atm}\); going up \(h\) to the vacuum (\(p\approx0\) absolute) subtracts \(\rho_\text{Hg}gh\).

Reading the formula
\(p_\text{atm}\)
the push of the air on each square metre of the dish.
\(\rho_\text{Hg}\,g\)
the weight of one cubic metre of mercury.
\(h\)
the height of mercury the air can hold up.
\(\rho_\text{Hg}gh\)
the weight of the column per square metre; it matches the air's push because the top of the tube is empty.
the numbers

1. Atmospheric pressure about 101 kPa (standard: 101.325 kPa). Mercury: \(\rho=13\,600\u{kg/m^3}\), "13.6 times heavier than water".

2. Column height:

\[\begin{aligned}h&=\frac{101\,325}{13\,600\times9.81}\\&=0.7595\u{m}\approx760\u{mm}\end{aligned}\]

This is why standard atmosphere = 760 mm Hg.

3. With water instead: \(h=101\,325/9810=10.33\u{m}\), more than 10 m high, 13.6 times taller: a water barometer would dwarf a person standing beside it.

It is conventional to give atmospheric pressure as the barometric height in mm of mercury (mmHg); 1 mmHg \(=13\,600\times9.81\times0.001\approx133.4\u{Pa}\). Blood pressure is also measured in mmHg: a reading of "120 over 80" means a gauge pressure of 120 mmHg (16.0 kPa) when the heart beats and 80 mmHg (10.7 kPa) between beats.

Going further · why mercury, and why not a perfect vacuum

Mercury is used because it is so dense (a short tube suffices) and it evaporates very little. The space at the top is not quite empty: it holds mercury vapour at its vapour pressure (H1), about 0.16 Pa at 20 °C, which is negligible. A water barometer would have water vapour at about 2.3 kPa at the top, a 2% error.

You can now: state the two measurement rules; find atmospheric pressure from a barometer height (and back); and convert between mmHg and Pa.

12Atmospheric pressure, weather and storm surge ~5 min

What is this?

Where the air presses less on the sea, the sea rises a little, until the extra water makes up for the missing air. Deep down, at one level, the pressure must be the same everywhere, or the water would flow sideways. A diver near the sea floor under a typhoon has more water but less air on top, and the two changes cancel.

The weather chart

Look at a Hong Kong Observatory surface weather chart of East Asia and the western Pacific (for example, 5 Oct 2026, 14:00 HK time). The curved lines are isobars: lines joining places with the same air pressure, labelled in hPa (millibar). \(1000\u{hPa}=100\u{kPa}=1\u{bar}\). Centres marked H (high) and L (low) are local pressure maxima and minima; a tropical cyclone appears as a tight set of closed isobars around a low.

  • The pressures on the chart are mean sea level pressures (MSLP): each station's measured pressure is adjusted for the station's altitude to what it would be at sea level, so that stations at different heights can be compared. (Section 8: across large heights, air pressure does change.)
  • The standard atmospheric pressure, 1013.25 mb (101.325 kPa), is measured at standard mean sea level.
  • Typically the local air pressure deviates by only about ±1% (about ±10 hPa) from the standard value.
  • Weather is good at a local maximum of pressure (a high: sinking, drying air) and bad at a local minimum (a low: rising air forms clouds and rain). Inclement (rainy, unsettled) weather occurs in a trough, an elongated region of low pressure.
  • A tropical cyclone (typhoon) is a region of low pressure; the pressure is lowest in the eye, its centre.

Storm surge

Near the centre of super typhoon Choi-Wan the atmospheric pressure was 990 mb, against the standard 1013 mb. The pressure drop is \(\Delta P=1013-990=23\u{mb}=2300\u{Pa}\). The sea under the low pressure is pushed up less, so it rises.

Under good weather (1013 mb) the sea is at its normal level; under the centre of the typhoon (990 mb) it stands higher by \(\Delta h\). At a fixed level deep below, the pressure is the same in both places.

Result · sea-level rise from a pressure drop \[\Delta h=\frac{\Delta p}{\rho g}\]

In words: the extra water depth must make up exactly for the missing air pressure, so that the total pressure at some level below the surface stays the same.

Reading the formula
\(\Delta p\)
how much less the air presses under the low.
\(\rho g\)
the pressure each extra metre of sea water adds.
\(\Delta h\)
the extra depth of water that puts the missing pressure back.
why \(\Delta h=\Delta p/(\rho g)\), and Choi-Wan's numbers

1. Pick a level a depth \(h\) below the normal sea surface, far below. In still water, the pressure on a horizontal level in the same fluid is the same everywhere (Rule 2), so it must be the same under the good weather and under the typhoon. If it differed, water would flow sideways until it was equal.

2. Good weather: \(p=p_\text{std}+\rho gh\). Under the typhoon: \(p=(p_\text{std}-\Delta p)+\rho g(h+\Delta h)\).

3. Set equal: \(\rho g\,\Delta h=\Delta p\), so \(\Delta h=\Delta p/(\rho g)\).

4. Choi-Wan (with \(\rho=1000\)): \(\Delta h=2300/(1000\times9.81)=0.23\u{m}\). With seawater (\(\rho\approx1025\)) it is 0.229 m: the same to two figures.

This is the pressure effect on the sea level in the open ocean; a useful rule: each 1 hPa of pressure drop raises the sea about 1 cm. Wind (pushing water towards the shore) and tides can add much more. As the storm moves, so does its low-pressure region, and it carries a mound of water with it, travelling in the sea as a wave. When the cyclone nears a coast, this water has gained considerable momentum and can surge up a shore slope as a large wave: the storm surge, similar to a tsunami in that a massive amount of water suddenly floods a coastal region.

Example

A very strong typhoon has a central pressure of 920 hPa. Open-ocean rise from pressure alone (seawater):

\[\Delta h=\frac{(1013-920)\times100}{1025\times9.81}=0.92\u{m}\]

Wind set-up near a shallow coast can add several metres more.

You can now: read isobars and relate highs and lows to the weather; and find the open-ocean sea-level rise from a pressure drop with \(\Delta h=\Delta p/(\rho g)\).

13Piezometer and simple U-tube manometer ~6 min

What is this?

A manometer turns the dive around. Instead of going down into the fluid to feel its pressure, you let the fluid climb up a tube until the column weighs, per square metre, exactly what the pressure pushes. Then you read the pressure as a height, with a ruler.

Definition · manometer

A manometer is a tube holding one or more liquids whose column heights show a pressure or a pressure difference. You read lengths with a ruler and convert them to pressure with \(\rho gh\). No springs, no electronics: just hydrostatics.

Piezometer

A piezometer is a vertical tube, open at the top, connected to a point A of a pipe or tank. The liquid in the system rises up the tube until its weight balances the pressure at A. It works whether the fluid in the system is static or flowing (the tube itself holds still liquid).

Piezometer: liquid (specific weight \(\gamma_1\)) rises a height \(h_1\) above A in the open tube.

Simple U-tube. Points (1) at A's level, (2) at the gauge-fluid interface, (3) at the same level in the open limb.

Result · piezometer \[p_A=\gamma_1h_1\]

In words: pressure at A = specific weight of the system liquid × the height it rises above A. (Start at the open top, \(p=0\), go down \(h_1\) through the same liquid to A's level.)

It works only when:

  • the fluid is a liquid: a gas would simply escape out of the open tube, with no column to read;
  • the pressure is not too large: 38 kPa of water pressure needs a tube \(38\,060/9810=3.88\u{m}\) tall.

It also cannot measure a negative gauge pressure: air would be sucked in through the open top. A U-tube with a heavy gauge fluid fixes all three problems.

Example

Water rises 0.6 m above the centre of a pipe in a piezometer. \(p_A=9810(0.6)=5886\u{Pa}=5.89\u{kPa}\).

Simple U-tube manometer

What is this? (U-tube)

A heavy liquid in the bend acts as a short, heavy column. Mercury is 13.6 times heavier than water, so a short mercury column balances a pressure that would need a very tall water tube. Because the heavy liquid seals the tube, gas cannot escape and air cannot be sucked in, so it also works for gases and for suction.

A U-shaped tube holds a gauge fluid (specific weight \(\gamma_2\), usually mercury) that does not mix with the system fluid (\(\gamma_1\)). The left limb connects to A; the right limb is open. The system fluid fills the tube from A down to the gauge fluid's surface in the left limb, a depth \(h_1\) below A; in the open limb, the gauge fluid stands \(h_2\) above that interface level.

Result · simple U-tube manometer \[p_A=\gamma_2h_2-\gamma_1h_1\]

In words: pressure at A = (gauge fluid's specific weight × its height difference) − (system fluid's specific weight × the depth of the interface below A). If the system fluid is a gas, \(\gamma_1h_1\) is negligible and \(p_A=\gamma_2h_2\).

Reading the formula
\(\gamma_2h_2\)
the gauge-fluid column in the open limb: the pressure at the interface level, measured from the open top.
\(-\gamma_1h_1\)
A sits \(h_1\) higher than that level, with less system fluid on top, so take that column off.
\(p_A\)
what is left: the gauge pressure at A.
the four steps

1. \(p_2=p_3\). Same horizontal level, connected by the same fluid (the gauge fluid, through the bend).

2. \(p_2=p_1+\gamma_1h_1\). (2) is \(h_1\) below (1), through the system fluid.

3. \(p_3=\gamma_2h_2\). (3) is \(h_2\) below the open surface, where \(p_\text{atm}=0\) gauge, through the gauge fluid.

4. \(p_A=p_1\). Same horizontal level, connected by the same fluid (the system fluid).

Hence \(p_1+\gamma_1h_1=\gamma_2h_2\), i.e. \(p_A=\gamma_2h_2-\gamma_1h_1\).

Example

(a) Water flows in a pipe; a mercury U-tube is connected at A. The mercury interface in the left limb is 0.2 m below A, and the mercury in the open limb stands 0.3 m higher than that interface.

\[\begin{aligned}p_A&=13.6(9810)(0.3)-9810(0.2)\\&=40\,025-1962=\boxed{38.06\u{kPa}}\end{aligned}\]

The piezometer above would need a 3.9 m tube for this pressure.

(b) The same U-tube on a tank of air reads \(h_2=5\u{cm}\) of mercury: \(p_A=13.6(9810)(0.05)=6.67\u{kPa}\); the air column is ignored (Section 8).

Try it · a simple U-tube manometer

Equal tube areas: whatever leaves one limb enters the other. Raise \(p_A\) and the gauge fluid is pushed down on the A side and up on the open side. Make \(p_A\) negative and the open-side level falls below the A side (the pattern of May 2024 Q1 and ENGG1205 2017–18 Q1). A lighter gauge fluid gives bigger, easier-to-read heights for the same pressure.

You can now: find a pressure from a piezometer or simple U-tube reading; say why a piezometer fails for gases, high or negative pressures; and drop the gas column when the system fluid is a gas.

14Differential U-tube manometer ~5 min

What is this?

Two points, two pushes: which is stronger, and by how much? A differential manometer joins both points to one U-tube. The gauge fluid in the bend is pushed down on the side with the higher pressure and up on the other, like a balance tipping towards the heavier pan. The tilt, read as a height, gives the difference.

A differential manometer measures the difference in pressure between two points A and B (for example, either side of a valve or of a narrowing in a pipe, F3). Both limbs are connected: the left to A (fluid \(\gamma_1\)), the right to B (fluid \(\gamma_3\)), with a gauge fluid \(\gamma_2\) in the bend.

(1) at A's level; the interface (2) is \(h_1\) below it; (3) is level with (2) in the right limb; the right-hand interface (4) is \(h_2\) above (3); (5), at B's level, is \(h_3\) above (4).

Result · differential U-tube manometer \[p_A-p_B=\gamma_2h_2+\gamma_3h_3-\gamma_1h_1\]

In words: walk from A down to the left interface (+), across the bend at the same level, up the gauge fluid (−) and up the fluid of B (−) to reach B; the sum of the changes is \(p_B-p_A\).

Reading the formula
\(-\gamma_1h_1\)
the fluid column from A down to the left interface: A is less deep than the interface.
\(\gamma_2h_2\)
the gauge-fluid column between the two interface levels.
\(\gamma_3h_3\)
the fluid column from the right interface up to B.
\(p_A-p_B\)
how much harder A pushes than B: the columns between them account for all of it.
the steps

1. \(p_2=p_3\). Same horizontal level, connected by the same fluid (the gauge fluid).

2. \(p_2=p_1+\gamma_1h_1=p_A+\gamma_1h_1\). (2) is \(h_1\) below (1); (1) and A are on one level in fluid 1.

3. Going down from B:

\[\begin{aligned}p_3&=p_4+\gamma_2h_2\\&=p_5+\gamma_3h_3+\gamma_2h_2\\&=p_B+\gamma_3h_3+\gamma_2h_2\end{aligned}\]

(3) is \(h_2\) below (4) through the gauge fluid; (4) is \(h_3\) below (5) through fluid 3; (5) and B are level in fluid 3.

4. Equate steps 2 and 3: \(p_A+\gamma_1h_1=p_B+\gamma_3h_3+\gamma_2h_2\), which rearranges to the boxed result.

The common special case

If A and B are at the same elevation (\(h_1=h_2+h_3\)) and the two containers hold the same fluid (\(\gamma_1=\gamma_3\)), then, with \(h=h_2\) the manometer differential reading (the height difference between the two gauge-fluid surfaces):

Result · same elevation, same fluid \[\Delta p=p_A-p_B=\Big(\frac{\rho_m}{\rho}-1\Big)\rho gh\]

In words: pressure difference = (manometer fluid density ÷ pipe fluid density − 1) × \(\rho gh\). The "−1" is there because the pipe fluid fills the rest of the limb on the low side and partly balances the gauge fluid.

Reading the formula
\(\rho_m/\rho\)
how many times heavier the gauge fluid is than the pipe fluid.
\(-1\)
over the height \(h\), the pipe fluid on the other side cancels one pipe-fluid column's worth.
\(\rho gh\)
the pressure of a column \(h\) of pipe fluid.
from the general result to the special case

1. Put \(\gamma_3=\gamma_1\) and \(h_1=h_2+h_3\): \(p_A-p_B=\gamma_2h_2+\gamma_1h_3-\gamma_1(h_2+h_3)\).

2. The \(\gamma_1h_3\) terms cancel: \(p_A-p_B=(\gamma_2-\gamma_1)h_2\).

3. Write \(\gamma_2=\rho_mg\), \(\gamma_1=\rho g\), \(h_2=h\): \((\rho_m-\rho)gh=(\rho_m/\rho-1)\rho gh\).

Example

(a) Water at A and B (same level), mercury gauge, reading \(h=0.15\u{m}\):

\[\Delta p=(13.6-1)(9810)(0.15)=18.5\u{kPa}\]

higher at A (the side where mercury is pushed down). (b) The same reading with air at A and B: \(\Delta p\approx13.6(9810)(0.15)=20.0\u{kPa}\) (the "−1" becomes −0.0012, negligible).

(c) Unequal levels, water at both ends, mercury gauge: \(h_1=0.6\u{m}\), \(h_2=0.2\u{m}\), \(h_3=0.9\u{m}\) (so B is 0.5 m above A). Use the general result:

\[\begin{aligned}p_A-p_B&=133\,416(0.2)+9810(0.9)\\&\quad-9810(0.6)=29.6\u{kPa}\end{aligned}\]

The special-case formula would give 24.7 kPa here: wrong, because A and B are not level.

Watch

The formula \((\rho_m/\rho-1)\rho gh\) is a result for a specific set-up. There is no need to memorise results like this one; in the exam, walk through the fluids point by point instead (Section 15). Then you never use it in the wrong situation.

You can now: find \(p_A-p_B\) from a differential manometer by walking through the fluids; and use \((\rho_m/\rho-1)\rho gh\) only when A and B are level and hold the same fluid.

15Manometer chains: the exam method ~8 min

What is this?

The manometer walk is the pool dive done along a tube. Start where you know the pressure and follow the fluid. Each time you go down, more fluid sits on top of you, so add; each time you go up, take it off; moving sideways at one level in one fluid changes nothing.

Every manometer question, however strange the picture, is solved by the same walk. This is exam type H2.2.

Ready answer · walking through a manometer (exam type H2.2)
  1. Do: Sketch and label every fluid surface and interface with a number; mark known pressures (open to air: 0 gauge). The numbered points are the stops on your dive; a known pressure is a place where the dive can start or finish.
  2. Do: Start at a point of known pressure (or at the unknown, as a symbol). Like a diver, you need the pressure where you begin; every later pressure is that value plus or minus columns of fluid.
  3. Do: Walk through the tubes: going down \(\Delta z\) through a fluid, add \(\gamma\,\Delta z\); going up, subtract it. Crossing sideways at one level in one continuous fluid: no change. Gas columns: no change. Going down adds \(\rho g\,\Delta z\) because more fluid sits on top of you; going up removes it; gas is too light to count (Section 8).
  4. Do: Jump across a U-bend only at a level where the same fluid is on both sides (usually the lower gauge-fluid surface). Across a horizontal line in the same continuous fluid the pressure is equal (Rule 2); at a level where the two limbs hold different fluids, the columns above differ and so do the pressures.
  5. Do: End at the other known point and set the sum equal to its pressure; solve. Keep symbols to the last line, then substitute. The walk gives one equation linking the start to the end, so the one unknown drops out; symbols keep each column visible for the marker and for your own sign check.
  6. Do: Check the sign: the side where the gauge fluid is pushed down has the higher pressure. The stronger push wins: it shoves the gauge fluid down on its own side and up the other limb.

Worked example · ENGG1205 2017–18 Q1(a) (older course, 10 marks)

Past paper · older course ENGG1205 · same lecturer

"For the tank shown in Figure C1(a), find the pressure of air inside the tank and the pressure gauge reading if the mercury manometer reading \(H=0.12\u{m}\). Relative density of mercury = 13.6." (Take \(\rho_w=1000\u{kg/m^3}\), \(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\).)

the walk, step by step

1. Start at the open mercury surface (right limb): \(p=0\). Open to the atmosphere.

2. Go across the bend to the level of that surface in the left limb: still mercury, same level, so still 0.

3. Go up \(H=0.12\u{m}\) through mercury to the mercury surface in the left limb:

\[\begin{aligned}p&=0-\rho_\text{Hg}gH\\&=-13.6(9810)(0.12)=-16.0\u{kPa}\end{aligned}\]

Up means subtract.

4. Through the air in the pipe up into the tank: no change (gas). Air weighs so little that a pipe of it adds no measurable pressure (Section 8). So \(p_\text{air}=-16.0\u{kPa}\) (gauge): a partial vacuum, which is why the mercury is sucked up on the tank side.

5. Down 4 m through the water to the gauge:

\[\begin{aligned}p_\text{gauge}&=-16.01+9.81(4)\\&=-16.01+39.24=23.23\u{kPa}\end{aligned}\]

\[\boxed{p_\text{air}=-16.0\u{kPa}}\qquad\boxed{p_\text{gauge}=23.2\u{kPa}}\]

These match the given solution.

Worked example · ENGG1205 2015–16 Q2(a): inverted U-tube (6 marks)

Past paper · older course ENGG1205 · same lecturer

"Determine the elevation difference, \(\Delta h\), between the water levels in the open tanks shown in Figure C2(a). The fluid in the inverted manometer has a relative density of 0.9."

Two open tanks; the left water level is 1 m above the floor and higher than the right by \(\Delta h\). An inverted U-tube joins them: water rises in both legs, and a lighter liquid (SG 0.90) fills the top of the bend and the top 0.4 m of the right leg. A is the top of the water in the left leg, B the same level in the right leg; C is in the left leg at the left free-surface level; \(x\) is the distance from the bottom of the SG 0.9 column down to that level; D is at the right free-surface level.

the walk, step by step

1. \(p_A=p_B\). Same level, joined through the SG 0.9 liquid across the top of the bend.

2. Left leg, from A down \(0.4+x\) through water to C, which is level with the left free surface and joined to it through water: \(p_A+\rho_wg(0.4+x)=p_C=0\).

3. Right leg, from B down 0.4 m of SG 0.9 liquid, then \(x+\Delta h\) of water to D at the right free surface: \(p_B+0.9\rho_wg(0.4)+\rho_wg(x+\Delta h)=0\).

4. Subtract step 2 from step 3 (\(p_A=p_B\), and the \(x\) terms cancel): \(0.36+\Delta h-0.4=0\).

\(\boxed{\Delta h=0.4(1-0.9)=0.04\u{m}}\). An inverted U-tube with a gauge liquid lighter than water magnifies small differences: here 0.4 m of reading for 4 cm of level difference.

When the reading changes: the "2x" rule

What is this? (reading change)

The gauge fluid is like water in a U-shaped hose. Push one side down by \(x\) and the same amount of liquid rises by \(x\) on the other side (equal tube areas), so the gap between the two levels grows by \(2x\), not \(x\).

Some questions give the reading for one state and ask for it in another (May 2022). If the pressure on one side rises, the gauge fluid in that limb drops by \(x\) and, with equal tube areas, rises by \(x\) in the other limb. The reading (the difference between the two gauge-fluid levels) grows by \(2x\), and the column of system fluid above the dropped interface gets longer by \(x\).

Example

A water-filled tube runs from point C down into a mercury U-tube whose other limb is open. With the water surface exactly at C (so \(p_C=0\)), the mercury in the open limb stands \(\Delta h=0.10\u{m}\) higher. Water is then added until it stands 1.0 m above C. Find the new reading.

before and after

1. Before: let \(y\) = the water column from C down to the mercury on the connected side. Balancing at that mercury level: \(\gamma_wy=\gamma_\text{Hg}(0.10)\), so \(y=13.6(0.10)=1.36\u{m}\). The heights not given in the question come out of the first state.

2. After: the mercury drops \(x\) on the connected side, rises \(x\) in the open limb. Water column above the new interface: \(1.0+1.36+x\). New reading \(0.10+2x\).

3. Balance at the new interface level: \(\gamma_w(1.0+1.36+x)=\gamma_\text{Hg}(0.10+2x)\), i.e. \(2.36+x=1.36+27.2x\).

4. \(x=1.0/26.2=0.0382\u{m}\); new reading \(0.10+2(0.0382)=\boxed{0.176\u{m}}\).

A common wrong answer adds the full 1 m of water as \(\gamma_w(1.0)=\gamma_\text{Hg}\Delta(\Delta h)\), giving \(1/13.6=0.074\) extra and forgetting the \(2x\) and the \(+x\) of water.

Going further · the well-type inclined manometer (exam type H2.1, May 2025 Q1)

Beyond the piezometer and the simple and differential U-tubes, May 2025 asked about the well-tube inclined manometer. It replaces one limb of the U-tube by a large reservoir (the well, area \(A\)) and the other by a narrow tube (area \(a\)) sloping at a small angle \(\theta\) to the horizontal. The reading \(L\) is taken along the sloping tube.

\[\Delta p=\rho_mgL\Big(\sin\theta+\frac{a}{A}\Big)\approx\rho_mgL\sin\theta\]
Reading the formula
\(L\sin\theta\)
how far the liquid rises vertically in the sloping tube.
\(L\,a/A\)
how far the well level falls: the liquid that moved into the tube, spread over the wide well.
\(\rho_mgL(\dots)\)
the weight per square metre of the total vertical height difference, which balances \(\Delta p\).

In words: the vertical rise is only \(L\sin\theta\), so a small pressure gives a long, easy-to-read length; the well is so wide that its level hardly falls (the \(a/A\) term is a small correction).

Advantages over a standard U-tube (what the question wanted, in marker-point form):

  1. Higher sensitivity / precision: the reading is magnified by \(1/\sin\theta\) (3.9 times at 15°, 5.8 times at 10°), so small pressures (a few hundred Pa, e.g. air ducts, low-speed wind tunnels) can be read accurately.
  2. Only one reading: the well level barely moves, so you read one scale instead of two levels and subtracting; less reading error, quicker.
  3. The scale can be calibrated directly in pressure units (Pa or mm water) once \(\theta\), \(\rho_m\) and \(a/A\) are fixed.
  4. Compact, and a light gauge oil can be used for extra sensitivity.

Example: 100 Pa with a gauge oil of SG 0.8 at \(\theta=15^\circ\): vertical rise \(100/(800\times9.81)=12.7\u{mm}\), but the reading along the tube is \(12.7/\sin15^\circ=49\u{mm}\). With a well 100 times the tube area, ignoring \(a/A\) causes an error of about \(0.01/\sin15^\circ\approx4\%\).

You can now: solve any manometer chain by walking through the fluids with the right signs; find a new reading after a change with the \(2x\) rule; and give the advantages of a well-type inclined manometer.

18Find the mistake

1 · A student's barometer

"A mercury barometer reads 750 mm. So \(p_\text{atm}=\rho gh=1000(9.81)(0.750)\) \(=7.36\u{kPa}\)."

Hint

Which liquid is in the column?

Answer

The column is mercury: \(p_\text{atm}=13\,600(9.81)(0.750)\) \(=100.1\u{kPa}\). 7.36 kPa is far below any real atmospheric pressure, a sign something is wrong.

2 · A student's U-tube on a water pipe

"The mercury interface is 0.2 m below A; the open-side mercury is 0.3 m higher. Going from A down 0.2 m of water and then up 0.3 m of mercury to the open end: \(p_A+9810(0.2)+133\,416(0.3)=0\), so \(p_A=-42.0\u{kPa}\)."

Hint

Which way does each step go, and what sign should it carry?

Answer

Going up 0.3 m of mercury subtracts: \(p_A+9810(0.2)-133\,416(0.3)=0\), so \(p_A=+38.06\u{kPa}\). Sign check: mercury is pushed down on the A side, so A is above atmospheric.

3 · A student's two layers

"A tank holds 2 m of oil (SG 0.8) on 1 m of water. Pressure at the bottom: \(9810(0.8)(3)=23.5\u{kPa}\)."

Hint

Is the whole 3 m oil?

Answer

Each layer uses its own specific weight:

\[\begin{aligned}p&=0.8(9810)(2)+9810(1)\\&=15.70+9.81=25.5\u{kPa}\end{aligned}\]

19Concept checks

1 · Direction

A small cube is held 2 m deep in water. Compare the pressure on its top face, its bottom face and a side face. Compare the directions of the pressure forces on them.

Answer

At (almost) the same point the pressure is the same in all directions (Pascal), about 19.6 kPa; the bottom is a little deeper, so slightly higher. The forces point into each face: down on the top, up on the bottom, sideways on the sides. The small excess up on the bottom is the buoyant force (H4).

2 · Same level?

Two open tanks, one of water and one of oil, stand side by side, both filled to 1 m. Is the pressure the same at the bottoms? What if they are joined by a pipe at the bottom?

Answer

No: water gives 9.81 kPa, oil (SG 0.8) 7.85 kPa: same level, but different fluids. If joined, the water flows towards the oil side until the pressures at the junction level match; the oil ends up standing higher than the water (1/0.8 = 1.25 times the column height above the junction).

3 · Gas or liquid?

Why can the air column in a manometer's connecting pipe be ignored, but not a water column?

Answer

\(\Delta p=\rho g\Delta z\): air is about 830 times lighter than water, so a 0.5 m air column changes the pressure by about 6 Pa, against 4905 Pa for water: negligible next to the mercury reading.

4 · Lighter gauge fluid

For the same pressure, does a manometer with gauge oil (SG 0.8) or mercury (SG 13.6) give the larger reading? Which would you use for a pressure of 50 Pa, and which for 50 kPa?

Answer

Reading \(h=\Delta p/(\rho_mg)\): oil gives 17 times the reading. 50 Pa: oil (6.4 mm, mercury would give 0.37 mm, unreadable). 50 kPa: mercury (0.37 m; oil would need 6.4 m of tube).

5 · Storm surge

A typhoon's eye has a pressure 40 hPa below standard. Estimate the open-ocean sea-level rise from pressure alone.

Answer

\(\Delta h=4000/(1000\times9.81)\approx0.41\u{m}\) (about 1 cm per hPa).

6 · Hydraulic lift

The small piston of a lift is pushed with 200 N; the large piston has 50 times its area. What load can it support? If the small piston moves 0.5 m, how far does the load rise?

Answer

\(F_2=200\times50=10\u{kN}\). Volume is conserved: the load rises \(0.5/50=1\u{cm}\).

May 2025 Q16 marksGoing furtherH2.1 pressure & manometer concepts

What fluid properties does the U-tube manometer measure? A variation of the U-tube manometer is the well-tube inclined manometer. Describe at least two advantages of it over the standard U-tube manometer.

Hint

A manometer turns a column height into what? For the inclined version, think about how long the reading is for a given vertical rise, and how many levels you must read.

Answer

Measures: the (gauge) pressure of a fluid at a point, relative to the atmosphere (one limb open), or the pressure difference between two points (differential manometer), from the height difference of a liquid column of known density: \(\Delta p=\rho_mgh\) (minus the system-fluid column).

Advantages of the well-type inclined manometer: (1) greater sensitivity: the reading along the sloping tube is \(L=h/\sin\theta\), magnified by \(1/\sin\theta\), so small pressures are read accurately; (2) only one level to read, because the wide well's level hardly moves (less reading error, scale can be marked directly in pressure units); (3) compact; suits low pressures such as air ducts.

The readings of the mercury manometers are given: \(h_1=3\u{cm}\), \(h_2=30\u{cm}\), and \(h_3=31\u{cm}\). The specific weight of water is 9.81 kN/m³, and the specific gravity of mercury is 13.6. (a) What is the air pressure (gage) \(p_0\) inside the vessel? (b) What is the water level \(H\) inside the vessel?

May 2022 Q113 marksH2.2 manometer chain

A conical container is connected to a U-shaped mercury differential pressure gauge at point \(C\). Injecting water below and up to point \(C\) while keeping the conical container empty, the differential pressure gauge reading is \(\Delta h=0.2\u{m}\). When the conical container is also full of water, what is the reading of the differential pressure gauge? Draw the setup when the conical container is full and clearly indicate how the liquid levels change. The specific gravity of mercury is 13.6.

Hint

The water column \(y\) from C down to the mercury is not given: find it from the first state. Then use the "2x" rule (Section 15).

Answer

1. Empty cone: \(p_C=0\). Balance at the right-limb mercury level: \(\gamma_wy=\gamma_\text{Hg}(0.2)\), so \(y=13.6(0.2)=2.72\u{m}\).

\[\boxed{\Delta h_\text{new}=0.2+2(0.1145)\approx0.429\u{m}}\]
