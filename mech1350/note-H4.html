MECH1350 · Hydrostatics · chapter 4

Buoyancy and flotation

0Where this chapter sits

Put a body in water and it makes a hole. Before the body was there, that hole was full of water, and the water all round held it up. The surrounding water keeps pushing in exactly the same way, so the body gets the same upward push: the weight of the water that used to fill the hole. This chapter uses that one picture again and again.

Sign convention for this chapter
  • Up is positive for vertical forces. Force balance: \(\sum F_\text{up}=\sum F_\text{down}\) for a body at rest (or \(\sum F=ma\) with \(a\) upward positive if it accelerates).
  • In every free-body diagram (FBD) the colours are fixed: weight \(W\) (red, down, at the centre of gravity G), buoyant force \(F_B\) (green, up, at the centre of buoyancy B), cable or rope tension \(T\) (teal, along the cable, pulling away from the body).
  • Pressures are gauge pressures (above atmospheric), as the exam states. Atmospheric pressure acts all round a body and cancels, so it never adds to \(F_B\).
  • Exam values: \(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\), \(\rho_\text{water}=1000\u{kg/m^3}\). If a question gives sea water (e.g. 1027 kg/m³) or a specific weight (e.g. \(\gamma_w=9.81\u{kN/m^3}\)), use the question's value.
  • Moments (sections 9–11): counter-clockwise positive; take moments about the point where an unknown force acts (an anchor or hinge), so that force drops out.

1Archimedes and the king's crown (slides 44–45) ~4 min

What is this?

A kilogram of feathers and a kilogram of sand weigh the same, but the feathers fill a far bigger bag. Gold and silver are the same: a lump of silver is bigger than a lump of gold of the same weight. Push a lump under water in a brim-full vessel and it makes a hole its own size; the water that spills out is exactly the water that used to fill that hole. So the overflow measures the size of the hole, and a crown with silver in it makes a bigger hole than pure gold.

Slide 44 introduces the last topic: Archimedes' principle, one of the earliest laws of mechanics, put forward by the Greek scientist Archimedes around 246 BC, long before anyone could explain it with hydrostatic pressure. (Section 3 gives that explanation.)

Slide 45 ("Eureka!") tells the classic story, quoting the Encyclopaedia Britannica. King Hiero II of Syracuse (the slide spells it "Heiron") had a crown made of what was meant to be pure gold, but suspected the goldsmith had mixed in cheaper silver. Archimedes took a lump of gold and a lump of silver, each equal in weight to the crown. He filled a vessel to the brim with water, put in the silver and measured how much water overflowed; he refilled the vessel and did the same with the gold. The gold displaced less water than the silver. The crown displaced more water than the gold, so it could not be pure gold: it contained silver. The famous scene of Archimedes leaping from his bath shouting "Eureka!" ("I have found it!") is thought to be a later embellishment.

Drawn for these notes. Three lumps of equal weight in brim-full vessels: the denser the material, the smaller its volume, the less water overflows.

Why it works. Equal weights means equal masses \(m\). Volume is \(V=m/\rho\), so the denser material (gold, about 19 300 kg/m³) takes up less space than silver (about 10 500 kg/m³). A body pushed fully under water pushes aside (displaces) exactly its own volume, so the overflow measures the volume. A crown with silver in it has a lower average density, a bigger volume and a bigger overflow.

Example · written for these notes · the crown test in numbers

A 1.000 kg crown is 70% gold and 30% silver by mass. Compare it with 1.000 kg of pure gold. Densities: gold 19 300, silver 10 500 kg/m³.

  1. Pure gold: \(V=1/19\,300\u{m^3}=51.8\u{cm^3}\). (For comparison 1 kg of silver: 95.2 cm³.)
  2. Crown: \(V=0.7/19\,300+0.3/10\,500\u{m^3}\), which is \(64.8\u{cm^3}\). So the crown overflows 13 cm³ (about 25%) more water than the gold: easy to see.
  3. Average density of the crown: \(1/(64.84\times10^{-6})\approx15\,400\u{kg/m^3}\), well below gold.

The same test can be done with a balance instead of overflow (section 4): weighed under water, the gold reads \(9.30\u{N}\) and the crown \(9.17\u{N}\), because the crown's bigger volume gets a bigger upthrust.

You can now: explain why equal weights of different metals displace different volumes of water; turn a mass and a density into a volume and an overflow; say why the crown with silver in it overflows more than pure gold.

2Archimedes' principle (slide 46) ~6 min

What is this?

Picture the water in a still pond and mark out any blob of it, say the shape of a fist. That blob just sits there: the water all round pushes on it, and the pushes add up to exactly its weight, upward. Now take the blob away and put a stone of the same shape in the hole. The surrounding water cannot tell the difference, so it pushes on the stone in exactly the same way. That push is the buoyant force.

Definition · buoyant force, displaced volume

The buoyant force (or upthrust) is the net upward force that a fluid exerts on a body that is partly or wholly inside it. The displaced volume is the volume of fluid the body pushes out of the way: the part of the body that is below the fluid surface (all of it, if the body is fully submerged).

Result · Archimedes' principle (law of buoyancy)

The buoyant force acting on a body immersed in a fluid, whether fully or partially, is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the body, and it acts upward through the centroid of the displaced volume.

\[F_B=\rho_\text{fluid}\,g\,V_\text{displaced}\]

In words: upthrust = density of the fluid × g × volume of the body that is under the surface. It is the weight of the fluid that would fill the hole the body makes.

Reading the formula
\(F_B\)
the push the surrounding fluid gives whatever sits in the hole.
\(V_\text{displaced}\)
the size of the hole: only the part of the body below the surface.
\(\rho_\text{fluid}\)
the density of the fluid that used to fill the hole, not of the body.
\(\rho_\text{fluid}\,g\,V_\text{displaced}\)
mass of that fluid times \(g\): its weight.
"through the centroid"
the fluid in the hole was held up at its own centre, so the push acts at the centre of the hole.

Three points the exam checks:

  • The density in \(F_B\) is the fluid's, never the body's. A steel ball and a wooden ball of the same size, both held under water, get the same upthrust.
  • Only the submerged volume counts. A floating log that is 60% under water has \(V_\text{displaced}=0.6\,V_\text{log}\).
  • The upthrust acts through the centre of buoyancy B: the centroid (geometric centre) of the displaced volume. This is usually not the centre of gravity G of the body: for a half-submerged cylinder floating upright, B is at half the draught, a quarter of the way up the cylinder. Where B is matters as soon as moments come in (sections 9–11). "State the Archimedes principle" has been asked six times in the older course (2–4 marks): say both parts, the size and the line of action.

The "fluid body" argument (slide 46 figure)

What is this? (the fluid body)

This is the pond picture drawn by the slide. The dashed outline is the hole; the blob inside it is the water that used to be there. If the water around can hold that blob still, it gives exactly the same push to any body that takes the blob's place.

The slide's figure shows a solid body under water, and next to it a fluid body of exactly the same shape at the same depth: an imaginary blob of the water itself, outlined by a dashed line. The surrounding water cannot tell the two apart: at every point of the outline the pressure is the same \(\rho g h\), so the total pressure force is the same on both. Now the fluid blob is just water at rest in water: it does not sink or rise, so the pressure force on it must exactly balance its own weight. Hence the pressure force on either shape is upward and equal to the weight of a blob of water of that shape. The balance also tells us where it acts: for the fluid blob, the upthrust must pass through the blob's centre of gravity C (otherwise the blob would start to spin), and C of a uniform blob is the centroid of its volume.

Slide 46, redrawn. Left: a solid body; its weight W acts at its own centre of gravity, the buoyant force \(F_B\) at the centroid C of the displaced volume. Right: a fluid body of the same shape: \(F_B\) is identical and here exactly balances the fluid body's weight \(W\), equal and opposite.

Example · written for these notes

A sealed box of volume \(0.02\u{m^3}\) is held fully under water. \(F_B=1000\times9.81\times0.02=196\u{N}\), whatever the box is made of or contains. In sea water (\(1025\u{kg/m^3}\)) it would be 201 N; in air (\(1.2\u{kg/m^3}\)) only 0.24 N, which is why buoyancy in air is usually ignored (section 4).

You can now: state Archimedes' principle in full, size and line of action; use the fluid's density and the submerged volume in \(F_B\); place the upthrust at the centroid of the displaced volume, not at G.

3Where the buoyant force comes from (slide 47) ~5 min

What is this?

Swim to the bottom of a pool and you feel the water pressing harder on your ears. The water in the hole feels the same: its bottom is deeper than its top, so the water underneath pushes up on it harder than the water above pushes down. The sideways pushes on its sides cancel. What is left over is an upward push, just big enough to hold up the water in the hole.

With hydrostatics (H2) we can now explain the upthrust instead of just stating it. Pressure grows with depth. The bottom of a submerged body is deeper than its top, so the water pushes up on the bottom harder than it pushes down on the top. The difference is an upward force: the buoyant force. The sideways pushes on the vertical sides cancel in pairs (same depth, opposite directions).

A box-shaped body

Take a block with horizontal top and bottom faces of area \(A\), height \(a\), its top at depth \(h\) (slide 47's first figure: arrows pointing down on the top face, up on the bottom face, and inward on the sides).

Pressure arrows on a submerged block: longer at the bottom.

Free-body diagram: the two face forces; their difference is \(F_B\).

the upthrust on a block from the pressures

1. Top face, depth \(h\): pressure \(\rho g h\), force down \(F_\text{top}=\rho g h A\). Force = pressure × area; pressure is uniform on a horizontal face (H3).

2. Bottom face, depth \(h+a\): force up \(F_\text{bottom}=\rho g(h+a)A\). Deeper, so bigger.

3. Side faces: each horizontal force has an equal and opposite partner on the opposite face. Same areas and the same pressures at each depth.

4. Net upward force:

\[\begin{aligned}F_B&=F_\text{bottom}-F_\text{top}\\&=\rho g(h+a)A-\rho g hA\\&=\rho g\,(aA)=\rho g V.\end{aligned}\]

5. The depth \(h\) has cancelled: the upthrust does not depend on how deep the body is (for a liquid of constant density). Both face forces grow with depth at the same rate.

Any shape (slide 47's second figure)

What is this? (any shape)

For a curved body, think of two stacks of water. The push down on the top of the body is the weight of the stack sitting on its upper surface. The push up on its bottom is as big as the weight of a stack reaching from its lower surface right up to the free surface, as if the body were not there. Take the first stack from the second and what is left is a body-shaped stack: the water that would fill the hole.

For a curved body, split its surface into an upper surface (surface 1, facing up) and a lower surface (surface 2, facing down), along the widest outline seen from above. From H3 (curved surfaces), the vertical force on a curved surface equals the weight of the fluid vertically above it, up to the free surface:

  • On surface 1 the water pushes down with \(F_V(1)\) = weight of the water column above surface 1.
  • On surface 2 the water pushes up with \(F_V(2)\) = weight of the (partly imaginary) water column above surface 2: everything up to the free surface, including the space the body occupies.
Result · upthrust as a difference of two vertical forces \[F_B=F_V(2)-F_V(1)=\rho g V_\text{body}\]

In words: the column above the lower surface minus the column above the upper surface leaves exactly a body-shaped column of water: its weight is the upthrust.

Reading the formula
\(F_V(2)\)
the upward push on the lower surface: the weight of a column from that surface up to the free surface, the body's own space included.
\(F_V(1)\)
the downward push on the upper surface: the weight of the water really sitting above it.
\(F_V(2)-F_V(1)\)
the two columns share everything above the body, so that part cancels.
\(\rho g V_\text{body}\)
what is left: the weight of a body-shaped piece of water, the water of the hole.

Slide 47's second figure, redrawn: hatched columns above surface 1 (pushing down) and above surface 2 (pushing up). The difference is the body-shaped region.

Example · written for these notes · checking the principle

A cube of side 0.2 m hangs in water with its top face 0.5 m below the surface. Top force \(1000\times9.81\times0.5\times0.04=196.2\u{N}\) down; bottom force \(1000\times9.81\times0.7\times0.04=274.7\u{N}\) up. Difference \(78.5\u{N}\) up, which equals \(\rho g V=1000\times9.81\times0.008=78.5\u{N}\). Move it to 2 m deep: both forces grow by the same amount and \(F_B\) is still 78.5 N.

Watch

A body sitting flat on the bottom with no water underneath (a suction cup, a block bedded in mud) gets no upward push on its base, so the usual upthrust does not act; the water then presses it down. Archimedes' principle assumes the fluid surrounds the submerged part.

You can now: explain the upthrust as the bottom force minus the top force; show with a block that the depth cancels; explain the curved-body case with two columns of water; say why a body bedded flat on the bottom gets no upthrust.

4Apparent weight and submerged density (slide 48) ~6 min

What is this?

Lift a heavy stone under water in a river and it feels lighter. Its weight has not changed; the water round it is simply pushing up, just as hard as it pushed on the water that used to fill the stone's hole. So the water carries part of the weight, and your hand (or a spring balance) supplies only the rest. That rest is the apparent weight.

Definition · apparent weight

The apparent weight \(W_\text{app}\) of an immersed body is the weight it seems to have: the reading of a spring balance holding it, or the force you need to hold it up. Because the fluid pushes up with \(F_B\), a body weighs less when immersed in a fluid.

Result · apparent weight of a fully immersed body \[F_B=\rho_\text{fluid}\,g\,V_\text{body}\] \[\begin{aligned}W_\text{app}&=W-F_B\\&=(\rho_\text{body}-\rho_\text{fluid})\,g\,V_\text{body}\end{aligned}\]

In words: apparent weight = true weight − upthrust = (body density − fluid density) × g × volume.

Reading the formula
\(W\)
the true weight: gravity's pull on the body, the same in air or water.
\(F_B\)
the share the water carries: the weight of the water that would fill the hole.
\(W-F_B\)
what is left for the balance or your hand.
\(\rho_\text{body}-\rho_\text{fluid}\)
how much heavier the body is than the fluid it replaced, per cubic metre.

The handout then defines the submerged density \(\rho'=\rho_\text{body}-\rho_\text{fluid}\), so that

\[W_\text{app}=\rho'\,g\,V_\text{body}\]

In words: under water a body behaves as if its density were reduced by the fluid's density. A rock of density 2600 kg/m³ "feels" like 1600 kg/m³ under water, which is why stones are easy to lift in a river.

A body hung from a spring balance in water.

FBD of the body: \(T+F_B=W\), so the reading \(T=W-F_B=W_\text{app}\).

Partly immersed. If only part of the body is under the surface (held by a balance), replace \(V_\text{body}\) in \(F_B\) by the submerged volume: \(W_\text{app}=\rho_\text{body}gV_\text{body}-\rho_\text{fluid}gV_\text{sub}\). Several older-course questions use "half submerged".

Buoyancy in air is usually ignored, because air is about 800 times less dense than water: a weight measured in air can be taken as the true weight. The exception is when the body itself is a gas or holds a large volume of gas (a balloon, an airship): then the air's upthrust is the whole story (section 7 and the balloon in section 8).

Example · written for these notes

A 2.0 kg block of aluminium (\(\rho=2700\u{kg/m^3}\)) hangs fully under water from a spring balance. Volume \(V=2/2700=7.407\times10^{-4}\u{m^3}\). True weight \(W=2\times9.81=19.62\u{N}\). Upthrust

\[\begin{aligned}F_B&=1000\times9.81\times7.407\times10^{-4}\\&=7.27\u{N}\end{aligned}\]

The balance reads

\[W_\text{app}=19.62-7.27=12.35\u{N}\]

Check with the submerged density: \(\rho'=1700\), \(1700\times9.81\times7.407\times10^{-4}=12.35\u{N}\).

Past-paper pattern: density from a ratio of weights

The older course (ENGG1205) liked questions that give apparent weights only as fractions of the true weight. The volume is never given, and it always cancels. Recipe:

  1. Do: write \(W=\rho_b gV\) and \(F_B=\rho_f gV_\text{sub}\) for each situation. The weight never changes; only the size of the hole (full, half) changes from one situation to the next.
  2. Do: put the stated ratio into an equation. The ratio is the only number given, so it is the one equation that links the two densities.
  3. Do: cancel \(gV\), solve. The weight and the upthrust are both proportional to the body's volume, so the unknown size drops out and only densities are left.
Past paper · ENGG1205 2015–16 Sem 2 Q2(a) · 6 marks

"The apparent weight of an object when completely submerged in water is half that when half submerged in water. What is the density of this object?"

the density from the two apparent weights

1. Fully submerged: \(W_1=(\rho_s-\rho_w)gV\). Upthrust uses the whole volume.

2. Half submerged: \(W_2=\rho_sgV-\tfrac12\rho_wgV\). Only half the volume displaces water.

3. Given \(W_1=\tfrac12W_2\); cancel \(gV\):

\[\begin{aligned}\rho_s-\rho_w&=\tfrac12\rho_s-\tfrac14\rho_w\\ \tfrac12\rho_s&=\tfrac34\rho_w\end{aligned}\]

4. \(\rho_s=1.5\rho_w\). \(\rho_w=1000\).

\(\boxed{\rho=1500\u{kg/m^3}}\)

Past paper · ENGG1205 2015–16 Sem 1 Q1(g) · 6 marks

"An object, when completely submerged in water, has an apparent weight equal to two-thirds of its true weight. When completely submerged in another fluid, the apparent weight becomes half the true weight. What is the relative density of this fluid?"

In water: \((\rho_s-\rho_w)gV=\tfrac23\rho_sgV\), so \(\rho_w=\tfrac13\rho_s\). In the fluid: \((\rho_s-\rho_f)gV=\tfrac12\rho_sgV\), so \(\rho_f=\tfrac12\rho_s\). Divide:

\[\text{RD}=\frac{\rho_f}{\rho_w}=\frac{1/2}{1/3}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\boxed{\text{RD}=1.5}\]

Sense check: the body loses more weight in the fluid (half) than in water (a third), so the fluid is denser than water.

Past paper · ENGG1205 2013–14 Sem 1 Q1(g) · 4 marks

"When half submerged in water, a spherical object of homogeneous material will have an apparent weight equal to two-thirds of its weight in air. What is the relative density of the material of the object?"

\(W-F_B=\tfrac23W\) with \(W=\rho_sgV\), \(F_B=\rho_wg\tfrac V2\): \(\rho_s-\tfrac12\rho_w=\tfrac23\rho_s\), so \(\tfrac13\rho_s=\tfrac12\rho_w\) and \(\boxed{\text{RD}=\rho_s/\rho_w=1.5}\).

You can now: find the apparent weight of a fully or partly immersed body; use the submerged density \(\rho'\); find a density or relative density from ratios of apparent weights.

5Float or sink? (slide 49) ~4 min

What is this?

Hold a body under water and let go. Gravity pulls it down with its weight; the water pushes it up with the weight of the water that used to fill its hole. The hole is the same size as the body, so the contest is simply: is the body heavier or lighter than the same volume of water? Heavier, it sinks. Lighter, it rises until part of it sticks out. Equal, it hovers.

Let go of a body fully under the surface. Two forces act: its weight \(\rho_\text{body}gV\) down and the upthrust \(\rho_\text{fluid}gV\) up. The same \(gV\) appears in both, so the comparison is simply between the two densities:

CaseDensitiesApparent weightDisplaced volume
1. Sinks\(\rho_\text{body}\gt\rho_\text{fluid}\)\(W_\text{app}\gt0\)\(V_\text{displaced}=V_\text{body}\)
2. Neutrally buoyant (stays wherever it is put, "suspended")\(\rho_\text{body}=\rho_\text{fluid}\)\(W_\text{app}=0\)\(V_\text{displaced}=V_\text{body}\)
3. Floats (rises until part sticks out)\(\rho_\text{body}\lt\rho_\text{fluid}\)\(W_\text{app}=0\)\(V_\text{displaced}\lt V_\text{body}\)

A floating body has zero apparent weight: it needs no support, the upthrust alone holds it. It rises until just enough volume is under water for the upthrust to equal its weight (section 6). For water: any material with a relative density above 1 sinks in water; below 1 it floats. "Body density" means the average density of the whole body, including any air inside it: a steel ship floats because its hull encloses a lot of air.

Slide 49's figure, redrawn: a floating body (\(\rho\lt\rho_f\)), a suspended, neutrally buoyant body (\(\rho=\rho_f\)) and a sinking body (\(\rho\gt\rho_f\)) in the same fluid.

Result · fraction of a floating body below the surface \[\frac{V_\text{sub}}{V_\text{body}}=\frac{\rho_\text{body}}{\rho_\text{fluid}}\]

In words: a floating body sinks in until the fraction under the surface equals the ratio of its density to the fluid's. Ice (917 kg/m³) in sea water (1025 kg/m³): 89.5% under, "the tip of the iceberg" is about a tenth.

Reading the formula
\(V_\text{sub}/V_\text{body}\)
the fraction of the body that is inside its hole, below the surface.
\(\rho_\text{body}/\rho_\text{fluid}\)
how heavy the body is compared with the same volume of fluid.
\(=\)
a floater settles where its hole is just big enough for the fluid in it to weigh what the body weighs.

Try it · sink, float or hover

A 0.1 m³ block released in a deep fluid. The readout gives its weight, the upthrust when fully under, its apparent weight, and (if it floats) the fraction below the surface. Try 917 in 1025 (an iceberg), 1000 in 1000 (neutral), 2700 is off the scale: aluminium always sinks.

"An object weighs less in a denser liquid"

What is this? (a denser liquid)

Same body, same hole, but now the hole used to be full of a heavier liquid. The liquid round it was holding up that heavier filling, so it pushes up harder on the body. The balance has less left to carry.

From \(W_\text{app}=(\rho_\text{body}-\rho_\text{fluid})gV\): the same body, fully submerged, has a smaller apparent weight in the denser liquid, because the denser liquid gives a bigger upthrust. This is the review sheet's phrase, and it was tested as a true/false statement in Dec 2024 (see the exam taster).

Watch

Do not reverse it. "Weighs more in liquid A than in liquid B" means the upthrust is smaller in A, so A is less dense than B.

You can now: decide sink, hover or float from the two densities; find the fraction of a floating body below the surface; say correctly in which liquid a body weighs less.

6The law of flotation (slide 50) ~8 min

What is this?

Lower a boat gently into the water. At first its hole is small, and the water that would fill it weighs less than the boat, so the boat sinks deeper. The hole grows as it sinks. It stops when the water that would fill the hole weighs exactly as much as the boat. Load cargo and it sinks further, until the extra hole holds the cargo's weight of water.

Result · law of flotation

A floating object displaces a weight of fluid equal to its own weight.

\[\rho_\text{body}\,g\,V_\text{body}=\rho_\text{fluid}\,g\,V_\text{sub}\]

In words: for anything floating freely, weight of the body (plus whatever it carries) = weight of fluid pushed aside = upthrust.

Reading the formula
\(\rho_\text{body}\,g\,V_\text{body}\)
the weight of the whole floating body (and anything it carries).
\(V_\text{sub}\)
the hole: only the part below the waterline.
\(\rho_\text{fluid}\,g\,V_\text{sub}\)
the weight of the fluid that would fill the hole: the upthrust.
\(=\)
floating freely, only these two forces act, so they must balance.

It is a corollary (a direct consequence) of Archimedes' principle: a freely floating body is in equilibrium under only two forces, so \(W=F_B\), and \(F_B\) is the weight of displaced fluid. The slide adds three remarks:

  • An object floats in a fluid only when it is lighter (less dense) on average than the fluid (section 5).
  • An object can only displace as much as its own weight of fluid when freely floating. Push it down further and it displaces more, but then your push is an extra force (section 8).
  • The displacement or displacement tonnage of a ship is its weight. The slide's example: a 10 000 lb vessel displaces about 156 ft³ (4.42 m³) of sea water. Check: sea water weighs about 64 lb per ft³, and \(10\,000/64=156\u{ft^3}\); in SI, 10 000 lb is 4536 kg and \(4536/1025\approx4.42\u{m^3}\).

Draught and cargo

For a body with vertical sides (a box-shaped barge, a cylinder floating upright) the displaced volume is (waterplane area) × (draught), so the law of flotation gives the draught directly, and a change in draught gives a change in load:

\[\Delta W=\rho_f\,g\,A_\text{wp}\,\Delta h\]

In words: extra load = weight of the extra slice of water displaced = specific weight × waterplane area × extra sinkage.

Reading the formula
\(A_\text{wp}\)
the footprint of the hull where it cuts the water surface.
\(\Delta h\)
how much deeper the body sits after loading.
\(A_\text{wp}\,\Delta h\)
the extra slice added to the hole.
\(\rho_f\,g\,A_\text{wp}\,\Delta h\)
the weight of water that would fill that slice, which must equal the extra load.

Gas bubbles and the "Bermuda Triangle" hypothesis (slide 51)

What is this? (gas bubbles)

Now fill the water under a ship with bubbles. The ship's hole is the same size, but the frothy water that would fill it is lighter. The froth round the ship can only hold up as much as that lighter filling, so the push on the ship falls and may drop below its weight.

The last slide applies the principle to a mystery. If a massive release of gas bubbles (for example an underwater gas eruption) happened under a ship, the bubbly water's average density could drop a lot. The upthrust \(\rho_\text{fluid}gV_\text{sub}\) would fall, possibly below the ship's weight, even with the whole hull submerged, and the ship would sink. This is only a hypothesis for the disappearance of ships in the Bermuda Triangle; there is no solid evidence for it so far.

Example · written for these notes

Water with 10% gas by volume has density about \(0.9\times1025+0.1\times1.2\approx923\u{kg/m^3}\). A ship that floats in clear sea water with 95% of its hull volume under water would need \(0.95\times1025/923=1.055\), i.e. 105.5% of its hull, to float in the bubbly water: impossible, so it sinks.

7The handout's concept questions (Some Concept Questions, pages 9–13) ~3 min

What is this?

Every question here is the hole picture in disguise. A floating iceberg makes a hole that holds exactly its own weight of water; when it melts, that same weight of water fills the hole again, so the level does not move. A gas lighter than air is pushed up by the air round it, like a cork in water. A porous material with its pores full of air weighs its solid part plus that air, which is more than the air that would fill its hole.

Melting of an iceberg (pages 9–10)

"Some people say that the sea level will rise as a result of the melting of an iceberg. How true is this statement?" The handout's answer: the statement is NOT true, and asks you to explain why.

Answer

A floating iceberg already displaces its own weight of water (law of flotation). When it melts, its mass does not change, and the melted water has the density of water, so it takes up exactly the volume of water that the floating ice was displacing. It fills its own "hole": the water level stays the same.

the argument with symbols

1. Floating ice of mass \(m\) displaces \(V_d=m/\rho_w\). Law of flotation: \(\rho_wgV_d=mg\).

2. Melted, the same mass occupies \(V_\text{melt}=m/\rho_w\). Same mass, now as water.

3. \(V_\text{melt}=V_d\): no change of level. The ice above the waterline is exactly the extra volume ice has over its meltwater.

Beyond the handouts · two refinements

(1) An iceberg is frozen fresh water floating in salt water. The meltwater (1000 kg/m³) is a little less dense than the sea water (1025 kg/m³) it was displacing, so it takes about 2.5% more volume than the "hole": a very slight rise. (2) Ice resting on land (Greenland, Antarctica) is not floating; when it melts or slides into the sea it does raise the sea level. That is the real concern in climate change.

Hydrogen (pages 11–12)

"Hydrogen is a dangerous gas. Fortunately, it does not exist in normal air. In terms of mechanics, explain why?"

Answer

(Handout answer.) Hydrogen is the lightest gas, with a density about 7.3% of the density of air (0.090 against 1.225 kg/m³). Anything lighter than air is lifted up by buoyancy, and keeps rising until it reaches an altitude where the air's density equals its own. So any hydrogen released rises out of the air we breathe, to the top of the atmosphere.

A material lighter than air? (page 13)

The page quotes a Ming Pao news report (in Chinese): researchers made an ultralight all-carbon aerogel, "carbon sponge", so light that it rests on the stamen of a flower without bending it; its density is only 0.16 mg per cubic centimetre, about one sixth of the density of air, a world record. The English text calls it aerographite, "the lightest material in the world: its density is one sixth of density of air! Even the petals of a flower can support a sizeable piece of the material." Question: "The material is lighter than air. Why it does not float in air?"

Answer

The quoted density counts only the solid carbon skeleton spread over the whole volume. The material is almost all empty pores, and in air those pores are full of air. The upthrust is the weight of air displaced by the outside volume, \(\rho_\text{air}gV\); the weight is the carbon plus the air inside, about \((0.16+1.2)gV\). Average density about 1.36 kg/m³ is greater than air's 1.2, so it does not float; its apparent weight is just the carbon's weight, \(0.16\,gV\) (tiny, which is why a flower can hold it up). It would float only if the pores held a vacuum or a light gas with a rigid, sealed skin, like a balloon.

0.16 mg/cm³ = 0.16 kg/m³, which is about 1/7.5 of air (1.2 kg/m³); "one sixth" on the page is a rounded figure. The answer does not depend on it.

8Tethered and hanging bodies: the force balance ~10 min

What is this?

Push a beach ball under the water in a pool and tie it to the bottom with a string. Its hole is now much bigger than when it floated, so the water pushes up far harder than the ball weighs. The string takes the difference. Turn it round for a stone hanging from a rope: the water carries part of its weight and the rope carries the rest.

The handout states the principle; the exam turns it into a free-body diagram problem. A body held by a cable (tied down from below, or hung from above) is in equilibrium under three vertical forces: weight, upthrust and tension. This is the most frequent buoyancy question in Section B (Dec 2019 to Dec 2024).

Ready method · body + cable + fluid
  1. Do: Sketch and FBD. Draw the body alone. Weight \(W\) down at G. Upthrust \(F_B=\rho_fgV_\text{sub}\) up at B. Tension \(T\) along the cable, pulling away from the body: down if the cable goes to the sea bed, up if the body hangs from a crane. These are the only vertical forces: gravity, the surrounding water's push on the hole, and the cable. The sideways water pushes cancel.
  2. Do: Volumes. \(V_\text{sub}\) = the part below the surface (whole body if fully submerged). Use the fluid named in the question (sea water 1027, not 1000). The upthrust is the weight of what would fill the hole, so you need the hole's size and the fluid that would fill it.
  3. Do: Balance. \(\sum F_\text{up}=\sum F_\text{down}\). Tied down: \(F_B=W+T\). Hanging: \(T+F_B=W\). The body is at rest, so the upward pushes and the downward pulls must cancel.
  4. Do: Two unknowns? Use two states (two depths, two tensions) and subtract, or take two bodies and write one balance each (or one for the whole system: internal cable forces cancel). Each state or body gives one equation. The weight is the same in both states, so subtracting leaves only what changes with the size of the hole.
  5. Do: Check the sign: a negative tension means the cable would have to push, i.e. it goes slack. A cable can only pull. If the balance needs a push, the body would really float up until the cable hangs loose.
Result · the two common balances \[\text{tied down: }T=F_B-W\] \[\text{hung from above: }T=W-F_B=W_\text{app}\]

In words: a cable holding a floater down carries the excess upthrust; a cable holding a sinker up carries its apparent weight.

Reading the formula
\(F_B\)
the push of the water on the hole; for a tied-down floater the hole is bigger than when it floated freely.
\(F_B-W\)
the part of that push the weight cannot balance: the cable to the bed must hold it down.
\(W-F_B\)
the part of the weight the water does not carry: the cable from above must hold it up.
\(W_\text{app}\)
the same number a spring balance would read (section 4).

What is this? (two states)

Pull a tethered floater deeper by shortening its cable. Each extra metre of depth adds one more slice to its hole, and for a body with vertical sides every slice holds the same weight of water. The weight has not changed, so the cable takes all of the extra push. Equal steps in depth give equal steps in tension: a straight line.

The commonest exam form gives a floating cylinder tied to the bed, with the cable tension at two depths of submergence \(h_1, h_2\), and asks for the cylinder's size and weight. In words: the extra tension equals the weight of the extra water displaced (this gives \(A\)); then any one state gives \(W\).

Reading the formula

\(T_2-T_1\)
the extra pull in the cable; the weight is unchanged, so the cable takes the whole extra push.
\(\rho gAh_1-T_1\)

9A floating body with moments: the anchored timber ~3 min

What is this?

Hold one end of a long wooden stick under water and let the other end poke out. Gravity pulls down at the middle of the stick. The water pushes up at the middle of the hole, and the hole is only the wet part, so that push acts nearer your hand. The two forces are not on one line, so they try to turn the stick; the balance of turning effects about your hand decides how far it sticks out.

When the forces on a body do not all act along one vertical line, force balance is not enough: the moments must balance too. This is where "the upthrust acts through the centroid of the displaced volume" (section 2) earns marks. The older course asked it twice with the same figure.

10Stability of a floating body: the metacentre ~9 min

What is this?

Watch a boat rock. As it heels over, one side dips deeper and the other rises, so its hole in the water changes shape: it grows on the low side and shrinks on the high side. The centre of the hole, where the upthrust acts, slides towards the low side. The weight still acts at G, which stays put in the boat. Whether the boat comes back upright depends on which way this pair of forces turns it.

Previous syllabus — not in the 2026 syllabus

Previous syllabus · why this is here

No 2026 handout or the Dec 2026 review sheet mentions stability or the metacentre, but three ENGG1350 parts asked for it (Dec 2018 Q3(b), May 2024 Q2(b), May 2025 Q4(b), 6–8 marks each), and the papers printed the formula \(MB=I/V_d\). It is taught here in full in case it returns; the solutions are on guide-prev.html · H4.4.

Definition · stable, unstable, neutral

A body in equilibrium is stable if, after a small disturbance (here a small tilt, called heel), the forces produce a moment that turns it back; unstable if the moment turns it further over; neutral if no moment arises and it stays in the tilted position. Think of a ball in a bowl (stable), on top of a dome (unstable), on a flat table (neutral).

The problem. A floating body has only two forces: \(W\) down through G and \(F_B=W\) up through B. Upright, they lie on one vertical line, so there is no moment. Tilt the body by a small angle \(\theta\): G stays put in the body, but the shape of the displaced volume changes (a wedge of hull comes out of the water on one side and a wedge goes in on the other), so B moves sideways towards the lower side, to B′. The two forces now form a couple. Which way does it turn?

Drawn for these notes. Left: upright; G above B on the centreline. Right: tilted by \(\theta\). B shifts to B′; the vertical through B′ meets the centreline at the metacentre M. Here M is above G, so \(W\) and \(F_B\) form a couple that turns the body back upright.

What is this? (metacentre)

For small rocking, the boat swings as if it hung from a pivot at M, like a pendulum. If M is above the weight at G, the boat hangs below its pivot and swings back upright. If M is below G, the boat is balanced on top of its pivot and falls further over. A wide hull moves the centre of the hole a long way for a small tilt, which puts M high.

Definition · metacentre M, metacentric height GM

The metacentre M is the point where the vertical line through the new centre of buoyancy B′ crosses the body's centreline (for small tilts it does not depend on \(\theta\)). The metacentric height GM is the distance from G up to M. K denotes the keel, the lowest point of the body, used as the reference for heights.

Result · metacentric height and stability \[BM=\frac{I}{V_d}\] \[GM=KB+BM-KG\]

\(GM\gt0\) (M above G): stable. \(GM\lt0\): unstable. \(GM=0\): neutral. The righting moment for a small tilt is \(W\cdot GM\sin\theta\).

In words: BM = second moment of area of the waterplane (the body's cross-section at the waterline) about the tilt axis ÷ displaced volume. The body is stable if the metacentre is above the centre of gravity.

Reading the formula
\(KB\)
the height of the centre of the hole above the keel.
\(BM=I/V_d\)
how high above B the new upthrust line crosses the centreline after a small rock: large for a wide waterplane (big \(I\)), small for a big hole (big \(V_d\)).
\(KG\)
the height of G, where the weight acts, above the keel.
\(GM\)
how far the pivot M is above G. Positive: the boat swings back.
why BM = I / V_d

1. Tilt by a small \(\theta\) about the waterplane's centre line (axis along the body). Take a strip of waterplane at distance \(y\) from the axis, area \(\mathrm dA\). For small tilts the axis passes through the waterplane's centroid.

2. The strip's waterline moves by \(y\theta\): on one side it is immersed by \(y\theta\), on the other it emerges. Volume moved: \(y\theta\,\mathrm dA\). Small angle: \(\tan\theta\approx\theta\).

3. Moving volume \(y\theta\,\mathrm dA\) from \(-y\) to \(+y\) shifts the centroid of the whole displaced volume sideways by

\[BB'=\frac{\int y\,(y\theta)\,\mathrm dA}{V_d}=\frac{\theta\,I}{V_d}\]

Moment of the moved volume ÷ total volume; \(\int y^2\mathrm dA=I\), the second moment of the waterplane about the tilt axis (H3).

4. In the figure, \(BB'=BM\,\theta\) (M is where the new vertical through B′ meets the centreline). So \(BM=I/V_d\). Arc of radius BM through the small angle \(\theta\).

5. The couple: \(W\) at G and \(F_B\) at B′ are a horizontal distance \(GM\sin\theta\) apart, so the moment is \(W\,GM\sin\theta\), righting if M is above G. Lever arm measured from G to the vertical through M.

Recipe

  1. Do: Draught \(d\) from the law of flotation (section 6). \(V_d\) = submerged volume. The draught fixes the hole: its depth and its volume.
  2. Do: \(KB\): centroid of the displaced volume above the keel. For a box or upright cylinder, \(KB=d/2\). B is the centre of the hole; with vertical sides the hole is a box or a cylinder, so its centre is halfway down.
  3. Do: \(KG\): centre of gravity above the keel. Uniform solid of height \(H\): \(KG=H/2\); otherwise given. G is where the weight acts, and it does not move when the boat rocks.
  4. Do: \(I\) of the waterplane about the axis it tilts about. Use the smallest \(I\) (the body tips the easiest way). Rectangle \(l\times b\) with \(b\lt l\): \(I_\text{min}=lb^3/12\) (the short side cubed). Circle: \(\pi D^4/64\). A boat rocks most easily across its narrow width, so it must be stable for that tilt.
  5. Do: \(BM=I/V_d\); \(GM=KB+BM-KG\); decide by its sign. This places the pivot M relative to G: above G the boat swings back, below G it falls over.

11Stability of a suspended, submerged body ~5 min

What is this?

A fully submerged body hanging from a cable makes a hole exactly its own shape, so the centre of the hole B sits at the body's centre G. Weight and upthrust then act at the same point and combine into one net pull downward. Hang the body from a point above G and it is a pendulum: tilt it and it swings back.

Previous syllabus — not in the 2026 syllabus

Previous syllabus · the hanging plate

Dec 2021 Q3(c) (same as May 2019) and Dec 2022 Q3(c), 4 marks each, ask whether the hanging plate of section 8 is stable. The stable/unstable/neutral classification is not taught in 2026, but the answer is just a moment balance after a small tilt, which the review sheet does cover ("balance of forces and moments"). Solutions on guide-prev.html · H4.5.

A body hanging from a cable is like a pendulum. Three forces act: \(T\) at the attachment point P (pulling up along the cable), \(W\) at G, \(F_B\) at B. For a uniform, fully submerged body, B and G coincide (the displaced volume is the body itself), so \(W\) and \(F_B\) combine into one net downward force \(W-F_B=W_\text{app}\) at G.

Result · suspended body
  • Attachment point P above G (and B): stable. A small tilt swings G sideways; the net downward force at G has a moment about P that swings it back, like a pendulum.
  • P at G (body pivoted through its centre): neutral. No lever arm, no moment; it stays at any angle.
  • P below G (body balanced on top of a support): unstable. The moment turns it further over.

In words: hang it from above its centre and it hangs steady; the restoring moment after a tilt \(\theta\) is \((W-F_B)\times PG\times\sin\theta\).

Reading the formula
\(W-F_B\)
the net downward pull at G: the apparent weight.
\(PG\)
the distance from the hanging point P to G: the length of the pendulum.
\(PG\sin\theta\)
how far G swings sideways from below P after a tilt \(\theta\): the lever arm.
product
force × lever arm: the moment that swings the body back.

Hanging upright: \(T\), \(W\), \(F_B\) on one vertical line.

Tilted by \(\theta\) about P: \(W-F_B\) at G now has a lever arm about P and turns the plate back.

Past paper · Dec 2021 Q3(c) · 4 marks (same as May 2019 Q3(c)); Dec 2022 Q3(c) the same with a 3 m plate

"The plate is assumed to be in a vertical position as shown. Is it stable, unstable or of neutral stability? Why?"

Answer: stable. The plate is uniform and fully submerged, so its centre of gravity G and centre of buoyancy B are both at its centre, 2 m (Dec 2022: 1.5 m) below the cable attachment at the top edge. Its weight exceeds the upthrust (\(\rho_\text{plate}\gt\rho_\text{sea}\)), so the net force \(W-F_B\) at G acts down, below the point of suspension. If the plate tilts slightly, this net force acts at a horizontal distance from the cable line and produces a moment about the attachment point that rotates the plate back to vertical, so the equilibrium is stable. (Dec 2022 numbers: \(W-F_B=1993\u{N}\) at 1.5 m below P; a 5° tilt gives a restoring moment \(1993\times1.5\times\sin5^\circ\approx261\u{N\,m}\).)

You can now: say whether a hanging, fully submerged body is stable, neutral or unstable from where it is attached relative to G; explain the answer as a pendulum with the net force \(W-F_B\) acting at G.

13Common mistakes

✗Using the body's density in \(F_B\). ✓\(F_B=\rho_\text{fluid}gV_\text{sub}\): the fluid's density, the body's submerged volume. ✗Using the whole volume for a floating body's upthrust. ✓Only the part below the surface displaces fluid. ✗Using 1000 kg/m³ when the question gives sea water (1027). ✓Use the density of the fluid the body is actually in. ✗Drawing the cable tension in the wrong direction. ✓A cable only pulls: away from the body, along the cable (down to an anchor, up to a crane). ✗Putting the upthrust at the body's centre of gravity when it is partly submerged. ✓\(F_B\) acts at the centroid of the displaced (submerged) volume. ✗"Weighs more in A" read as "A is denser". ✓More apparent weight means less upthrust: A is less dense. ✗Thinking a deeper body gets more upthrust. ✓In a liquid of constant density \(F_B\) does not depend on depth once fully submerged. ✗Mixing kN and N, or \(\gamma\) and \(\rho\). ✓\(\gamma=\rho g\): 9.81 kN/m³ is the same as 1000 kg/m³ × 9.81 m/s². ✗(Prev) Using the largest \(I\), or \(I\) of the body's vertical section. ✓\(I\) of the waterplane about the axis it tips about: the smallest one (\(lb^3/12\), short side cubed).

1 · A student's Dec 2022 Q3(b)

"Steel plate 3 m × 2 m × 5 mm, density 7800 kg/m³, fully submerged in ocean water of density 1027 kg/m³, hung from a cable. \(V=0.03\u{m^3}\). \(W=7800\times9.81\times0.03=2295.5\u{N}\). \(F_B=1000\times9.81\times0.03=294.3\u{N}\). Tension \(T=W+F_B=2589.8\u{N}\)."

Hint

Two mistakes: which density goes in \(F_B\), and which way does the upthrust push?

Answer

The plate is in ocean water: \(F_B=1027\times9.81\times0.03=302.3\u{N}\) (the 1000 at the head of the paper is the default; the question overrides it). And the upthrust helps the cable: \(T+F_B=W\), so \[\begin{aligned}T&=2295.5-302.3\\&=1993.3\u{N}\approx1.99\u{kN}\end{aligned}\] Adding \(F_B\) would mean the water pulls the plate down.

2 · A student's floating cylinder

"A cylinder of relative density 0.6, height 2 m, floats upright. Weight = upthrust: \(0.6\rho_wgAH=\rho_wgAH\), which is impossible, so it must sink."

Hint

Which volume displaces water when it floats?

Answer

Only the submerged length \(L\) displaces water: \(0.6\rho_wgA(2)=\rho_wgAL\), so \(L=1.2\u{m}\). Setting the whole volume in \(F_B\) assumes it is fully under water, which is only true for a sinking or held-down body.

3 · A student's anchored timber

"Timber 10 m long, 8 m under water, anchored at A. Moments about A: \(W\times5=F_B\times5\), so \(\rho_s\times10=\rho_w\times8\) and \(\rho_s=800\u{kg/m^3}\)."

Hint

Where does the upthrust act?

Answer

At the centroid of the submerged 8 m, 4 m from A, not at mid-length: \(\rho_s(10)(5)=\rho_w(8)(4)\), \(\rho_s=640\u{kg/m^3}\). (800 would be right only if both forces acted at the same point, and then the moment equation would say nothing.)

15Concept checks

Written for these notes.

1 · Into the river

A ship sails from the sea (1025 kg/m³) into a fresh-water river. Does it float higher or lower? By how much does its displaced volume change?

Answer

Lower. Its weight is unchanged, so it must displace the same weight of a less dense fluid: \(V_\text{river}/V_\text{sea}=1025/1000\), 2.5% more volume. (Ships carry load lines marked "F" for fresh water above the sea marks.)

2 · Ice cube in a glass

A glass is filled to the brim with water with an ice cube floating in it. When the cube melts, does the water overflow?

Answer

No. Fresh ice in fresh water: the meltwater exactly fills the volume the floating ice displaced (section 7). The level does not change.

3 · Anchor overboard

A boat floats in a small pond carrying a heavy iron anchor. The anchor is thrown overboard and sinks to the bottom. Does the pond's water level rise, fall, or stay the same?

Hint

In the boat, the anchor displaces its weight of water; on the bottom it displaces its volume.

Answer

Falls. In the boat the anchor makes the boat displace \(m/\rho_w\); on the bottom it displaces only its own volume \(m/\rho_\text{iron}\), about 8 times smaller.

4 · Steel on mercury

Does a steel bolt (7850 kg/m³) sink in mercury (13 600 kg/m³)? If not, what fraction is under the surface?

Answer

It floats, because it is less dense than mercury; fraction under \(=7850/13\,600\approx0.58\).

5 · Deeper, more upthrust?

A sealed steel can is lowered on a rope from 1 m to 10 m below the surface of a lake. Ignoring any squashing of the can, does the rope tension change?

Answer

No. \(F_B=\rho gV\) does not depend on depth (section 3: top and bottom pressures both grow, their difference does not), so \(T=W-F_B\) is the same.

6 · Two bodies on one wire

A floating cylinder carries a sphere hanging from it on a wire, fully under water. Why can the cylinder's density be found from one equation for the whole system, without knowing the wire tension?

Answer

The wire pulls up on the sphere and down on the cylinder with the same \(T\): for the system as a whole it is an internal force and cancels. Total weight = total upthrust then involves only densities and volumes (exam taster, Dec 2023).
