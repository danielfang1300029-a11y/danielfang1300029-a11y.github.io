Hydrostatic forces on plane and curved surfaces

0Where this chapter sits

Picture water leaning on a dam or a gate. Every patch of the wall is pushed, gently near the surface and hard near the bottom. This chapter adds all those pushes into one equivalent push and finds where it sits: always below the middle, because the bottom is pushed hardest.

Chapter on one page

  2. §2 Draw the pushes first: arrows square to the wall, longer with vertical depth.
  3. §3 Many small pushes can be replaced by one equivalent push with the same total and the same turning effect.
  4. §4 A wall from the surface down: average pressure × area, acting two-thirds of the way down.
  5. §5 Size of the push on any flat surface: pressure at the centroid × wetted area.
  6. §6 The centroid is the balance point of the shape.
  7. §7 The second moment of area measures how far the shape spreads up and down. Formulas given in the exam.

  9. §9 Triangles and uniform loads need no formulas: read the answer off the sketch.
  10. §10 Gas or an upper liquid adds an even extra push: treat it as extra imaginary depth.
  11. §11 Gates: take moments about the hinge. Core exam type

Sign convention for this chapter
  • Pressure is gauge pressure (pressure above atmospheric), as the exam states. The free surface of an open tank is at \(p=0\). Air at atmospheric pressure on the back of a gate exerts no force.
  • Depth \(h\) (or \(z\)) is measured vertically downward from the free surface. Distances along a sloping surface are called \(y\), measured from the point O where the surface (extended if needed) meets the free surface. For a surface at angle \(\theta\) to the horizontal, \(h=y\sin\theta\).
  • Pressure force acts normal to the surface and pushes on it (from the fluid into the surface). In pressure diagrams the arrows are drawn perpendicular to the surface, pointing at it.
  • Forces: horizontal positive to the right, vertical positive upward, unless the question names another direction. A vertical component \(F_V\) is stated with its direction ("upward" or "downward").
  • Moments: state the sense as seen in the side view drawn ("clockwise" / "anticlockwise"). Take moments about the hinge, so the unknown hinge reaction drops out.
  • Constants: \(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\), \(\rho_\text{water}=1000\u{kg/m^3}\), so \(\rho g=9.81\u{kN/m^3}\). Use the paper's own values if it gives others.
  • "Per unit width", "unit length" or "a 1 m run" means: take the breadth into the page as 1 m, so forces come out in kN per metre.

1Why forces on surfaces matter (slides 23–26) ~3 min

What is this?

Stand behind a dam and picture the water leaning on it. Every patch of the upstream face is pushed, and the push grows the deeper the patch is, because more water is piled above it. Near the surface the push is gentle; near the bed it is strong. A canal gate or a tank wall feels the same thing. This growing push is the picture for the whole chapter.

Slide 23 calls this "the core subject matter of fluid statics": the hydrostatic force acting on a submerged surface. A submerged surface is any surface with fluid on at least one side: a dam face, a gate, a tank wall, a ship's hull. Hydrostatic means the fluid is at rest, so the only stress in it is pressure (H1: a fluid at rest carries no shear stress).

Slides 24–26 show three engineering structures that must be designed against this force:

  • Gravity dam (slide 24, Hoover Dam photo). A massive concrete wall holds back the reservoir (upstream side). The water pressure pushes horizontally on the upstream face, trying to slide and overturn the dam. The dam resists by its own weight (hence "gravity" dam). Water seeping under the base also pushes up on it: the uplift pressure. Spillway and sluiceway are channels for releasing water.
  • Control gate in a canal, spillway or shipyard (slide 25, photos of a vertical lift gate, a curved "radial" gate and a flap gate). A gate must be strong enough for the water force, and its hinge, stops or hoist must hold the force and the moment it makes.
  • Earth-retaining wall (slide 26). An L-shaped concrete wall (vertical "stem", horizontal base with "heel" and "toe") holds back soil. Soil pressure and water pressure push on the stem; the weight of the wall and of the soil on its heel holds it in place.

Redrawn for these notes: the water (blue) pushes on the structure (grey) with a pressure that grows with depth; the arrows show the pressure, longest at the bottom.

In each case the engineer needs two things: how big the total water force is, and where it acts. The rest of the chapter answers exactly these two questions, first for flat (plane) surfaces, then for curved ones.

You can now: name the structures that must resist the water's push (gravity dam, gate, retaining wall); say that a design needs both the size of the force and where it acts.

2Pressure distribution diagrams (slide 27; review sheet) ~7 min

What is this?

Draw the dam face and, at each point, an arrow showing how hard the water pushes there: short arrows near the surface, long ones near the bed. The arrow tips line up along a straight slope, because each metre deeper adds the same extra push. That sketch is the pressure distribution diagram: the load on the dam, drawn so you can see it.

Before any formula, draw the pressure distribution diagram: a sketch of the surface with an arrow at each point whose length is proportional to the pressure there. The exam asks for these sketches directly (H3.1, 4–5 marks), and they show at once where the force is biggest. Two facts from H2 are all you need:

Result · hydrostatic pressure (from H2) \[p=p_0+\rho g z\]

In words: pressure = pressure at the surface + specific weight × vertical depth below that surface. With \(p_0=0\) (open to the air, gauge pressure), \(p=\rho g z\): pressure grows linearly with depth.

Reading the formula
\(p_0\)
the push already there at the surface: zero for an open tank (gauge pressure), not zero under pressurised air or an upper liquid.
\(\rho g\)
the extra pressure for each metre deeper: the weight of a one-metre column of the liquid on each square metre.
\(z\)
the vertical depth of the point, however the wall leans.
\(\rho g z\)
the weight of liquid piled above each square metre at that depth: why the dam is pushed harder lower down.
Definition · pressure force on a surface (review sheet)

The pressure force is a vector, always perpendicular to the surface and pushing inward on it (from the fluid onto the surface). Pressure itself is a scalar: at a point it is the same in every direction (Pascal's law), so a sloping surface feels the same pressure \(\rho g z\) as a vertical one at the same depth; only the direction of the force changes.

Spread over a surface, the pressure force is a distributed load: a force spread continuously over an area, like snow on a roof, rather than concentrated at one point.

Rules for drawing

  1. Do: Mark the free surface and the depth \(z\) of each corner or kink of the surface. Only vertical depth matters, not the distance along a sloping wall. Pressure is set by how much liquid is piled above a point, and that depends only on its vertical depth.
  2. Do: Work out \(p\) at the top and bottom of each straight piece (and at every interface between two fluids). Between those points the pressure changes steadily, so the end values fix the whole diagram.
  3. Do: Draw arrows perpendicular to the surface, pointing onto it, with lengths proportional to \(p\). Join the arrow tails with a straight line (pressure is linear in depth within one fluid). A fluid at rest can only push, never drag along the wall, so each push is square to the surface.
  4. Do: Label the largest and smallest values with numbers and units. The shape shows where the wall is pushed hardest; the numbers give how hard, and they carry the marks.

The review sheet lists the shapes you must be able to draw. They all come from the same two rules:

  • Triangular: the surface starts at the free surface (\(p=0\) at the top) and goes down to depth \(h\) (\(p=\rho gh\) at the bottom).
  • Trapezoidal: the surface is completely under water, its top at depth \(h_1\) and bottom at \(h_2\). The diagram is a rectangle of height \(\rho gh_1\) plus a triangle (slide 27, figure b).
  • Uniform (rectangular): the surface is horizontal, so every point is at the same depth and \(p=\rho gh\) everywhere on it.
  • Two-layer (bilinear): a lighter liquid (oil) floats on a heavier one (water). Pressure is continuous at the interface (H2), but below it the line gets steeper, because each metre of water adds \(\rho_w g\), more than a metre of oil. The diagram has a kink at the interface.
  • Inclined surface: the arrows are perpendicular to the slope, but their lengths still depend only on vertical depth.
  • Curved surface (such as a circular arc): the arrows are perpendicular to the curve everywhere (for a circle, they all point along radii, through the centre), and their lengths grow with depth.

Try it · two-layer pressure on an inclined wall

A tank holds oil over 1 m of water against a wall at angle \(\theta\) to the bed (the Dec 2023 exam set-up has \(h_o=1\) m, relative density 0.8, \(\theta=45°\)). The arrows are drawn perpendicular to the wall. Move the oil depth to 0 for a single triangle; set the relative density to 1 and the kink disappears; change the angle and see that the pressures at A and B do not change.

Watch

On an inclined wall, the pressure at a point is \(\rho g\times\) its vertical depth, never \(\rho g\times\) the slant distance along the wall. And the arrows must be perpendicular to the wall, not horizontal.

You can now: sketch triangular, trapezoidal, uniform, two-layer, inclined and curved pressure diagrams with arrows normal to the surface; compute the pressures at corners and interfaces from vertical depth.

3From distributed load to resultant force (slides 27–29) ~4 min

What is this?

Instead of thousands of small pushes all over the dam face, imagine one big hand pressing on it at a single point. If the hand pushes with the same total force, and at the right height so that it turns the dam just as much, the dam cannot tell the difference. That one hand is the resultant force. Because the water pushes harder low down, the hand must press below the middle.

Definition · resultant force (slide 27)

The resultant force \(F_R\) is a single point force that can replace the whole distributed pressure load. It is "equivalent" to the distributed load if it satisfies the same static equilibrium conditions: it has the same total push and the same turning effect.

Slide 27's figures show this. In figure (a) a vertical wall holds water; the pressure arrows grow from zero at the surface. A rectangular plate (shaded) sits on the wall from depth \(h_1\) to depth \(h_2\); only the pressure on the plate matters for the plate, a trapezoid ABCE. Figure (b) splits that trapezoid into a rectangle ABDE of width \(\gamma h_1\) (the pressure at the top of the plate) and a triangle BCD of base \(\gamma(h_2-h_1)\). Each piece has its own resultant: \(F_1\) at the middle of the rectangle and \(F_2\) at two-thirds of the way down the triangle. Their sum is \(F_R\), which acts at a depth \(y_A\) between them.

Slide 28 states the two conditions:

Result · the two equilibrium conditions (slide 28) \[\sum F=0\qquad\sum M=0\]

In words: net force = 0 fixes the magnitude of \(F_R\) (it must equal the total of all the little pressure forces); net moment = 0 fixes its line of action (where it must act so that it turns the surface exactly as much as the spread-out load does).

The boxed statement on slide 28: as far as the equilibrium of the surface is concerned, it makes no difference whether the surface carries the distributed pressure or the single point force \(F_R\) at the right place. So in every gate problem you replace the pressure by \(F_R\) and draw an ordinary free-body diagram.

One way to picture it (slide 29)

Imagine a thin plate with the distributed load (red arrows, growing from left to right) on top and a single upward point force \(F_R\) underneath. The plate stays still only if \(\sum F=0\) (so \(F_R\) equals the total load) and \(\sum M=0\) (so \(F_R\) sits under the "centre" of the load, nearer the heavy end). That point force is exactly the resultant.

Analogy: two children on a see-saw are a distributed load in miniature. The single support that balances them must carry both weights and must be closer to the heavier child. The resultant of a pressure distribution is that single support, reversed.

Slide 30 then sets the plan: first plane (flat) surfaces, where the direction of \(F_R\) is known (perpendicular to the plane) and formulas exist for its size and position; later, curved surfaces (section 12).

You can now: say what a resultant force is; explain that \(\sum F=0\) fixes its size and \(\sum M=0\) fixes its line of action; replace a pressure diagram by one force in a free-body diagram.

4The simplest case: a vertical rectangle piercing the surface (Plane Surface supplement) ~5 min

What is this?

Take a plain vertical wall that starts at the water surface. Slice it into thin horizontal strips, like the planks of a fence. Each plank gets its own push, small at the top and large at the bottom. Add the plank pushes to get the size of the single push; add their turning effects to find where it sits. It lands two-thirds of the way down, below the middle, because the low planks push hardest.

The supplement "Hydrostatic Force on a Plane Surface" works one case in full by integration, which shows where all the later formulas come from. Take a vertical rectangular wall of breadth \(b\) (into the page) holding water of depth \(h\). The wall "pierces through the free surface": it starts above the water, so the wetted area is \(A=hb\).

  1. Do: Cut the wetted area into thin horizontal strips. A strip at depth \(z\) has height \(\mathrm dz\) and area \(\mathrm dA=b\,\mathrm dz\). Every point of a thin horizontal strip is at the same depth, so the strip feels one pressure.
  2. Do: The pressure on the strip is \(p=\rho g z\), so its force is \(\mathrm dF=p\,\mathrm dA=\rho g z\,b\,\mathrm dz\) (perpendicular to the wall). Force = pressure × area works on each strip because the pressure on it is uniform.
  3. Do: Add all the strips (\(\sum F=0\) for the wall): The single push must equal the total of all the plank pushes.
\[\begin{aligned}F_R&=\int_A \mathrm dF=\rho g b\int_0^h z\,\mathrm dz\\&=\tfrac12\rho g h^2 b=\rho g\,\frac h2\,(hb)\end{aligned}\]

Pressure varies linearly from \(p_\text{min}=0\) at the top to \(p_\text{max}=\rho gh\) at the bottom, so the average pressure is \((p_\text{max}+p_\text{min})/2=\rho g(h/2)\): exactly the pressure at the centre of area of the wetted rectangle (depth \(h/2\)). So

Result · magnitude (supplement; valid for any plane surface) \[F_R=p_\text{ave}\times A\]

In words: resultant force = pressure at the centre of area × wetted area = average pressure × wetted area.

Reading the formula
\(F_R\)
the one equivalent push that replaces all the strip pushes.
\(p_\text{ave}\)
the gentle push at the top and the hard push at the bottom, averaged: on a wall from the surface, the pressure halfway down.
\(A\)
the wetted area: only the part of the wall the water touches.

4. For the position, take moments about the top edge O (\(\sum M=0\)): the moment of \(F_R\) about O must equal the total moment of all the strips. Let \(h_R\) be the depth where \(F_R\) acts:

\[\begin{aligned}F_R h_R&=\int_A z\,\mathrm dF=\rho g b\int_0^h z^2\,\mathrm dz\\&=\tfrac13\rho g h^3 b\end{aligned}\] \[\tfrac12\rho g h^2 b\;h_R=\tfrac13\rho g h^3 b\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{h_R=\tfrac23 h}\]

The point where \(F_R\) acts is the centre of pressure, because it is the centre of the pressure distribution (here, the centroid of the pressure triangle, two-thirds of the way down). The supplement stresses: \(h_R=\frac23h\) is valid only for a triangular pressure distribution on a rectangle, that is, a rectangle that starts at the free surface. For anything else, use the general formula of section 8.

Example · written for these notes · a vertical tank wall

A vertical tank wall is 2 m wide and holds water 3 m deep. Find the resultant force and where it acts.

  1. Pressures: 0 at the surface, \(p_\text{max}=9.81\times3=29.43\u{kPa}\) at the bottom: a triangle.
  2. Average pressure \(=29.43/2=14.715\u{kPa}\) (the pressure at depth 1.5 m, the centre of area).
  3. \(F_R=14.715\times(3\times2)=88.29\u{kN}\), or directly \(\tfrac12\rho gh^2b=\tfrac12(9.81)(3^2)(2)\).
  4. \(h_R=\tfrac23(3)=2\u{m}\) below the surface (1 m above the bottom). The pressure diagram is a triangle, heaviest at the bottom, and a triangle balances two-thirds of the way from its tip.
\[\boxed{F_R=88.3\u{kN},\quad h_R=2\u{m}}\]

acting horizontally on the wall, 2 m below the surface.

FBD of the wall: the single arrow \(F_R\) replaces the triangle; the wall's supports must supply an equal and opposite force and an equal and opposite moment.

You can now: find the force on a vertical rectangular wall by adding strips; show that the average pressure is the pressure at the centre of area; place the force two-thirds of the way down for a rectangle starting at the surface.

5Any plane surface: the size of the force (slides 30–33) ~5 min

What is this?

Now the gate can be any shape and lean at any angle. The size of the push still comes from one idea: the shallow part of the gate is pushed less than average and the deep part more, and because pressure grows steadily with depth these exactly balance about the centroid. So the total push is the pressure at the centroid times the wetted area, as if the whole gate sat at the centroid's depth.

The integration (slide 32)

A small piece of area \(\mathrm dA\) at distance \(y\) along the plane from O is at depth \(h=y\sin\theta\). Its pressure force is \(\mathrm dF=\rho g h\,\mathrm dA\). Add them all:

\[\begin{aligned}F_R&=\int_A \mathrm dF=\int_A\rho g h\,\mathrm dA\\&=\rho g\sin\theta\int_A y\,\mathrm dA\end{aligned}\]

The integral \(\int_A y\,\mathrm dA\) is the first moment of area about the axis through O (section 7). By the definition of the centroid it equals \(y_cA\), where \(y_c\) is the distance of the centroid from O. So

\[F_R=\rho g\sin\theta\,y_cA=\rho g h_cA,\qquad h_c=y_c\sin\theta\]
Result · magnitude of the resultant force (slides 32–33) · memorise \[F_R=\rho g h_c A\]

In words: resultant force = pressure at the centroid × wetted area. The angle \(\theta\) does not appear: only the depth of the centroid and the area matter.

Reading the formula
\(\rho g\)
pressure gained per metre of depth.
\(h_c\)
the vertical depth of the gate's balance point (its centroid), not its distance along a slope.
\(\rho g h_c\)
the pressure at the centroid, which is the average pressure over the gate: the shallow part's shortfall and the deep part's excess cancel.
\(A\)
the wetted area: dry parts carry no push.
no \(\theta\)
tilting the gate about its centroid raises one half as much as it lowers the other, so the average push is unchanged.

What the formula means (slide 33)

Pressure is not uniform over the surface; is there an average pressure \(p_\text{ave}\) with \(F_R=p_\text{ave}A\)? Matching this with \(F_R=\rho gh_cA\) gives

Result · average pressure (slide 33) \[p_\text{ave}=\rho g h_c\]

In words: the average pressure on a plane surface is the pressure acting at its centroid. So \(F_R\) = pressure at the centroid × wetted area.

This is true for any plane shape at any angle, because pressure is linear in depth: the deeper half of the area has extra pressure that exactly balances the shallower half's shortfall, about the centroid.

Watch

\(h_c\) is the vertical depth of the centroid, not its distance \(y_c\) along a sloping gate. On a gate at 60°, using \(y_c\) instead of \(h_c\) makes the force about 15% too big.

How to find the size of the force on a plane surface:

  1. Do: Find the wetted area \(A\) (only the part in contact with the fluid). Only the wetted part of the gate is pushed.
  2. Do: Locate the centroid C of that area (section 6) and its vertical depth \(h_c\) below the free surface; on a slope, \(h_c=y_c\sin\theta\). The pressure at C is the average pressure over the whole gate.
  3. Do: Multiply: \(F_R=\rho g h_c A\). Average push per square metre × number of square metres = total push.
  4. Do: Draw \(F_R\) perpendicular to the surface, pointing from the fluid onto it. On a flat surface every small push is square to it, so they are all parallel and so is their total.

You can now: find \(F_R=\rho gh_cA\) for any plane surface at any angle; explain why only the centroid's vertical depth and the wetted area matter.

6Centroids (slides 34–35) ~2 min

What is this?

The centroid is where you could balance a cut-out of the gate on one fingertip. For a rectangle or a circle it is the middle; for a triangle it sits nearer the wide side, because that is where most of the area is. In this chapter it matters because the pressure at the centroid is the average pressure on the gate.

Definition · centroid (slide 34)

The centroid (or centre of area) is the geometric centre of a two-dimensional shape:

  • it is the centre of the distribution of area of the shape;
  • it is the point where a thin plate of that shape could be perfectly balanced on a single pin;
  • for regular shapes (rectangle, square, circle) it is the centre of the figure;
  • for a triangle it is where the three medians meet (a median joins a corner to the midpoint of the opposite side). The centroid divides each median in the ratio 2 : 1, two parts from the corner, one part from the side.

Slide 35: the centroid of a 2-D figure coincides with the centre of mass of a thin uniform plate of the same size and shape. So "where would it balance?" is a quick check.

Ready answer · centroid positions to know
  • Rectangle of height \(L\): at mid-height, \(L/2\) from either edge.
  • Circle: at the centre.
  • Triangle of height \(L\): \(L/3\) from the base, \(2L/3\) from the apex (the 2 : 1 rule along the median).
  • Semicircle of radius \(R\): on the axis of symmetry, \(4R/(3\pi)\approx0.424R\) from the straight edge.

Why it matters here: \(h_c\) is the depth of the centroid. For a triangular gate with its apex at the top, the centroid is two-thirds of the way down, not halfway: the wide base holds most of the area.

You can now: locate the centroid of a rectangle, circle, triangle and semicircle; use its depth \(h_c\) in the force formula.

7Second moment of area (slide 38; Moments of Area supplement, optional) ~5 min

What is this?

The second moment of area measures how much of the gate lies far above or below its centroid. A tall, thin gate has a lot of area far from the middle; a short, wide gate of the same area keeps it close. Area far from the centroid counts heavily (its distance is squared). In this chapter it decides how far the push sits below the centroid.

The position of the centre of pressure (section 8) needs one more property of the shape: its second moment of area. Slide 38 gives the values for four shapes, and the review sheet says these formulas will be provided in the exam if needed. You do need to know what they mean and which axis they refer to.

Moments of Area supplement · marked optional by the handout

The handout labels this supplement Optional; the exam gives the formulas. It explains what "moments of area" are. For any plane shape and an axis GG in its plane, the \(n\)th moment of area is \(\int_A y^n\,\mathrm dA\), where \(y\) is the (signed) distance of each bit of area \(\mathrm dA\) from GG: negative on the other side of the axis.

  • Zeroth moment (\(n=0\)): \(\int_A\mathrm dA=A\), the total area.
  • First moment (\(n=1\)): \(\int_A y\,\mathrm dA=y_cA\), where \(y_c\) is the distance of the centroid from GG. This is the definition of the centroid. About an axis through the centroid, \(y_c=0\), so the first moment is zero.
  • Second moment (\(n=2\)): \(\int_A y^2\,\mathrm dA=I_{GG}\). It measures the spread of the area about the axis, like the variance of a statistical distribution: narrow and sharp, or broad and flat.
  • Third moment (\(n=3\)): measures how symmetric the shape is about the centroidal axis (zero for a symmetric shape).
the four moments of a rectangle \(b\times d\) about its centreline

Strip of width \(b\), height \(\mathrm dy\) at distance \(y\) from the centreline, \(-d/2\le y\le d/2\); \(\mathrm dA=b\,\mathrm dy\).

\(\int b\,\mathrm dy=b\,[y]_{-d/2}^{d/2}=bd=A\). Zeroth moment: the area.

\(\int y\,b\,\mathrm dy=b\,[y^2/2]_{-d/2}^{d/2}=0\). First moment zero: the axis passes through the centroid.

\(I_c=\int y^2\,b\,\mathrm dy\) \(=b\,[y^3/3]_{-d/2}^{d/2}=\dfrac{bd^3}{12}\). Proportional to \(d^3\): very sensitive to the extent in \(y\).

\(\int y^3\,b\,\mathrm dy=b\,[y^4/4]_{-d/2}^{d/2}=0\). Third moment zero: symmetric about the centroidal axis.

Result · parallel axis theorem \[I_O=I_c+Ay_c^2\]

In words: second moment about any axis OO = second moment about the parallel axis through the centroid + area × (distance between the axes)².

Reading the formula
\(I_O\)
how spread out the area is, measured from the line OO (for a gate, the free-surface line).
\(I_c\)
the spread of the shape about its own centroid: its own up-and-down extent.
\(Ay_c^2\)
the extra that comes from the whole shape sitting a distance \(y_c\) away from OO.
proof of the parallel axis theorem

Let \(y'\) be the distance of \(\mathrm dA\) from OO and \(y\) its distance from the centroidal axis GG, with GG a distance \(y_c\) from OO, so \(y'=y_c-y\) (or \(y_c+y\), depending on the side; the result is the same).

\[\begin{aligned}I_O&=\int_A (y_c-y)^2\,\mathrm dA\\&=y_c^2\!\int_A\!\mathrm dA-2y_c\!\int_A\! y\,\mathrm dA\\&\quad+\int_A y^2\,\mathrm dA\end{aligned}\]

The first integral is \(A\). The middle integral is the first moment about the centroidal axis, which is zero. The last is \(I_c\). So \(I_O=y_c^2A+I_c\). This is the step used on slide 36.

Final remark of the supplement: moments are a statistical tool for describing distributions; in mechanics the "second moment of mass" is the moment of inertia. There is no need to dig deeper for this course.

You can now: read \(I_c\) about the horizontal centroidal axis for a rectangle, circle, triangle and semicircle; (optional) move it to another axis with the parallel axis theorem.

8Where the force acts: the centre of pressure (slides 36–37) ~7 min

What is this?

Back to the dam: the low part of the face is pushed harder than the high part. So the single equivalent push must sit lower than the centroid, nearer the hard-pushed bottom, just as a see-saw's support must be nearer the heavier child. The deeper the whole gate is, the more even the pushes are from its top to its bottom, and the closer the push gets to the centroid. On a horizontal plate every point is pushed equally, so the push sits right at the centroid.

The point of application of \(F_R\) is the centre of pressure (CP). Slide 36 finds it with \(\sum M=0\): the moment of \(F_R\) about O must equal the total moment of all the little forces \(\mathrm dF\) about O.

the derivation of \(y_R\) and \(h_R\) (slide 36)

Moments about the axis through O, in the plane of the surface:

\[F_R\,y_R=\int_A y\,\mathrm dF\] Each piece \(\mathrm dF\) is perpendicular to the plane, at distance \(y\) from O along the plane, so its moment arm is \(y\). \[(\rho g y_c\sin\theta\,A)\,y_R=\int_A y\,(\rho g y\sin\theta\,\mathrm dA)\] Substitute \(F_R=\rho g\sin\theta\,y_cA\) (section 5) and \(\mathrm dF=\rho g\,y\sin\theta\,\mathrm dA\). \[(y_cA)\,y_R=\int_A y^2\,\mathrm dA\] Cancel \(\rho g\sin\theta\) from both sides. \[\int_A y^2\,\mathrm dA=I_O=I_c+Ay_c^2\] The integral is the second moment about O; the parallel axis theorem moves it to the centroid. \[(y_cA)\,y_R=Ay_c^2+I_c\ \Rightarrow\ y_R=y_c+\frac{I_c}{y_cA}\] Divide by \(y_cA\).

Multiply by \(\sin\theta\) to turn distances along the plane into depths (\(h_R=y_R\sin\theta\), \(h_c=y_c\sin\theta\), so \(y_c=h_c/\sin\theta\)):

\[h_R=h_c+\frac{I_c\sin^2\theta}{h_cA}\]
Result · centre of pressure (slides 36–37) · memorise \[y_R=y_c+\frac{I_c}{y_cA}\]

or, in depths,

\[h_R=h_c+\frac{I_c\sin^2\theta}{h_cA}\]

In words: depth of the centre of pressure = depth of the centroid + (second moment about the centroidal axis × sin²θ) ÷ (depth of centroid × area). Along the plane: distance of CP from O = distance of centroid from O + \(I_c\) ÷ (that distance × area).

Reading the formula
\(h_c\)
start at the centroid's depth.
\(+\)
the push is always lower than the centroid (never above), because the bottom of the gate is pushed harder.
\(I_c\)
a shape that spreads far up and down has a bigger difference between its gently pushed top and its hard-pushed bottom, so the shift is bigger.
\(\sin^2\theta\)
a gate lying flatter spans less depth from top to bottom, so its pushes are more even and the shift is smaller; zero for a horizontal plate.
\(h_cA\) below the line
the deeper the gate, the more even the pushes over it, and the smaller the shift.

How to find the centre of pressure:

  1. Do: Find \(A\), the centroid C and its depth \(h_c\) (or its distance \(y_c\) along the plane from O, where the plane meets the free surface). The centre of pressure is found as a shift from C.
  2. Do: Find \(I_c\) about the horizontal axis through C lying in the surface (section 7; given in the exam). It measures how far the area spreads up and down the slope from C, which sets how uneven the pushes are.
  3. Do: Add the shift: \(I_c/(y_cA)\) along the plane, or \(I_c\sin^2\theta/(h_cA)\) in depth. The push sits below C because the deeper part of the gate is pushed harder.
  4. Do: Place CP on the line of symmetry running down the slope. The left and right halves of a symmetric gate are pushed alike, so the push has no reason to move sideways.

Past papers write the along-the-plane version as \(y_{cp}=\bar y+I_{xx,c}/(\bar yA)\), where \(\bar y\) is the centroid's distance from the free-surface line along the plane. Same formula, different letters. For a vertical surface \(\theta=90°\), \(\sin\theta=1\), and \(y\) and \(h\) are the same thing.

Why the centre of pressure is below the centroid (slide 37)

The centre of pressure is the centre of the pressure distribution, not of the area. Deeper bits of area carry more pressure, so in the moment \(\int y\,\mathrm dF\) the bits farther from O are weighted more heavily. The balance point shifts down. Mathematically, the extra term \(I_c\sin^2\theta/(h_cA)\) is never negative:

\[h_R\begin{cases}\gt h_c & \text{inclined or vertical plane, }\theta\ne0\\ =h_c & \text{horizontal plane, }\theta=0\end{cases}\]

For a horizontal plane the pressure is uniform, so the centre of pressure coincides with the centroid. The exam has asked this as a true/false statement (section 22).

Watch

The shift \(I_c\sin^2\theta/(h_cA)\) gets smaller as the surface goes deeper (\(h_c\) in the denominator). Deep down, the pressure is nearly uniform over the surface, so CP is almost at the centroid. Near the surface the shift is largest: for a rectangle starting at the surface it reaches \(h/6\) (from \(h/2\) to \(2h/3\)).

Sideways position. For a shape that is symmetric about a line running down the slope (rectangle, circle, isosceles triangle with the apex up or down), the centre of pressure lies on that line of symmetry. All exam shapes are of this kind.

Example · written for these notes · an inclined rectangular gate

A rectangular gate 2 m long (down the slope) and 1.5 m wide lies on a slope at \(\theta=60°\) to the horizontal. Its top edge is 1 m below the water surface. Find the resultant force and the depth of the centre of pressure.

  1. Area: \(A=2\times1.5=3\u{m^2}\).
  2. Centroid: halfway down the gate, 1 m along the slope below the top edge, so 1 sin 60° = 0.866 m deeper: \(h_c=1+0.866=1.866\u{m}\). On a slope, a step along the gate takes you only \(\sin\theta\) of that step deeper.
  3. Force: \(F_R=\rho gh_cA\) \(=9.81\times1.866\times3=54.92\u{kN}\).
  4. Second moment about the horizontal centroidal axis (in the gate's plane; the height measured along the slope is 2 m): \(I_c=bL^3/12=1.5\times2^3/12=1\u{m^4}\).
  5. Centre of pressure:
\[\begin{aligned}h_R&=1.866+\frac{1\times\sin^2 60°}{1.866\times3}\\&=1.866+0.134=2.000\u{m}\end{aligned}\] \[\boxed{F_R=54.9\u{kN},\quad h_R=2.00\u{m}}\]

Along the gate: \(y_c=1.866/\sin60°=2.155\u{m}\) from O, and \(y_R-y_c=I_c/(y_cA)\) \(=1/(2.155\times3)=0.155\u{m}\). So CP is \(1+0.155=1.155\u{m}\) down the gate from its top edge, on its centreline.

FBD: \(F_R\) is perpendicular to the gate, pointing from the water onto the gate, at CP.

9Cases where you don't need the formulas (slides 39–40) ~4 min

What is this?

Sometimes the pressure sketch is so simple that you can read the answer off it. A wall starting at the surface carries a triangle of pushes, and the single push sits where the triangle balances, two-thirds of the way down. A horizontal plate carries the same push everywhere, like an even layer of sand, so the push sits in the middle. A wall wholly under water carries a trapezoid: an even layer with a triangle added.

Two shapes of pressure diagram are so simple that you can read off the answer from the diagram itself.

A rectangle piercing the free surface: triangular pressure (slide 39)

A plane rectangle of length \(H\) (measured along the plane) and breadth \(B\) starts at the free surface and slopes down at \(\theta\). Its bottom edge is at depth \(H\sin\theta\), and the pressure diagram is a triangle.

\[A=HB,\quad h_c=\tfrac12H\sin\theta,\quad h_R=\tfrac23H\sin\theta\] \[F_R=\tfrac12\rho g H^2B\sin\theta\]

In words: the resultant acts two-thirds of the way down the wetted length, and its size is half the bottom pressure × the wetted area. For a vertical wall (\(\theta=90°\), slide 39's second picture) this is section 4's result: \(F_R=\tfrac12\rho gH^2B\), at \(H/3\) above the bottom.

check: the general formula gives the same \(h_R\)

\(I_c=BH^3/12\) (the height in the plane is \(H\)), \(A=HB\), \(h_c=\tfrac12H\sin\theta\).

\[\frac{I_c\sin^2\theta}{h_cA}=\frac{(BH^3/12)\sin^2\theta}{\tfrac12H\sin\theta\cdot HB}=\tfrac16H\sin\theta\]

\(h_R=\tfrac12H\sin\theta+\tfrac16H\sin\theta=\tfrac23H\sin\theta\). ✓ Both routes agree, as they must.

A horizontal surface: uniform pressure (slide 40)

A horizontal plate at depth \(h\) (a tank bottom, the flat top of a submerged box) has the same pressure \(p=\rho gh\) everywhere on it. So

Result · horizontal surface (slide 40) \[F=\rho g h A\]

In words: force on a horizontal surface = pressure at its depth × its area = the weight of the column of liquid standing on it. It acts at the centroid (centre of pressure = centre of area).

Splitting a trapezoid (slide 27, figure b)

For a rectangle wholly under water, the pressure diagram is a trapezoid. Instead of the \(I_c\) formula you may split it:

  1. Do: Split the trapezoid into a rectangle (uniform pressure equal to the top pressure) and a triangle (the extra pressure growing from zero at the top). Each piece is a load whose balance point you already know.
  2. Do: Find each piece's force: the area of that piece of the diagram × the breadth. Pressure × height is the push on one metre of breadth, and that is the area of the piece.
  3. Do: Each piece's force acts at its own centroid: the middle of the rectangle, and two-thirds of the way down the triangle. The even layer balances in the middle; the triangle is heavier at the bottom.
  4. Do: Then combine with moments. The single push must match both the total and the turning effect of the two pieces (section 3).
Example · written for these notes · trapezoid split vs formula

A vertical rectangular gate, 2 m high and 1 m wide, has its top edge 1 m below the water surface. Find \(F_R\) and the depth of the centre of pressure both ways.

  1. Pressures: top \(p_1=9.81\times1=9.81\u{kPa}\); bottom \(p_2=9.81\times3=29.43\u{kPa}\).
  2. Rectangle part: \(F_1=9.81\times(2\times1)=19.62\u{kN}\), acting at mid-height, 1 m below the top edge.
  3. Triangle part: extra pressure \(29.43-9.81=19.62\u{kPa}\) at the bottom; \(F_2=\tfrac12\times19.62\times2=19.62\u{kN}\), acting \(\tfrac23\times2=1.333\u{m}\) below the top edge.
  4. Total \(F_R=F_1+F_2=39.24\u{kN}\). Moments about the top edge: \(F_R\,d=F_1(1)+F_2(1.333)\), so \(d=1.1667\u{m}\): depth \(h_R=1+1.1667=2.167\u{m}\).
  5. Formula check: \(h_c=2\u{m}\), \(A=2\u{m^2}\), \(F_R=9.81\times2\times2=39.24\u{kN}\) ✓; \(I_c=1\times2^3/12=0.667\u{m^4}\), \(h_R=2+0.667/(2\times2)=2.167\u{m}\) ✓.
\[\boxed{F_R=39.2\u{kN},\quad h_R=2.17\u{m}}\]

10Gas pressure above the liquid, and layered liquids (H2 slide 16 idea; review sheet) ~6 min

What is this?

Put air under pressure on top of the water, or float a layer of oil on it. Every point of the gate below now gets the same extra push added on, as if more water were piled on top. So you can pretend the water surface is higher by just the right amount and use the ordinary formulas from that imaginary surface. Or draw the extra push as an even strip on the pressure sketch and handle the pieces one by one.

Two exam favourites (H3.3) break the simple "free surface at \(p=0\)" picture: a closed tank with pressurised (or partly evacuated) air above the liquid, and two liquids in layers. H2 (slide 16) gave the key idea: the pressure at the bottom of the upper layer is a surcharge, transmitted uniformly to every depth of the liquid below. Two methods handle it.

Method 1 · equivalent depth (the "piezometric head" method)

Replace the surcharge \(p_s\) by an imaginary extra depth of the liquid that would produce the same pressure:

\[h_\text{eq}=\frac{p_s}{\rho g}\]

In words: equivalent depth = surcharge pressure ÷ specific weight of the liquid you are working in.

  1. Do: Then pretend the free surface is \(h_\text{eq}\) higher (an imaginary free surface), and measure all depths from it. The surcharge adds the same push at every depth, exactly what \(h_\text{eq}\) of extra liquid on top would add.
  2. Do: Use \(F_R=\rho gh_cA\) and \(h_R=h_c+I_c\sin^2\theta/(h_cA)\) as usual. Measured from the imaginary surface, the pressure is zero at the "surface" again, which is what the formulas assume.
  3. Do: At the end, convert \(h_R\) back to a real depth by subtracting \(h_\text{eq}\). The gate sits in the real tank, so its answer must be given from the real surface.

For an oil layer of thickness \(h_o\) on water, \(p_s=\rho_og h_o\), so \(h_\text{eq}=(\rho_o/\rho_w)h_o\): 1.5 m of oil of relative density 0.8 acts on the water like 1.2 m of water. For air at gauge pressure \(p_\text{air}\), \(h_\text{eq}=p_\text{air}/(\rho g)\); if the air is below atmospheric, \(h_\text{eq}\) is negative and the imaginary surface is below the real one.

May 2022 calls this "the piezometric head method": a piezometer tube (H2) fitted at the top of the water layer would show water rising to exactly this imaginary level.

Method 2 · split the pressure diagram

  1. Do: Draw the full pressure diagram and cut it into rectangles (uniform surcharge) and triangles (growth with depth in each layer). Each piece is a simple load: an even layer or a steady growth.
  2. Do: Find each piece's force (area of the piece × breadth) and its line of action (rectangle: middle; triangle: two-thirds of the way from the zero end). Each piece's push sits where that piece balances.
  3. Do: Then combine by moments. The total push must turn the wall exactly as much as all the pieces together.

This is slide 27(b) applied piece by piece.

11Gates in equilibrium: moments about the hinge (Plane Surface supplement; review sheet) ~6 min

What is this?

A gate on a hinge is a door with water pushing on it. Whether it stays shut depends not only on how hard the water pushes but on where: a push far from the hinge turns the door more. Take moments about the hinge, and the hinge's own unknown force drops out, because it acts right at the pivot.

The supplement opens with the principle used for every structure in this chapter (a gate, a wall, a dam):

Result · static equilibrium of a structure \[\sum\text{Forces}=0\qquad\sum\text{Moments}=0\]

In words: a structure at rest under hydrostatic force, its own weight and support reactions has no net force and no net moment. The review sheet adds: take moments about a point of convenience, for a gate the hinge, because the unknown hinge reaction then has no moment.

Ready answer · gate recipe (H3.4)
  1. Do: Sketch the gate (side view) with the free surface, hinge, stops and every given dimension. Every depth and every moment arm comes from these dimensions.
  2. Do: FBD of the gate alone: each hydrostatic resultant \(F_R\) (perpendicular to the gate, at its centre of pressure), the gate's weight at its centroid if given, the applied force or stop reaction, and the hinge reaction (two unknown components at the hinge). Each pressure diagram is replaced by its single equivalent push (section 3), so the gate becomes an ordinary statics problem.
  3. Do: Find each \(F_R=\rho gh_cA\) and \(h_R\) (or \(y_R\)); turn \(h_R\) into a moment arm about the hinge, measured perpendicular to the line of the force (along the gate for a plane gate). A push turns the door by force × distance from the hinge; depth below the surface is not yet that distance.
  4. Do: \(\sum M_\text{hinge}=0\) gives the unknown force or moment. State its direction or sense. The hinge reaction acts at the pivot, so it has no moment and only one unknown is left.
  5. Do: If asked, \(\sum F_x=0\), \(\sum F_y=0\) give the hinge reaction. With every other force known, force balance leaves only the hinge components.
Example · written for these notes · holding a gate shut

The vertical gate of section 9 (2 m high, 1 m wide, top edge 1 m below the surface) is hinged along its top edge, with air on the other side. What horizontal force \(P\), applied at its bottom edge, holds it closed? What does the hinge carry?

  1. From section 9: \(F_R=39.24\u{kN}\), acting 1.167 m below the hinge (the top edge).
  2. Water pushes the gate towards the air side; \(P\) must push back, at 2 m below the hinge.
  3. \(\sum M_\text{hinge}=0\): \(P\times2=39.24\times1.167\), so \(P=22.89\u{kN}\).
  4. \(\sum F_x=0\): hinge reaction \(=39.24-22.89=16.35\u{kN}\), pointing towards the water (it pulls the top of the gate back). \(P\) is smaller than the water's push, so the hinge must supply the rest in the same direction as \(P\).
\[\boxed{P=22.9\u{kN},\quad\text{hinge }16.4\u{kN}}\]

Gate weight is ignored here (a vertical gate's weight acts along the line of the hinge, so it has no moment about it anyway).

Self-opening gates and the "resultant passes through the hinge" idea

What is this? (self-opening gate)

Think of a door with water on one side and its pivot part-way down. If the single push lands above the pivot it turns the gate one way; if it lands below, the other way. As the water rises, the push creeps up towards the centroid, so at some water level it crosses the pivot and the gate swings open.

Many exam gates are automatic: hinged somewhere along their length, they stay shut while the resultant passes on one side of the hinge and swing open once it passes to the other side. The limiting case is when the resultant passes exactly through the hinge (zero moment). So "at what water level does it open?" or "what range of hinge heights lets it open?" means: find where the centre of pressure is and compare it with the hinge.

Two ideas make these quick:

  • As the water rises, the centre of pressure on a fixed gate moves up towards the centroid (section 8). A gate pivoted a little below its centroid opens when \(h_R\) rises above the pivot.
  • A gate pivoted exactly at its centroid (a "butterfly" gate turning about its centre) always has CP below the pivot, a distance \(I_c/(y_cA)\) down the gate. Its moment about the centre is
\[F_R\,(y_R-y_c)=\rho g\,I_c\sin\theta\]

which does not depend on the depth at all: the water's turning effect on a centre-pivoted gate is the same however high the water is (as long as the gate is fully submerged). Something (a stop, an applied moment) must always hold it.

Section 20 (group work) works the supplement's exercise: the same gate as a rectangle, a circle and a triangle, held by a moment at its top hinge.

You can now: draw the free-body diagram of a gate; find the force or moment that holds it from \(\sum M_\text{hinge}=0\) and the hinge reaction from force balance; find when a self-opening gate opens.

12Curved surfaces: horizontal and vertical components (slides 41–43) ~10 min

What is this?

On a curved gate the small pushes all point in different directions, so they cannot be added as plain numbers. Instead, split the water's push into a sideways part and an up-or-down part. The sideways part is the push the water would put on a flat vertical wall the size of the gate's shadow. The downward part is the weight of the water sitting on top of the gate, all the way up to the surface.

Slide 41 lists what changes when the surface is curved (a radial gate, a rounded tank corner, a cylinder):

  • the direction of the resultant force is not known in advance (a priori): each little pressure force is perpendicular to the surface, and on a curve these directions all differ. The direction must be found as part of the problem;
  • no new formulas are needed: everything comes from the plane-surface results and the weight of some fluid;
  • the resultant is found through its horizontal and vertical components, \(F_H\) and \(F_V\).

The trick: a free-body diagram of the fluid (slide 42)

Slide 42 draws a tank whose wall has a curved part AC (an S-shaped bend). Draw the horizontal line through A and the vertical line through C; they meet at B. AB (length \(a\)) is the horizontal projection of the curved surface and BC (length \(b\)) is its vertical projection: the "shadows" the curve casts on a horizontal and on a vertical plane. The liquid inside ABC is a fluid block, and it is at rest, so the forces on it balance.

The forces on the block ABC (slide 42's free-body diagram, per unit width):

  • \(F_1\) on the top face AB: the pressure of the liquid above, pushing down. It is a uniform load equal to the weight of the liquid above AB (a "surcharge load").
  • \(W\): the weight of the block itself, acting down through its centre of gravity.
  • \(F_2\) on the vertical face BC: the pressure of the liquid on the right, pushing left. It is the force on a plane vertical surface of height \(b\), so section 5 and section 8 give its size and position.
  • The force from the curved surface on the block, with components \(F_H\) and \(F_V\). By Newton's third law the fluid pushes on the surface with the same components in the opposite directions.
\[\begin{aligned}\textstyle\sum F_x=0:&\quad F_H=F_2\\ \textstyle\sum F_y=0:&\quad F_V=F_1+W\end{aligned}\]

\(F_1+W\) is the weight of the block plus the weight of all the liquid above it: the weight of all the liquid vertically above AC. This gives slide 42's two statements, written out as a rule on slide 43:

Result · force on a curved surface (slide 43) · memorise \[F_H=\rho g\,h_{c,\text{proj}}\,A_\text{proj}\] \[F_V=\rho g\,V_\text{above}\] \[F_R=\sqrt{F_H^2+F_V^2},\qquad \phi=\tan^{-1}\frac{F_V}{F_H}\]

In words: horizontal component = force on the plane area formed by projecting the curved surface onto a vertical plane, acting at the centre of pressure of that projection. Vertical component = weight of the entire column of liquid (physical or imaginary) above the curved surface, up to the level of the free surface, acting through the centroid of that column. Resultant = Pythagoras of the two, at angle \(\phi\) to the horizontal.

Reading the formula
\(F_H\)
the sideways push on the curve.
\(h_{c,\text{proj}}A_\text{proj}\)
depth of the centroid and area of the curve's shadow on a vertical wall: sideways, the curve is pushed exactly like that flat shadow.
\(F_V=\rho g V_\text{above}\)
the weight of the liquid column standing on the curve, up to the free-surface level.
\(\sqrt{F_H^2+F_V^2}\)
the two parts are at right angles, so they combine like the sides of a right-angled triangle.
\(\phi\)
how steeply the total push is tilted from the horizontal.

Slide 43's figure 2.14 makes two more points. In (a) a closed curved body under water has a vertical projection; the horizontal forces on the two sides of a closed body are equal and opposite (they act on the same projection). In (b) the free-body diagram of the fluid above a curved surface ab shows why the column goes all the way up: the column is cut at c–b into a lower part (weight \(W_2\)) and an upper part (weight \(W_1\)), the horizontal forces \(F_1\) on the two sides of the upper column cancel, and above the free surface d–e the air (weight \(W_\text{air}\)) is ignored because we use gauge pressure. Only the weight of liquid counts.

Result · circular arcs (slide 43)

On a curved surface that is a circular arc, the resultant force always passes through the centre of the circle.

Why: every little pressure force is perpendicular to the arc, and a line perpendicular to a circle is a radius. So every little force points along a radius, through the centre. Forces that all pass through one point have a resultant through that point (none of them has a moment about it).

This is a gift in gate problems: you know one point on the line of action without any centroid calculation. Moments of \(F_H\) and \(F_V\) about the centre cancel exactly.

Watch

\(F_V\) is the weight of liquid above the surface up to the free-surface level, not just the liquid in some region near the curve. If the tank has gas pressure on top, add the equivalent depth \(p/(\rho g)\) (section 10) before computing the column.

Example · written for these notes · a tank with a rounded corner (water above the curve)

The bottom right-hand corner of a long open tank is a quarter-circle of radius \(R=2\) m, joining the vertical wall to the flat floor. The water is 4 m deep. Find the force on the curved corner per metre length, and show it passes through the centre of the circle.

  1. Horizontal. The vertical projection of the arc is a 2 m high strip of the wall from depth 2 m to depth 4 m; its centroid is at \(h_c=3\) m. \(F_H=9.81\times3\times(2\times1)=58.86\u{kN}\), to the right (towards the wall). It acts at the centre of pressure of the strip: \(h_R=3+\dfrac{1\times2^3/12}{3\times2}=3.111\u{m}\).
  2. Vertical. The water above the arc up to the surface: a 2 m × 2 m rectangle (from the arc's top level, depth 2 m, up to the surface) plus the quarter-disc region between the arc and that level, area \(\tfrac14\pi R^2=\pi\). Volume per metre \(=4+\pi=7.142\u{m^3}\). The downward push is the weight of all the water standing on the arc, right up to the surface, not just the water next to the curve. \(F_V=9.81\times7.142=70.06\u{kN}\), downward (real water sits on the curve).
  3. Resultant.
\[\begin{aligned}F_R&=\sqrt{58.86^2+70.06^2}\\&=91.50\u{kN}\end{aligned}\] \[\phi=\tan^{-1}\frac{70.06}{58.86}=50.0°\] \[\boxed{F_R=91.5\u{kN/m}\text{ at }50.0°}\]

below the horizontal, pointing down and towards the wall, along a radius through the centre C.

line of action of \(F_V\) and the check that \(F_R\) passes through C

Put the wall at \(x=0\) and measure \(x\) to the left. The rectangle (area 4) has its centroid 1 m from the wall.

The quarter disc (area \(\pi\)) is bounded by the vertical line through C (\(x=2\)) and the horizontal line through C. Its centroid is \(4R/(3\pi)=0.849\) m from each straight edge: \(x=2-0.849=1.151\) m from the wall. Quarter disc: the semicircle centroid of section 6 applies in each direction.

\[\bar x=\frac{4(1)+\pi(1.151)}{4+\pi}=1.067\u{m}\] Area-weighted average of the two centroids: the column's centroid, where \(F_V\) acts.

Moments about C, which is 2 m from the wall and 2 m above the floor:

\(F_H\) acts \(4-3.111=0.889\) m above the floor, so \(2-0.889=1.111\) m below C: moment \(58.86\times1.111=65.4\u{kN\,m}\) anticlockwise. A rightward push below C turns anticlockwise about C.

\(F_V\) acts \(2-1.067=0.933\) m from C towards the wall: moment \(70.06\times0.933=65.4\u{kN\,m}\) clockwise. A downward push to the right of C turns clockwise.

Net moment about C = 0 ✓, so the resultant passes through C, as slide 43 promises.

FBD: the curved corner (taken as a free body) carries \(F_H\) to the right at 3.111 m depth and \(F_V\) down 1.067 m from the wall; together they make \(F_R\) along the radius through C. The tank's supports must supply the opposite force.

13Water underneath: force on the underside of a curved surface (Curved Surface supplement) ~7 min

What is this?

Now the water is under the curved gate, pushing it up and out, and there is no water on top to weigh. But pressure depends only on depth. If the same curve had water on top up to the same level, every point would feel the same pressure, just from the other side. So the upward push equals the weight of that imaginary column of water above the gate.

The supplement "Hydrostatic Force Acting on the Underside of a Curved Surface" answers the question slide 43 leaves open: what if the liquid is below the curve, so there is no real liquid above it?

The set-up (supplement example). Liquid stands to the left of a vertical wall, open to the air. The wall stops at A. From A a quarter-circle gate AB curves outwards (into the air on the right) and down to B on the floor. O, the centre of the arc, is on the floor directly below A. The liquid fills the quarter-circle space AOB under the gate, so it pushes up and outwards on the gate; air is above and to the right. Find the resultant force \(F_R\) on a unit length of the gate.

The supplement takes the free-body diagram of the fluid block AOB (O is the centre of the arc) and works in six steps:

  1. Do: Resolve \(F_R\) (the force of the fluid on the gate) into \(F_H\) and \(F_V\). The gate pushes back on the block with \(F_H\) to the left and \(F_V\) downward. The curve's push has no fixed direction, but each of its two parts can be found from a simple balance.
  2. Do: Horizontal equilibrium of the block: \(F_H=F_x\), where \(F_x\) is the force of the surrounding liquid on the vertical face AO. AO is the vertical projection of AB, so \(F_x\) comes from the plane-surface formulas. The block AOB is at rest, so the sideways push from the gate cancels the push on its flat vertical face: a plane-wall problem.
  3. Do: Vertical equilibrium: \(F_V=F_y-W_{AOB}\), where \(F_y\) is the upward force on the horizontal face OB and \(W_{AOB}\) is the weight of the block. Upward, the floor pushes; downward, the block's weight and the gate push; at rest these balance.
  4. Do: What is \(F_y\)? The pressure on OB is \(\rho g\times\)(depth of the floor). So \(F_y=\rho g\times\text{depth}\times\overline{OB}\times1\) = \(\rho g\times\)(volume of a column on OB up to the free-surface level) = the weight of a fluid column, imagined, above OB (the column CDBO, with C and D at the free-surface level above O and B). OB is flat and level, so it carries an even pressure: the weight of a column standing on it up to the surface level (section 9).
  5. Do: So \(F_V=W_{CDBO}-W_{AOB}=W_{CADB}\): the weight of an imaginary column of fluid filling the space above the gate AB up to the free-surface level (C–D), acting upward through the centre of mass of that imaginary column. A column on OB minus the water in AOB leaves exactly the space above the gate.
  6. Do: Finally \(F_R=\sqrt{F_H^2+F_V^2}\), at \(\phi=\tan^{-1}(F_V/F_H)\) to the horizontal; since AB is a circular arc, \(F_R\) passes through the centre O. On an arc every small push points along a radius, so the total does too.
Result · liquid below the curved surface (supplement summary)

Horizontal component \(F_H\) = force \(F_x\) on AO, the projection of AB onto a vertical plane, acting through the centre of pressure of \(F_x\).

Vertical component \(F_V\) = weight of fluid vertically above AB, on imagining that the space is occupied by the same fluid to the same height, acting upward through the centre of mass of this imaginary fluid.

In words: the same rule as slide 43, with two changes: the liquid column above the curve is imaginary, and the force points up. Liquid above the curve pushes it down; liquid below pushes it up; the size is the same "weight of liquid up to the free-surface level" either way.

Why the imaginary column works: if the same curve had liquid on top instead (up to the same level), the pressure at every point of the curve would be exactly the same (it depends only on depth), so the force would be the same size, just from the other side. With liquid on top, the force is the weight of that liquid.

Example · written for these notes · numbers for the supplement's gate

In the supplement's set-up, take the arc radius \(R=2\) m and let A be 3 m below the free surface (so the floor is 5 m deep). Find the force on the gate per metre length.

  1. Horizontal. Projection AO: vertical, 2 m high, from depth 3 m to 5 m, centroid at 4 m. \(F_H=9.81\times4\times2=78.48\u{kN}\), to the right. Centre of pressure: \(4+\dfrac{2^3/12}{4\times2}=4.083\u{m}\) deep.
  2. Vertical, the long way (block AOB): \(F_y=9.81\times5\times2=98.1\u{kN}\) up on OB; \(W_{AOB}=9.81\times\tfrac14\pi(2^2)=30.82\u{kN}\); \(F_V=98.1-30.82=67.28\u{kN}\).
  3. Vertical, the short way (imaginary column CADB): rectangle 2 m × 5 m minus the quarter disc, \(10-\pi=6.858\u{m^3}\) per metre; \(F_V=9.81\times6.858=67.28\u{kN}\), upward ✓.
  4. Resultant.
\[\begin{aligned}F_R&=\sqrt{78.48^2+67.28^2}\\&=103.4\u{kN}\end{aligned}\] \[\phi=\tan^{-1}\frac{67.28}{78.48}=40.6°\] \[\boxed{F_R=103.4\u{kN/m}\text{ at }40.6°}\]

above the horizontal, pushing up and to the right, along a radius through O.

check that the resultant passes through O

Line of \(F_V\): centroid of the imaginary column = rectangle (area 10, centroid 1 m right of the wall line) minus quarter disc (area \(\pi\), centroid \(4R/(3\pi)=0.849\) m right of it).

\[\bar x=\frac{10(1)-\pi(0.849)}{10-\pi}=1.069\u{m}\] Subtracting a shape: use negative area in the weighted average.

Moments about O: \(F_H\) acts \(5-4.083=0.917\) m above O, moment \(78.48\times0.917=72.0\u{kN\,m}\) clockwise; \(F_V\) acts 1.069 m right of O, moment \(67.28\times1.069=71.9\u{kN\,m}\) anticlockwise. Equal within rounding: net moment about O is zero, so \(F_R\) passes through O.

Watch

The commonest error with liquid below: taking \(F_V\) as the weight of the liquid in the quarter circle (\(W_{AOB}\) alone), or drawing it downward. The supplement's step 3 shows \(W_{AOB}\) is only a correction: the main term is the pressure on the floor OB.

You can now: find the upward \(F_V\) on a curved surface with liquid below from the imaginary column up to the free-surface level; check it by the block method.

14Circular arcs: try it, and what exam gates look like ~3 min

What is this?

A quarter-circle gate is a slice of a round drum. Every small push on it is square to the curve, so it points straight at the centre of the circle, like a spoke of a wheel. So the single push also passes through the centre, and that fixes its line without any centroid work.

Exam curved surfaces are almost always quarter circles (a radial gate, a rounded corner, part of a cylinder). Sections 12 and 13 give everything you need: \(F_H\) from the projection, \(F_V\) from the (real or imaginary) column, and the resultant through the centre. Move the sliders to see how the pieces change.

Try it · force on a quarter-circle surface

Defaults reproduce section 12 (\(R=2\), top 2 m deep, liquid above). Choose "below" and set the top depth to 3 m for section 13. The shaded column is the real (blue) or imaginary (dashed) liquid whose weight is \(F_V\); the red arrow is \(F_R\), always along a radius. Ready answer · curved surface (H3.5)

  2. Do: Shade the column between the curve and the free-surface level; decide real (liquid on top, \(F_V\) down) or imaginary (liquid below, \(F_V\) up). 15Two containers: the hydrostatic paradox (Some Concept Questions, pages 1–8) ~5 min

What is this?

Two containers hold water to the same depth on the same floor area, but one holds far less water. The floors feel the same push, because pressure depends only on depth. Where does the extra push come from in the container with less water? From its ceiling: the water presses up on it, and it presses back down on the water.

The concept-question handout asks a sequence of questions about two containers. Both are filled to the same depth and have the same bottom area. One is an ordinary straight-sided container. The other is a "T" turned upside down: a wide, low chamber at the bottom with a thin tube rising from the middle of its ceiling, so it holds much less water.

  1. Straight container: what is the force on the bottom? The handout's answer: the weight of fluid in the container. Here pressure × area and weight agree: \(\rho g h A=\rho g(hA)=\rho gV\).
  2. Narrow-necked container: what is the force on the bottom? The handout writes "Weight of fluid in the container?" with a question mark: the obvious answer is wrong here.
  3. Which container has the larger force on the bottom? Answer: the force is the same. Pressure on the bottom is \(\rho gh\) in both (same depth), and the area is the same, so \(F=\rho ghA\) in both (section 9, horizontal surface).
  4. Why the same force, despite the different volumes of fluid? See below.

The resolution is a free-body diagram of the water in the narrow-necked container. The water is pushed up by the bottom with force \(\rho ghA\). It is pulled down by its own weight, which is much smaller. The difference is supplied by the ceiling of the wide chamber: the water presses up on the ceiling with pressure \(\rho g\times\)(depth of the ceiling), and the ceiling presses back down on the water with the same force. So

\[F_\text{bottom}=W_\text{water}+F_\text{ceiling}\]

In words: force on the bottom = weight of the water + downward push of the chamber's ceiling. In the straight container there is no ceiling and the bottom force is just the weight. The pressure depends only on depth, not on how much water is "above" in a sideways sense: this is the hydrostatic paradox (beyond the handouts: the name is the textbook one).

Example · written for these notes · numbers for the two containers

Both containers: water depth 0.5 m, bottom area 0.2 m². The narrow-necked one has a chamber 0.05 m tall and a neck 0.45 m tall holding 0.01 m³, so its total volume is 0.02 m³ (neck area 0.0222 m²).

  1. Bottom force, both: \(9810\times0.5\times0.2=981\u{N}\).
  2. Straight container: water weight \(9810\times(0.5\times0.2)=981\u{N}\) = bottom force ✓.
  3. Narrow-necked: water weight \(9810\times0.02=196.2\u{N}\). Ceiling at depth 0.45 m, area \(0.2-0.0222=0.178\u{m^2}\): \(F_\text{ceiling}=9810\times0.45\times0.178\) \(=784.8\u{N}\).
  4. Check: \(196.2+784.8=981\u{N}\) ✓.

What would a scale under each container read? The weight of container plus water, not the bottom force. In the narrow-necked one, the water pushes down on the bottom with 981 N but up on the ceiling with 784.8 N; the container feels the difference, 196.2 N, which is the water's weight. Nothing is created from nothing.

The same idea is behind \(F_V\) on curved surfaces: the vertical force on a surface is the weight of the liquid column above it up to the free-surface level, real or imagined, not the weight of the liquid that happens to be in the container. The rest of "Some Concept Questions" (the melting iceberg) is in H4.

You can now: show that the force on a container's bottom is \(\rho ghA\) whatever its shape; explain the difference from the water's weight with a free-body diagram of the water.

16Beyond the handouts: a block on a slope, with water behind and under it (older course ENGG1205) ~4 min

What is this?

Water behind a block on a slope does two bad things. It pushes the block down the slope, and water seeping under the block lifts it a little, so the block presses less on the slope and friction can grip less. The block slides if the push down the slope beats the most friction available.

Beyond the handouts: two papers of the older course ENGG1205 (2014–15 Sem 1 and 2016–17 Sem 1, same lecturer) ask whether a block resting on a slope slides when water gets behind and under it. Friction and seepage uplift are not in the 2026 handouts, although the review sheet does list "equilibrium of a hydraulic structure… balance of forces". It may or may not be examined: check Moodle. The type is H3.6 in the guide.

Definitions · friction, uplift (beyond the handouts)
  • Normal reaction \(R\): the push of the slope on the block, perpendicular to the slope.
  • Friction: the slope's grip along the contact. It can take any value up to a maximum \(\mu R\), where \(\mu\) is the coefficient of friction. If the force trying to slide the block is bigger than \(\mu R\), the block slides.
  • Seepage uplift: water that seeps into the fine gaps under the block presses up on its base. The papers assume this pressure falls linearly from the full hydrostatic value at the back to zero at the front.
Example · written for these notes · does the block slide?

A concrete block (relative density 2.2) of rectangular cross-section rests on a slope at \(\alpha=20°\). Its base BC, along the slope, is 1.0 m long; its back face AB, perpendicular to the slope, is 0.6 m. A gap behind AB is full of water up to A. Water seeps under the base: the uplift pressure falls linearly from its value at B to zero at C. The pressure on AB is zero at A. Coefficient of friction \(\mu=0.5\). Per 1 m length, does the block slide?

  1. Pressure at B. AB is perpendicular to the slope, so it is tilted 20° from vertical: B is \(0.6\cos20°=0.564\) m below A. \(p_B=9.81\times0.6\cos20°=5.531\u{kPa}\).
  2. Thrust on AB (triangle, perpendicular to AB, so parallel to the slope, pushing downslope): \(F_{AB}=\tfrac12\times5.531\times0.6=1.659\u{kN}\).
  3. Uplift on BC (triangle, perpendicular to the slope, pushing the block away from it): \(F_{BC}=\tfrac12\times5.531\times1.0=2.766\u{kN}\).
  4. Weight: \(W=2.2\times9.81\times(0.6\times1.0)\) \(=12.949\u{kN}\); components \(W\cos20°=12.168\) into the slope and \(W\sin20°=4.429\u{kN}\) downslope.
  5. Normal reaction (perpendicular to the slope): \(R=W\cos20°-F_{BC}\) \(=12.168-2.766=9.402\u{kN}\). The uplift takes away part of how hard the block presses on the slope, and friction can grip only in proportion to that. Maximum friction \(\mu R=0.5\times9.402=4.701\u{kN}\).
  6. Driving force down the slope: \(F_{AB}+W\sin20°\) \(=1.659+4.429=6.088\u{kN}\).
\[6.088\gt 4.701\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\text{the block slides}}\]

Without the water: friction available \(0.5\times12.168=6.084\u{kN}\) against a driving force of 4.429 kN, so the dry block stays put. The water does damage twice: it pushes (thrust on AB) and it lifts (uplift on BC reduces \(R\), and with it the friction).

You can now: check whether a block on a slope slides, including the water thrust behind it and the seepage uplift under it.

21Concept checks

Written for these notes.

1 · Deeper means closer

A vertical square plate, 1 m × 1 m, is lowered from "top edge at the surface" to "top edge 9 m deep". How far below its centroid is the centre of pressure in each case?

Hint

\(h_R-h_c=I_c/(h_cA)\) with \(I_c=1/12\).

Answer

At the surface: \(h_c=0.5\), shift \(=(1/12)/0.5=0.0556\u{m}\) (CP at 0.667 m, the two-thirds rule). At 9 m: \(h_c=9.5\), shift \(=0.00877\u{m}\). Deep down the pressure is nearly uniform over the plate, so CP is almost at the centroid.

2 · Doubling the depth behind a dam

A vertical dam face holds water 10 m deep. Find the force per metre. If the depth doubles, by what factor do the force and its moment about the base grow?

Answer

\(F=\tfrac12\rho gh^2\) \(=\tfrac12(9.81)(10^2)=490.5\u{kN/m}\). \(F\propto h^2\): doubling gives ×4. The arm about the base is \(h/3\), so the moment \(\propto h^3\): ×8. This is why dams are much thicker at the bottom.

3 · A big lake or a narrow channel

Two identical dams hold water 10 m deep. One holds back a lake 10 km long; the other holds a channel only 1 m long behind it. Which dam feels the larger force?

Answer

The same. Pressure depends only on depth, so the force on the face depends only on the depth and the face area, not on how much water is behind it. (Same idea as the two containers, section 15.)

4 · Tilting a gate

A rectangular gate is turned about its centroid from vertical to 45°, so its centroid stays at the same depth. What happens to \(F_R\) and to the centre of pressure?

Answer

\(F_R=\rho gh_cA\) is unchanged (same \(h_c\), same \(A\)). The depth shift \(I_c\sin^2\theta/(h_cA)\) halves (\(\sin^245°=\tfrac12\)), so CP moves up towards the centroid. The direction of \(F_R\) turns with the gate (always perpendicular to it).

5 · A closed body under water

A long submerged cylinder lies on its side, completely surrounded by water. What is the net horizontal hydrostatic force on it? The net vertical force?

Answer

Horizontal: zero. Each side's horizontal force equals the force on the same vertical projection, in opposite directions (slide 43, figure a). Vertical: the upper surface is pushed down by the real water above it; the lower surface is pushed up by a (real-or-imaginary) column reaching the same free surface. The difference is the weight of water the size of the cylinder, pushing up: Archimedes' buoyancy, the subject of H4.

6 · Which way does FV point?

For each case, is \(F_V\) on the curved surface up or down: (a) the rounded corner at the bottom of a tank; (b) a quarter-circle gate bulging out of a wall with water under it; (c) the top half of a submerged pipe's wall?

Answer

(a) down: real water above the curve. (b) up: water below, imaginary column. (c) down on the outer surface: the water above the pipe presses on it.
