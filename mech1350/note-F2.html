MECH1350 · Fluid dynamics · chapter 2

The continuity equation

0Where this chapter sits

Put your thumb over the end of a garden hose and the water squirts faster. The water per second coming down the hose must all get out through the small gap under your thumb, so it has to go faster. This chapter turns that, and the plain fact that what goes in must come out, into the first conservation law of flow: mass. F1 described flows in words (steady, uniform, viscous, laminar); F3 adds energy (the Bernoulli equation) and F4 momentum (forces from jets).

  2. §2 A system is whatever you draw a line around. Closed: no mass crosses. Open: mass crosses.

  9. §9 At a junction flow rates add; speeds do not. Example 3.
  10. §10 The garden-hose nozzle: the thumb in numbers. Example 4.

You need: F1's words for flows (steady, streamline, incompressible), the area of a circle and unit conversions. In the exam: continuity is the first step of most Section C questions (the speed at each section), plus short flow-rate questions and control-volume or streamline sketches. See the F2 exam guide.

Conventions for this chapter
  • Sections are numbered along the flow: section 1 is upstream (where the fluid comes from), section 2 downstream. In a branching pipe, number the branches as the question does.
  • Every area \(A\) is the cross-section perpendicular to the flow (for a round pipe, \(A=\pi D^2/4\) with \(D\) the inner diameter).
  • \(V\) is the average velocity over that cross-section, always positive; the direction ("in" or "out" of the control volume) is shown by which side of the equation the term is on, not by a sign.
  • Steady flow (nothing changes with time) and one-dimensional inlets and outlets (one speed per section) are assumed throughout, unless a question says otherwise.
  • All lengths in metres before you calculate: 10 cm = 0.1 m, 50 mm = 0.05 m, 1 cm² = \(10^{-4}\) m², 1 L = \(10^{-3}\) m³.
  • Section C papers state their own data: \(\rho_\text{water}=1000\u{kg/m^3}\), \(g=9.81\) (some older papers \(9.8\)) m/s². Use the paper's values.

1Fluid mechanics: statics versus flow ~2 min

What is this?

Before you can say how well a hose, a pump or a wing works, you need rules that always hold, whatever the shape. Those rules are conservation laws: some quantity, such as mass, is never created or destroyed, only moved around. Water standing still in a bucket is easy: its depth and density give the pressure everywhere. Water rushing through a hose is harder, because its speed changes from place to place and depends on the hose's shape and on what happens at its ends.

Fluid-mechanics problems usually use conservation laws (laws that say some quantity, such as mass or energy, is neither created nor destroyed) to work out how well a device performs. Compare the two halves of the course:

  • Statics problems (Section B, chapters H1–H4: fluid at rest) need only the density of the fluid and the position of the free surface. From those two, \(p=\rho gh\) gives the pressure everywhere.
  • Flow problems need much more: the fluid's motion can vary from point to point and moment to moment, and is set by the geometry (the shape of the pipe, wing or machine), the boundary conditions (what happens at the walls, inlets and outlets: for example, fluid does not pass through a solid wall) and the laws of mechanics.

Three devices whose performance is worked out this way: an airfoil (a wing section, with streamlines bending over it), a pump (a spinning impeller that pushes fluid out, adding energy) and a turbine (fluid flowing through blades and turning a shaft, taking energy out).

The three devices. Arrows show the flow.

You can now: say why flow problems are solved with conservation laws; list what a statics problem needs (density, free surface) and what a flow problem needs (geometry, boundary conditions, laws of mechanics); and say what an airfoil, a pump and a turbine do to a flow.

2Systems, surroundings and boundaries ~3 min

What is this?

Before you count anything you must say what you are counting. Draw a line around the thing you care about: that is the system, and everything else is the surroundings. You could draw it around a sealed bottle of water, or around one metre of garden hose. Where the line goes is your choice, and a good choice makes the problem easy.

Definitions · system, surroundings, boundary
  • A system is a quantity of matter, or a region in space, that you choose to study.
  • Everything outside the system is the surroundings.
  • The real or imaginary surface separating the system from its surroundings is the boundary. A boundary can be fixed (it stays where it is) or movable (for example a piston that slides).

The point of the word "choose": the laws of physics apply to anything, so engineers draw the boundary wherever it makes the problem easiest. The same pipe can be studied as one system or chopped into several.

What is this? (closed and open systems)

A sealed bottle of water is a closed system: you can warm it in your hand, but no water gets in or out. A metre of garden hose with water running through it is an open system: water keeps crossing its two ends. For flows the open kind is the useful one, because the interesting thing is the water passing through.

Definitions · closed and open systems
  • A closed system, also called a control mass: a fixed amount of mass. No mass can cross its boundary (nothing enters or leaves), but energy can (heat and work).
  • An open system, also called a control volume: a well-chosen region in space, usually around a device with fluid flowing through it, such as a compressor, turbine or nozzle. Both mass and energy can cross its boundary.

A system, its boundary and the surroundings.

Gas in a cylinder under a piston is a closed system: mass cannot cross the boundary, energy can.

Analogy: a closed system is like following one particular group of tourists wherever they walk (the same people all the time). A control volume is like standing at the doors of a shop and counting who goes in and who comes out (the same place all the time, different people).

Watch

"Closed" does not mean "insulated". A closed system can be heated or cooled and can do work (the gas under the piston pushes the piston up). Only mass is shut in.

You can now: define system, surroundings and boundary; tell a closed system (control mass: no mass crosses, energy can) from an open system (control volume: mass and energy cross); and explain why closed does not mean insulated.

3Control volume and control surface ~5 min

What is this?

Picture a see-through box drawn around the nozzle end of a hose. The box does not move; water flows in through one face and out through another. That box is a control volume, and its faces are the control surface. Some faces are real walls (the inside of the nozzle); the faces across the water are imaginary, just lines you drew where you want to count.

Definition · control surface

The boundary of a control volume is called the control surface. Parts of it can be real (an actual wall) or imaginary (a surface drawn across the flow where no wall exists).

Here are two control volumes (CV), each with its boundary drawn as a dashed line:

  • (a) A nozzle (a pipe that narrows to speed the fluid up). The inner wall of the nozzle is the real boundary. The inlet and outlet openings are imaginary boundaries: flat surfaces across the flow, with no physical surface there. Fluid crosses them.
  • (b) A piston–cylinder device with an inlet pipe at the bottom. The cylinder walls and base are a fixed boundary; the underside of the piston is a moving boundary (it moves as gas flows in).

(a) A control volume with real and imaginary boundaries (a nozzle).

(b) A control volume with fixed and moving boundaries.

Definition · control volume

A control volume is a finite region chosen carefully by the analyst for a particular problem, with open boundaries through which mass, momentum and energy may cross.

What is this? (the budget)

Keep accounts for the box like a bank account: money in, money out, and the change in the balance. For the hose box the money is water. If more goes in than comes out, water piles up inside; if the flow is steady, nothing piles up, so what goes in must come out. The accounts give you the totals, not what each drop did inside.

The analyst makes a budget (a balance), like a bank statement: what came in, what went out, and the change in what is stored inside. From that budget you get the gross properties (overall quantities) of the device: the net force on it, its total power output, the total heat transfer. You do not find out the details of the flow inside the control volume, and you do not need them.

Result · the budget idea for any quantity \[\begin{aligned}&\text{rate of change stored inside}\\&\quad=\text{rate in}-\text{rate out}\end{aligned}\]

In words: whatever accumulates inside the control volume per second equals what enters per second minus what leaves per second. If the flow is steady, nothing accumulates, so in = out.

Reading the formula
rate of change stored inside
how fast the amount of water in the box grows (negative if the box is emptying).
rate in
water crossing the inlet faces per second.
rate out
water crossing the outlet faces per second.
steady flow
nothing inside changes, so the left side is 0 and in = out: the hose rule.
Ready answer · "sketch the control volume" (type F2.2)
  1. Do: Redraw the device and draw the control volume as a dashed line around it (or around the fluid in it). The dashed line is the box you keep accounts for; dashed shows it is drawn, not a wall.
  2. Do: Where fluid enters or leaves, let the dashed line cut straight across the stream, perpendicular to the flow, at the section where you know (or want) \(V\), \(A\) and \(p\). Number these sections as the question does. Water then crosses each face straight through, so the face's area is the true flow area and one speed describes it.
  3. Do: Draw and label an arrow for each inflow and outflow (\(V_1\), \(A_1\), …), and the axes if forces will be needed later. Each arrow is one entry in the budget: every term of the continuity equation comes from one of them.
  4. Do: If a support force is wanted later (F4: a scale, a bolt, a bracket), let the boundary cut through the support so that force appears on your sketch. Only forces acting where the boundary cuts something enter the box's force budget, so the support must be cut to count.
  5. Do: One sentence: "Steady flow; the control volume is fixed; mass enters at 1 and leaves at 2 and 3." It states the assumptions that let you write in = out.

You can now: define control surface and control volume, with real and imaginary, fixed and moving boundaries; write the budget stored = in − out and reduce it to in = out for steady flow; and sketch a control volume for an exam device.

4Control volume versus differential analysis ~3 min

What is this?

There are two ways to study the water in a hose. You could follow one drop and track every twist of its journey, which needs detailed calculus. Or you could stand at the nozzle with a bucket and a stopwatch and simply count what passes. The second is the control-volume way, the one this course uses: it gives the totals without the drop-by-drop detail.

There are two ways of describing a flow:

  • Following the fluid. Track the individual "chunks" (small parcels) of fluid as they move, and describe the flow pattern in detail at every point \((x,y,z)\). This is the differential approach; it needs calculus on small elements and belongs to a later fluid-mechanics course.
  • The control-volume approach (the one this course uses). Fix a region, let fluid move through it, and do the budget of mass, momentum and energy across its boundary, without working out what every fluid particle does.

Picture a fixed control volume (dotted outline) and a system (a fixed lump of fluid) that fills it at time \(t\). A moment later, at time \(t+\mathrm dt\), the system has moved along with the flow velocity \(\vec V\) and only partly overlaps the control volume: some of its fluid has left through the outlet side, and new fluid has entered through the inlet side.

This picture is how the laws of mechanics (written for a system, Section 5) are turned into control-volume form: the change of the system's mass equals the change of what is in the CV plus what flowed out minus what flowed in during \(\mathrm dt\).

Going further · Lagrangian and Eulerian

"Following each fluid particle" is called the Lagrangian description and "watching fixed points or a fixed region" the Eulerian description; the formula that converts one into the other for a control volume is the Reynolds transport theorem. You do not need either name for MECH1350; just remember that the control-volume method is the easier way.

You can now: compare following the fluid (the differential approach) with the control-volume approach; and explain the picture of a system moving through a fixed control volume.

5The three basic laws of fluid mechanics ~4 min

What is this?

Three rules keep the accounts for any flow. Water is not created or destroyed (mass). A stream pushes on whatever it hits, as you feel when a hose jet hits your hand (momentum). A pump that drives the water must supply energy, which the water carries away (energy). Each rule is first written for a fixed lump of fluid, then turned into box form.

The laws of mechanics say what happens when a system interacts with its surroundings. There are three, each first written for a system (a fixed quantity of mass \(m\)).

Law 1 · conservation of mass \[m_\text{syst}=\text{const},\qquad\frac{\mathrm dm}{\mathrm dt}=0\]

In words: a system is a fixed amount of matter, so its mass never changes.

Law 2 · Newton's second law, the linear momentum relation \[\vec F=m\vec a=m\frac{\mathrm d\vec V}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(m\vec V)\] \[F_x=ma_x,\quad F_y=ma_y,\quad F_z=ma_z\]

In words: the net force from the surroundings equals the rate of change of the system's momentum (mass × velocity). It is a vector law: one equation for each direction.

Law 3 · energy relation, the first law of thermodynamics \[\delta Q-\delta W=\mathrm dE\] \[\dot Q-\dot W=\frac{\mathrm dE}{\mathrm dt}\]

In words: heat added to the system minus work done by the system equals the increase in the system's energy (per second, in the rate form). This is the same first law as in chapter T4.

Reading the formula
\(\delta Q\)
heat put into the lump of fluid.
\(-\,\delta W\)
work the lump does on its surroundings (pushing a piston or a turbine blade) is energy leaving it, hence the minus.
\(\mathrm dE\)
what is left over is stored: the rise in the lump's internal, kinetic and potential energy.
dots, per \(\mathrm dt\)
the same balance per second, as rates.

Here \(\delta Q\) is a small amount of heat added, \(\delta W\) a small amount of work done by the system, and \(E\) the system's total energy (internal + kinetic + potential). The \(\delta\) (rather than "d") reminds you that heat and work are amounts transferred, not properties the system "has".

The course puts these three laws into control-volume form, and each gives one of the main equations of Section C:

PrincipleEquation it producesChapter
conservation of massthe continuity equationF2 (this one)
conservation of energythe Bernoulli equationF3
conservation of linear momentumequations for the dynamic forces exerted by flowing fluids (jets on plates, bends)F4
Ready answer · "What are the fundamental physical laws that govern fluid flow?" (F2.2)

(i) Conservation of mass (gives the continuity equation); (ii) Newton's second law / conservation of linear momentum (gives the force on a body from a flow); (iii) conservation of energy, the first law of thermodynamics (gives the Bernoulli/energy equation). If the question asks for two (as ENGG1205 2013–14 did; its own answer: mass and momentum), give mass and momentum, and mention energy as the third.

You can now: state the three basic laws for a system (conservation of mass, Newton's second law, the first law of thermodynamics); match each to the equation it gives (continuity F2, forces F4, Bernoulli F3); and answer the fundamental-laws question.

6Flow rates and momentum flux ~5 min

What is this?

Hold a bucket under the hose and time how long it takes to fill. Litres per second is the volume flow rate. It depends on two things: how fast the water moves and how big the opening is. Fast water through a big opening fills the bucket quickest.

Set up a general control volume: a tank, a reservoir or a compartment inside a machine, with a number of one-dimensional inlets and outlets (each inlet or outlet is a pipe where the fluid crosses with one speed, straight across the opening). Sketch one with, say, two inlets and three outlets. For each inlet and outlet we write:

  • \(V\) = velocity of the fluid in the stream;
  • \(A\) = cross-sectional area of the stream;
  • \(p\) = pressure of the fluid in the stream;
  • \(\rho\) = density of the fluid.

Volume flow rate, or discharge

Definition · volume flow rate (discharge) \(Q\)

The volume flow rate, also called the discharge, is the volume of fluid crossing a section per unit time. Unit: m³/s (also L/s; 1 L/s = \(10^{-3}\) m³/s).

Result · discharge \[Q=VA\]

In words: volume flow rate = average speed × cross-sectional area.

Reading the formula
\(Q\)
how fast the bucket fills: cubic metres of water per second.
\(V\)
how far the water travels in one second.
\(A\)
the size of the opening, measured straight across the flow.
\(VA\)
the slab of water, \(A\) across and \(V\) long, that crosses in one second.
why \(Q=VA\)

1. Look at the fluid that is just about to cross a section of area \(A\). In a short time \(\Delta t\) it moves forward a distance \(V\Delta t\). Distance = speed × time.

2. All the fluid in the slab of length \(V\Delta t\) behind the section crosses it in that time. The slab's volume is \(A\times V\Delta t\). Volume of a prism = cross-section × length.

3. Volume per unit time: \(Q=AV\Delta t/\Delta t=VA\). Divide by the time.

4. Units: (m/s)(m²) = m³/s ✓. If the speed varies across the pipe (slower near the walls), \(V\) is defined as the average speed that gives the right \(Q\).

Mass flow rate

What is this? (mass flow rate)

Weigh the bucket instead of reading its volume: kilograms per second is the mass flow rate. For water each litre has a mass of about 1 kg, so the two numbers match. For a gas, whose density can change, mass is the one that is truly conserved.

Definition · mass flow rate \(\dot m\)

The mass flow rate is the mass of fluid crossing a section per unit time. Unit: kg/s.

Result · mass flow rate \[\dot m=\rho VA=\rho Q\]

In words: mass flow rate = density × volume flow rate (each cubic metre that crosses carries \(\rho\) kilograms).

Momentum flux

What is this? (momentum flux)

Put your hand in the hose jet and you feel a push. The jet carries momentum with it, and the momentum arriving per second is the momentum flux. A faster jet through the same opening pushes harder for two reasons: more mass arrives each second, and each kilogram is moving faster.

Definition · momentum flux

The momentum flux is the momentum of the fluid crossing a section per unit time:

\[\text{momentum flux}=\dot mV=\rho QV=\rho AV^2\]

In words: momentum flux = mass flow rate × velocity. Its unit is (kg/s)(m/s) = kg·m/s² = N, a force: it is the "push" a stream carries, used in F4 for forces on plates and bends.

Reading the formula
\(\dot m\)
kilograms of water reaching your hand each second.
\(V\)
how fast each of those kilograms is moving.
\(\dot mV\)
momentum arriving per second, which is a force in newtons.
\(\rho AV^2\)
\(V\) appears twice, once inside \(\dot m\) and once as the speed, so doubling the speed through the same opening makes the push four times larger.
Definition · flux in general (May 2019 Q1(c))

A flux is the amount of some quantity (volume, mass, momentum, energy, heat) carried across a surface per unit time. \(Q\) is a volume flux, \(\dot m\) a mass flux, \(\dot mV\) a momentum flux. (Some books also call flow per unit area a flux, as in heat flux W/m² in chapter T3; say which you mean.)

Going further · weight flow rate

Exam questions (Dec 2022, Dec 2023 Q1) also ask for the weight flow rate: the weight of fluid crossing a section per second, \(\dot W=\gamma Q=\rho gQ=\dot m g\), in N/s. The specific weight \(\gamma=\rho g\) (N/m³) is the weight of one cubic metre of fluid (water: about 9810 N/m³ = 9.81 kN/m³).

Example · the four flow measures

Water (\(\rho=1000\u{kg/m^3}\)) flows at \(V=3\u{m/s}\) through a section of area \(A=0.02\u{m^2}\). How much crosses in 2 s, and what are \(Q\), \(\dot m\) and the momentum flux?

  1. Slab volume in 2 s: \(AV\Delta t=0.02(3)(2)=0.12\u{m^3}\). The water that crosses in 2 s is the slab behind the section: at 3 m/s it is 6 m long.
  2. \(Q=VA=3(0.02)=0.06\u{m^3/s}\) (60 L/s).
  3. \(\dot m=\rho Q=1000(0.06)=60\u{kg/s}\).
  4. Momentum flux \(\dot mV=60(3)=180\u{N}\).

You can now: compute \(Q=VA\), \(\dot m=\rho Q\), the weight flow \(\gamma Q\) and the momentum flux \(\dot mV\) with the right units; and explain what a flux is.

7The continuity equation ~6 min

What is this?

Put your thumb over the end of a hose. The water per second coming down the hose must all get out through the gap under your thumb: it has nowhere else to go, and the hose does not fill up. The gap is smaller than the hose, so the water must go faster. That is the continuity equation: in steady flow, what goes in must come out.

If the flow is steady, the total mass of fluid inside the control volume cannot change with time. So the mass flowing in per second must exactly balance the mass flowing out per second:

Total mass outflow = Total mass inflow

Result · continuity equation, steady flow \[\sum_{i=1}^{M}(\rho_iV_iA_i)_\text{in}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\rho_iV_iA_i)_\text{out}\]

\(M\) = number of inlets, \(N\) = number of outlets.

In words: the sum of the mass flow rates \(\rho VA\) of all the inlets equals the sum over all the outlets.

Reading the formula
\(\rho_iV_iA_i\)
kilograms per second through one opening: density × speed × area.
\(\sum_\text{in}\), \(\sum_\text{out}\)
add every opening where water enters, and every opening where it leaves; there can be any number of each.
\(=\)
steady flow: nothing piles up inside the box, so the two totals must match.
from "mass is conserved" to the continuity equation

1. Budget for mass in the control volume (Section 3): rate of change of mass inside = mass in per second − mass out per second. Mass is neither created nor destroyed (Law 1).

2. Each inlet brings \(\rho_iV_iA_i\) kg/s; each outlet removes \(\rho_iV_iA_i\) kg/s. Section 6: \(\dot m=\rho VA\).

3. Steady flow: nothing inside changes, so the rate of change of mass inside is 0. Definition of steady.

4. Therefore \(0=\sum\dot m_\text{in}-\sum\dot m_\text{out}\), which is the equation above.

Incompressible fluids

What is this? (incompressible)

You cannot squash water into a hose: a litre stays a litre. So for water you can count litres instead of kilograms. Air can be squashed, but when it moves slowly it hardly is, so it behaves the same way.

Definition · incompressible

A fluid is incompressible if its density \(\rho\) stays effectively constant, whatever the pressure. Liquids such as water are treated as incompressible; gases are too when they move slowly (F1: Mach number below about 0.3).

If \(\rho\) is the same at every inlet and outlet, it cancels from both sides:

Result · continuity, incompressible steady flow \[\sum_{i=1}^{M}(V_iA_i)_\text{in}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(V_iA_i)_\text{out}\] \[\text{or}\quad\sum(Q_i)_\text{in}=\sum(Q_i)_\text{out}\]

For a single pipe with one inlet (1) and one outlet (2):

\[Q=V_1A_1=V_2A_2=\text{constant}\]

In words: for water, the volume flowing in per second equals the volume flowing out per second; along one pipe the discharge is the same at every section, so where the pipe is narrower the water must go faster.

Reading the formula
\(V_1A_1\)
litres per second coming down the hose.
\(V_2A_2\)
litres per second leaving through the gap under your thumb.
\(=\text{constant}\)
the same at every section of one pipe, because no water can pile up between them.
small \(A_2\)
forces a large \(V_2\): the thumb on the hose.

Picture a pipe that runs horizontally and then bends upwards, with section (1) across the horizontal part and section (2) across the sloping part, both drawn perpendicular to the pipe. The same \(Q\) passes both.

Result · velocity and diameter in a round pipe \[\frac{V_2}{V_1}=\frac{A_1}{A_2}=\left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)^{2}\]

In words: speed is inversely proportional to area, so inversely proportional to the square of the diameter. Halve the diameter and the speed becomes four times as large.

Reading the formula
\(V_2/V_1\)
how many times faster the water leaves than it arrives.
\(A_1/A_2\)
how many times bigger the hose opening is than the gap.
the square on \(D_1/D_2\)
an opening's area grows with its diameter squared, so a small change in diameter makes a big change in speed.
the diameter rule

1. \(V_1A_1=V_2A_2\), so \(V_2/V_1=A_1/A_2\). Continuity for one inlet, one outlet.

2. \(A=\pi D^2/4\): the \(\pi/4\) cancels in the ratio, leaving \(D_1^2/D_2^2\). Saves calculating areas when only a speed is wanted.

Try it · water through a pipe that changes diameter

Top: the pipe, with streamlines (they crowd together where the pipe narrows, Section 11) and velocity arrows drawn to scale. Bottom: \(V_2\) against \(D_2\) for the chosen \(D_1\) and \(V_1\): a \(1/D^2\) curve, so shrinking a narrow pipe further makes the speed shoot up. The dot marks your \(D_2\).

Example · a general control volume

A tank like the one in Section 6 has two inlets and three outlets, all water, steady. Inlets: 0.02 m² at 3 m/s and 0.01 m² at 2 m/s. Outlets: 0.01 m² at 2 m/s, 0.005 m² at 4 m/s, and a third of area 0.01 m². Find the speed in the third outlet.

  1. \(\sum Q_\text{in}=0.02(3)+0.01(2)=0.08\u{m^3/s}\).
  2. Known outflow: \(0.01(2)+0.005(4)=0.04\u{m^3/s}\).
  3. Third outlet carries the rest: \(0.08-0.04=0.04\u{m^3/s}\), so \(V=0.04/0.01=\boxed{4\u{m/s}}\). Steady: the tank neither fills nor empties, so whatever the other outlets do not carry must leave by the third.

You can now: write the continuity equation for any number of inlets and outlets; cancel \(\rho\) for a liquid to get \(V_1A_1=V_2A_2\); and use the diameter rule to find a speed without working out areas.

8Examples 1 and 2: a pipe that widens, and a branch ~3 min

What is this?

Example 1 is the thumb in reverse: the pipe gets wider, so the same water per second spreads over a bigger opening and slows down. Example 2 is a hose with a Y-splitter: the water from one pipe is shared between two, so the two outflows add up to the inflow.

Worked example 1

The diameters of a pipe at sections 1 and 2 are 10 cm and 15 cm respectively. Find the discharge through the pipe if the velocity of water flowing through the pipe at section 1 is 5 m/s. Determine also the velocity at section 2.

  1. Data in metres: \(D_1=0.1\u{m}\), \(D_2=0.15\u{m}\), \(V_1=5\u{m/s}\). Areas then come out in m² and speeds in m/s.
  2. Areas: \(A_1=\tfrac{\pi}{4}(0.1)^2=0.007854\u{m^2}\); \(A_2=\tfrac{\pi}{4}(0.15)^2=0.01767\u{m^2}\).
  3. (i) Discharge: \(Q=A_1V_1=0.007854\times5\), so \(\boxed{Q=0.03927\u{m^3/s}}\) (39.3 L/s).
  4. (ii) Continuity \(A_1V_1=A_2V_2\): The same water per second passes section 2, but over a bigger area, so it must move more slowly.
\[V_2=\frac{A_1V_1}{A_2}=\frac{0.007854}{0.01767}\times5\] \[\boxed{V_2=2.22\u{m/s}}\]

Check with the diameter rule: \(V_2=5(10/15)^2=5(0.444)=2.22\u{m/s}\) ✓. The pipe widens, so the water slows down.

extra: mass flow and momentum flux at each section

Mass flow is the same at both sections: \(\dot m=\rho Q=1000(0.03927)\) \(=39.27\u{kg/s}\). Continuity: mass in = mass out.

Momentum flux at 1: \(\dot mV_1=39.27(5)=196.3\u{N}\); at 2: \(\dot mV_2=39.27(2.22)=87.3\u{N}\). Momentum flux is not conserved along a pipe: the change, 109 N, is what the pipe wall and pressure must supply (F4).

Worked example 2 · one pipe splits into two

A 30 cm diameter pipe, conveying water, branches into two pipes of diameters 20 cm and 15 cm respectively. If the average velocity in the 30 cm diameter pipe is 2.5 m/s, find the discharge in this pipe. Also determine the velocity in the 15 cm pipe if the average velocity in the 20 cm diameter pipe is 2 m/s.

  1. Label: pipe 1 (\(D_1=0.30\u{m}\), \(V_1=2.5\u{m/s}\)) is the inlet; pipes 2 (\(D_2=0.20\u{m}\), \(V_2=2\u{m/s}\)) and 3 (\(D_3=0.15\u{m}\), \(V_3=?\)) are the outlets.
  2. Areas: \(A_1=\tfrac{\pi}{4}(0.3)^2=0.07068\u{m^2}\); \(A_2=\tfrac{\pi}{4}(0.2)^2=0.0314\u{m^2}\); \(A_3=\tfrac{\pi}{4}(0.15)^2=0.01767\u{m^2}\).
  3. Continuity (one inlet, two outlets): \(Q_1=Q_2+Q_3\). Every litre arriving in pipe 1 leaves by pipe 2 or pipe 3; none piles up at the split.
  4. (i) \(Q_1=A_1V_1=0.07068\times2.5\), so \(\boxed{Q_1=0.1767\u{m^3/s}}\).
  5. (ii) \(Q_2=A_2V_2=0.0314\times2\) \(=0.0628\u{m^3/s}\), so \(Q_3=0.1767-0.0628\) \(=0.1139\u{m^3/s}\).
\[V_3=\frac{Q_3}{A_3}=\frac{0.1139}{0.01767}\] \[\boxed{V_3=6.44\u{m/s}}\]
a one-line version without areas

\(\pi/4\) appears in every term of \(D_1^2V_1=D_2^2V_2+D_3^2V_3\), so cancel it:

\[V_3=\frac{D_1^2V_1-D_2^2V_2}{D_3^2}\] \[=\frac{0.09(2.5)-0.04(2)}{0.0225}=6.44\u{m/s}\]

You can now: find \(Q\) and the speed at each section of a pipe that changes diameter; check the answer with the diameter rule; and solve a one-into-two branch with \(Q_1=Q_2+Q_3\).

9Example 3: a pipe that branches ~3 min

What is this?

A big pipe that splits is like a hose feeding a Y-splitter into two sprinklers. The litres per second from the main pipe are shared out, and the shares add up to the total. The speeds do not add: a thin branch can run faster than the main pipe even though it carries less water.

Worked example 3

Water flows through a pipe AB 1.2 m diameter at 3 m/s and then passes through a pipe BC 1.5 m diameter. At C, the pipe branches. Branch CD is 0.8 m in diameter and carries one-third of the flow in AB. The flow velocity in branch CE is 2.5 m/s. Find the volume rate of flow in AB, the velocity in BC, the velocity in CD and the diameter of CE.

Given: \(D_{AB}=1.2\u{m}\), \(V_{AB}=3.0\u{m/s}\), \(D_{BC}=1.5\u{m}\), \(D_{CD}=0.8\u{m}\), \(V_{CE}=2.5\u{m/s}\). Let \(Q\) be the flow in AB, \(Q_1\) in CD and \(Q_2\) in CE; \(Q_1=Q/3\), so \(Q_2=Q-Q/3=2Q/3\).

  1. (i) Flow in AB:
\[Q=V_{AB}\,\frac{\pi}{4}D_{AB}^2=3.0\times\frac{\pi}{4}(1.2)^2\] \[\boxed{Q=3.393\u{m^3/s}}\]
  1. (ii) AB and BC are one pipe in series: the same \(Q\). Continuity \(V_{AB}A_{AB}=V_{BC}A_{BC}\), divide by \(\pi/4\): Nothing leaves between A and C, so all the water from AB passes through BC.
\[3.0(1.2)^2=V_{BC}(1.5)^2\] \[V_{BC}=\frac{3\times1.44}{2.25}=\boxed{1.92\u{m/s}}\]
  1. (iii) Branch CD: \(Q_1=Q/3=3.393/3=1.131\u{m^3/s}\), and \(A_{CD}=\tfrac{\pi}{4}(0.8)^2=0.5027\u{m^2}\):
\[V_{CD}=\frac{1.131}{0.5027}=\boxed{2.25\u{m/s}}\]
  1. (iv) Branch CE: \(Q_2=Q-Q_1=3.393-1.131\) \(=2.262\u{m^3/s}\), and \(Q_2=V_{CE}\tfrac{\pi}{4}D_{CE}^2\): Whatever arrives at C and does not go down CD must go down CE.
\[2.262=2.5\times\frac{\pi}{4}D_{CE}^2\] \[D_{CE}=\sqrt{\frac{2.262\times4}{2.5\pi}}=\sqrt{1.152}\] \[\boxed{D_{CE}=1.073\u{m}}\]
a check on the whole junction

Inflow at C: \(Q=3.393\). Outflows: CD \(2.25\times0.5027=1.131\); CE \(2.5\times\tfrac{\pi}{4}(1.0733)^2=2.262\). Sum \(3.393\) ✓. Continuity at the junction C: what arrives from BC leaves through CD and CE.

Note that the velocities do not add: \(V_{BC}=1.92\) is not \(2.25+2.5\). Only flow rates add.

Try it · splitting a flow at a junction

Main pipe 30 cm. The defaults are Example 2 (2 m/s in the 20 cm branch is a 36% share). The arrow widths are proportional to the flow rates: they always add up at the junction, while the speeds do not.

You can now: solve a pipe in series followed by a junction; turn a stated share (one-third) into the flow in each branch; and find an unknown diameter from \(Q=V\pi D^2/4\).

10Example 4: water flow through a garden-hose nozzle ~2 min

What is this?

This is the thumb on the hose, with numbers. The bucket and stopwatch measure the litres per second. Water cannot pile up inside the hose, so the same litres per second leave the nozzle; the nozzle opening is much smaller than the hose, so the water leaves much faster.

Worked example 4

A garden hose attached with a nozzle is used to fill a 10-gal bucket. The inner diameter of the hose is 2 cm, and it reduces to 0.8 cm at the nozzle exit. If it takes 50 s to fill the bucket with water, determine (a) the volume and mass flow rates of water through the hose, and (b) the average velocity of water at the nozzle exit.

Assumptions: (1) water is incompressible; (2) the flow through the hose is steady; (3) no water is wasted by splashing. Property: \(\rho=1000\u{kg/m^3}=1\u{kg/L}\).

A gallon (gal) is a US unit of volume: 1 gal = 3.7854 L.

  1. (a) 10 gal are delivered in 50 s: Volume collected ÷ time taken is exactly the bucket-and-stopwatch picture of \(Q\).
\[Q=\frac{V}{\Delta t}=\frac{10\u{gal}}{50\u{s}}\times\frac{3.7854\u{L}}{1\u{gal}}\] \[\boxed{Q=0.757\u{L/s}}=7.57\times10^{-4}\u{m^3/s}\] \[\dot m=\rho Q=(1\u{kg/L})(0.757\u{L/s})\] \[\boxed{\dot m=0.757\u{kg/s}}\]
  1. (b) Nozzle exit area, radius 0.4 cm: The exit diameter is 0.8 cm, so the radius is half of that.
\[A_e=\pi r_e^2=\pi(0.4\u{cm})^2=0.5027\u{cm^2}\] \[A_e=0.5027\times10^{-4}\u{m^2}\]

The volume flow rate through the hose and the nozzle is the same (continuity), so

\[V_e=\frac{Q}{A_e}=\frac{0.757\times10^{-3}}{0.5027\times10^{-4}}\] \[\boxed{V_e\approx15.1\u{m/s}}\]
extra: the speed in the hose itself

\(A_\text{hose}=\pi(0.01)^2=3.142\times10^{-4}\u{m^2}\), so

\[V_\text{hose}=\frac{7.57\times10^{-4}}{3.142\times10^{-4}}=2.41\u{m/s}\]

Same \(Q\) through the hose.

Ratio \(V_e/V_\text{hose}=(2/0.8)^2=6.25\) ✓: the nozzle makes the jet six times faster without changing how quickly the bucket fills. That is why you put your thumb over a hose to make it squirt further: you reduce \(A\), so \(V\) rises.

Watch

Convert cm² to m² with \(10^{-4}\) (1 cm = \(10^{-2}\) m, squared), and litres to m³ with \(10^{-3}\). Forgetting the \(10^{-4}\) in (b) gives a "speed" of 0.0015 m/s.

For practice, use the concept checks (Section 15) and the exam taster (Section 16).

You can now: convert gallons, litres and cm² to SI units; find \(Q\) and \(\dot m\) from a fill time; and find a nozzle's exit speed from \(Q\) and the exit area.

11Continuity in exam questions ~6 min

What is this?

Exam questions dress the hose up as a channel, a tank, a jet hitting a plate or the first step of a Bernoulli question. The rule is the same each time: what goes in must come out. The only new thinking is which opening the water actually crosses, and how big that opening is.

The worked examples so far are all pipes. Past papers use the same equation in a few other settings. Each one is still "\(\sum Q_\text{in}=\sum Q_\text{out}\)", with care over which area to use.

Streamlines in a channel that widens

Recall from F1 that a streamline is a line drawn tangent to the velocity everywhere; no fluid crosses it. Between two streamlines the discharge is fixed, so where the flow speeds up the streamlines must crowd together, and where it slows down they spread apart. In a diverging (widening) channel, \(A_2\gt A_1\): \(Q_1=Q_2=Q\), but \(V_2=Q/A_2\lt V_1=Q/A_1\), and the streamlines fan out, following the walls.

A tank with several openings

A tank with water entering at one opening and leaving at others is exactly the general control volume of Section 6. If the water level is steady, \(\sum Q_\text{in}=\sum Q_\text{out}\). Two equal outlets at the same depth carry equal shares by symmetry.

Going further · a tank whose level changes

If the level is not steady, the budget of Section 3 still works: the volume stored per second is \(A_\text{tank}\,\mathrm dh/\mathrm dt\), so

\[A_\text{tank}\frac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}=Q_\text{in}-Q_\text{out}\]

In words: the rate the level rises × tank area = inflow − outflow.

Reading the formula
\(A_\text{tank}\dfrac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}\)
volume stored per second: the tank's floor area times how fast the water level rises.
\(Q_\text{in}-Q_\text{out}\)
the water per second that comes in but does not go out.
a negative answer
more leaves than arrives, so the level falls.

Example: a tank of area 0.1 m² receives 0.03 m³/s and loses 0.04 m³/s, so \(\mathrm dh/\mathrm dt=(0.03-0.04)/0.1=-0.1\u{m/s}\): the level falls 100 mm every second.

A jet that spreads into a thin sheet

What is this? (a radial sheet)

Point a hose straight at a flat plate and the water spreads out sideways in a thin, round sheet. It leaves through a thin ring at the plate's edge. That ring is the opening it crosses: its length is the plate's circumference and its height is the sheet's thickness.

When a round jet hits a flat plate and spreads out radially as a thin sheet of thickness \(t\), the fluid leaves through the side of a short cylinder at the plate's edge. That outlet area is the cylinder's curved surface, \(A_2=\pi Dt\) (circumference × thickness), not \(\pi D^2/4\).

Example: a plate of diameter 0.1 m with a sheet 0.4 mm thick gives \(A_2=\pi(0.1)(0.0004)\) \(=1.257\times10^{-4}\u{m^2}\). (May 2019 Q3 uses exactly this; see the exam taster.)

Continuity as step 1 of a Bernoulli question

Many F3 questions give the speed at one section and need it at another: a nozzle at the end of a pipe (May 2021 Q1(a)), a duct that narrows (Dec 2024 Section B Q4(b): exit area half the inlet area, so \(V_2=2V_1\)). Write continuity symbolically first when the question says "relate symbolically": \(A_1V_1=A_2V_2\Rightarrow V_2=(A_1/A_2)V_1\).

Going further · compressible flow (gases)

For a gas whose density changes along the flow, keep \(\rho\) in the equation: \(\rho_1V_1A_1=\rho_2V_2A_2\) (the general form of Section 7). Mass is conserved; volume is not.

Example: air enters a nozzle with \(\rho_1=1.20\u{kg/m^3}\), \(V_1=20\u{m/s}\), \(A_1=0.05\u{m^2}\), and leaves through \(A_2=0.01\u{m^2}\) with \(\rho_2=1.00\u{kg/m^3}\). Then \(\dot m=1.20(20)(0.05)=1.2\u{kg/s}\) and

\[V_2=\frac{\dot m}{\rho_2A_2}=\frac{1.2}{1.00(0.01)}=120\u{m/s}\]

Treating it as incompressible would give 100 m/s, 17% too low.

Ready answer · continuity questions (type F2.1)
  1. Do: Sketch the pipe or tank; number the sections; draw the control volume (dashed) cutting each inlet and outlet perpendicular to the flow. The box and the numbered faces tell you exactly which openings go into the budget.
  2. Do: Convert every length to metres; find each area (\(\pi D^2/4\) for a pipe, \(\pi Dt\) for a radial sheet). The area must be the opening the water actually crosses, in m², or every speed comes out wrong.
  3. Do: Write \(\sum(VA)_\text{in}=\sum(VA)_\text{out}\) (or with \(\rho\) for a gas) and solve for the unknown. This is what goes in must come out, written for this device.
  4. Do: Then \(Q=VA\), \(\dot m=\rho Q\), weight flow \(\gamma Q\), with units m³/s, kg/s, N/s. Once one speed and area are known, every flow measure follows from the same \(Q\).
  5. Do: Sense check: smaller area → faster; flow rates add at a junction, speeds do not. The thumb on the hose: a smaller opening always means faster water, and a splitter shares litres, not speeds.

You can now: draw streamlines in a widening channel; apply continuity to a tank (steady or with a changing level), a radial sheet (\(A=\pi Dt\)) and a gas; and use continuity as step 1 of a Bernoulli question.

13Common mistakes

✗Using the diameter ratio instead of its square: \(V_2=V_1(D_1/D_2)\). ✓Area goes with \(D^2\): \(V_2=V_1(D_1/D_2)^2\). ✗Adding velocities at a junction: \(V_1=V_2+V_3\). ✓Add flow rates: \(V_1A_1=V_2A_2+V_3A_3\). ✗Using radius in \(\pi D^2/4\), or diameter in \(\pi r^2\). ✓Pick one: \(\pi D^2/4=\pi r^2\). ✗Leaving cm or cm² in the formula. ✓1 cm = 0.01 m; 1 cm² = \(10^{-4}\) m²; 1 L = \(10^{-3}\) m³. ✗Mixing up mass flow and weight flow: \(\dot m=\gamma Q\). ✓\(\dot m=\rho Q\) (kg/s); weight flow \(=\gamma Q\) (N/s). ✗For a radial sheet, using the plate's face area \(\pi D^2/4\). ✓The fluid leaves through the rim: \(A=\pi Dt\). ✗Drawing a control-volume boundary along the flow at an inlet. ✓Cut each inlet and outlet straight across, perpendicular to the flow. ✗Dropping \(\rho\) for a gas whose density changes. ✓Only an incompressible fluid lets you cancel \(\rho\).

14Find the mistake

1 · A student's Dec 2023 Q1(a)

"Diameters 50 mm and 100 mm, \(V_1=8\u{m/s}\). The pipe is twice as wide, so \(V_2=8\times\tfrac{50}{100}=4\u{m/s}\)."

Hint

Twice the diameter is how many times the area?

Answer

Area goes with \(D^2\): doubling \(D\) makes \(A\) four times larger, so \(V_2=8(50/100)^2=2\u{m/s}\).

2 · A student's Example 3

"At C the flow splits, so \(V_{BC}=V_{CD}+V_{CE}\), giving \(V_{CD}=1.92-2.5=-0.58\u{m/s}\)."

Hint

Which quantity is conserved at a junction? A negative speed should have rung a bell.

Answer

Flow rates add, not speeds: \(Q_{BC}=Q_{CD}+Q_{CE}\). With \(Q_{CD}=Q/3=1.131\u{m^3/s}\) and \(A_{CD}=0.5027\u{m^2}\), \(V_{CD}=2.25\u{m/s}\).

3 · A student's Example 4(b)

"\(A_e=\pi(0.4)^2=0.5027\), so \(V_e=0.757\times10^{-3}/0.5027\) \(=1.5\times10^{-3}\u{m/s}\)."

Hint

What unit was the 0.4 in?

Answer

0.4 cm gives 0.5027 cm², which is \(0.5027\times10^{-4}\u{m^2}\). Then \(V_e=15.1\u{m/s}\). A jet from a hose crawling at 1.5 mm/s should have looked wrong.

4 · A student's mass flow rate

"Specific weight 9.59 kN/m³, \(Q=0.01571\u{m^3/s}\), so the mass flow rate is \(9590\times0.01571=150.6\u{kg/s}\)."

Hint

What are the units of \(\gamma Q\)?

Answer

\(\gamma Q\) is the weight flow rate, 150.6 N/s. Mass flow uses density: \(\dot m=978(0.01571)=15.4\u{kg/s}\).

15Concept checks

1 · Closed or open?

Which of these are best studied as a closed system and which as a control volume? (a) air in a sealed balloon being heated; (b) a jet engine in flight; (c) a car tyre being pumped up.

Answer

(a) Closed system (no mass crosses; heat does). (b) Control volume (air and fuel flow through). (c) Control volume (air enters through the valve); or, if you follow all the air that ends up inside, a closed system with a moving boundary. The choice is yours; pick the one that makes the budget easy.

2 · Halving the diameter

Water flows at 1 m/s in a pipe. The pipe narrows to half its diameter. What is the new speed? What happens to \(Q\) and \(\dot m\)?

Answer

Area falls to a quarter, so speed rises to \(1\times2^2=4\u{m/s}\). \(Q\) and \(\dot m\) are unchanged (steady, incompressible).

3 · Two equal outlets

A steady tank receives 0.06 m³/s and drains through two identical openings at the same depth. How much leaves each?

Answer

0.03 m³/s each: continuity gives the total, symmetry splits it equally.

4 · Turning up the tap

In Example 4 the tap is opened so the bucket fills in a third of the time. What is the new exit speed?

Hint

Same nozzle area.

Answer

\(Q\) triples, so \(V_e\) triples: about 45.2 m/s (from 15.06 m/s).

5 · Gas in a pipe of constant area

Air flows steadily through a straight pipe of constant area and gets hotter, so its density falls. Does its speed rise, fall or stay the same?

Answer

Rises: \(\rho VA\) is constant and \(A\) is fixed, so \(V\propto1/\rho\). For water (\(\rho\) constant) the speed would not change.

6 · Momentum flux

Two jets carry the same mass flow rate, one at twice the speed of the other. Compare their momentum fluxes and their areas.

Answer

Momentum flux \(\dot mV\) is twice as big for the faster jet; its area is half (same \(\dot m=\rho VA\)). Fast, thin jets push harder for the same water use.

(a) Show that the velocity at section (1) is related to that at section (2) by \(V_1=1.6V_2\). ((b)–(d): chapters F3 and F4.)

Hint

Section (2) is the rim of a thin sheet: what shape is its area?

Answer

Section (1): round jet, \(A_1=\tfrac{\pi}{4}(0.01)^2\) \(=7.854\times10^{-5}\u{m^2}\). Section (2): the sheet leaves through a cylinder of diameter 0.1 m and height 0.4 mm, \(A_2=\pi(0.1)(0.0004)\) \(=1.257\times10^{-4}\u{m^2}\).

Continuity \(A_1V_1=A_2V_2\):

\[V_1=\frac{A_2}{A_1}V_2=\frac{\pi(0.1)(0.0004)}{\tfrac{\pi}{4}(0.01)^2}V_2\] \[\boxed{V_1=1.6\,V_2}\]
