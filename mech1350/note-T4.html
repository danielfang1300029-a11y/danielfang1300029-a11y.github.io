Laws of thermodynamics: ideal gases, the first law and the second law

0Where this chapter sits

T2 described a substance's state and T3 how fast heat moves. This chapter keeps the books. Think of a gas's internal energy as a bank balance: heat in is a deposit, work out is a withdrawal (first law). Then it asks which way energy may flow at all, like water that only runs downhill (second law). It is the last thermal chapter; H1 starts hydrostatics (Section B).

Sign convention for this chapter
  • Heat \(Q\) into the system is positive; heat leaving the system is negative.
  • Work \(W\) done BY the system is positive (for example a gas pushing a piston out); work done ON the system is negative (a piston compressing the gas, a paddle stirring a liquid). This is the convention used throughout these notes and in the exam.
  • With these signs the first law reads \(Q-W=\Delta U\): heat in raises the stored energy, work out lowers it.
  • \(\Delta\) always means final minus initial: \(\Delta U=U_2-U_1\), \(\Delta T=T_2-T_1\).
  • In \(PV=mRT\) the pressure must be absolute (not gauge) and the temperature must be in kelvin: \(T/\mathrm K=T/{}^\circ\mathrm C+273.15\). A temperature difference is the same in °C and K, so \(\Delta u=c_v\Delta T\) may use either.
  • Units: the thermal tables use kJ, kPa and kJ/(kg·K). Since 1 kPa·m³ = 1 kJ, \(PV\) in kPa·m³ is directly an energy in kJ.

1The equation of state and the ideal gas ~8 min

What is this?

Picture a box of tiny balls flying about and bouncing off the walls. Pressure is the drumming of all those hits on the walls. Heat the gas and the balls fly faster, so they hit harder and more often: the pressure rises. Give them more room and each patch of wall is hit less often: the pressure falls. The ideal-gas equation is the exact rule for this drumming when the balls are far apart and ignore each other.

Definition · equation of state

Pressure \(P\), temperature \(T\) and specific volume \(v\) of a substance are not independent: fix two of them and the third is decided. The relation between them is called the equation of state, written in general as

\[F(P,T,v)=0\]

In words: there is one rule connecting pressure, temperature and specific volume; this chapter uses it for gases (the vapour or gaseous phase of a "simple compressible substance", meaning a substance whose state is changed only by heating and by squeezing or expanding it).

From Boyle and Charles to the ideal gas

From HKDSE Physics you know two laws for a fixed amount of gas at low pressure:

  • Boyle's law: at constant temperature, \(PV\) is constant (squeeze a gas to half its volume and its pressure doubles).
  • Charles' law: at constant pressure, \(V\) is proportional to the absolute temperature \(T\) (heat a gas from 300 K to 600 K at constant pressure and its volume doubles).

Combining them, \(Pv/T\) is the same in every state, so \(Pv=RT\), or equivalently,

Result · ideal-gas equation of state \[P=R\left(\frac{T}{v}\right)\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad Pv=RT\]

In words: pressure × specific volume = gas constant × absolute temperature.

Reading the formula
\(P\)
the drumming on each square metre of wall; absolute, counted from a perfect vacuum where nothing hits.
\(v\)
room per kilogram of gas: more room, fewer hits on each patch of wall.
\(T\)
how fast the balls fly, in kelvin, counted from absolute zero rather than from the freezing point of water.
\(R\)
the gas's own conversion factor: lighter molecules mean more balls in each kilogram, so a bigger \(R\) (Section 2).

\(R\) is the constant of proportionality, called the gas constant; it has a different value for each gas. Some values, in kJ/(kg·K): air 0.2870, helium 2.0769, argon 0.2081, nitrogen 0.2968. A gas that obeys \(Pv=RT\) is called an ideal gas.

Going further · what "ideal" means

In an ideal gas the molecules are treated as tiny points that do not attract or repel each other except when they collide. That is a good picture when the molecules are far apart: at low pressure and at temperatures well above the temperature where the gas would condense. Air at room conditions is very close to ideal. Near condensation (for example steam in a boiler) the molecules are close together, they attract each other, and \(Pv=RT\) fails (Section 3).

The universal gas constant

Every gas has its own \(R\), but they all come from one number, the universal gas constant \(R_u\), valid for all substances, divided by the gas's molar mass \(M\) (also called molecular weight: the mass of one kilomole, in kg/kmol):

Result · gas constant of a particular gas \[R=\frac{R_u}{M},\qquad R_u=8.314\u{kJ/(kmol\,K)}\]

In words: gas constant of this gas = universal gas constant ÷ mass of one kilomole of this gas.

A mole is a fixed number of molecules (Avogadro's number); a kilomole (kmol) is 1000 moles. The molar mass is the ratio of mass to moles, \(M=m/N\), and has the same numerical value in any unit system (32 g/mol = 32 kg/kmol for oxygen). For air, \(M_\text{air}=28.97\u{kg/kmol}\): one kilomole of air has a mass of 28.97 kg. So

\[m=NM\] \[R_\text{air}=\frac{8.314}{28.97}=0.2870\u{kJ/(kg\,K)}\]

which matches the value quoted above.

Many ways to write the same law

The law comes in several forms. They are all the same equation; they differ only in whether you count the gas per kilogram, in total, or per kilomole.

FormUse it when you know…

\(P\dfrac{V}{m}=RT\)the same, with \(v=V/m\) written out

Watch

\(T\) in kelvin and \(P\) absolute. Putting 27 °C into \(PV=mRT\) instead of 300.15 K gives an answer more than ten times too small. A tyre gauge reading of 200 kPa means an absolute pressure of about 301 kPa.

Example · mass of air in a room

A classroom is 8 m × 6 m × 3 m. The air is at 25 °C and 100 kPa. Find the mass of air.

  1. \(V=8\times6\times3=144\u{m^3}\); \(T=25+273.15=298.15\u{K}\); \(R=0.2870\u{kJ/(kg\,K)}\). The drumming is set by the absolute temperature; 0 °C is not where the molecules stop.
  2. Rearrange \(PV=mRT\): \(m=\dfrac{PV}{RT}=\dfrac{100\times144}{0.2870\times298.15}\).
  3. Units: kPa·m³ = kJ, divided by kJ/(kg·K) × K, leaves kg. ✓

\(\boxed{m\approx168\u{kg}}\), the mass of two adults. Air is not "nothing".

the same answer counted in kilomoles

1. Form \(PV=NR_uT\):

\[\begin{aligned}N&=\frac{PV}{R_uT}=\frac{100\times144}{8.314\times298.15}\\&=5.809\u{kmol}\end{aligned}\]

2. \(m=NM=5.809\times28.97=168.3\u{kg}\). \(m=NM\).

3. Same answer, because \(R=R_u/M\) makes the two forms identical.

You can now: write the ideal-gas law in any of its forms; find \(R\) as \(R_u/M\); convert to absolute pressure and kelvin; and find the mass of gas in a given volume.

For an ideal gas the two specific heats always differ by exactly the gas constant:

\[c_p-c_v=R,\qquad k=\frac{c_p}{c_v}\]

3Is water vapour an ideal gas? ~4 min

What is this?

The ideal-gas rule assumes the molecules are far apart and ignore each other, like strangers spread across an empty square. Squeeze them together (high pressure) or cool them towards condensing, and they start to tug on each other, like people in a packed crowd. Then the simple rule gives the wrong volume. Steam is spread out and well behaved at low pressure, but crowded and badly behaved at boiler pressures.

This question cannot be answered with a simple yes or no. A chart of the percentage error made if steam's specific volume is calculated from \(Pv=RT\) (with \(R=0.4615\)) instead of being read from the steam tables tells the story:

\[\text{error}=\frac{|v_\text{table}-v_\text{ideal}|}{v_\text{table}}\times100\%\]

In words: how far the ideal-gas answer is from the true (table) answer, as a percentage of the true answer.

The chart is a \(T\)-\(v\) diagram (temperature up, specific volume across on a logarithmic scale, from 0.001 to 100+ m³/kg) with the saturation dome (T2) and lines of constant pressure from 0.8 kPa to 30 MPa. Numbers printed along each pressure line give the error at that point. The shaded region on the right, at large \(v\), is where the error is under 1%: the ideal-gas region.

Schematic, not to scale: the \(T\)-\(v\) error chart with a few of its labels. Error percentages next to the dots; the shaded region is where steam is ideal to within 1%.

What the figure says

  • Below 10 kPa, steam can be treated as an ideal gas at any temperature, with negligible error (under 0.1% on the chart).
  • At higher pressures the error becomes unacceptable, especially near the critical point and close to the saturated vapour line, where it exceeds 100% (271% at 30 MPa and 400 °C).
  • So in air-conditioning, the water vapour in air (at a partial pressure of a few kPa) is an ideal gas with essentially no error.
  • In steam power plants, pressures are high (MPa), so ideal-gas relations should not be used: use the steam tables of T2.

4Specific heats ~5 min

What is this?

Back to the bank account: internal energy is the balance. Specific heat is the price of a one-degree rise for one kilogram. In a rigid tank every kilojoule of heat goes into the balance. Under a free piston the gas also expands and pays some of the heat straight back out as work, so the same one-degree rise costs more. That is why there are two prices, \(c_v\) and \(c_p\).

Definition · specific heat

The specific heat is the energy needed to raise the temperature of one kilogram of a substance by one degree. For gases it depends on how the heating is done, so there are two:

  • \(c_v\): specific heat for a constant-volume process (gas in a rigid, sealed tank);
  • \(c_p\): specific heat for a constant-pressure process (gas under a freely sliding piston).

For an ideal gas, the specific internal energy \(u\) (energy stored in the molecules' motion, per kg) and the specific enthalpy \(h\) depend on temperature only, and their changes are

Result · energy changes of an ideal gas \[\Delta u=c_v(T_2-T_1)\] \[\Delta h=c_p(T_2-T_1)\]

In words: change in internal energy per kg = constant-volume specific heat × temperature change; change in enthalpy per kg = constant-pressure specific heat × temperature change. For mass \(m\): \(\Delta U=mc_v\Delta T\).

Reading the formula
\(\Delta u\)
the change in the balance, per kilogram.
\(c_v\)
how many kilojoules of balance each kilogram holds per degree.
\(T_2-T_1\)
final minus initial; a difference, so °C and K give the same number.
any process
for an ideal gas the balance depends on temperature only, so you can read it off a thermometer, however the energy got in.

Important: \(\Delta u=c_v\Delta T\) holds for an ideal gas in any process, not only constant-volume ones, because \(u\) depends only on \(T\). That is why the exam questions use \(\Delta U=mc_v\Delta T\) for a gas whose volume changes (an engine piston moving out). The "\(v\)" in \(c_v\) tells you how \(c_v\) is measured, not when it may be used.

What is this? (enthalpy)

Enthalpy is internal energy plus a "rent". A gas at pressure \(P\) that fills a volume \(V\) has had to push the surroundings out of the way to make that room, and \(PV\) is the work that took: the rent for its space. \(H=U+PV\) counts the balance and the rent together. That is handy whenever a gas is heated at a steady pressure: the heat then pays for the extra internal energy and the extra rent, so it equals the rise in \(H\).

Going further · enthalpy, and why \(c_p\gt c_v\)

Enthalpy is defined as \(h=u+Pv\) (T2 used it in the steam tables). For an ideal gas \(Pv=RT\), so \(h=u+RT\) and \(\Delta h=\Delta u+R\Delta T\), which gives \(c_p=c_v+R\).

Physically: heat a gas in a rigid tank and all the heat goes into the molecules' motion. Heat it under a free piston and the gas also expands, pushing the piston and the atmosphere back: some of the heat leaves again as work. More heat is needed for the same temperature rise, so \(c_p\gt c_v\). The work done per kg per kelvin in that expansion is exactly \(R\).

Example · rigid tank versus piston

2 kg of air is heated from 300 K to 400 K, (a) in a rigid tank, (b) under a freely moving piston at constant pressure. Use Table A-2. How much heat is needed in each case?

  2. (b) Constant pressure: Under the piston part of the heat leaves again as work, the rent for the extra room, so more heat is needed.\[\begin{aligned}Q&=mc_p\Delta T=2(1.005)(100)\\&=201.0\u{kJ}\end{aligned}\]

\(\boxed{Q_a=143.6\u{kJ},\quad Q_b=201.0\u{kJ}}\). In both cases \(\Delta U=143.6\u{kJ}\): same temperature change, same internal-energy change.

You can now: use \(\Delta U=mc_v\Delta T\) for an ideal gas in any process; explain why \(c_p\gt c_v\) and that \(c_p-c_v=R\); and say what enthalpy adds to internal energy.

5Energy transfer: heat and work ~5 min

What is this?

Draw a line round the thing you study: that is the system, and the line is its boundary. In the bank-account picture, only two kinds of transaction can cross that line: heat and work. Heat \(Q\) counts as a deposit when it comes in. Work \(W\) counts as a withdrawal when the system does it on the outside. So a positive \(Q\) adds to the balance, a positive \(W\) takes from it, and a negative \(W\) (work done on the system) is money paid in through the work channel.

Definitions · system, closed system, boundary
  • A system is the piece of matter we choose to study (the gas in a cylinder, the water in a kettle). Everything else is the surroundings. The surface separating them is the boundary.
  • A closed system has a fixed mass: no matter crosses the boundary. The boundary may still move (a piston sliding out) and energy may still cross it.

Energy can cross the boundary of a closed system in only two forms, heat and work.

Definitions · heat and work
  • Heat \(Q\): energy crossing the boundary because of a temperature difference (a flame under a pot, a hot wall). T3 was about how fast heat moves.
  • Work \(W\): any other energy crossing the boundary, typically a force moving through a distance: a gas pushing a piston, a paddle wheel turning in a liquid, an electric current driven through a heater wire inside the system.
Result · sign of work

\(W\gt0\): work done by the system (gas expands and pushes a piston out). \(W\lt0\): work done on the system (piston compresses the gas; a stirrer is turned inside it). Heat: \(Q\gt0\) into the system, \(Q\lt0\) out.

In words: heat is counted positive when it comes in; work is counted positive when it goes out. This mirrors a heat engine, which takes heat in and gives work out.

A closed system: gas under a piston. Heat \(Q\) enters through the base; the gas pushes the piston up, doing work \(W\) on the surroundings. Both are positive in this picture.

Four everyday pictures of energy changing form: chemical → motion (food energy, an ice-cream cone, turned into a cyclist's motion), chemical → motion (petrol from a fuel pump turned into a car's motion), radiant → chemical (sunlight stored by a tree through photosynthesis) and electrical → thermal (a plug feeding an electric cooker). Energy is never lost in these changes, only converted: that is the first law (Section 7).

Going further · boundary work \(P\Delta V\) and the area under the \(p\)-\(V\) curve

When a gas at pressure \(P\) pushes a piston of area \(A\) out by a distance \(\mathrm dx\), the force is \(PA\) and the work is \(PA\,\mathrm dx=P\,\mathrm dV\). Adding up over a process:

\[W=\int_{V_1}^{V_2}P\,\mathrm dV\]

In words: boundary work = area under the process curve on a pressure–volume diagram; positive when the volume grows (expansion), negative when it shrinks (compression), zero when the volume stays constant.

At constant pressure this is simply \(W=P(V_2-V_1)\), and for an ideal gas \(P\Delta V=mR\Delta T\). It is needed for the cycle questions (Sections 8–9 and type T4.3).

Reading the formula
\(P\)

\(\mathrm dV\)
the extra room gained in one small step: piston area × distance moved.
\(P\,\mathrm dV\)
force × distance for that step: one small withdrawal.
\(\int_{V_1}^{V_2}\)
add up all the steps: the area under the curve on a \(p\)-\(V\) diagram.
sign

You can now: define system, surroundings, boundary and closed system; tell heat from work; give \(Q\) and \(W\) their signs; and find boundary work as \(P\Delta V\) or as the area under a \(p\)-\(V\) curve.

6Energy balance example: the slap ~7 min

What is this?

In the bank picture the slap is a closed account with no deposits and no withdrawals: nothing crosses the boundary. Money can still move between pockets inside it. The hand's kinetic energy is one pocket and the tissue's internal energy is another. The hand stops, its pocket empties, and the tissue's pocket fills by the same amount, which shows up as a temperature rise.

Worked example · temperature rise due to slapping

Being slapped raises the temperature of the affected area of the face by 1.8 °C. The slapping hand has a mass of 1.2 kg, and about 0.150 kg of tissue (on the face and the hand) is affected. Estimate the velocity of the hand just before impact. Take the specific heat of the tissue as 3.8 kJ/(kg·°C).

Assumptions: (1) the hand is brought to a complete stop by the impact; (2) the face takes the blow without significant movement; (3) no heat is transferred from the affected area to the surroundings, so the process is adiabatic (no heat transfer); (4) no work is done on or by the system; (5) potential energy does not change, \(\Delta PE=0\), so \(\Delta E=\Delta U+\Delta KE\).

The system: the hand and the affected portion of the face. It is a closed system: a fixed amount of mass, no mass crossing the boundary. During the slap the hand's kinetic energy decreases (its velocity drops to zero) and the internal energy of the tissue increases (its temperature rises). No significant energy crosses the boundary.

  1. Do: Energy balance: \(E_\text{in}-E_\text{out}=\Delta E_\text{system}\). Left: net energy transfer by heat, work and mass, here zero. Right: change in internal, kinetic, potential energies.   2. Do: So \(0=\Delta U_\text{tissue}+\Delta KE_\text{hand}\), i.e. Nothing crossed the boundary, so energy can only move between pockets: what the hand loses, the tissue gains.

In words: the kinetic energy the hand loses = the internal energy the tissue gains.

7The first law of thermodynamics ~8 min

What is this?

This is the bank account itself. Internal energy \(U\) is the balance. Heat into the system is a deposit; work done by the system is a withdrawal. The balance changes only by deposits minus withdrawals, \(Q-W\). Energy is never created or destroyed inside the account.

Definition · first law

The first law of thermodynamics is the conservation of energy: "Energy can change from one form to another but the total amount of energy remains constant. That is, energy cannot be created or destroyed."

For a closed system, neglecting changes in kinetic and potential energy (the system as a whole is not speeding up or rising), the form used most often is

Result · first law for a closed system \[Q_\text{net}-W_\text{net}=\Delta U\]

In words: net heat into the system − net work done by the system = increase in the system's internal energy.

Reading the formula
\(Q_\text{net}\)
net deposits: heat in minus heat out. Negative if more heat left than came in.
\(W_\text{net}\)
net withdrawals: work done by the system. Negative if more work was done on it.
the minus sign
withdrawals lower the balance.
\(\Delta U\)
the change in balance, final minus initial.

\(Q_\text{net}\) is the net heat transfer (all heat in minus all heat out), \(W_\text{net}\) the net work done by the system, \(\Delta U\) the change of internal energy. Analogy: a bank account. Heat is a deposit, work done by the system is a withdrawal, \(U\) is the balance. The balance changes only by deposits and withdrawals; money is never created inside the account.

Two ways to raise internal energy

What is this? (heat or work, same result)

You can pay into an account in cash or by bank transfer, and afterwards the balance does not remember which. Water is the same. Heating it (a heat deposit) and stirring it (work done on it, a negative withdrawal) both raise its internal energy, and the warm water cannot tell you which was used.

Compare boiling (a glass pan of water on a gas flame) and stirring (a rod stirring a glass of water):

  • Boiling (heat transfer). \(Q_\text{net}\gt0\), \(-W_\text{net}=0\), so \(\Delta U=Q_\text{net}\gt0\).
  • Stirring (work done). \(Q_\text{net}=0\), \(-W_\text{net}\gt0\) (work is done on the water, so \(W_\text{net}\) is negative), so \(\Delta U=-W_\text{net}\gt0\).

Both raise the water's temperature. Once the water has warmed up, nobody can tell from the water which way it was done: internal energy is a property of the state, while heat and work are only energy in transit across the boundary.

Worked example · heat added while the gas does work

50 kJ of heat are added to 340 g of air, initially at 500 K, while the gas performs 75 kJ of work. What is the final temperature of the air? Given \(c_v=0.733\u{kJ/(kg\,K)}\) and \(\Delta U=mc_v\Delta T\).

  1. Do: Signs: heat added, \(Q=+50\u{kJ}\); work done by the gas, \(W=+75\u{kJ}\). Heat added is a deposit and work done by the gas is a withdrawal; in the sign convention of Section 5 both count as positive.
  2. Do: First law: \(\Delta U=Q-W=50-75=-25\u{kJ}\). The gas gave out more energy as work than it received as heat, so its internal energy falls. Change in balance = deposits − withdrawals; taking out 75 after paying in 50 leaves the balance 25 lower.
  3. Do: \(\Delta U=mc_v\Delta T\): \(-25=(0.34)(0.733)\Delta T\), so \(\Delta T=-100\u{K}\) (more precisely −100.3 K). For an ideal gas the balance is read off the thermometer, so a fall in \(U\) is a fall in \(T\).
  4. Do: \(T_\text{final}=500-100=400\u{K}\). \(\Delta T\) is final minus initial, so the final temperature is the starting one plus \(\Delta T\).

\(\boxed{T_\text{final}\approx400\u{K}}\)

\(-25/(0.34\times0.733)=-100.31\), so strictly \(T_\text{final}=399.7\u{K}\), which rounds to 400 K. Note \(c_v=0.733\) is air's value near 450 K (Table A-20), not the 300 K value 0.718.

Watch

The most common slip: adding instead of subtracting. "Heat is added AND the gas does work" means \(Q\) and \(W\) are both positive and \(\Delta U=Q-W\). If instead "work is done ON the gas" (compression, stirring), \(W\) is negative and \(\Delta U=Q+|W|\).

8The first law for a cycle ~4 min

What is this?

Picture an account that you bring back to exactly the same balance at the end of every month. Whatever you took out that month, you must have paid in. A cycle is the same: the gas returns to its starting state, so its internal energy (the balance) is back where it started. The net heat paid in must equal the net work paid out.

Definition · thermodynamic cycle

A cycle is a series of processes that brings the system back to its starting state: same pressure, same temperature, same volume. The fluid that goes round the cycle (air in an engine, refrigerant in a fridge) is called the working fluid.

Internal energy is a property of the state, so after a whole cycle it is back where it started: \(\Delta U_\text{cycle}=0\). The first law then gives

Result · first law for a cycle \[Q_\text{net}-W_\text{net}=\Delta U_\text{cycle}=0\] \[Q_\text{net}=W_\text{net}\]

In words: over a complete cycle, net heat in = net work out. A cycle cannot make work out of nothing: every kilojoule of net work must be paid for with a kilojoule of net heat.

Reading the formula
\(\Delta U_\text{cycle}=0\)
same state at the end of the lap, so the same balance.
\(Q_\text{net}\)
heat paid in over one lap, minus the heat thrown out.
\(W_\text{net}\)
work paid out over one lap, minus the work put in: what an engine delivers.
\(Q_\text{net}=W_\text{net}\)
with the balance unchanged, everything paid out was paid in.

Sketch a closed loop on a \(P\)-\(V\) diagram and write \(Q_\text{net}=W_\text{net}\) inside it. On a \(P\)-\(V\) diagram the work of each process is the area under its curve (Section 5), so:

  • the net work of a cycle is the area enclosed by the loop;
  • a loop traversed clockwise gives positive net work (expansion at high pressure, compression at low pressure): an engine;
  • a loop traversed anticlockwise needs net work put in: a refrigerator or heat pump (Section 13).

Left: a cycle on a \(P\)-\(V\) diagram. Right: why the enclosed area is the net work; the expansion (top) has a bigger area under it than the compression (bottom).

How a combustion engine works

A car engine (the internal combustion engine) is the everyday example. An engine is a cycle: in each cylinder, air and fuel are drawn in, compressed by the piston, burned (heat added, pressure rises sharply), expanded (the gas pushes the piston: work out) and pushed out as exhaust. Fresh air is drawn in and the cycle repeats. Over each cycle the engine converts net heat into net work, \(Q_\text{net}=W_\text{net}\). The detailed engine cycle (the Otto cycle) is in the Previous-syllabus Section 13.

You can now: explain why \(\Delta U=0\) over a cycle; use \(Q_\text{net}=W_\text{net}\); read the net work as the area of the loop; and tell an engine (clockwise) from a refrigerator or heat pump (anticlockwise).

9Ideal-gas processes and the three diagrams (type T4.3) ~7 min

What is this?

A cycle is a round trip, and the three diagrams \(p\)-\(V\), \(V\)-\(T\) and \(p\)-\(T\) are three maps of the same trip. \(PV=mRT\) lets you translate one map into another. For each leg the bank account gives the signs: work follows the volume (expanding is a withdrawal), the balance \(U\) follows the temperature, and heat is whatever deposit makes the books agree.

What is this?

Two rigid tanks joined by a valve are two bank accounts merged into one. Rigid walls mean no piston can move, so there are no withdrawals by work: \(W=0\). Any change in the combined balance must therefore be heat, paid in (positive) or taken out (negative). Each tank's gas adds its own change, from its own starting temperature to the common final one.

A favourite long question (Dec 2021, 17 marks) connects two rigid tanks of gas through a valve, lets them mix while heat flows in or out, and asks for the final pressure and the heat. It needs only \(PV=mRT\), \(\Delta U=mc_v\Delta T\) and the first law. Exam type T4.1.

11Towards the second law ~4 min

What is this?

Water runs downhill by itself, never uphill. A water wheel can take useful work out of it, but only while the water falls: no drop, no work. Heat behaves the same way. It flows by itself from hot to cold, and an engine placed in that flow can turn part of it into work, but only if there is a temperature drop to fall through. Sending heat back up, from cold to hot, needs work from outside, just as pumping water uphill does. The first law counts the energy; the second law says which way it runs.

The first law places no restriction on the direction of a process. A process can conserve energy perfectly and still never happen. The second law of thermodynamics is introduced to decide whether a process can take place.

Definition · second law
  • The second law says that processes occur in a certain direction, not in just any direction.
  • It determines the quality of energy (how useful it is), while the first law deals only with its quantity.
  • A process cannot take place unless it satisfies both the first and the second laws.

Can cold air heat up a hot coffee? (picture a mug of hot coffee losing heat to the room). Suppose 10 kJ of heat flowed from the cool room air into the hot coffee. The air would lose 10 kJ and the coffee gain 10 kJ: total energy conserved, first law satisfied. Yet it never happens: heat only flows from the hot coffee to the cool air. The first law alone cannot explain this; the second law can.

Nature runs one way

Physical processes in nature proceed spontaneously (by themselves) toward equilibrium (a state with no differences left to drive change):

  • Water flows down a waterfall (from high to low height). It never climbs back up by itself.
  • Gases expand from a high pressure to a low pressure (picture gas molecules in a jar pushing a lid up). They never squeeze back by themselves.
  • Heat flows from a high temperature to a low temperature (picture a red "HOT" block passing heat to a blue "COLD" block).

Each one could be reversed only by spending something from outside: a pump to lift the water, a compressor to squeeze the gas, a refrigerator (driven by work) to move heat from cold to hot.

Going further · "quality" of energy

Work and electricity are the highest-quality energy: they can be turned completely into anything else, including heat. Heat at a high temperature is fairly useful (an engine can turn part of it into work). Heat at room temperature is almost useless: a whole room full of energy at 25 °C cannot drive an engine if everything around is also at 25 °C. Every real process lowers the quality of energy (degrades it) while keeping its quantity: the first law counts the energy, the second law keeps track of how much of it is still useful. Entropy (Section 12) is the measure.

You can now: explain why satisfying the first law does not make a process possible; state the second law's ideas of direction and quality; and give examples of processes that run one way.

12Entropy ~10 min

What is this?

Entropy keeps score of how much useful drop has been wasted. When heat falls from hot to cold through a perfect engine, the drop is used to make work. When it falls straight through a wall, or energy is lost to friction, the drop is spent and nothing is gained: entropy is generated. Generated entropy can never be destroyed, so the total entropy of everything involved can only stay the same or grow. It is the same one-way rule as the bedroom below, which gets messy by itself but never tidies itself.

Definition · entropy

The second law leads to a new property called entropy \(S\):

  • a quantitative measure of microscopic disorder of a system (picture a neatly ordered mosaic ball next to the same pieces scattered: going from ordered to disordered is an increase in entropy);
  • a measure of energy that is no longer available to do useful work in the present environment;
  • a measure of the quality of a system's energy.

Unit: kJ/K (total), or kJ/(kg·K) for specific entropy \(s\).

The working definition of entropy comes from the Clausius inequality (next box); it is not derived here. Picture entropy increase as two drawings of the same bedroom: (A) tidy, (B) messy. A room becomes messy by itself; it never tidies itself.

Order to disorder: the same molecules, packed in a corner and then spread through the whole box. The spread-out state has higher entropy, and the change happens by itself only in this direction.

Going further · the Clausius inequality

For any cycle, the heat received at each moment divided by the absolute temperature at which it is received, added up round the cycle, can never be positive:

\[\oint\frac{\delta Q}{T}\le0\]

In words: round a complete cycle, the sum of (heat in ÷ absolute temperature) is zero for a perfect (reversible) cycle and negative for every real one.

For a reversible cycle the sum is exactly zero, which means \(\delta Q/T\) along a reversible path is the change of a property: that property is entropy, \(\mathrm dS=(\delta Q/T)_\text{rev}\).

Entropy balance and entropy generation

Write the change of a system's entropy between states 1 and 2 as two parts:

Result · entropy change of a system \[\Delta S_\text{sys}=S_2-S_1=\int_1^2\frac{\delta Q_\text{net}}{T}+S_\text{gen}\]

In words: change in the system's entropy = entropy carried in with heat (each bit of heat divided by the absolute temperature at which it crosses) + entropy generated inside by irreversibilities. Unit kJ/K.

Reading the formula
\(\Delta S_\text{sys}=S_2-S_1\)
the change in the system's entropy, final minus initial.
\(\displaystyle\int_1^2\frac{\delta Q_\text{net}}{T}\)
entropy carried across the boundary with heat: positive for heat in, negative for heat out. The same heat carries more entropy when it crosses at a lower temperature.
\(S_\text{gen}\)
entropy made inside by friction, mixing, or heat falling through a finite temperature difference: the wasted drop. Never negative.
the \(+\)
heat can bring entropy in or take it out; generation can only add.

Picture a system (gas under a piston, with mass \(m\), heat \(Q\) and work \(W\) crossing its boundary) sitting inside its surroundings, and a big boundary drawn round both, the isolated system boundary, with \(m=0\), \(Q=0\), \(W=0\): nothing crosses it. For the isolated whole, no heat crosses the outer boundary, so all entropy change is generated:

Result · increase-of-entropy principle \[S_\text{gen}=\Delta S_\text{total}=\Delta S_\text{sys}+\sum\Delta S_\text{surr}\ge0\]
\(S_\text{gen}\)Process
\(\gt0\)irreversible (every real process)
\(=0\)reversible (ideal limit)
\(\lt0\)impossible

In words: the total entropy of a system plus its surroundings can only stay the same (reversible process) or grow (irreversible process); it can never fall.

The system and its surroundings together form an isolated system.

What is this? (reversible or not)

Film a ball rolling to and fro in a smooth, frictionless bowl, and the film looks just as natural run backwards: that is a reversible process. Film a hot cup cooling, or air rushing out of a burst balloon, and the backward film is obviously fake: those are irreversible, and they generate entropy.

Definitions · reversible, irreversible, isentropic
  • A reversible process can be run backwards, leaving no trace on the system or the surroundings. It generates no entropy (\(S_\text{gen}=0\)). It is an ideal limit, approached by slow, frictionless processes with heat moving across tiny temperature differences.
  • An irreversible process generates entropy (\(S_\text{gen}\gt0\)). Causes: friction, heat transfer across a finite temperature difference, unrestrained expansion of a gas, mixing of different substances, electrical resistance.
  • An isentropic process has constant entropy: \(\Delta s=0\). A process that is both adiabatic (no heat, so no entropy carried in) and reversible (no entropy generated) is isentropic.
Example · why heat flows from hot to cold

1 kJ of heat flows from a large hot body at 400 K to a large cold body at 300 K (both so big their temperatures do not change). Is this possible? Is the reverse possible?

  1. Hot body loses 1 kJ at 400 K: \(\Delta S_\text{hot}=-1/400=-0.00250\u{kJ/K}\). Heat leaving at 400 K carries entropy \(Q/T\) out of the hot body.
  2. Cold body gains 1 kJ at 300 K: \(\Delta S_\text{cold}=+1/300=+0.00333\u{kJ/K}\).
  3. Total: \(S_\text{gen}=+0.00083\u{kJ/K}\gt0\) (sum of the two): possible, and irreversible. The same 1 kJ divided by a smaller temperature gives more entropy, so heat falling from hot to cold always raises the total.
  4. Reverse (cold to hot): every sign flips, \(S_\text{gen}=-0.00083\u{kJ/K}\lt0\): impossible.

\(\boxed{S_\text{gen}=+8.3\times10^{-4}\u{kJ/K}}\) for hot → cold; the reverse would make \(S_\text{gen}\) negative, so it is impossible. This is the coffee of Section 11: the first law allows both directions, the entropy count allows only one.

Ready answer · first law versus second law (May 2025 Q4, 5 marks)
  • First law: conservation of energy; energy changes form but is never created or destroyed; for a closed system \(Q_\text{net}-W_\text{net}=\Delta U\), for a cycle \(Q_\text{net}=W_\text{net}\). It deals with the quantity of energy.
  • Second law: processes go in a definite direction (heat flows hot to cold, gases expand from high to low pressure); it deals with the quality of energy.
  • The first law places no restriction on direction; satisfying it does not guarantee a process can happen. A process must satisfy both laws.
  • The second law defines entropy: \(S_\text{gen}=\Delta S_\text{total}\ge0\); \(=0\) reversible, \(\gt0\) irreversible, \(\lt0\) impossible.
  • Example: a hot coffee cooling in cold air obeys both; the coffee heating up from the cold air would obey the first law but break the second.
Going further · which property stays constant? (May 2023 Q4(ii))

The exam asked which property stays essentially constant in three processes:

  • (a) Saturated liquid water enters a boiler, saturated vapour leaves: pressure and temperature (a phase change at constant pressure happens at the saturation temperature, T2).
  • (b) Air enters a reversible adiabatic compressor at 300 K, 1 bar and leaves at 500 K: entropy (reversible + adiabatic = isentropic). The temperature and pressure both rise.
  • (c) An ideal gas expands adiabatically through a valve just opened (a throttling process): enthalpy stays constant (a result for flowing streams); for an ideal gas \(h\) depends only on \(T\), so the temperature also stays constant. Entropy rises: throttling is irreversible.

13Heat engines, the Carnot and Otto cycles, refrigerators and COP ~17 min

What is this?

A water wheel can only use the drop between the top pond and the bottom pond. A heat engine is the same: heat falls from \(T_H\) to \(T_L\), and at best the engine turns into work the fraction of the fall it gets to use, \((T_H-T_L)/T_H\), with heights measured from absolute zero. To turn all the heat into work you would need a cold side at absolute zero, so every engine must throw some heat away. One difference from water: all the water reaches the bottom, but in an engine part of the heat becomes work and only the rest, \(Q_L\), reaches the cold side. A refrigerator is the wheel run backwards as a pump: work lifts heat from cold to hot.

Heat engines and thermal efficiency

Definitions · reservoir, heat engine
  • A thermal reservoir is a body so large that taking heat from it or giving heat to it does not change its temperature (the atmosphere, the sea, a furnace kept hot by burning fuel). \(T_H\) is the high-temperature reservoir, \(T_L\) the low-temperature one.
  • A heat engine is a device that runs in a cycle, receives heat \(Q_H\) from a hot reservoir, converts part of it into net work \(W_\text{net}\) and rejects the rest, \(Q_L\), to a cold reservoir. Car engines and power stations are heat engines.

Left: a heat engine (heat flows down from hot to cold and some of it is turned into work). Right: a refrigerator or heat pump (work is spent to push heat up from cold to hot). Arrow widths show the energy amounts; in each case energy in = energy out.

Result · thermal efficiency \[\eta=\frac{W_\text{net}}{Q_H}=1-\frac{Q_L}{Q_H}\]

In words: efficiency = useful work out ÷ heat put in. Since \(Q_H=W_\text{net}+Q_L\) (first law for a cycle), it is also 1 − (heat thrown away ÷ heat put in).

Reading the formula
\(W_\text{net}\)
useful work out: the wheel's output.
\(Q_H\)
heat taken from the hot side: what you pay for in fuel.
\(Q_L\)
heat thrown to the cold side: the part that reaches the bottom without turning the wheel.
\(1-Q_L/Q_H\)
since \(Q_H=W_\text{net}+Q_L\), whatever is not thrown away is work.

The second law (Kelvin–Planck statement) says no engine can have \(\eta=1\): some heat must always be rejected to a cold reservoir.

The Carnot cycle

What is this? (Carnot cycle)

The Carnot cycle is the perfect water wheel: no friction and no splashing. Heat is taken in only at the top temperature and given out only at the bottom one, each time across a vanishingly small temperature difference, so no drop is wasted and no entropy is generated. Nothing working between the same two temperatures can do better.

Definition · Carnot cycle

The Carnot cycle is the ideal, fully reversible cycle between two reservoirs. It has four reversible processes:

  1. 1 → 2 isothermal expansion at \(T_H\): the gas absorbs heat \(Q_H\) from the hot reservoir while expanding (its temperature stays at \(T_H\) because it does work as fast as heat enters).
  2. 2 → 3 adiabatic (isentropic) expansion: no heat; the gas keeps expanding and cools from \(T_H\) to \(T_L\).
  3. 3 → 4 isothermal compression at \(T_L\): the gas rejects heat \(Q_L\) to the cold reservoir.
  4. 4 → 1 adiabatic (isentropic) compression: no heat; the gas is compressed and warms from \(T_L\) back to \(T_H\).

On a \(P\)-\(V\) diagram: two isotherms (\(PV=\) constant) and two steeper adiabats (\(PV^k=\) constant). The engine goes round clockwise, so the enclosed area is positive net work.

Result · Carnot efficiency \[\eta_\text{Carnot}=1-\frac{T_L}{T_H}\]

In words: the best possible efficiency of any engine between two temperatures = 1 − (cold absolute temperature ÷ hot absolute temperature). Temperatures in kelvin.

Reading the formula
\(T_H\)
the height of the top pond, measured from absolute zero.
\(T_L/T_H\)
the fraction of that height the wheel cannot use: from the bottom pond down to absolute zero.
\(1-T_L/T_H\)
the usable fraction: the drop \((T_H-T_L)\) over the whole height \(T_H\).
only temperatures
the limit does not depend on the gas or the machine, only on the two reservoirs.

Example: between 600 K and 300 K, \(\eta_\text{Carnot}=1-300/600=0.50\). No real engine between these temperatures can do better than 50%.

The reversed Carnot cycle: heat pumps and refrigerators

Run every Carnot process backwards (anticlockwise on \(P\)-\(V\)): the gas now expands isothermally at \(T_L\), absorbing heat \(Q_L\) from the cold space; is compressed adiabatically to \(T_H\); is compressed isothermally at \(T_H\), rejecting heat \(Q_H\) to the warm surroundings; and expands adiabatically back to \(T_L\). Net work must be put in. This moves heat from cold to hot, which the second law allows only when work is supplied.

  • A refrigerator (or freezer) uses this to keep a cold space cold: the useful effect is \(Q_L\) taken out of the cold space.
  • A heat pump uses the same cycle to warm a building: the useful effect is \(Q_H\) delivered to the warm space.

Carnot cycles (the two isotherms are \(PV=\) constant; the adiabats are drawn steeper; not to scale, because for real gases the loop is a thin sliver). Left: engine, clockwise, heat in on the top isotherm. Right: heat pump or refrigerator, the same loop anticlockwise, heat absorbed on the bottom isotherm and rejected on the top one.

Result · coefficient of performance (COP) \[\text{COP}_R=\frac{Q_L}{W_\text{in}},\qquad\text{COP}_{HP}=\frac{Q_H}{W_\text{in}}\] \[\text{COP}_{R,\text{Carnot}}=\frac{T_L}{T_H-T_L}\] \[\text{COP}_{HP,\text{Carnot}}=\frac{T_H}{T_H-T_L}\]

In words: COP = useful heat moved ÷ work paid. For the ideal reversed Carnot cycle it depends only on the two absolute temperatures. A COP is normally bigger than 1 (it is not an efficiency), and \(\text{COP}_{HP}=\text{COP}_R+1\).

Reading the formula
\(Q_L\) (refrigerator)
heat lifted out of the cold space: what you want.
\(W_\text{in}\)
work paid to drive the pump.
\(T_H-T_L\) on the bottom
the height of the lift: the bigger the lift, the more work each kilojoule of cooling costs.
\(\text{COP}_{HP}=\text{COP}_R+1\)
the heat delivered at the top includes the pump work itself: \(Q_H=Q_L+W_\text{in}\).

A ton of refrigeration is an old unit of cooling rate (the rate that melts one ton of ice in a day): about 211 kJ/min = 3.5 kW. The Dec 2018 paper gives 1 ton = 210 kJ/min.

Past paper · Dec 2018 Q2(b) · 4 marks · T4.6

A fish freezing plant requires 20 tons of refrigeration. The freezing temperature is −40 °C while the ambient temperature is 24 °C. If the performance of the plant is 25% of the theoretical reversed Carnot cycle working within the same temperature limits, state whether it is a heat engine problem or heat pump problem. Calculate the power required in kW. Given: 1 ton of refrigeration = 210 kJ/min.

  1. Heat is moved from the cold freezer (−40 °C) to the warmer surroundings (24 °C) by spending work: a reversed cycle, so a heat pump (refrigerator) problem, not a heat engine. Heat is being lifted uphill, which needs a pump, not a wheel.
  2. Kelvin: \(T_L=233.15\u{K}\), \(T_H=297.15\u{K}\).
  3. Carnot COP:\[\text{COP}_\text{Carnot}=\frac{233.15}{297.15-233.15}=3.643\]
  4. Actual \(\text{COP}=0.25\times3.643=0.911\).
  5. Load: \(20\times210=4200\u{kJ/min}=70\u{kW}\).
  6. Power \(=\dfrac{\text{load}}{\text{COP}}=\dfrac{70}{0.911}\). COP is cooling per unit of work, so the work needed is the cooling divided by the COP.

\(\boxed{\dot W\approx76.9\u{kW}}\) (heat-pump/refrigerator problem).

why the temperatures must be in kelvin

1. In °C the formula would give \(-40/(24-(-40))=-0.625\): a negative COP, which is meaningless. The Carnot formulas come from \(Q_L/Q_H=T_L/T_H\), a ratio of absolute temperatures.

2. The difference \(T_H-T_L=64\) is the same in °C and K; only the numerator needs converting. Use 273.15; with 273 the answer is 76.9 kW to three figures as well.

The Otto cycle: the ideal petrol engine

What is this? (Otto cycle)

The Otto cycle is a cartoon of a petrol engine: it keeps the order of events (squeeze, burn, push, exhaust) and replaces each messy real stage with a clean ideal one, such as burning that happens instantly. The real engine's diagram is the cartoon with its corners rounded off.

A four-stroke petrol engine repeats four piston strokes (two turns of the crankshaft): intake (piston moves down, drawing in fuel–air mixture), compression (piston moves up, valves closed), power (the spark ignites the mixture, pressure jumps, the gas pushes the piston down) and exhaust (piston moves up, pushing burnt gas out). The Otto cycle is the idealised model of this, and an indicator diagram is the measured (or idealised) \(p\)-\(V\) plot of the gas in the cylinder.

Past paper · Dec 2018 Q3(a) · 6 marks · T4.5

Figure A3 shows the idealized and practical forms of indicator diagram for a four stroke internal combustion engine (Otto cycle). Identify and explain three differences between the two diagrams.

  1. Rounded combustion peak, lower maximum pressure. Ideal: heat added instantly at constant volume (vertical C → D). Real: burning takes time while the piston is moving (and the spark fires slightly before top dead centre), so the pressure rise is rounded and the peak is lower.
  2. Rounded release instead of a vertical drop. Ideal: heat rejected instantly at bottom dead centre (vertical E → B). Real: the exhaust valve opens before bottom dead centre and the gas takes time to blow down, so the corner is rounded and some expansion work is lost.
  3. A pumping loop. Ideal: intake and exhaust both on the atmospheric line (no area). Real: the intake is slightly below and the exhaust slightly above atmospheric pressure (valves and pipes resist the flow), forming a thin negative-work loop: pumping loss.
  4. Also acceptable: real compression and expansion are not adiabatic (heat is lost to the cooled cylinder walls) and there is friction, so the curves are not ideal isentropes and the real loop's area (net work) is smaller.

In short: the real loop has rounded corners, a lower peak, a pumping loop and a smaller area (less net work).

17Concept checks

1 · Heat in, yet colder

Can a gas get colder while heat is being added to it? Give an example.

Answer

Yes, if it does more work than the heat it receives: \(\Delta U=Q-W\lt0\). The Section 7 worked example: 50 kJ in, 75 kJ of work out, the air cools from 500 K to 400 K.

2 · Rigid tank

Gas in a sealed rigid steel tank is heated with 30 kJ. How much work does it do, and what is \(\Delta U\)?

Answer

No volume change, so \(W=0\) and \(\Delta U=Q=30\u{kJ}\). All the heat is stored.

3 · Isothermal expansion

An ideal gas expands at constant temperature and does 12 kJ of work. Find \(\Delta U\) and \(Q\).

Hint

For an ideal gas \(U\) depends on \(T\) only.

Answer

\(\Delta T=0\), so \(\Delta U=0\) and \(Q=W=+12\u{kJ}\): every joule of work is paid for by heat flowing in.

4 · A cycle

In one cycle an engine's gas receives 800 kJ of heat and rejects 500 kJ. What is the net work? What is \(\Delta U\) for the cycle?

Answer

\(Q_\text{net}=800-500=300\u{kJ}=W_\text{net}\) (work out); \(\Delta U_\text{cycle}=0\).

5 · Entropy of a cooling coffee

A hot coffee cools in a room, so its entropy decreases. Does this break the second law?

Answer

No. The room receives the same heat at a lower temperature, so its entropy rises by more than the coffee's falls (\(Q/T_\text{room}\gt Q/T_\text{coffee}\)). \(S_\text{gen}=\Delta S_\text{total}\gt0\): irreversible, allowed.

6 · Steam as an ideal gas

Water vapour in humid air at 25 °C has a partial pressure of about 2 kPa. May you use \(Pv=RT\) for it? What about steam at 8 MPa in a power-station boiler?

Answer

At 2 kPa, yes: below 10 kPa the error is negligible. At 8 MPa, no: errors of 10–20% or more near saturation; use the steam tables.

Consider a combustion engine with 2 kg of air at a temperature of 500 K initially. 200 kJ of heat is added to the air, while the piston of the engine expands such that the air performs 150 kJ of work. What is the final temperature of the air? Given that the specific heat at constant volume \(C_v=0.733\u{kJ/kg\cdot K}\) and \(\Delta U=mC_v\Delta T\).

Hint

The Section 7 worked example with new numbers. Heat in and work by the air are both positive.

Answer

\(\Delta U=Q-W=200-150=50\u{kJ}\).

\(\Delta T=\dfrac{\Delta U}{mC_v}=\dfrac{50}{2(0.733)}=34.1\u{K}\).

\[\boxed{T_\text{final}=500+34.1\approx534\u{K}}\]

Dec 2023 Q6 (1 kg, 100 kJ in, 75 kJ out) gives \(\Delta U=25\u{kJ}\) and the same 534 K.

Dec 2021 Q2(a)(b)8 + 9 marksT4.1 ideal gas with an energy balance

Two tanks are connected by a valve. One tank contains 2 kg of carbon monoxide gas at 77 °C and 0.7 bar. The other tank holds 8 kg of the same gas at 27 °C and 1.2 bar. The valve is opened and the gases are allowed to mix while receiving energy by heat transfer from the surroundings. The final equilibrium temperature is 428 C. Using the ideal gas model with constant \(C_p\), determine

(a) the final equilibrium pressure, in bars. (8 marks)
(b) the heat transfer for the process, in kJ. (9 marks)

The paper appends Table A-20 (ideal-gas specific heats against temperature); for carbon monoxide it gives \(c_p=1.040\), \(c_v=0.744\) at 300 K and \(c_p=1.043\), \(c_v=0.746\) at 350 K, in kJ/(kg·K).

"428 C" is read as 42 °C (degree sign printed as 8; the original problem has 42 °C). "Constant \(C_p\)" is read as constant specific heats: a rigid-tank energy balance needs \(c_v\). Results for 428 °C are given at the end.

Hint

(a) Each tank's volume from \(V=mRT/P\) with \(R=c_p-c_v\); then \(P_f=m_\text{tot}RT_f/V_\text{tot}\). Kelvin! (b) Rigid tanks: \(W=0\), \(Q=\Delta U\), each portion from its own starting temperature.

Answer

(a) \(R=1.040-0.744=0.296\u{kJ/(kg\,K)}\) (or \(8.314/28.01=0.2968\)). Pressures in kPa: 70 and 120.

\[V_1=\frac{2(0.296)(350.15)}{70}=2.961\u{m^3}\] \[V_2=\frac{8(0.296)(300.15)}{120}=5.923\u{m^3}\] \[\begin{aligned}P_f&=\frac{10(0.296)(315.15)}{8.884}\\&=105.0\u{kPa}\end{aligned}\] \[\boxed{P_f\approx1.05\u{bar}}\]

(Either value of \(R\) gives the same \(P_f\): \(R\) cancels, Section 10.)

(b) Take \(c_v=0.745\u{kJ/(kg\,K)}\) (Table A-20 between 300 and 350 K; 0.744 gives 37.2 kJ).

\[\begin{aligned}Q&=\Delta U\\&=m_1c_v(T_f-T_1)+m_2c_v(T_f-T_2)\\&=0.745[2(42-77)+8(42-27)]\\&=0.745(-70+120)\end{aligned}\] \[\boxed{Q\approx+37.3\u{kJ}\ \text{(heat received)}}\]

If 428 °C is taken literally (same method, constant \(c_v=0.745\)): \(P_f=233.6\u{kPa}\approx2.34\u{bar}\) and \(Q\approx2.91\times10^{3}\u{kJ}\). Such a large temperature rise from mixing two gases near room temperature is not plausible, which is why 42 °C is adopted.

Dec 2023 Q13 marksT4.4 second law & reversibility

Which of the following requirement is necessary for a reversible thermodynamic process (can be more than one answer)? (a) Total energy is conserved. (b) Internal energy is conserved. (c) Entropy generation is zero. (d) Entropy generation is positive. (e) Entropy generation is negative.

Hint

Every process must satisfy the first law; what extra does "reversible" demand?

Answer

(a) and (c). Total energy is conserved in every possible process (first law), so it is necessary here too; a reversible process generates no entropy, \(S_\text{gen}=0\). (b) is not required: a reversible process can change \(U\) (a slow reversible compression heats a gas). (d) describes irreversible processes, (e) impossible ones.

May 2025 Q45 marksT4.4 second law & reversibility

State the differences between the first law and the second law of thermodynamics.

Hint

Quantity versus quality; what each law allows; how entropy enters. Five marks: five clear points.

Answer
  • First law: energy is conserved; it can change form but cannot be created or destroyed (\(Q_\text{net}-W_\text{net}=\Delta U\); \(Q_\text{net}=W_\text{net}\) for a cycle).
  • Second law: processes occur in a definite direction (heat flows from hot to cold, gases expand from high to low pressure).
  • First law: quantity of energy. Second law: quality of energy (how much can still do useful work).
  • The first law places no restriction on direction; a process obeying it may still be impossible. A process must satisfy both.
  • The second law introduces entropy: \(S_\text{gen}=\Delta S_\text{total}\ge0\) (\(=0\) reversible, \(\gt0\) irreversible, \(\lt0\) impossible). Example: cold air cannot heat hot coffee, though energy would be conserved.
