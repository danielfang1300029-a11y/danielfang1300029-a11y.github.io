Heat transfer: conduction, convection and radiation

0Where this chapter sits

T1–T2 described a substance's state; this chapter asks how fast heat moves from hot to cold. Heat is passed through a material, carried off by a moving fluid, or sent across a gap as infrared light. Each of these acts like a resistor, so a whole wall becomes a simple circuit.

Sign convention for this chapter
  • Heat flows from hot to cold, never the other way by itself. Draw the heat-flow arrow from the hotter side to the colder side.
  • A heat rate \(q\) is positive in the direction of the arrow you drew. If you guessed the direction wrong, \(q\) comes out negative: the heat really flows the other way, with the same size.
  • In \(q=\Delta T/R\), \(\Delta T\) is "upstream temperature minus downstream temperature" (the side the arrow starts from, minus the side it points to), so it is positive when the arrow is right.
  • Walking along the arrow through a resistance \(R\), the temperature drops by \(qR\): \(T_\text{next}=T-qR\).
  • A temperature difference is the same in °C and in K (a change of 1 °C is a change of 1 K), so \(k\), \(h\) and \(R\) may be quoted "per °C" or "per K" interchangeably. But radiation uses absolute temperatures: every \(T\) inside \(T^4\) must be in kelvin (\(T/\mathrm K = T/^\circ\mathrm C + 273.15\)).
  • Everything is steady (nothing changes with time) and one-dimensional (heat flows straight through the wall, along \(x\)), as in every example here.

1Heat transfer and its three modes ~6 min

What is this?

Think of a campfire. Heat reaches you in three ways. Hold a metal poker in it and the handle warms, because heat is passed along the metal from particle to particle (conduction). Blow on a bowl of hot soup and the moving air carries heat away from its surface (convection). Sit across the room from a fire and your face still feels warm, although no hot air reaches you, because the fire sends out infrared light (radiation). These three pictures come back in every section.

Definition · heat transfer

Heat transfer is the study of how thermal energy (the energy of the random jiggling of molecules) moves from one place to another because of a temperature difference. It happens in three ways, called modes:

  • Conduction: energy passed on by molecular interaction, molecule to neighbouring molecule, through a material that stays still (a solid, or a fluid at rest).
  • Convection: energy carried by fluid motion: a moving liquid or gas picks up heat at a hot surface and carries it away.
  • Radiation: energy carried by electromagnetic waves (mainly infrared light). It needs no material at all and crosses a vacuum, which is how sunlight reaches the Earth.

The three modes. Left: a metal rod in a flame gets hot along its length (conduction). Middle: air heated by a hot plate rises and is replaced by cooler air (convection). Right: a hot body sends infrared waves across empty space to a cooler one (radiation).

Four everyday scenes where all three modes act at once:

  • A campfire. A hand holding a metal poker in the fire feels the handle warm up (conduction along the poker). A hand held above the flames feels the rising hot air (convection). A hand held to the side, out of the rising air, still feels warm (radiation from the glowing fire).
  • A heated house in winter. A heater warms the room air, which circulates around the room (convection); heat leaks out through the walls and roof (conduction); the sun warms the roof (radiation). The picture marks 70 °F (21 °C) inside and 30 °F (−1 °C) outside.
  • A lizard on a rock. It gains heat from the sun (radiation) and from the warm rock under its belly (conduction), loses heat to the breeze (convection) and by evaporation of moisture.
  • The Earth. Heat moves out from the hot core by conduction and by slow convection of the molten mantle, and the Earth radiates heat into space.

Many machines are designed around convection: a water kettle (water boiler), the heat exchanger (finned coil) in an air conditioner, a computer case with fans, a pressure cooker, a car engine (cooled by circulating coolant) and a room radiator (heater). In each one a fluid carries the heat to or from a surface.

Going further · describing a heating process step by step

An exam favourite (May 2019 Q2, 8 marks) asks you to describe heating soup on a stove, mode by mode. The pattern: (1) the stove element radiates and conducts heat to the pot base; (2) heat conducts through the metal base; (3) soup touching the base is heated by conduction, becomes less dense and rises, cooler soup sinks: natural convection currents mix the pot; (4) at the base, bubbles of vapour form once the soup reaches its boiling point; the pot also loses heat from its sides and lid by convection and radiation. Draw the pot with arrows for each step. The full answer skeleton is in the exam guide.

Thinking of heat flow like electricity

2Heat, heat rate and heat flux ~3 min

What is this?

Three different questions about the same flow of heat. How much heat got through in total? How fast is it getting through? How fast is it getting through each square metre? A big window and a small window, made of the same glass in the same wall, have the same flux but very different heat rates.

Definitions · three ways to measure "how much heat"
  • Heat \(Q\): the amount of thermal energy transferred. Unit: joule (J).
  • Heat flow rate (heat rate) \(q\), also written \(\dot Q\) ("Q-dot", the dot means "per second"): heat transferred per unit time, \(q=Q/t\). Unit: J/s = watt (W).
  • Heat flux \(Q''\) ("Q double-prime"): heat rate through a surface per square metre of that surface. Unit: W/m².
Result · heat flux \[Q''=\frac{\dot Q}{A}=\frac{q}{A}\]

In words: heat flux = heat rate ÷ area it passes through.

Analogy: think of rain. \(Q\) is the total litres of rain that fell, \(q\) is litres per second falling on your whole roof, and \(Q''\) is the rain intensity (litres per second per square metre), which is the same for a small roof and a large one. Flux is useful because it does not depend on how big the wall is: double the wall area and \(q\) doubles, but \(Q''\) stays the same.

Example

A 500 W heater runs for one hour. The heat delivered is \(Q=qt=500\times3600\u{J}\), which is \(1.8\times10^{6}\u{J}=1.8\u{MJ}\). If a window of area 1.5 m² lets out \(4500\u{W}\), the heat flux through it is \(Q''=4500/1.5=3000\u{W/m^2}\).

Watch

Read the question's units. "Rate of heat loss per unit area" means flux (W/m²); "heat flow rate across the wall" means \(q\) (W). Several examples set \(A=1\u{m^2}\), so the two numbers are equal there, but in exam walls \(A\) is 10–20 m².

You can now: convert between heat, heat rate and heat flux; and tell from the units whether a question wants \(q\) (W) or flux (W/m²).

3Conduction and the temperature gradient ~5 min

What is this?

Picture heat working its way through a wall like a shove passing through a packed crowd. People at the hot side are jostling hard. Each person only bumps their neighbours, and the bumping is passed on row by row towards the calm side. Energy crosses the crowd although nobody walks across it. That is conduction: molecules stay roughly where they are and pass energy on by collisions.

What conduction depends on

Picture a close-up of a surface between a hotter region (left) and a colder region (right). A high-energy (fast) molecule from the hot side collides with a low-energy (slow) molecule near the surface; after the collision the slow one moves faster. Energy has crossed the surface although no molecule travelled far. Repeated billions of times, this is heat conduction \(Q\) from hot to cold.

The rate of conduction depends on four things:

  1. the temperature gradient \(\Delta T/\Delta x\) (how steeply temperature changes with distance);
  2. the cross-section area \(A\) the heat crosses;
  3. the path length \(L\) (thickness);
  4. the material, through its thermal conductivity \(k\).

It does not depend on the mass of the material (May 2023 Q1 asked exactly this).

Temperature gradient

What is this? (temperature gradient)

In the crowd, the gradient is how quickly the jostling dies away as you move through the rows. If it fades sharply over a few rows, energy is being handed on fast. If every row is about as restless as the next, very little is passed on. Heat always goes down the gradient, from the restless side to the calm side.

Definition · temperature gradient

The temperature gradient between two points \(A\) and \(B\) a distance \(\Delta x\) apart is the temperature change per unit length:

\[\text{gradient}=\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta x}=\frac{T_B-T_A}{\Delta x}\quad(\mathrm{K/m}).\]

It says in which direction, and how quickly, temperature changes at a place. It is the "slope" of the graph of \(T\) against position \(x\).

Heat always flows down the gradient, from hotter to colder, until the temperature difference has disappeared and the two places are in thermal equilibrium (same temperature). (People sometimes say "temperature flows"; it is heat that flows. Temperature is a property of a place; heat is what moves.)

Steady conduction gives a straight-line profile

What is this? (steady state)

Steady state is a crowd in which every row hands on exactly as much as it receives each second. No row gets more restless or calmer over time, even though energy keeps streaming through. In a uniform material each row needs the same difference from its neighbour to pass on that same amount, so the restlessness falls off in even steps: a straight line.

Definition · steady state

A process is steady (in steady state) when nothing at any point changes with time: every temperature stays fixed, even though heat keeps flowing through.

Picture a bar between a hot object (left, red) and a cold object (right, blue), both kept at constant temperature. Look at one thin slice of the bar, thickness \(\mathrm dx\) (mass \(\mathrm dm\)). Heat \(\dot Q_x\) enters its left face and \(\dot Q_{x+\mathrm dx}\) leaves its right face.

Result · steady 1-D conduction, no heat generated inside \[\frac{\mathrm d^2T}{\mathrm dx^2}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T(x)\ \text{is a straight line}\]

In words: in steady state, with no heat made inside the material, the temperature falls in a straight line through a uniform layer.

why the profile must be a straight line

1. Steady state: the slice's temperature is not changing, so its stored energy is not changing. Energy in = energy out for the slice.

2. So \(\dot Q_x=\dot Q_{x+\mathrm dx}\): the same heat rate crosses every slice. No heat is made or absorbed inside (no "heat generation").

3. The heat rate through a slice is proportional to the local slope \(\mathrm dT/\mathrm dx\) (Section 4: \(q=-kA\,\mathrm dT/\mathrm dx\)). With \(k\) and \(A\) the same everywhere, equal \(q\) means equal slope everywhere.

4. A constant slope means \(\mathrm d^2T/\mathrm dx^2=0\) (the slope does not change), and a graph with constant slope is a straight line: \(T=T_1+(\text{slope})\,x\).

Because the profile is straight, any temperature inside the layer follows by linear interpolation. With \(T_1\) on the hot face, \(T_2\) on the cold face, total thickness \(\Delta x\), and \(T_x\) at distance \(\Delta x_1\) from the hot face (\(\Delta x_2\) from the cold face):

\[\frac{T_2-T_1}{\Delta x}=\frac{T_x-T_1}{\Delta x_1}=\frac{T_2-T_x}{\Delta x_2}\]

In words: the slope is the same whether you measure it over the whole layer or over either part.

Example

A 0.1 m thick plate has faces at \(T_1=100\) °C and \(T_2=20\) °C. Find the temperature 25 mm from the hot face.

\[\begin{aligned}T_x&=T_1+\frac{T_2-T_1}{\Delta x}\,\Delta x_1\\&=100+\frac{-80}{0.1}(0.025)\\&=\boxed{80\ ^\circ\mathrm C}\end{aligned}\]

The gradient is \(-800\u{K/m}\): the temperature falls 8 °C every centimetre.

You can now: list what the conduction rate depends on (and that mass is not one of them); find a temperature gradient; explain why steady conduction gives a straight-line profile; and find a temperature inside a layer by linear interpolation.

4Thermal conductivity and the conduction law ~7 min

What is this?

Back to the crowd. Make its front wider (a bigger area \(A\)) and more people pass the shove on side by side, so more heat gets through. Make it deeper (a bigger thickness \(L\)) and the shove has more rows to cross, so less gets through. Jostle harder at the hot side (a bigger temperature difference) and more gets through. Some crowds pass the shove on eagerly and some sluggishly: that eagerness is the conductivity \(k\).

Here is the classic experiment. A metal bar of cross-section area \(A\) and length \(L\) is wrapped in lagging (insulation, so no heat escapes from its sides). A heater at the left end keeps that end at \(T_1\); cooling water flowing through a sink at the right end keeps it at \(T_2\). The heat carried away by the water (from its flow rate and temperature rise) equals the heat rate through the bar.

Experiments like this show:

  • \(q\) is proportional to the temperature difference per length: \(q\propto (T_1-T_2)/L\) (twice the difference or half the length: twice the heat);
  • \(q\) is proportional to the area: \(q\propto A\) (two bars side by side carry twice the heat).

Combining the two and naming the constant of proportionality \(k\):

Law · conduction through a plane layer (Fourier's law) \[q=\frac{Q}{t}=kA\,\frac{T_1-T_2}{L}=kA\,\frac{\Delta T}{L}\] \[Q''=\frac{q}{A}=k\,\frac{T_1-T_2}{L}\]

In words: conduction heat rate = conductivity × area × (temperature difference ÷ thickness). Flux = conductivity × temperature drop per metre.

Reading the formula
\(q\)
the heat crossing the layer each second: how much of the shove gets through the crowd.
\(k\)
how readily the material passes heat on: an eager crowd (metal) or a sluggish one (foam, still air).
\(A\)
the width of the crowd's front: more people side by side carry more.
\(T_1-T_2\)
how much harder the hot side jostles than the cold side: what drives the flow.
\(\div L\)
the depth of the crowd: the same difference spread over more rows is a gentler gradient, so less heat.
Definition · thermal conductivity \(k\)

The thermal conductivity \(k\) is a property of the material: the heat rate (W) through a 1 m² by 1 m thick block with a 1 K temperature difference across it. Unit: W/(m·K) = W/(m·°C). Large \(k\) (metals) = good conductor; small \(k\) (wood, foam, still air) = good insulator.

Going further · the minus sign and typical values

In its general form Fourier's law is written \(q=-kA\,\mathrm dT/\mathrm dx\): the minus sign says heat flows towards decreasing temperature. These notes write \(T_1-T_2\) (hot minus cold) instead, which already makes \(q\) positive. Typical \(k\) in W/(m·K) near room temperature: copper ≈ 400, aluminium ≈ 200 (alloys 50–200), steel ≈ 15–50, glass ≈ 0.8–1, concrete ≈ 1–1.5, common brick ≈ 0.7, water ≈ 0.6, wood ≈ 0.1–0.2, mineral wool / foam ≈ 0.03–0.07, still air ≈ 0.026.

Going further · why metals conduct faster than wood

(Asked in Dec 2018 Q1(b), 10 marks.) Heat is carried through solids in two ways. (1) Lattice vibrations: atoms are bonded to their neighbours, so a vibrating atom shakes the next one, passing energy along like a wave in a chain of springs. All solids do this. (2) Free electrons: metals have electrons that are not tied to one atom and move fast through the whole metal. They pick up energy in hot regions and carry it quickly to cold regions, colliding with atoms there. This is why good electrical conductors are also good heat conductors. Wood has no free electrons and is porous (full of trapped air, \(k\approx0.026\)), so it conducts only by slow lattice vibrations through a tangled structure: its \(k\) is hundreds of times smaller (Dec 2018 gives 54 for an aluminium alloy, 0.147 for wood: a factor of about 370).

Example

A single-glazed window is 4 mm thick, area 1.5 m², glass \(k=0.8\u{W/(m\,K)}\). Its inner surface is at 20 °C and its outer surface at 5 °C. Find the heat rate.

\[q=kA\frac{\Delta T}{L}=0.8(1.5)\frac{20-5}{0.004}=\boxed{4500\u{W}}\]

That is huge. Section 12 shows why a real window loses far less: the thin layers of still air on each side, not the glass, set the heat rate.

Melting ice by conduction

A classic demonstration: two similar-looking dark blocks (one aluminium, one plastic), each with a rubber ring on top to hold an ice cube, and a stopwatch reading 0.0. Both blocks are at room temperature.

The ice on the aluminium block melts in a minute or two; the ice on the plastic block hardly melts. Aluminium's high \(k\) lets heat from the whole block flow quickly into the ice; plastic's low \(k\) lets only a trickle through. The same idea explains why a metal block feels colder than a plastic one at the same temperature: it pulls heat out of your hand faster.

You can now: use \(q=kA\,\Delta T/L\) and its flux form; say what \(k\) means and which materials have a large or small \(k\); and explain why metals conduct faster than wood.

5Thermal resistance and the electrical analogy ~3 min

What is this?

Thermal resistance answers one question: how many degrees of temperature difference does it take to push one watt through this layer? A thick layer, a poor conductor or a small area needs a big push, so its resistance is large. Written this way, heat flow obeys the same rule as current in a wire, push ÷ resistance, and everything you know about resistors works for walls.

Rearranging the conduction law:

\[k=\frac{q\,L}{A\,\Delta T}\quad\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}\]
Result · thermal resistance of a conducting layer \[R=\frac{\Delta T}{q}=\frac{L}{kA}\quad(\mathrm{K/W})\]

In words: thermal resistance = temperature difference needed per watt of heat flow = thickness ÷ (conductivity × area).

Reading the formula
\(\Delta T/q\)
degrees of push needed for each watt that gets through.
\(L\) on top
a thicker layer: more crowd to cross, more resistance.
\(k\) below
a better conductor resists less.
\(A\) below
a wider layer gives the heat more room, so it resists less.
Definition · thermal resistance

The thermal resistance \(R\) (or \(R_\text{th}\)) between two surfaces is the temperature difference between them that drives a heat rate of 1 W. A thick layer (large \(L\)), a poor conductor (small \(k\)) or a small area all make \(R\) large, so less heat gets through for the same temperature difference. Unit: K/W, often written °C/W (the same thing).

Then \(q=\Delta T/R\), which has exactly the shape of Ohm's law for an electric circuit, \(I=V/R_\text{elect}\). Put a wire with a battery and a resistor beside a slab between two temperatures, and match the quantities:

Electric circuitHeat ("thermal") circuit
current \(I\) (A)heat flow rate \(q=\dot Q\) (W)
voltage (potential difference) \(V\) (V)temperature difference \(\Delta T\) (K)
constant-current sourceheat source (e.g. a heater of fixed power)
electric resistance \(R_\text{elect}\) (Ω)thermal resistance \(R_\text{th}\) (K/W)
\(V=IR_\text{elect}\)\(\Delta T=\dot Q\,R_\text{th}\)

So everything you know from HKDSE Physics about resistors carries over: resistances one after another add (series); resistances side by side combine by reciprocals (parallel); the "voltage drop" across each resistor is (current) × (its resistance). A node (dot in the circuit) is a place with one temperature, such as a surface or an interface between layers.

Watch

\(R=L/(kA)\) has the area inside. Some examples quote "\(AR\)" (unit m²·K/W, the resistance of 1 m² of wall) instead. Keep track of which one you have: \(q=\Delta T/R\) but \(Q''=\Delta T/(AR)\).

You can now: compute \(R=L/(kA)\); use \(q=\Delta T/R\) like Ohm's law; and keep \(R\) (K/W) apart from \(AR\) (m²·K/W).

6Conduction through a composite slab ~5 min

What is this?

Picture water creeping slowly through a row of narrow pipes joined end to end. Every second the same amount of water passes through each pipe: it has nowhere else to go and cannot pile up at a joint. The pressure drops along the pipes add up to the total drop, and the narrowest pipe takes the biggest share. Layers of a wall do exactly this with heat: the same heat rate through each, temperature drops that add up, and the layer with the largest resistance takes the largest drop.

Definition · composite slab (composite wall)

A composite slab is a wall made of several layers of different materials pressed together, such as brick + insulation + plaster. Heat crosses the layers one after another.

Problem. Two layers, conductivities \(k_1,k_2\), thicknesses \(L_1,L_2\), the same area \(A\). The left face is at \(T_L\), the right face at \(T_R\) (\(T_L\gt T_R\)). Assume steady, one-dimensional heat flow. Find the heat rate \(q\), the temperature \(T_i\) at the interface (the contact plane between the layers), and the temperature \(T_x\) anywhere.

Two layers; the temperature falls in a straight line in each, with a kink at the interface.

Its circuit: two resistances in series.

Heat rate and interface temperature

In steady state the same heat rate \(q\) crosses layer 1 and layer 2: heat cannot pile up at the interface (if more came in than went out, the interface would warm up, which is not steady). So

\[q=\frac{T_L-T_R}{R_\text{resultant}}=\frac{T_L-T_i}{R_1}=\frac{T_i-T_R}{R_2}\] \[R_1=\frac{L_1}{k_1A},\qquad R_2=\frac{L_2}{k_2A}\] \[R_\text{resultant}=R_1+R_2\]
Reading the formula
\(\dfrac{T_L-T_R}{R_\text{resultant}}\)
the whole wall: total temperature drop ÷ total resistance.
\(\dfrac{T_L-T_i}{R_1}\)
layer 1 on its own: its own drop ÷ its own resistance.
\(\dfrac{T_i-T_R}{R_2}\)
layer 2 on its own.
all equal to \(q\)
the same heat goes through each layer, like the same water through each pipe.
\(R_1+R_2\)
resistances in a row add, as the pressure drops along joined pipes add.
Result · series layers

In words: the same heat rate goes through every layer in series; the total resistance is the sum; each layer's temperature drop is \(q\) times its own resistance, so the layer with the biggest \(R\) takes the biggest share of the temperature drop.

the interface temperature \(T_i\)

1. Total resistance \(R_1+R_2\) and \(q=(T_L-T_R)/(R_1+R_2)\). Series resistances add.

2. Walk from the left face through layer 1: \(T_i=T_L-qR_1\). Temperature drops by \(qR\) across a resistance, in the direction of flow.

3. Substitute \(q\):

\[\begin{aligned}T_i&=T_L-\frac{R_1}{R_1+R_2}(T_L-T_R)\\&=\frac{R_2T_L+R_1T_R}{R_1+R_2}.\end{aligned}\]

4. Check: if \(R_1=0\) (layer 1 a perfect conductor) then \(T_i=T_L\); if \(R_2=0\) then \(T_i=T_R\). The interface sits close to the face of the layer with the smaller resistance.

Temperature anywhere

Inside each layer the profile is a straight line (Section 3). Measuring \(x\) from the left face:

\[0\lt x\lt L_1:\quad T_x=T_L+\frac{T_i-T_L}{L_1}\,x\] \[L_1\lt x\lt L_1+L_2:\] \[T_x=T_i+\frac{T_R-T_i}{L_2}(x-L_1)\]

In words: start at the temperature at the start of the layer and add (slope of that layer) × (distance into that layer).

Any number of layers

Each layer has its own constant gradient, so the method extends to three or more layers. With faces at \(T_1,T_2,T_3,T_4\) and thicknesses \(L_1,L_2,L_3\):

\[\text{slab 1: }T_x=T_1+\frac{T_2-T_1}{L_1}x\] \[\text{slab 2: }T_x=T_2+\frac{T_3-T_2}{L_2}(x-L_1)\] \[\text{slab 3: }T_x=T_3+\frac{T_4-T_3}{L_3}(x-L_1-L_2)\]

Picture a steep drop across a thin dark slab 1, a gentle slope through a thick light slab 2 and a steep drop across thin slab 3. Steep slope = low \(k\) (the same \(q\) needs a steep gradient to push through a poor conductor, since \(q/A=k\times\text{slope}\)).

Try it · temperature profile through a three-layer wall

Starts as Example 2 (Section 8): insulation \(k=0.07\), 3 cm / brick \(k=0.7\), 10 cm / insulation 3 cm, faces at 150 °C and 10 °C, per 1 m². The \(k\) sliders are logarithmic (0.01 to 100 W/(m·K)). 7Series and parallel thermal resistances ~5 min

What is this?

Layers one after another are the row of pipes from Section 6: their resistances add. Now put materials side by side across the flow, like a metal bolt pushed through foam. Heat can take either route at once, each route carries its own share, and the shares add up. An extra route can only help the heat get through, so the combined resistance is smaller than that of any single route.

Result · combining resistances \[R_\text{series}=R_1+R_2+R_3+\cdots\] \[\frac{1}{R_\text{parallel}}=\frac{1}{R_1}+\frac{1}{R_2}+\frac{1}{R_3}+\cdots\]

In words: layers one after another along the heat path: add the resistances. Paths side by side (heat can take either one): add the reciprocals, then take the reciprocal of the sum. A parallel combination is always smaller than its smallest member: an extra path can only make it easier for heat to get through.

For two in parallel, the shortcut "product over sum" is quicker: \(R_\text{par}=\dfrac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2}\) (e.g. 2 and 3 give 6/5 = 1.2).

Picture a wall of thickness \(\Delta x\) between \(T_A\) and \(T_B\), built as three horizontal strips stacked on top of each other: material 1 (area \(A_1\)) on top, material 2 (\(A_2\)) in the middle, material 3 (\(A_3\)) at the bottom. Heat \(Q\) flows left to right through all three at once. Each strip has \(R_{t,i}=\Delta x/(k_iA_i)\) and the three are in parallel between nodes \(T_A\) and \(T_B\).

Three materials side by side across the flow.

Three resistances in parallel between \(T_A\) and \(T_B\).

A bolt through insulation

An example of parallel resistance: a metal bolt passing through an insulating layer. Draw it three ways: the physical wall (insulation \(k_1\) with a bolt \(k_2\) through it), the two heat paths (through the insulation and through the bolt, side by side), and the circuit (\(R_1\) and \(R_2\) in parallel). With conduction resistances \(R=L/(kA)\) for each path:

\[\frac{1}{R_\text{th,parallel}}=\frac{1}{R_1}+\frac{1}{R_2}=\frac{k_1A_1}{L_1}+\frac{k_2A_2}{L_2}\] \[\dot Q=\dot Q_1+\dot Q_2\]

In words: the total heat rate is the sum of the heat through each path; each path's "conductance" \(kA/L\) adds.

Example · how much do bolts matter?

A 1 m² panel of insulation (\(k=0.04\u{W/(m\,K)}\), 5 cm thick) is held by steel bolts (\(k=45\)) whose total cross-section is 0.002 m² (0.2% of the area). The faces are at 20 °C and 0 °C. Find the heat rate with and without the bolts.

  1. Insulation path: \(A_1=1-0.002=0.998\u{m^2}\), \(R_1=\dfrac{0.05}{0.04(0.998)}=1.2525\u{K/W}\).
  2. Bolt path: \(R_2=\dfrac{0.05}{45(0.002)}=0.5556\u{K/W}\). The bolts, only 0.2% of the area, have a smaller resistance than all the insulation. Steel's \(k\) is over a thousand times the foam's, which far outweighs its tiny area.
  3. Heat through each (same \(\Delta T=20\u{K}\) across both): \(q_1=20/1.2525=15.97\u{W}\), \(q_2=20/0.5556=36.0\u{W}\). Both routes start on the same warm face and end on the same cold face.
  4. Total \(q=51.97\u{W}\) (or \(20/R_\text{par}\) with \(R_\text{par}=0.3849\u{K/W}\)). Without bolts: \(20/(0.05/0.04)=16.0\u{W}\).

\(\boxed{q\approx52\u{W}\text{ with bolts, }16\u{W}\text{ without}}\): the bolts carry 69% of the heat. Engineers call such a path a thermal bridge.

Going further · when is "parallel" exact?

Treating side-by-side materials as parallel resistors assumes each one has the same temperature on its left face and the same on its right face (the nodes of the circuit). Really, heat also leaks sideways between a good and a poor conductor, so the answer is an approximation; it is the standard one, and it is what every exam question expects.

You can now: combine resistances in series and in parallel (product over sum for two); treat side-by-side materials as parallel paths, each with its own area; and explain a thermal bridge.

What is this?

Every example here is a row of layers: the pipes in a row of Section 6. The recipe never changes. Find each layer's resistance, add them, and divide the temperature difference by the total. To find a temperature inside the wall, walk through it and take off each layer's drop.

Worked example 1 · composite slab with three layers

Three layers (green, pink, blue) of conductivities \(k_1,k_2,k_3\) and thicknesses \(\Delta x_1,\Delta x_2,\Delta x_3\), all of area \(A\), carry a known heat rate \(\dot Q\) from face \(A\) (temperature \(T_A\)) to face \(B\) (\(T_B\)). Find \(T_A-T_B\).

  1. Three layers one after another: series. \(R_\text{th,total}=R_\text{th,1}+R_\text{th,2}+R_\text{th,3}\).
  2. Each layer: \(R=\Delta x/(kA)\), so
\[R_\text{th,total}=\frac{\Delta x_1}{k_1A}+\frac{\Delta x_2}{k_2A}+\frac{\Delta x_3}{k_3A}\]

3. "Ohm's law": \(\boxed{T_A-T_B=\dot Q\times R_\text{th,total}}\)

Worked example 2 · brick wall insulated on both sides

A brick wall 0.1 m thick (\(k_2=0.7\u{W/(m\,{}^\circ C)}\)) has 0.03 m of insulation (\(k_1=k_3=0.07\)) on each side. The outer face of the left insulation is at \(T_1=150\) °C and the outer face of the right insulation at \(T_4=10\) °C. Calculate the heat flux.

  1. Series: \(R_\text{resultant}=R_1+R_2+R_3\) with each \(R=L/(kA)\). The area is not given, so work per square metre with \(AR=L/k\). Every layer has the same \(A\), so it cancels from the flux \(Q''=q/A\).
  2. \(AR_1=AR_3=\dfrac{0.03}{0.07}=0.4286\) and \(AR_2=\dfrac{0.1}{0.7}=0.1429\), both in m²·°C/W.
  3. Sum: \(AR=0.4286+0.1429+0.4286\), which is \(1.000\u{m^2\,{}^\circ C/W}\).
  4. Flux: \(Q''=\dfrac{T_1-T_4}{AR}=\dfrac{150-10}{1.000}\), so \(\boxed{Q''=140\u{W/m^2}}\).

Rounding the three terms to 0.42 + 0.42 + 0.14 = 0.98 would give 142 W/m². With unrounded values the sum is exactly 1.000 (0.03/0.07 = 3/7, 0.1/0.7 = 1/7, total 7/7), so 140 W/m² is the precise answer. Keep 3–4 significant figures in intermediate steps.

Worked example 3 · temperature at a point inside an oven wall

An oven's inside is kept at 850 °C. Its walls are 500 mm thick, \(k=0.3\u{W/(m\,{}^\circ C)}\), and the outside wall surface is at 250 °C. Wall area 1 m². Find the thermal resistance, the heat flow rate, and the temperature 200 mm from the inner side.

The interior temperature of 850 °C is taken as the inner surface temperature (no convection film inside).

  1. \(R_\text{th}=\dfrac{L}{kA}=\dfrac{0.5}{0.3\times1}=1.667\u{{}^\circ C/W}\).
  2. \(\dot Q=\dfrac{T_1-T_2}{R_\text{th}}=\dfrac{850-250}{1.667}=360\u{W}\).
  3. Linear profile, at \(x=0.2\u{m}\) from the inside: Steady state, one material and no heat made inside, so the temperature falls in a straight line (Section 3).

Worked example 4 · slab heated by a radiant flux

A plane slab 60 cm thick, \(k=17.5\u{W/(m\,{}^\circ C)}\), absorbs a net radiant heat flux \(Q''=530\u{W/m^2}\) on its left face. The right face is held at \(T_2=38\) °C. Steady state, no heat generation. Find the temperature at the mid-plane (30 cm in) and the maximum temperature in the slab.

  1. Steady state: all the absorbed energy is conducted through the slab, so the conducted flux is 530 W/m². Nothing can build up inside, so whatever arrives at the left face must leave by the right face.
  2. Conduction law in flux form, \(Q''=k(T_1-T_2)/L\): \[\begin{aligned}530&=\frac{17.5(T_1-38)}{0.6}\\T_1-38&=\frac{530(0.6)}{17.5}=18.17\\T_1&=56.2\ {}^\circ\mathrm C\end{aligned}\]
  3. Heat flows from left to right, so the left face is the hottest point: \(T_\text{max}=T_1=56.2\) °C. Heat only flows downhill in temperature, and the straight-line profile has no bump in the middle.
  4. Mid-plane: \(T_x=56.17+\dfrac{38-56.17}{0.6}(0.3)=47.1\) °C (exactly halfway between, since 0.3 m is half of 0.6 m).
\[\boxed{T_\text{mid}=47.1\ {}^\circ\mathrm C,\quad T_\text{max}=56.2\ {}^\circ\mathrm C}\]

The maximum is on the left (heated) face.

What is this?

Here you know how much heat you are willing to let through, and you design the wall to match. In the pipe picture: to cut the flow to a quarter with the same pressure difference, the row of pipes must resist four times as much in total. The new layer supplies the extra resistance.

These two examples work "backwards": the heat rate or a target reduction is known and a property of one layer (its \(k\) or its thickness) is the unknown. This is exam type T3.4.

Worked example 6 · insulation thickness for a 75% cut

An exterior wall is 10 cm of common brick (\(k=0.75\)) followed by 4 cm of gypsum plaster (\(k=0.5\)), area 1 m². What thickness of loosely packed rock-wool insulation (\(k=0.065\)) must be added to reduce the heat loss (or gain) through the wall by 75%? Steady state, no heat generation.

  1. \(R_\text{brick}=\dfrac{0.1}{0.75(1)}=0.1333\u{{}^\circ C/W}\); \(R_\text{plaster}=\dfrac{0.04}{0.5(1)}=0.08\u{{}^\circ C/W}\); \(R_\text{wool}=\dfrac{L_\text{wool}}{0.065(1)}=15.38\,L_\text{wool}\).
  2. Without wool, for an overall temperature difference \(\Delta T\): \(\dot Q_\text{no wool}=\dfrac{\Delta T}{0.1333+0.08}=4.6875\,\Delta T\).
  3. "Reduced by 75%" means 25% is left: \(\dot Q_\text{with}=0.25\,\dot Q_\text{no wool}\), which is \(1.172\,\Delta T\).
  4. With wool, the same heat rate written with the new total resistance: \[\frac{\Delta T}{0.2133+15.38L_\text{wool}}=1.172\,\Delta T\] \(\Delta T\) cancels: Adding insulation does not change the inside and outside temperatures, so only the total resistance decides the heat rate.
\[0.2133+15.38\,L_\text{wool}=\frac{1}{1.172}=0.8533\]

\(\boxed{L_\text{wool}=\dfrac{0.64}{15.38}=0.0416\u{m}\approx4.2\u{cm}}\)

Ready answer · the faster route for "reduce the heat by f"
  1. Do: Same \(\Delta T\) before and after, so \(q\propto1/R_\text{total}\): \[R_\text{new}=\frac{R_\text{old}}{1-f}\] With the same push, leaving only the fraction \(1-f\) of the flow needs the total resistance divided by that fraction.
  2. Do: Then the added layer's resistance is \(R_\text{new}-R_\text{old}\). The old layers are still there; the new layer only has to supply the difference.
  3. Do: \(L=kA(R_\text{new}-R_\text{old})\) or \(k=L/[A(R_\text{new}-R_\text{old})]\). This is \(R=L/(kA)\) solved for whichever property of the new layer is unknown.

For Example 6: \(R_\text{new}=0.2133/0.25=0.8533\), added \(0.64\u{{}^\circ C/W}\), \(L=0.065(1)(0.64)=0.0416\u{m}\).

Watch

"Reduced by 75%" leaves 25% (multiply \(q\) by 0.25, so \(R\) by 4). "Reduced to 75%" leaves 75%. Writing \(R_\text{new}=R_\text{old}/0.75\) for "by 75%" gives a wool layer ten times too thin (4.6 mm).

Which layer should you thicken?

May 2021 Q1(d) asked, without calculation, which one layer of a wall to make 50% thicker to cut the heat loss most. Thickening a layer by 50% multiplies its \(R=L/(kA)\) by 1.5, so it adds half of that layer's resistance to the total. The biggest addition comes from the layer with the largest \(L/(kA)\): usually the low-\(k\) insulation (large \(L/k\), and check its area too if layers have different areas). A thin metal layer is the worst choice: its \(R\) is tiny, so 50% more of it changes almost nothing.

You can now: find an unknown conductivity or thickness from a target heat rate; turn "reduce by \(f\)" into \(R_\text{new}=R_\text{old}/(1-f)\); and pick the layer whose thickening cuts the heat most.

10Drawing the thermal circuit ~3 min

What is this?

A thermal circuit is a map of every route the heat can take from the hot side to the cold side. Whatever the heat must cross one after another sits in a row, like the pipes of Section 6; parts it can cross side by side sit in parallel. Follow the heat with your finger and draw what it meets.

Worked example 7

A composite wall of area \(A\) between \(T_1\) (left) and \(T_2\) (right) has material \(E\) across the whole height, then a middle column split into \(F\) (top) and \(G\) (bottom) with equal thicknesses \(L_F=L_G\), then material \(H\) across the whole height. The top and bottom edges are insulated (shaded). Here \(\lambda_E,\lambda_F,\lambda_G,\lambda_H\) are the thermal resistances of the plates. Draw the circuit and write the resultant resistance.

  1. Follow the heat from left to right. It must go through \(E\) first: \(\lambda_E\) in series.
  2. Then it can go through either \(F\) or \(G\): two side-by-side paths between the same two planes, so \(\lambda_F\parallel\lambda_G\). Heat that goes through \(F\) does not also go through \(G\): each carries part of the flow.
  3. Then through \(H\): \(\lambda_H\) in series.
\[R_\text{resultant}=\lambda_E+\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda_F}+\frac{1}{\lambda_G}\Big)^{-1}+\lambda_H\]

the symbol \(\lambda\) means a resistance in this example but a conductivity in Example 9. Always check which one a question means; in exam papers \(k\) is conductivity and resistances are \(R\).

Ready answer · drawing any composite-wall circuit (exam type T3.2)
  1. Do: Mark the heat-flow direction (hot side to cold side) and put a node at every temperature you know or are asked for: fluid temperatures, surfaces, interfaces. A node is a place with one temperature, so these are the points the circuit has to join up.
  2. Do: Fluid on a face: a convection resistance \(1/(hA)\) between \(T_\infty\) and the surface node (Section 12). If radiation also acts on that face, put \(1/(h_\text{rad}A)\) in parallel with it (Section 16). Heat must cross the fluid film before the moving fluid carries it off; radiation leaves the same surface at the same time by a second route.
  3. Do: Each full-height layer: \(L/(kA)\) in series, with the whole area. All of the heat must cross a full-height layer, so it is one more pipe in the row.
  4. Do: A column split into parts stacked across the flow: one resistor per part, \(L/(k_iA_i)\) using that part's own area, all in parallel between the two planes of that column. Each part carries heat only through its own share of the area, and all the parts span the same two planes.
  5. Do: Label every resistor (\(R_1\), \(R_\text{conv,o}\), …) and every node (\(T_{\infty,o}\), \(T_{s,o}\), …), and draw \(q\) with an arrow. You will walk through these labels later to find each temperature, and the arrow fixes which way every drop goes.

11Convection and Newton's law of cooling ~5 min

What is this?

Blow across a bowl of hot soup and it cools faster. The air touching the soup warms up; your breath sweeps that warm air away and brings fresh cool air in to take its place. (Faster evaporation also helps, but set that aside.) That is convection: a moving fluid carries heat away from a surface. Blow harder and the warm air is swept away sooner, so heat leaves faster. Even if you do not blow, the warmed air rises by itself and cooler air moves in: that is free convection.

Definition · convection

Heat convection is heat transfer due to fluid motion: a liquid or gas flowing past a surface. Right at the surface the fluid is still and heat enters it by conduction; the moving fluid then carries the warmed (or cooled) fluid away and brings fresh fluid in.

There are two kinds:

Forced convectionA fan (or pump, or wind) drives the fluid. Example: a fan blows air at \(T_\infty\) across a hot plate at \(T_s\).
Free (natural) convectionNothing drives the fluid except buoyancy: fluid warmed at a hot surface becomes less dense and rises; cooler, denser fluid sinks to replace it. Example: a pot of water on a gas flame, with warm molecules rising up the middle and cool molecules sinking, forming circulating "convection cells".

A classic demonstration of hot and cold fluids exchanging places: two bottles joined neck to neck, hot water dyed red in the upper bottle and cold water dyed blue in the lower one. With hot (less dense) water on top the two stay separate; with the bottles the other way round (cold on top), the dense cold water sinks and the hot water rises, and they mix quickly. That density-driven motion is natural convection.

Law · Newton's law of cooling \[q=\frac{Q}{t}=hA\,(T_s-T_\infty)=hA\,\Delta T\]

In words: convection heat rate = heat transfer coefficient × surface area × (surface temperature − fluid temperature).

  • \(q\): heat flow rate (W), from the surface to the fluid if \(T_s\gt T_\infty\).
  • \(h\): the convective heat transfer coefficient, W/(m²·°C) (Section 13 gives typical values).
  • \(A\): the area of the surface in contact with the fluid (m²).
  • \(T_s\): the surface temperature where convection takes place.
  • \(T_\infty\): the free-stream temperature: the temperature of the bulk fluid, far enough from the surface not to be affected by it.

Definition · heat transfer coefficient \(h\)

\(h\) is the heat rate per square metre of surface per degree of difference between surface and fluid. It is not a property of the fluid alone: it depends on the fluid (air, water, oil), how fast and in what pattern it flows (free or forced, slow or fast), and the shape of the surface. Faster flow sweeps warm fluid away sooner, so \(h\) is larger.

Worked example 8

A fluid flows over a plane surface 1 m by 1 m. The surface is at 50 °C, the fluid at 20 °C, and \(h=2000\u{W/(m^2\,{}^\circ C)}\). Find the convective heat transfer from the surface.

\[\begin{aligned}q&=hA\Delta T\\&=2000(1\times1)(50-20)\\&=60\,000\u{W}=\boxed{60\u{kW}}\end{aligned}\]

\(h=2000\) is typical of flowing water (Section 13); for air it would be 10–100.

You can now: tell forced from free convection; use Newton's law of cooling \(q=hA(T_s-T_\infty)\); and say what \(h\) depends on.

12Convection resistance; convection plus conduction ~5 min

What is this?

Back to the soup. Right at its surface is a thin layer of air that the breeze cannot quite reach, and heat must cross that film before it is carried away. The film behaves like one more resistor, \(1/(hA)\): blowing harder (a bigger \(h\)) thins it and lowers its resistance. A wall with fluid on both sides is then a row of resistors: film, layers, film.

Newton's law has the same shape as \(q=\Delta T/R\), so a fluid film also behaves like a resistor:

Result · convection resistance \[R_\text{th,convection}=\frac{1}{hA}\]

In words: the resistance of a fluid film = 1 ÷ (heat transfer coefficient × area). A weak coefficient (still air) gives a big resistance.

The window of Section 4 (glass 4 mm, \(k=0.8\), 1.5 m²) now separates room air at 20 °C (\(h_i=8\u{W/(m^2\,K)}\), still indoor air) from outside air at 5 °C (\(h_o=25\), windy). Find the heat rate.

14Radiation and the black body ~4 min

What is this?

Sit across the room from a fire and your face feels warm, although no hot air reaches you and nothing touches you. The fire sends out infrared light, which crosses the room in a straight line and is absorbed by your skin. Everything above absolute zero does this, you included, and a hotter object does it far more strongly. Your face also radiates back towards the fire, much more weakly; what you feel is the difference.

Definition · thermal radiation

Thermal radiation is electromagnetic radiation emitted by all matter at a temperature above absolute zero, in the wavelength range 0.1 μm to 100 μm (from ultraviolet through visible light to infrared). At everyday temperatures it is mainly infrared. It is caused by the thermal agitation (random motion) of the molecules that make up the body: moving charges emit electromagnetic waves.

Unlike conduction and convection, radiation needs no material in between, and it travels at the speed of light. Every body both emits radiation and absorbs radiation from its surroundings; the net heat transfer is the difference.

What is this? (black body)

A black body is the ideal case: a surface that soaks up all the radiation falling on it and, at its temperature, glows as strongly as anything can. Real surfaces fall short of it (Section 15).

Definition · black body; absolute temperature

A black body is an ideal surface that absorbs all radiation falling on it (none is reflected or transmitted) and, at a given temperature, emits the most radiation possible. Its name comes from the fact that, absorbing all visible light, it would look black when cold.

The absolute temperature is the temperature measured from absolute zero, in kelvin: \(T/\mathrm K=T/{}^\circ\mathrm C+273.15\).

Law · Stefan–Boltzmann \[q=\sigma T^4A\quad\text{(one body)}\] \[q=\sigma\big(T_1^4-T_2^4\big)A\quad\text{(net, two bodies)}\]

with \(\sigma=5.6703\times10^{-8}\u{W/(m^2\,K^4)}\), the Stefan–Boltzmann constant; \(T\) the absolute temperature in K; \(A\) the surface area of the emitting body (m²).

In words: a black body radiates power proportional to its area and to the fourth power of its absolute temperature. The net exchange is "what body 1 emits minus what it receives back from body 2".

Reading the formula
\(\sigma T_1^4A\)
what the fire sends out: more for a bigger glowing surface.
\(T^4\), in kelvin
twice the absolute temperature gives sixteen times the glow; the count starts from absolute zero, where the glow stops.
\(-\,\sigma T_2^4A\)
what comes back from the cooler body (your face, the room).
\(\sigma\)
a fixed constant, the same for every black body.

The two-body formula is for a body 1 (area \(A\)) that "sees" only body 2, such as a small object inside a large room or vessel whose walls are at \(T_2\): every exam question of this kind (a pipe or column in a large enclosure) is set up this way.

Worked example 10 · radiation from the sun

The sun's surface is at 5800 K. Treating the sun as a black body, find the radiation energy emitted per unit area.

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{q}{A}&=\sigma T^4=(5.6703\times10^{-8})(5800)^4\\&=\boxed{6.42\times10^{7}\u{W/m^2}}\end{aligned}\]

Every square metre of the sun gives out about 64 MW.

Watch

Doubling the absolute temperature multiplies the emission by \(2^4=16\). Doubling a Celsius temperature means nothing here: 20 °C to 40 °C is only 293 K to 313 K, a 30% increase in emission. Always convert to kelvin before raising to the fourth power.

You can now: say what thermal radiation is and why it needs no material; and use \(q=\sigma T^4A\) and the net two-body formula with temperatures in kelvin.

15Grey bodies and emissivity ~3 min

What is this?

Most real surfaces glow less than a black body at the same temperature. A polished metal reflects most of the radiation that falls on it and gives out very little of its own. A dull or non-metal surface, such as brick, cloth or water, gives out nearly as much as a black body. Emissivity is the fraction of the ideal glow that a surface actually manages.

Real surfaces are not perfect absorbers. Of the radiation falling on a surface (the incident radiation or irradiation), part is reflected, part is absorbed, and (for a see-through material) part is transmitted through it.

Definition · grey body; emissivity

A grey body emits a fixed fraction \(\varepsilon\) of what a black body at the same temperature would emit. \(\varepsilon\) is its emissivity (emissivity coefficient): \(\varepsilon=1\) for a black body, \(0\lt\varepsilon\lt1\) for real surfaces. It depends on the material, the surface finish and the temperature.

Law · grey-body emission \[q=\varepsilon\sigma T^4A\]

In words: a real surface emits its emissivity times the black-body amount.

For a grey surface the fraction it absorbs equals its emissivity (Kirchhoff's law). That is why shiny foil is used both to keep heat in (a polished surface emits little) and to keep radiant heat out (it reflects most of it). For a small grey body of area \(A\) inside large surroundings at \(T_\text{surr}\), the net radiation is \(q=\varepsilon\sigma(T_s^4-T_\text{surr}^4)A\): the black-body formula of Section 14 with \(\varepsilon\) put in.

You can now: use \(q=\varepsilon\sigma T^4A\); rank surfaces by emissivity (shiny metals lowest, water highest in the table); and explain why good absorbers are good emitters.

16Radiation as a resistance; convection and radiation together ~5 min

What is this?

Radiation is not proportional to the temperature difference, but we can still ask: at these two temperatures, how many watts per square metre does it carry for each degree of difference? That number is \(h_\text{rad}\). Once radiation is written like this, it is just one more resistor in the circuit.

Radiation is not proportional to \(\Delta T\) (it goes as \(T^4\)), but to fit it into a circuit we write it in the same form as Newton's law of cooling:

Definition and result · radiant heat transfer coefficient \[q=\frac{Q}{t}=h_\text{rad}A\,(T_s-T_\text{surr})\] \[R_\text{th,radiation}=\frac{1}{h_\text{rad}A}\]

\(T_s\): temperature of the emitting surface; \(T_\text{surr}\): temperature of the surroundings; \(h_\text{rad}\): the coefficient of radiant heat transfer, W/(m²·°C).

In words: \(h_\text{rad}\) is "the radiation heat rate per square metre per degree of difference", chosen so that radiation looks like convection. To find it, compute the real radiation \(q\) from Stefan–Boltzmann (kelvin!), then divide by \(A(T_s-T_\text{surr})\).

Reading the formula
\(q\)
the real net radiation, always from Stefan–Boltzmann in kelvin.
\(T_s-T_\text{surr}\)
a plain difference, so °C or K give the same number here.
\(h_\text{rad}\)
the real \(q\) repackaged per square metre per degree; it holds only near these two temperatures.
Going further · a formula for \(h_\text{rad}\)

Factorising \(T_s^4-T_\text{surr}^4\) (difference of two squares, twice):

\[h_\text{rad}=\varepsilon\sigma\big(T_s^2+T_\text{surr}^2\big)\big(T_s+T_\text{surr}\big)\]

So \(h_\text{rad}\) grows roughly like \(T^3\): about \(4\sigma T^3\approx6\u{W/(m^2\,K)}\) for a black surface near room temperature (300 K), but over 100 at 1000 K. Radiation is comparable to free convection at room temperature and dominates in furnaces.

Convection and radiation from the same surface

What is this? (convection and radiation together)

Think of the bowl of soup in a cool room. Its surface loses heat to the moving air and, at the same moment, glows towards the cooler walls. Two separate routes leave the same surface together, so they are in parallel, and their coefficients simply add.

Consider a solid's surface (area \(A_s\), temperature \(T_s\)) loses \(\dot Q\), which splits into \(\dot Q_\text{conv}\) to the air at \(T_\infty\) through \(R_\text{conv}\) and \(\dot Q_\text{rad}\) to the surroundings at \(T_\text{surr}\) through \(R_\text{rad}\). The two leave from the same node \(T_s\) and (when \(T_\infty=T_\text{surr}\)) arrive at the same temperature: they are in parallel.

Result · combined convection and radiation \[\begin{aligned}\frac{1}{R_\text{combine}}&=\frac{1}{R_\text{conv}}+\frac{1}{R_\text{rad}}\\&=(h_\text{conv}+h_\text{rad})A_s\end{aligned}\] \[h_\text{combine}=h_\text{conv}+h_\text{rad}\] \[\dot Q=\dot Q_\text{conv}+\dot Q_\text{rad}=h_\text{combine}A_s\Delta T\]

In words: on one face, convection and radiation are two side-by-side paths, so their coefficients simply add.

Worked example 11

A pipe of surface area 1 m², surface temperature 480 K, sits in a large enclosure whose walls are at 380 K. Treat the pipe surface as a black body. Find the coefficient of radiant heat transfer. If the total coefficient (radiation + convection) is 34.9 W/(m²·°C), find the convective coefficient.

  1. Net radiation (temperatures already in K):
\[\begin{aligned}\dot Q&=\sigma(T_1^4-T_2^4)A\\&=5.6703\times10^{-8}(480^4-380^4)(1)\\&=1828\u{W}\end{aligned}\]

2. Same \(\dot Q\) written with \(h_\text{rad}\): \(1828=h_\text{rad}(1)(480-380)\), so \(h_\text{rad}=18.3\u{W/(m^2\,{}^\circ C)}\). This makes radiation look like convection: the same heat rate, divided by area and by the plain temperature difference.

3. \(h_\text{total}=h_\text{rad}+h_\text{conv}\): \(34.9=18.3+h_\text{conv}\).

\(\boxed{h_\text{rad}=18.3,\quad h_\text{conv}=16.6\u{W/(m^2\,{}^\circ C)}}\)

Rounded, \(\dot Q\approx1830\) W; unrounded it is 1827.7 W. \(h\) values are the same to 3 significant figures.

the fourth powers, step by step

\(480^4=(480^2)^2=230\,400^2\), which is \(5.3084\times10^{10}\) square twice; a calculator's \(x^y\) key also works

\(380^4=144\,400^2=2.0851\times10^{10}\)

Difference \(=3.2233\times10^{10}\u{K^4}\); times \(\sigma\): \(5.6703\times10^{-8}\times3.2233\times10^{10}\), which is \(1827.7\u{W/m^2}\). \(10^{-8}\times10^{10}=10^{2}\)

Divide by \(A\,\Delta T=1\times100\): \(h_\text{rad}=18.28\). A temperature difference of 100 K = 100 °C, so the unit W/(m²·°C) is fine.

Try it · convection versus radiation from a surface

The blue curve is \(h_\text{rad}=\varepsilon\sigma(T_s^2+T_\text{surr}^2)(T_s+T_\text{surr})\) against surface temperature; the red line is a fixed \(h_\text{conv}\); the dot marks your \(T_s\). It starts at Example 11 (black pipe at 480 K in a 380 K enclosure: \(h_\text{rad}=18.3\), \(h_\text{conv}=16.6\)). Raise \(T_s\) to see radiation take over; lower \(\varepsilon\) (a polished metal) to see it vanish.

You can now: find \(h_\text{rad}\) from a Stefan–Boltzmann heat rate; combine convection and radiation from one surface as \(h_\text{conv}+h_\text{rad}\); and find one coefficient when the total is given.

17Everything together: example 12 ~4 min

What is this?

Every picture at once. Heat leaves the liquid through its film, crosses the wall layers like water through pipes in a row, and on the air side leaves by two routes at the same time: carried off by the moving air and radiated to the surroundings. Draw that, combine the parallel pair, then add up the row.

Worked example 12 · liquid, two-layer wall, air with radiation

Heat flows from a liquid stream at \(T_y\) (coefficient \(h_y\)) through a composite wall (layer 1: \(k_1,\Delta x_1\); layer 2: \(k_2,\Delta x_2\); area \(A\)) to an air stream at \(T_z\) (coefficient \(h_z\)). The air side also exchanges radiation with surroundings at \(T_\text{surr}=T_z\). Write the heat flow.

Follow the heat: liquid film, layer 1, layer 2 in series; then on the air side, convection and radiation in parallel (same surface, same far temperature):

\[\dot Q=\frac{T_y-T_z}{R_\text{conv,y}+R_\text{cond,1}+R_\text{cond,2}+R_{z}}\] \[R_z=\Big[\frac{1}{R_\text{conv,z}}+\frac{1}{R_\text{rad}}\Big]^{-1}\]
Reading the formula
\(T_y-T_z\)
the whole drop, liquid to air.
\(R_\text{conv,y}\)
the liquid's film on the wall.
\(R_\text{cond,1}+R_\text{cond,2}\)
the two wall layers, one after another.
\(R_z\)
the air side: convection and radiation leave the same surface side by side, so they are combined first.

In words: heat rate = overall temperature difference ÷ (series sum, where the air-side pair is first combined in parallel). It can also be written in one line with braces and \((\ldots)^{-1}\) powers; it is the same thing.

Example · example 12 with numbers

Liquid at \(T_y=80\) °C, \(h_y=500\u{W/(m^2\,K)}\); layer 1 concrete, 10 cm, \(k_1=1.2\); layer 2 insulation, 5 cm, \(k_2=0.04\); air at \(T_z=20\) °C with \(h_\text{conv}=10\) and \(h_\text{rad}=6\u{W/(m^2\,K)}\); area 2 m². Find the heat rate, all the surface and interface temperatures, and how the heat leaving the air side is split between convection and radiation.

  1. Liquid film: \(R_y=\dfrac{1}{500(2)}=0.0010\u{K/W}\).
  2. Concrete: \(R_1=\dfrac{0.10}{1.2(2)}=0.04167\u{K/W}\).
  3. Insulation: \(R_2=\dfrac{0.05}{0.04(2)}=0.625\u{K/W}\).
  4. Air side, parallel: \(R_z=\dfrac{1}{(10+6)(2)}=0.03125\u{K/W}\). On one face the coefficients add, so the pair is one resistor \(1/[(h_\text{conv}+h_\text{rad})A]\).
  5. Total: add the four, \(R=0.6989\u{K/W}\).
  6. \(q=\dfrac{80-20}{0.6989}=\boxed{85.9\u{W}}\).
walking through the circuit for the temperatures

Start at 80 °C and subtract \(qR\) at each resistor, in the direction of the flow:

liquid-side surface: \(80-85.85(0.0010)=79.91\) °C the liquid film barely matters: \(h\) is large

concrete/insulation interface: \(79.91-85.85(0.04167)=76.34\) °C

air-side surface: \(76.34-85.85(0.625)=22.68\) °C the insulation takes 53.7 °C, 89% of the total 60 °C

air: \(22.68-85.85(0.03125)=20.00\) °C ✓ returns exactly to \(T_z\): the arithmetic checks

Split at the air side (both see 22.68 − 20 = 2.68 °C): \(q_\text{conv}=10(2)(2.68)=53.7\u{W}\), \(q_\text{rad}=6(2)(2.68)=32.2\u{W}\); sum 85.9 W ✓.

The temperature profile: almost flat through the liquid film and concrete, a steep fall across the insulation, a small step at the air side.

Ready answer · solving any circuit (exam type T3.3)
  1. Do: Compute every resistance with its own area: \(L/(kA)\), \(1/(hA)\), \(1/(h_\text{rad}A)\). Each resistor is one piece of the heat's route, and a piece carries heat only through its own area.
  2. Do: Combine each parallel group first (reciprocals), then add everything in series. A parallel group acts as a single resistor in the row; once it is replaced, the circuit is a plain row of pipes that adds up.
  3. Do: \(q=(T_\text{hot}-T_\text{cold})/R_\text{total}\) using the two known end temperatures. The total temperature difference drives the same heat through the whole row.
  4. Do: For any inside temperature: start from a known end and subtract \(qR\) resistor by resistor; inside a layer, interpolate linearly (a point halfway through a layer is the average of its two faces). Each resistor takes its own share of the drop, as each pipe takes its own share of the pressure drop.
  5. Do: Check: walking to the far end must return the other known temperature. The drops must add up to the total difference; if you miss the far end, an arithmetic slip is hiding.

You can now: solve a full wall with fluid films, layers and radiation: the heat rate, every surface and interface temperature, and the split between convection and radiation.

21Concept checks ~2 min

1 · Two layers in series

A wall is a thin copper sheet bonded to a thick layer of foam. In steady state, is the heat rate larger through the copper, through the foam, or the same? Where is the temperature drop larger?

Answer

The same heat rate: in steady state heat cannot build up at the interface. The temperature drop is much larger across the foam (bigger \(R=L/(kA)\)); the copper's drop is tiny.

2 · Changing a slab

A slab's thickness is doubled and its area halved, with the same face temperatures. What happens to \(q\)? To the flux?

Answer

\(q=kA\Delta T/L\) falls to \(\tfrac12\times\tfrac12=\tfrac14\). The flux \(q/A=k\Delta T/L\) halves (only the thickness matters).

3 · A half-and-half layer

A layer of thickness \(L\) and area \(A\) is made of two materials side by side, each covering half the area, with \(k=1\) and \(k=3\u{W/(m\,K)}\). What single conductivity would carry the same heat?

Hint

Parallel: conductances \(kA/L\) add.

Answer

\(k_\text{eq}A/L=1(A/2)/L+3(A/2)/L\), so \(k_\text{eq}=2\u{W/(m\,K)}\), the area-weighted average. (In series, equal thicknesses, it would be \(1.5\): the poor conductor dominates.)

4 · Fourth power

A black body at 27 °C is heated to 327 °C. By what factor does its emitted radiation increase?

Answer

In kelvin: 300 K to 600 K, a factor 2, so emission rises by \(2^4=16\) (not \((327/27)^4\)).

5 · Why add the coefficients?

Why is \(h_\text{combine}=h_\text{conv}+h_\text{rad}\) on one face, when resistances in a wall add instead?

Answer

Convection and radiation leave the same surface at the same time by two different routes: parallel paths. In parallel, conductances \(hA=1/R\) add. Layers of a wall are crossed one after another: series, where resistances add.

6 · Which layer to thicken?

A wall is 2 cm of steel (\(k=45\)), 5 cm of foam (\(k=0.03\)) and 10 cm of brick (\(k=0.7\)), all the same area. You may make one layer 50% thicker. Which one?

Answer

The foam: \(L/k\) is 0.00044 for steel, 1.67 for foam, 0.14 for brick (per m²). Adding 50% of the largest one adds the most resistance.
