Properties of fluids

0Where this chapter sits

The first hydrostatics chapter (Section B): after the thermal-science chapters T1–T4, it sets out the material of fluid mechanics. H2 then studies pressure in a fluid at rest, H3 the forces on walls and gates, H4 buoyancy; viscosity and compressibility return in F1–F4. Two pictures run through it: a deck of cards whose layers slide over each other (viscosity), and a stretched skin on a liquid's surface (surface tension).

Conventions for this chapter
  • Section B of the paper states its own data line; for Dec 2026: \(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\), density of water \(\rho_w=1000\u{kg/m^3}\), and "pressure" means gauge pressure (pressure above the atmosphere, Section 2). Older papers sometimes give specific weights instead (\(\gamma_w=9.81\u{kN/m^3}\)): use whatever the paper gives.
  • Shear stress \(\tau\) acts along a surface. In a simple shear flow it is taken positive in the direction the faster fluid (or the moving plate) is going; the fluid then drags a slower surface forward and a faster surface backward.
  • \(y\) is measured across the flow, away from the fixed wall; \(u\) is the velocity along the flow. The shear rate is \(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\) (s⁻¹).
  • In force balances (free-body diagrams), forces are positive in the direction of motion. A viscous force always opposes the motion of the body it acts on.
  • Temperatures in the perfect gas law are absolute (kelvin, \(T/\mathrm K=T/^\circ\mathrm C+273.15\)) and the pressure is absolute; everywhere else in Section B pressures are gauge.
  • Keep N and kN apart: \(\gamma_w=9810\u{N/m^3}=9.81\u{kN/m^3}\).

1What a fluid is: fluid versus solid ~7 min

What is this?

Lay a deck of playing cards on a table, with a thin film of oil between the cards, and push the top card sideways. Each card drags the one below it, and the deck leans over. Glue the cards into a rubber block instead and it leans a fixed amount, then stops: that is a solid. With the oil between them the cards keep sliding for as long as you push, however gently: that is a fluid.

Fluid mechanics is used almost everywhere: aerospace (how things fly, rocket propulsion), acoustics (noise reduction, silent submarines), automotive (drag on vehicles, engines), building services (ventilation, indoor air quality), chemical engineering (biomolecule production, microfluidics), civil engineering (rivers, ports, coastal and offshore engineering), environmental engineering (air and water quality), energy (wind farms, shale gas extraction), electrical engineering (power plants), medical engineering (blood circulation, breathing), ocean engineering (waves, ships, offshore platforms), and mechanical engineering, which involves all of these and more.

What is this? (normal and shear stress)

Press straight down on the top card and you squash the deck: that is a normal stress. Push the top card sideways and the cards try to slide over each other: that is a shear stress. Both are a force divided by the area it acts on.

Definition · normal and shear stress

A force \(F\) acting on a small patch of surface of area \(\mathrm dA\) can be split into two parts: the part \(F_n\) perpendicular (normal) to the surface and the part \(F_t\) along (tangent to) the surface. Divided by the area:

  • normal stress = \(F_n/\mathrm dA\): pushes or pulls straight on the surface (pressure is a normal stress);
  • shear stress \(\tau=F_t/\mathrm dA\): the tangential force per unit area, which tries to make layers slide over each other, as when you push the top of a pack of cards sideways.

A force on a small area \(\mathrm dA\) split into a normal part \(F_n\) and a tangential part \(F_t\).

Definition · fluid

A fluid is a substance that deforms continuously under a shear stress, however small that stress is. It has no shear strength: it cannot resist a shear stress by deforming a fixed amount and then stopping, as a solid does. It keeps flowing for as long as the shear stress acts. Liquids and gases are both fluids.

It follows that a fluid at rest carries no shear stress: if there were one, the fluid would be deforming, so it would not be at rest.

Result · how solids and fluids respond to shear
  • Solid: has shear strength; deforms to a finite extent and stops. The relationship is between stress and strain (how much it has deformed).
  • Fluid: no shear strength; flows (continuous deformation without a finite limit). The relationship is between stress and strain rate (how fast it is deforming).

In words: shear a solid and it bends a certain amount; shear a fluid and it keeps moving. For a solid you ask "how far?", for a fluid "how fast?".

What is this? (strain and strain rate)

Strain is how far the deck has leaned over. Strain rate is how fast it is leaning further. A solid answers a steady push with a fixed lean; a fluid answers it with a steady speed of leaning.

Definition · shear strain and strain rate

Shear strain \(\alpha\) is the angle (in radians) through which a vertical line in the material has been tipped over by the shear. The strain rate is how fast that angle grows, \(\mathrm d\alpha/\mathrm dt\) (s⁻¹). For a slab of thickness \(h\) whose top moves a distance \(\delta a\) in time \(\delta t\) relative to its bottom, \(\alpha\approx\delta a/h\) and the strain rate is \(U/h\), where \(U=\delta a/\delta t\) is the top's speed.

Two pictures show this:

  • A rubber block glued between two plates (contact area \(A\)). A force \(F\) on the top plate gives a shear stress \(\tau=F/A\); the rubber tilts over by a shear strain \(\alpha\) and stays there. Double \(F\) and \(\alpha\) roughly doubles: stress is related to strain.
  • A block of material over time (four frames). (a) No force: the block is square; it could be a solid or a fluid. (b) A force \(F\) is applied to the top plate and the block tilts over: it could still be either. (c), (d) The force is kept on. A solid would stop at (b); a fluid keeps tilting further and further (the dashed lines show the earlier positions): only a fluid does this.

Under the same steady force \(F\), a solid stops at a fixed tilt, but a fluid keeps deforming as time goes on.

Ready answer · "distinguish a fluid from a solid" (exam type H1.1)

Under a shear stress a solid deforms by a finite amount and then resists (it has shear strength; stress is related to strain). A fluid has no shear strength: under any shear stress, however small, it deforms continuously (flows) for as long as the stress acts (stress is related to strain rate). Hence a fluid at rest has zero shear stress.

Going further · things that are hard to classify

Some materials sit in between: toothpaste, ketchup and wet sand hold their shape under a small stress but flow under a large one (Section 8 calls them Bingham plastics). Pitch and glacier ice flow so slowly that they look solid. The definition above still works: ask whether it keeps deforming under a small, steady shear.

You can now: define a fluid by how it responds to shear; split a force on a surface into normal and shear stress; explain why a fluid at rest carries no shear stress; and write the "distinguish a fluid from a solid" answer.

2Continuum, the plan of fluid mechanics, temperature and pressure ~6 min

What is this?

Look at a photo on a phone screen. At reading distance it is smooth; zoom in far enough and you see separate coloured dots. A fluid is the same: close up it is separate molecules, but at any size an engineer cares about, the molecules blur into a smooth material with a density and a pressure at every point. That smooth picture is the continuum.

The continuum hypothesis

A fluid is really made of molecules with empty space between them. But we never want to follow each molecule. Instead we treat the fluid as a continuum: a smooth material that fills space completely, with a density, a velocity and a pressure defined at every point.

Definition · continuum hypothesis

The continuum hypothesis describes a fluid from a macroscopic viewpoint (properties averaged over many molecules) rather than a microscopic (molecule-by-molecule) one. The density at a point is defined as

\[\rho=\lim_{\delta V\to\delta V^*}\frac{\delta m}{\delta V}\]

In words: the density at a point = mass ÷ volume of a small blob around that point, where the blob is shrunk down to a tiny size \(\delta V^*\), but not smaller.

Two pictures explain the "but not smaller". (a) A region of fluid whose density changes from place to place (contours \(\rho=1000\), 1100, 1200, 1300 kg/m³), with a small elemental volume \(\delta V\) drawn around one point. (b) The density measured in that blob, plotted against the blob's size:

  • if the blob is too large, it takes in fluid of different densities, so the answer wanders ("macroscopic uncertainty");
  • if the blob is too small (smaller than about \(\delta V^*\approx10^{-9}\u{mm^3}\)), it holds only a few molecules, and the count jumps up and down as molecules move in and out ("microscopic uncertainty");
  • in between there is a range where the answer is steady (1200 kg/m³ in the picture): that steady value is the continuum density.

Density measured in a blob of volume \(\delta V\). A cube of \(10^{-9}\u{mm^3}\) is 1 μm on a side, and still holds about \(3\times10^{10}\) molecules of water, which is why the continuum idea works so well.

So for Section B (fluids at rest) the properties that matter are density (which sets how pressure grows with depth) and surface tension. Viscosity does nothing in a fluid at rest; it is in this chapter because it is a property of fluids, and it is examined here.

Temperature

In this course temperature is not a controlling factor: temperature changes (for example from heat transfer) are taken as negligible, fluid properties are taken as constants, and fluid mechanics is decoupled from thermodynamics (the two can be solved separately). Temperature is therefore left out of the list of variables in elementary fluid mechanics. It still appears through property values, such as "the viscosity of water at 20 °C".

Pressure

What is this? (gauge and absolute pressure)

A tyre gauge reads how much harder the air inside the tyre pushes than the air outside it. That extra push above the atmosphere is gauge pressure. Absolute pressure counts from a perfect vacuum, so it includes the push of the atmosphere as well.

Result · gauge and absolute pressure \[p_\text{abs}=p_\text{gauge}+p_\text{atm}\]

In words: absolute pressure = gauge pressure + atmospheric pressure (about 101 kPa). A tyre "at 200 kPa" is at 200 kPa gauge, about 301 kPa absolute. Open air is at 0 gauge.

Section B uses gauge pressure throughout (the paper says so). The atmosphere pushes on both sides of most walls and gates, so it cancels and gauge pressure gives the right net force. H2 covers this in detail.

3Density, specific weight and relative density ~5 min

What is this?

Fill three identical buckets with oil, water and mercury. They hold the same volume but weigh very different amounts. Density is how much mass is packed into each cubic metre; specific weight is how much that cubic metre weighs; relative density says how many times heavier it is than the same bucket of water.

Definitions · density, specific weight, relative density
  • Density \(\rho\) ("rho") = mass per unit volume, \(\rho=\delta m/\delta V\) (or \(m/V\) for a uniform substance). Dimension [ML⁻³], unit kg/m³.
  • Specific weight \(\gamma\) ("gamma") = the force of the Earth's gravity on a unit volume of the substance (its weight per cubic metre), \(\gamma=\rho g\). Dimension [ML⁻²T⁻²], unit N/m³.
  • Relative density (also called specific gravity, SG) = the density of the substance ÷ the density of water at a standard temperature and pressure, \(\rho/\rho_w\). It has no units (non-dimensional).
Result · the three conversions \[\gamma=\rho g,\qquad \text{RD}=\frac{\rho}{\rho_w}=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma_w}\] \[W=mg=\rho gV=\gamma V\]

In words: specific weight = density × g; relative density = how many times denser than water; weight of a volume of fluid = specific weight × volume.

Reading the formula
\(\gamma=\rho g\)
mass in each cubic metre × gravity = the weight of each cubic metre (the bucket on a scale, in newtons).
\(\text{RD}=\rho/\rho_w\)
the bucket compared with the same bucket of water; the units cancel, so RD is a pure number.
\(W=\gamma V\)
weight of one cubic metre × number of cubic metres = weight of the whole volume.

Relative density below 1 means lighter than water (it floats: oil, ice, wood); above 1 means denser than water (it sinks: steel, mercury). Reference values: water at 20 °C, \(\rho\approx1000\u{kg/m^3}\); dry air at 20 °C and 101 kPa, \(\rho\approx1.20\u{kg/m^3}\). Air is about 830 times lighter than water.

the dimensions of \(\rho\) and \(\gamma\)

\([\rho]=\dfrac{[\text{mass}]}{[\text{volume}]}=\dfrac{\mathrm M}{\mathrm L^3}=[\mathrm{ML^{-3}}]\). kg/m³.

\([\gamma]=[\rho][g]=\mathrm{ML^{-3}}\cdot\mathrm{LT^{-2}}=[\mathrm{ML^{-2}T^{-2}}]\). \(g\) is an acceleration, [LT⁻²].

Check with units: N/m³ = (kg·m/s²)/m³ = kg/(m²·s²). ✓ 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

Example

A 2-litre bottle is filled with oil of relative density 0.85. Take \(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\). Find the oil's density, its specific weight, and the weight of the oil.

  1. \(\rho=\text{RD}\times\rho_w\), so \(\rho=0.85\times1000=850\u{kg/m^3}\).
  2. \(\gamma=\rho g=850\times9.81=8338.5\u{N/m^3}\), which is \(8.34\u{kN/m^3}\).
  3. Volume: 2 L = \(2\times10^{-3}\u{m^3}\) (1 m³ = 1000 L). Weight \(W=\gamma V=8338.5\times0.002\), so \(W=16.7\u{N}\) (mass 1.7 kg). \(\gamma\) is the weight of one cubic metre, so the volume must be in cubic metres before you multiply.
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}\rho&=850\u{kg/m^3}\\ \gamma&=8.34\u{kN/m^3}\\ W&=16.7\u{N}\end{aligned}}\]
Watch

Papers give either \(\rho_w=1000\u{kg/m^3}\) with \(g=9.81\), or \(\gamma_w=9.81\u{kN/m^3}\) directly: they are the same thing. "Specific gravity 0.73" means \(\rho=730\u{kg/m^3}\), not 0.73 kg/m³. A "weight" is a force (N); a "mass" is in kg.

Density in thermodynamics and in fluid mechanics

  • In thermodynamics the inverse of density, the specific volume \(v=1/\rho\) (m³/kg), is used instead. It is one of the state variables and can be part of the answer (as in T2's steam tables).
  • In fluid mechanics density can be constant or variable. When a fluid is treated as incompressible (its density does not change with pressure), the density is a known constant, not something to solve for. Density may change with temperature and/or pressure in high-speed air flow (above a Mach number of about 0.3, Section 13), and with salinity (the amount of dissolved salt) in the sea.

Why density matters so much

Density appears in every fluid mechanics problem, at rest or moving, constant or variable. It sets how heavy the fluid is, and therefore:

  • how hydrostatic pressure grows with depth (\(p=\rho gh\), chapter H2);
  • the hydrostatic force on a wall or gate (H3);
  • the inertia of the fluid when it is set moving (how hard it is to accelerate);
  • the momentum of a fluid stream (mass flow × velocity, F4), and so on.

You can now: convert between density, specific weight and relative density; find the weight of a volume of fluid in newtons; and say why density appears in every fluid problem.

4Water is densest at 4 °C ~5 min

What is this?

Think of a lake as a stack of layers sorted by weight, just as oil always ends up floating on water: the lighter layer stays on top. Most liquids are heaviest when coldest, but water is heaviest at 4 °C. So 4 °C water collects at the bottom, and colder, lighter water floats above it, which is the water that freezes first.

Most liquids get steadily denser as they cool. Water does not: cooling from room temperature, it gets denser only down to 4 °C; below 4 °C it gets less dense again, and ice (about 917 kg/m³) is less dense still. This is called the anomalous (unusual) density variation of water.

Result · the density maximum of water

Water is the densest at 4 °C, where \(\rho\approx1000\u{kg/m^3}\). The density falls from 1000.0 kg/m³ at about 4 °C to about 997.0 kg/m³ at 25 °C.

Try it · density of water against temperature

The curve is the standard fit for pure water at 1 atm (Tanaka et al., 2001). Move \(T\) to see the maximum at 4 °C and how small the changes are: from 0 °C to 30 °C the density changes by less than 0.5%, which is why Section B treats water as 1000 kg/m³.

Temperature in a lake

Picture a lake in summer (warm water, 8 °C, 7 °C, 6 °C, 5 °C, down to 4 °C at the bottom) and in winter (0 °C at the top, then 1 °C, 2 °C, 3 °C, down to 4 °C at the bottom), with fish in both. Whatever the surface temperature, the water column keeps a stable stratification: lighter water lies above denser water. A fluid cannot displace a heavier fluid below it: a lighter layer cannot sink through a denser one. Since 4 °C water is the densest, it is always at the bottom.

In summer and in winter the densest water (4 °C) sits at the bottom.

Water freezes from the top down

Now picture air at −10 °C above a lake, with a layer of ice (between −10 °C at its top and 0 °C at its bottom); water freezing at 0 °C just under the ice; and 3.98 °C at the bottom of the lake, where a fish swims. Ice always forms first at the top: as the surface water cools below 4 °C it becomes lighter, so it stays at the surface instead of sinking, and it is the first to reach 0 °C. The ice then insulates the water below. Deep water stays at about 4 °C, so organisms survive a freezing winter.

Going further · why does water do this?

Water molecules form hydrogen bonds that, near freezing, start to lock them into an open, hexagonal arrangement (the arrangement of ice), which takes up more room. Cooling below 4 °C makes more of these open clusters, so the density falls, even though slower molecules on their own would pack closer. Above 4 °C the ordinary effect (warmer = more spread out) wins.

You can now: state that water is densest at 4 °C; explain the stable layering of a lake in summer and in winter; and explain why a lake freezes from the top down.

5Viscosity and Newton's law of viscosity ~5 min

What is this?

Go back to the deck of cards from Section 1, and change what is between the cards. With water between them they slide easily; with honey they resist. Viscosity is how sticky that film between the cards is. Push the top card and every card drags the one below: the faster neighbouring cards slide past each other, the harder you must push.

Definition · viscosity

Viscosity is a measure of the "stickiness" of a fluid, or of its resistance to flow. Oil is stickier than water, so oil is more viscous: under the same forcing oil flows more slowly than water, and it is harder for an object to move through oil than through water.

Two more points:

  • Viscosity in fluids is like friction in solids: both turn mechanical energy into heat (they dissipate energy). Viscosity is responsible for energy dissipation in fluid flow.
  • Viscosity has no effect when the fluid is at rest (no shear stress at rest, Section 1), but a great effect when it moves, because it decides the flow rate (how much flows through a pipe), the shear stress, the skin-friction drag (the drag on a surface from fluid rubbing along it) and the energy loss.
Law · Newtonian fluid \[\tau=\mu\,\frac{\mathrm du}{\mathrm dy}\]

\(\tau\): shear stress; \(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\): shear rate, which in a simple shear flow is the velocity gradient (how fast the velocity \(u\) changes as you move across the flow, in direction \(y\)); \(\mu\) ("mu"): the coefficient of dynamic viscosity.

In words: shear stress = viscosity × shear rate. For a Newtonian fluid this relation is a straight line: twice the shear rate needs twice the stress. Common fluids such as water, motor oil and air are Newtonian.

Reading the formula
\(\tau\)
the sideways push per unit area of card needed to keep the cards sliding.
\(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\)
how fast each card slides past its neighbour, per unit thickness of the deck: the shear rate.
\(\mu\)
the stickiness of the film between the cards. For a Newtonian fluid it is a fixed number: double the sliding rate and you double the push.
Definition · viscosity is not "stress over strain"

Viscosity relates shear stress to shear rate (strain rate). It is not a coefficient relating stress and strain; that would be a solid's stiffness (its shear modulus). Dec 2024 Q1(b) offered "Viscosity is a coefficient relating stress and strain" as a true/false statement: it is false.

Velocity profile in a flowing layer: the velocity \(u\) changes across the flow; the slope \(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\) is the shear rate. Layers slide over each other, and the faster layer drags the slower one along through the shear stress \(\tau\).

Units, kinematic viscosity, ideal fluid

What is this? (kinematic viscosity)

Dynamic viscosity \(\mu\) says how sticky the fluid is. Kinematic viscosity divides that by the density: how sticky compared with how heavy. Air is far less sticky than water, but it is so light that its small stickiness goes a long way, because there is so little mass for it to hold back.

Definitions · units, kinematic viscosity, ideal fluid
  • Dynamic viscosity has dimension [ML⁻¹T⁻¹]; SI unit Pa·s (= N·s/m² = kg/(m·s)).
  • All real fluids have a non-zero viscosity. An ideal fluid is an imaginary fluid with zero viscosity, also called inviscid or non-viscous. It is a useful simplification (F3's Bernoulli equation assumes it).
  • Kinematic viscosity \(\nu\) ("nu") is dynamic viscosity ÷ density; dimension [L²T⁻¹], unit m²/s:
\[\nu=\frac{\mu}{\rho}\]

In words: kinematic viscosity = how sticky the fluid is, per unit of its density (stickiness compared with inertia).

the dimensions and units of \(\mu\) and \(\nu\)

From \(\tau=\mu\,\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\): \(\mu=\tau/(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy)\). Rearrange the law.

\([\tau]\) = force/area = \(\mathrm{MLT^{-2}}/\mathrm L^2=\mathrm{ML^{-1}T^{-2}}\); \([\mathrm du/\mathrm dy]=\mathrm{LT^{-1}}/\mathrm L=\mathrm T^{-1}\).

So \([\mu]=\mathrm{ML^{-1}T^{-2}}/\mathrm T^{-1}=[\mathrm{ML^{-1}T^{-1}}]\); in units Pa ÷ s⁻¹ = Pa·s. 1 Pa·s = 1 N·s/m² = 1 kg/(m·s).

\([\nu]=[\mu]/[\rho]\), which is \(\mathrm{ML^{-1}T^{-1}}/\mathrm{ML^{-3}}=[\mathrm{L^2T^{-1}}]\): m²/s. The mass cancels: \(\nu\) is "kinematic" (only length and time).

Going further · other viscosity units

Older books and some papers use the poise (P): 1 P = 0.1 Pa·s, so 1 centipoise (cP) = 1 mPa·s, about the viscosity of water. Kinematic viscosity is sometimes in stokes (St): 1 St = 1 cm²/s = \(10^{-4}\u{m^2/s}\), so water is about 1 centistokes.

You can now: define viscosity and state Newton's law of viscosity; explain why viscosity is not "stress over strain"; give the units and dimensions of \(\mu\) and \(\nu\); and say what an ideal fluid is.

6Measuring viscosity: simple shear flow, and forces from thin oil films ~10 min

What is this?

Now put the deck between a table and your hand. The bottom card sticks to the table and the top card moves with your hand. Every card slips the same small amount over the one below, so the speed rises steadily from zero at the table to your hand's speed at the top. A thin oil film under a sliding plate, a block or a piston is just such a deck.

The basic viscosity experiment: a viscous fluid is sandwiched between two parallel flat plates a distance \(h\) apart. The lower plate is fixed; the upper plate (area of contact with the fluid \(A\)) is pulled by a force \(P\) and moves at a constant velocity \(U\) in its own plane. Once the flow is fully developed (settled down), the fluid velocity varies linearly across the gap: \(U\) at the top, 0 at the bottom.

Definition · no-slip condition

A fluid touching a solid surface moves with that surface: zero relative velocity, no sliding at the wall. This is why the fluid is at \(U\) at the moving plate and at 0 at the fixed plate. (It is the boundary condition at a solid surface, Section 15.)

Result · simple shear flow \[\tau=\frac{P}{A},\qquad \frac{\mathrm du}{\mathrm dy}=\frac{U}{h}\] \[\mu=\frac{\tau}{\mathrm du/\mathrm dy}=\frac{P/A}{U/h}=\frac{Ph}{AU}\]

In words: the shear stress is the pulling force per area of contact; the shear rate is the plate speed ÷ the gap; viscosity = stress ÷ rate.

Reading the formula
\(P/A\)
the pull spread over the area of the top card: the shear stress.
\(U/h\)
the top card's speed ÷ the thickness of the deck: how fast each layer slides past the next.
\(\mu=\dfrac{Ph}{AU}\)
stress ÷ shear rate. Everything on the right is measured on the bench, so this is how a viscosity is found.
why the profile is linear and the stress is the same everywhere

1. The top plate moves at constant speed, so the net force on it is zero: the pull \(P\) is balanced by the fluid's drag \(\tau A\) on its underside. So \(\tau=P/A\). Newton's first law; FBD of the plate.

2. Take any thin horizontal layer of fluid. It is not accelerating either, so the shear stress on its top face equals the shear stress on its bottom face. Same argument, layer by layer.

3. So \(\tau\) is the same at every height. With \(\tau=\mu\,\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\) and \(\mu\) constant, the slope \(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\) is the same at every height: \(u\) is a straight line in \(y\). Constant slope = straight line.

4. The line goes from \(u=0\) at \(y=0\) to \(u=U\) at \(y=h\) (no slip), so \(u=Uy/h\) and \(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy=U/h\).

The test for a Newtonian fluid:

  1. Do: repeat the experiment at several speeds. Each plate speed gives a different shear rate \(U/h\), so you get several points to compare.
  2. Do: plot \(\tau\) against \(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\). This puts the push needed against how fast the cards slide, the two things Newton's law relates.
  3. Do: the fluid is Newtonian if the points fall on a straight line through the origin; the slope of that line is \(\mu\). A straight line means the stickiness is the same at every speed; through the origin means even the gentlest push makes the cards slide.

Typical measurements give four such lines: crude oil (60 °F, steepest), water (60 °F), water (100 °F, a little less steep) and air (60 °F, almost flat). Viscosity falls with temperature for liquids and rises for gases (Section 7).

Oil fills a 2 mm gap between two plates. The upper plate has a contact area of 0.5 m² and moves at 0.4 m/s when pulled by 30 N. Find the oil's dynamic and kinematic viscosities if its relative density is 0.9.

Turn the experiment round: if \(\mu\), the gap \(h\), the area \(A\) and the speed \(U\) are known, the viscous (drag) force on the moving surface is

Result · viscous force on a surface sliding over a thin film \[F_\text{visc}=\tau A=\mu\,\frac{U}{h}\,A\]

In words: viscous drag = viscosity × (speed ÷ film thickness) × wetted area. It acts on the moving body against its motion. It is valid when the film is thin (the velocity profile across it is a straight line).

Example · a block sliding down an oiled slope

A block weighing 150 N slides down a plane inclined at 20° to the horizontal on an oil film 0.4 mm thick. Its bottom face is 0.4 m × 0.25 m and the oil has \(\mu=0.8\u{Pa\,s}\). Find the terminal speed.

\[W\sin\theta=\mu\,\frac{U}{h}\,A\] \[U=\frac{W\sin\theta\;h}{\mu A}\]

why the speed settles at a terminal value

At first the block is slow, so \(F_\text{visc}=\mu UA/h\) is small and the block speeds up. Net force down the slope = \(W\sin\theta-\mu UA/h\gt0\).

As \(U\) grows, the viscous force grows in proportion. \(F_\text{visc}\propto U\).

When \(\mu UA/h\) reaches \(W\sin\theta\) the net force is zero and the speed stops changing: that is the terminal speed. Same idea as the falling sphere in Section 9.

The normal reaction \(N=W\cos\theta\) plays no part: the oil film has no friction coefficient, only viscosity.

Example · a piston in an oiled cylinder

A piston of diameter 80 mm and length 120 mm slides along the inside of a cylinder at 1.5 m/s. The gap (the same all round) is 0.05 mm and is filled with oil of \(\mu=0.08\u{Pa\,s}\). Find the force needed to overcome the oil.

FBD of the piston: the pull \(F\) one way, the oil's viscous drag on its curved side the other (gap exaggerated).

  1. The wetted area is the curved side of the piston: \(A=\pi DL=\pi(0.08)(0.12)\), so \(A=0.03016\u{m^2}\). (Not the end face: the oil film wraps round the side.)
  2. \(\tau=\mu U/h=0.08\times1.5/0.00005\), so \(\tau=2400\u{Pa}\).
  3. \(F=\tau A=2400\times0.03016=\boxed{72.4\u{N}}\).

The gap (0.05 mm) is tiny compared with the radius (40 mm), so the curved film is treated like a flat one, and using the piston's diameter or the cylinder's makes no visible difference.

Ready answer · thin-film problems (exam type H1.5)

7How temperature changes viscosity ~3 min

What is this?

In a liquid, the cards are held together by the attraction between neighbouring molecules. Heat them and the molecules jostle more, the attraction weakens and the cards slide more easily. A gas has almost no attraction: its stickiness comes from molecules hopping between layers, like passengers jumping between two trains running side by side at different speeds. Each jumper carries its speed across and tugs on the other train. Hotter molecules move faster and hop across more often, so the tug grows.

Result · temperature effect
  • Liquids: viscosity decreases as temperature increases. Cooking oil flows more readily when heated; engine oil thickens noticeably on cold days, which affects how a car runs.
  • Gases: viscosity increases as temperature increases. A gas resists flow more when it is hotter.

In words: heat a liquid and it runs more easily; heat a gas and it gets "stickier".

Viscosity against temperature, falling for a liquid, rising for a gas. Values are typical: water falls from 1.8 to 0.3 mPa·s between 0 and 100 °C; air rises from 17 to 22 μPa·s.

Why liquids and gases behave oppositely

  • Liquid: viscosity comes from the attractive forces between neighbouring molecules, which are packed closely. Heated, the molecules are more energetic and spend less time "in contact" with their nearest neighbours, so the average intermolecular forces are weaker, and the layers slide past each other more easily.
  • Gas: molecules are far apart and hardly attract each other. Viscosity comes from molecules flying between faster and slower layers and carrying momentum with them (a fast molecule landing in a slow layer speeds it up, and vice versa). Hotter molecules move faster and collide more often (the mean free path, the average distance between collisions, gets shorter), so momentum is exchanged between layers at a higher rate: the viscosity rises.
Watch

"Oil flows more readily when it is heated up" (Dec 2024 Q1(c)): true. Do not generalise to air: hot air is more viscous than cold air.

You can now: state how the viscosity of a liquid and of a gas changes with temperature; and explain each, by weaker attraction (liquid) or more momentum exchange (gas).

8Non-Newtonian fluids ~6 min

What is this?

For water, the film between the cards has the same stickiness however fast you slide them. For some fluids it changes. Ketchup's film gets runnier the harder you work it; corn starch in water stiffens when you hit it; toothpaste will not slide at all until you push hard enough. These are non-Newtonian fluids.

Definition · non-Newtonian fluid

Any fluid whose shear stress is not proportional to its shear rate (a nonlinear relation, in whatever form) is a non-Newtonian fluid. Another way to say it: a non-Newtonian fluid has an apparent viscosity that changes with the stress or the shear rate, while a Newtonian fluid has a constant viscosity, unaffected by stress or shear rate.

Definition · apparent viscosity

On a curved \(\tau\)–\(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\) graph there is no single slope. The apparent viscosity \(\mu_\text{ap}\) at a point is the slope of the curve there (a little slope triangle, \(\mu_\text{ap}\) over 1, drawn on the curve): the viscosity the fluid "seems" to have at that shear rate.

Result · three kinds
  • Dilatant = shear thickening: viscosity increases with stress. The curve bends up (gets steeper). Example: concentrated corn starch in water.
  • Pseudoplastic = shear thinning: viscosity decreases with stress. The curve bends down (gets flatter). Examples: ketchup, paint, blood, shampoo.
  • Bingham plastic: has a yield stress \(\tau_\text{yield}\). Below it the material behaves like a solid (does not flow); above it, it flows like a fluid. Its curve starts on the \(\tau\) axis at \(\tau_\text{yield}\), not at the origin. Examples: toothpaste, ketchup, mud.

In words: Newtonian = constant viscosity (straight line through the origin); shear thinning = gets runnier the harder you work it; shear thickening = gets stiffer the harder you work it; Bingham = will not move at all until pushed hard enough.

Try it · shear stress against shear rate

The dashed line is a Newtonian fluid (\(\mu=1\u{Pa\,s}\)). The coloured curve is \(\tau=\tau_\text{yield}+K(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy)^n\) with \(K=1\) (in SI units). \(n\lt1\): shear thinning; \(n=1\): Newtonian (or Bingham if \(\tau_\text{yield}\gt0\)); \(n\gt1\): shear thickening. The dot marks the chosen shear rate; the readout gives the apparent viscosity there, \(\mu_\text{ap}=\tau/(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy)\). This "power-law" formula is just a convenient way to draw the three kinds of curve.

The apparent viscosity is defined here by the slope triangle at a point (the local slope); many books instead define \(\mu_\text{ap}=\tau/(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy)\) (the slope of the line from the origin). The two agree for a Newtonian fluid and rise or fall together for thinning and thickening fluids, so either answers the exam's "increases/decreases" questions. The slider readout uses \(\tau/(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy)\).

Examples

Ketchup is shear thinning (also a Bingham plastic); concentrated corn starch in water is shear thickening. Other non-Newtonian fluids: blood, paint, yogurt, syrup, mud, shampoo.

  • Ketchup sits in an upturned bottle without moving (its weight gives a stress below the yield stress); shake or tap the bottle hard and it suddenly flows, and once moving it thins. So "Ketchup may remain static even when it is sheared" (Dec 2024 Q1(f)) is true: it stays put while the shear stress is below its yield stress.
  • Corn starch in water ("oobleck") pours slowly like a liquid, but a quick punch meets a surface that feels solid: a sudden large shear rate makes it very viscous.
  • Paint is shear thinning: it spreads easily under the brush (high shear rate) but does not run down the wall afterwards (low shear rate, high viscosity).
Ready answer · "Newtonian vs non-Newtonian" (exam type H1.1)

A Newtonian fluid has shear stress proportional to shear rate, \(\tau=\mu\,\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\): constant viscosity, a straight line through the origin (water, air, motor oil). A non-Newtonian fluid has a nonlinear relation, so its (apparent) viscosity depends on the stress or shear rate: shear thinning (viscosity falls with stress, e.g. ketchup, paint), shear thickening (viscosity rises with stress, e.g. corn starch in water), Bingham plastic (no flow until a yield stress is exceeded, e.g. toothpaste). Sketch the four curves on \(\tau\) against \(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\) axes.

You can now: define a non-Newtonian fluid and apparent viscosity; sketch and name the Newtonian, shear-thinning, shear-thickening and Bingham curves; and explain ketchup, corn starch and paint.

9Terminal speed of a falling sphere ~7 min

What is this?

Drop a marble into a jar of honey. It starts from rest and speeds up, but the faster it goes, the harder the honey holds it back. Soon the drag exactly balances the marble's weight minus the honey's upthrust, and it sinks at a steady speed. Time that steady speed and you can work out how sticky the honey is.

A laboratory way to measure viscosity: drop a small ball into the liquid and time how fast it falls once it has stopped speeding up. A sphere of diameter \(D\) falling through a viscous fluid feels three forces:

Result · the three forces on a falling sphere \[W=\rho_s\Big(\frac{\pi D^3}{6}\Big)g\qquad\text{(weight)}\] \[B=\rho_f\Big(\frac{\pi D^3}{6}\Big)g\qquad\text{(buoyant force)}\] \[F=3\pi\mu DV\qquad\text{(viscous drag)}\]

\(\rho_s\): density of the sphere; \(\rho_f\): density of the fluid; \(\pi D^3/6\): volume of the sphere; \(V\): falling speed. The drag formula is Stokes' law, valid for Stokes flow (very slow, "low-Reynolds-number" flow).

In words: weight = sphere's density × volume × g; buoyant force (upthrust) = weight of the fluid the sphere pushes aside (Archimedes, chapter H4); drag = 3π × viscosity × diameter × speed: proportional to both viscosity and speed.

FBD of the falling sphere: \(W\) down, \(B\) and \(F\) up.

Speed against time: rising fast at first, then levelling off at the terminal speed.

How the motion develops. The sphere is released from rest. At first the speed is tiny, so the drag is tiny (\(V\approx0\)), and the sphere accelerates downward under its apparent weight \(W-B\) (weight minus upthrust). As it speeds up, the drag grows. Eventually the drag is large enough to balance the apparent weight: the net force is zero, \(W-B-F=0\), and the sphere falls at a constant speed, the terminal speed.

Result · terminal speed in Stokes flow \[(\rho_s-\rho_f)\Big(\frac{\pi D^3}{6}\Big)g=3\pi\mu DV\] \[\Longrightarrow\quad V=\frac{(\rho_s-\rho_f)\,gD^2}{18\,\mu}\]

In words: terminal speed = (density difference × g × diameter²) ÷ (18 × viscosity). A more viscous fluid gives a slower fall; a bigger or denser ball falls faster. Rearranged, \(\mu=(\rho_s-\rho_f)gD^2/(18V)\): measure \(V\), get \(\mu\), which is the purpose of the experiment.

Reading the formula
\(\rho_s-\rho_f\)
how much denser the ball is than the liquid: this is what makes it sink at all.
\(gD^2\)
weight minus upthrust grows with the volume, \(D^3\), but the drag only with \(D\); so a bigger ball falls faster, in proportion to \(D^2\).
\(18\mu\)
the stickiness of the liquid: a more viscous liquid holds the ball back harder, so it falls more slowly.
\(V\)
the steady speed reached when the drag has grown to balance the apparent weight.
from the force balance to \(V\)

1. \(W-B=(\rho_s-\rho_f)\dfrac{\pi D^3}{6}g\). Both have the same volume; take the difference.

2. Set equal to the drag: \((\rho_s-\rho_f)\dfrac{\pi D^3}{6}g=3\pi\mu DV\). Net force zero at the terminal speed.

3. Divide both sides by \(3\pi\mu D\): \(V=\dfrac{(\rho_s-\rho_f)D^3g}{6\times3\,\mu D}\). The \(\pi\) cancels.

4. \(D^3/D=D^2\) and \(6\times3=18\): \(V=\dfrac{(\rho_s-\rho_f)gD^2}{18\mu}\).

Example · a steel ball in glycerine

A steel ball (\(\rho_s=7800\u{kg/m^3}\)) of diameter 2 mm falls through glycerine (\(\rho_f=1260\u{kg/m^3}\), \(\mu=1.41\u{Pa\,s}\)). Find its terminal speed. Then: in a test on an unknown oil (\(\rho_f=900\u{kg/m^3}\)), the same ball falls 200 mm in 4.0 s at its steady speed. Find the oil's viscosity.

  1. Glycerine: \[V=\frac{(7800-1260)(9.81)(0.002)^2}{18(1.41)}\] which is \(\dfrac{0.25663}{25.38}=0.0101\u{m/s}\) (about 1 cm/s).
  2. Oil: \(V=0.2/4.0=0.05\u{m/s}\). The ball is already at its terminal speed over the timed 200 mm, so distance ÷ time is \(V\).
  3. Viscosity: \[\mu=\frac{(7800-900)(9.81)(0.002)^2}{18(0.05)}\] which is \(0.27076/0.9=0.301\u{Pa\,s}\).
\[\boxed{V_\text{glycerine}=10.1\u{mm/s},\quad\mu_\text{oil}=0.30\u{Pa\,s}}\]

Try it · a sphere falling in a viscous liquid

Starts at the glycerine example (\(\rho_f=1260\u{kg/m^3}\)). The curve solves \(m\,\mathrm dV/\mathrm dt=W-B-3\pi\mu DV\) from rest; the dashed line is the terminal speed. The \(\mu\) slider is logarithmic (0.1 to 10 Pa·s). The readout also gives the Reynolds number \(\rho_fVD/\mu\): Stokes' law is accurate only when it is below about 1.

Going further · how quickly is the terminal speed reached?

Newton's second law with mass \(m=\rho_s\pi D^3/6\) gives \(V(t)=V_t\big(1-e^{-t/t_0}\big)\) with time constant \(t_0=\rho_sD^2/(18\mu)\). For the steel ball in glycerine \(t_0=7800(0.002)^2/(18\times1.41)\approx1.2\u{ms}\): the ball is at its terminal speed almost at once, which is why timing it over 200 mm works. (A more exact treatment also counts the fluid that must be pushed aside, the "added mass"; it changes \(t_0\) slightly, not \(V_t\).) The Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho_fVD/\mu\) compares inertia with viscosity; "low-Reynolds-number flow" means Re well below 1. For the glycerine example Re ≈ 0.018, so Stokes' law applies.

Watch

"A bubble in water rises up more quickly than a bubble of the same size in oil does" (Dec 2024 Q1(d)): true. Same idea with the signs reversed: the bubble is lighter than the liquid, so upthrust beats weight and it rises, and the rising speed is set by the viscous drag. 10Surface tension ~9 min

What is this?

Picture the surface of a liquid as a thin skin stretched over it, like a drum skin. Cut a slit in the skin and the two edges pull apart: the skin is in tension everywhere. Surface tension is how hard the skin pulls per metre of slit. Unlike rubber, this skin pulls just as hard however far it is stretched. Because it always tries to shrink, a free drop pulls itself into a sphere, the shape with the least skin for its volume.

Start with an analogy: blowing up a balloon is like blowing a soap bubble. The tension in the stretched rubber of a balloon is like the surface tension in the thin film of a bubble. A liquid surface behaves like a stretched elastic skin.

Definition · surface tension

Surface tension is an interfacial phenomenon: a property of a liquid felt at the interface (boundary) between the liquid and another fluid, usually a gas. Imagine making a cut of length \(L\) in the surface. The two sides of the cut pull on each other with a tensile force \(\sigma L\), acting perpendicular to the cut line and tangential to (lying in) the surface. So

\[\sigma=\frac{\text{pulling force}}{\text{length of cut}}\qquad(\mathrm{N/m})\]

In words: surface tension = pulling force per unit length of a line drawn on the surface. It has the dimension of force per length, [MT⁻²], which is the same as energy per area (J/m² = N·m/m² = N/m).

Picture a block of liquid whose top face is the surface; cut it into two halves; along the cut, each half's surface pulls on the other with arrows perpendicular to the cut line and parallel to the surface.

Where it comes from

Molecules in a liquid attract each other (cohesive forces). A molecule deep inside is pulled equally in every direction, so the pulls cancel. A molecule at the surface has liquid neighbours on one side only: it feels a net inward pull, and it is pulled sideways by its neighbours on the surface. The surface molecules hold together more strongly than those inside, making an elastic-like skin: any attempt to pull a surface molecule inward is resisted by the strong forces among the surface molecules, so the surface is always in tension. Surface molecules are at a higher energy than interior ones, so the liquid settles into the shape with the least surface area for its volume.

Inside, a molecule is pulled equally all round; at the surface, the pull is sideways (along the surface) and inward.

Effects of surface tension: the droplet

What is this? (pressure inside a droplet)

A curved skin presses on whatever it wraps, just as a blown-up balloon squeezes the air inside it. So the pressure inside a droplet is higher than outside. For the same pull in the skin, a more tightly curved skin (a smaller drop) squeezes harder.

  • A liquid droplet is spherical because a sphere has the smallest surface area for a given volume. The cohesive pull from inside shrinks the surface to the minimum area possible.
  • The pressure is higher inside the droplet than outside. The difference is inversely proportional to the radius: the smaller the droplet, the higher the pressure inside.
Result · pressure jump across a droplet's surface \[\Delta p_\text{droplet}=p_\text{in}-p_\text{out}=\frac{2\sigma}{R}\]

In words: excess pressure inside a droplet = 2 × surface tension ÷ radius.

Reading the formula
\(\Delta p\)
how much harder the inside pushes than the outside.
\(\sigma\)
the pull of the skin per metre: a tighter skin squeezes harder.
\(2\)
from the half-drop balance: the skin pulls along a rim of length \(2\pi R\), the pressure pushes on a face of area \(\pi R^2\).
\(R\)
the radius: a smaller drop is more tightly curved, so its excess pressure is larger.

FBD of half a droplet. The pressure excess pushes on the flat cut face with \(\pi R^2\Delta p\); the surface tension pulls along the rim with \(2\pi R\sigma\).

deriving \(\Delta p=2\sigma/R\) from the half-droplet

1. Cut the droplet in half through its centre and look at the lower half A free body of fluid.

2. Pressure force on the flat cut face: the excess pressure \(\Delta p\) acts on the circle of area \(\pi R^2\), pushing the half outward: \(\pi R^2\Delta p\). The outside pressure acts all round and only the excess matters.

3. Surface-tension force: the rim of the cut has length \(2\pi R\), and the surface of the other half pulls on it with \(\sigma\) per metre, along the surface (back toward the other half): \(2\pi R\sigma\). Definition: force = \(\sigma\) × length of cut.

4. Equilibrium: \(\pi R^2\Delta p=2\pi R\sigma\), so \(\Delta p=\dfrac{2\sigma}{R}\). Divide by \(\pi R^2\).

Example

Water at 20 °C (\(\sigma=0.0728\u{N/m}\)). Find the excess pressure inside a raindrop of radius 1 mm and inside a fog droplet of radius 1 μm.

\(R=10^{-3}\u{m}\): \(\Delta p=2(0.0728)/10^{-3}=145.6\u{Pa}\). \(R=10^{-6}\u{m}\): \(\Delta p=145.6\u{kPa}\), more than an extra atmosphere.

\[\boxed{146\u{Pa}\text{ and }146\u{kPa}}\]

A thousand times smaller radius, a thousand times the excess pressure.

Going further · a soap bubble has two surfaces

A soap bubble is a thin film with an inner and an outer surface, so the rim of the cut has two lines of tension: \(\pi R^2\Delta p=2(2\pi R\sigma)\), giving \(\Delta p_\text{bubble}=4\sigma/R\). A gas bubble inside a liquid has one surface: \(2\sigma/R\), like a droplet. In every case the pressure is higher on the concave (inner) side.

More effects

  • Because of hydrogen bonds, water has a higher surface tension than most other liquids: \(\sigma=72.8\times10^{-3}\u{N/m}\) at 20 °C.
  • A needle, placed carefully, can float on water because of surface tension: the needle rests in a small dip in the surface, and the surface-tension forces along the dip, pointing up and outward, hold up its weight. It is not floating by buoyancy: steel is 7.8 times denser than water.
  • Surface tension can be greatly reduced by a surfactant (surface-active agent), such as soap or detergent. Add a drop of detergent and the floating needle sinks.
Going further · the force along a contact line (exam type H1.6)

Where a liquid surface meets a solid (a wire ring, a tube, a floating cylinder), the surface pulls on the solid along the whole contact line with \(\sigma\) per metre, in the direction of the liquid surface, which leaves the solid at the contact angle \(\theta\) (Section 11). The vertical component is

\[F_\text{st}=\sigma\,L_\text{contact}\cos\theta\]

In words: vertical surface-tension force = surface tension × total length of every contact line × cos(contact angle).

A thin-walled tube or ring touches the surface along its outside and inside perimeters, so \(L_\text{contact}\approx2\pi D\). For \(\theta\lt90^\circ\) (wetting) the pull is downward on the solid (the surface clings to it and drags it down), so an extra upward force is needed to hold it or lift it out.

Example. A thin-walled glass tube of diameter 50 mm is held vertically, just touching clean water (\(\sigma=0.0728\u{N/m}\), \(\theta=0^\circ\)). The extra pull needed to lift it off the surface is

\[\begin{aligned}F&=\sigma(2\pi D)\cos\theta\\&=0.0728(2\pi)(0.05)(1)\\&=0.0229\u{N}\end{aligned}\]

11Contact angle, wetting and capillarity ~8 min

What is this?

Where the skin meets a solid wall, it either clings to the wall or pulls away from it. Water's skin clings to clean glass, so water spreads out over it. Mercury's skin pulls away from glass, so mercury balls up into beads. The contact angle measures which: the angle between the skin and the wall, measured through the liquid.

Definition · contact angle

Where a liquid surface meets a solid surface, the line along which liquid, gas and solid all meet is the contact line. The contact angle \(\theta\) is the angle between the liquid surface and the solid surface, measured through the liquid. It depends on both the liquid and the solid.

  • \(\theta\lt90^\circ\): the liquid wets the solid; the solid is hydrophilic ("water-loving").
  • \(\theta\gt90^\circ\): the liquid is non-wetting (the gas is the wetting phase); the solid is hydrophobic ("water-fearing").

In words: a liquid that spreads out flat on a surface (small angle) wets it; a liquid that balls up into beads (large angle) does not.

A drop on a solid. Left, a wetting drop spreads, \(\theta\lt90^\circ\); right, a non-wetting drop beads up, \(\theta\gt90^\circ\). In both, \(\theta\) is measured inside the liquid.

Ready answer · "sketch the contact angle; when is a liquid wetting?" (exam type H1.2)
  1. Do: Sketch a drop on a flat solid with the gas above. The contact line is where all three meet: liquid, gas and solid.
  2. Do: At the contact line draw the tangent to the liquid surface and mark \(\theta\) between it and the solid, inside the liquid. The tangent is the direction in which the skin leaves the wall; measuring through the liquid makes a small angle mean "clings".
  3. Do: Wetting if \(\theta\lt90^\circ\) (e.g. water on clean glass, \(\theta\approx0^\circ\)); non-wetting if \(\theta\gt90^\circ\) (e.g. mercury on glass, \(\theta\approx135^\circ\)). Below 90° the skin leans out over the solid and the drop spreads; above 90° it curls back and the drop beads up.

Water and mercury on glass

  • Pure water on glass: \(\theta=0^\circ\). Glass is hydrophilic: it has a great affinity for water and is wetted by water.
  • Mercury on glass: \(\theta\approx135^\circ\). Mercury "dislikes" adhering to glass; glass is not wetted by mercury; it is wetted by air instead.

Capillarity

What is this? (capillarity)

In a narrow tube the skin is attached all round the inside wall. If it clings to the wall (water in glass), it pulls up at the edge and hauls the liquid up the tube, until the weight of the raised column balances the pull. If it pulls away from the wall (mercury in glass), the pull at the edge points down and the liquid in the tube sits lower than outside.

Definitions · adhesion, cohesion, meniscus, capillarity

Adhesion: attraction between the liquid and the solid. Cohesion: attraction of the liquid to itself. The meniscus is the curved liquid surface in a tube or near a wall. Capillarity (capillary action) is the rise or fall of a liquid in a narrow tube caused by surface tension.

Result · capillary rise and depression
  • Wetting liquid (adhesion > cohesion), e.g. water in glass: capillary rise (\(h\gt0\)); the meniscus curves downward in the middle (it is concave, like a bowl, as seen from above), climbing up the walls at the edges.
  • Non-wetting liquid (cohesion > adhesion), e.g. mercury in glass: capillary depression (\(h\lt0\)); the meniscus curves upward in the middle (convex, a dome), pulled down at the walls.

In words: water climbs up a glass tube and its surface dips in the middle; mercury is pushed down in a glass tube and its surface bulges up in the middle.

(a) Water in a glass container, surface climbing the wall (small angle \(\phi\)); (b) mercury, surface rounded down at the wall (large \(\phi\)); then a narrow tube dipped in each: water rises (\(h\gt0\)), mercury is depressed (\(h\lt0\)).

"Curving downward/upward" describes the middle of the meniscus (water's surface sags down in the middle; mercury's bulges up). Some solutions describe the same shapes as "concave" (water) and "convex" (mercury); draw the shape and there is no ambiguity.

Watch

"A liquid wets a solid if the contact angle is greater than 90 degrees" (Dec 2024 Q1(i)): false (less than 90°). "Mercury is drawn by surface tension to rise up in a capillary tube" (Dec 2024 Q1(j)): false: mercury in glass is depressed.

Going further · how high?

Take a tube of radius \(R\) dipped in a liquid, with the liquid risen a height \(h\) and meeting the wall at angle \(\theta\). Balance the column of liquid that has risen: the surface pulls up round the inside perimeter, \(2\pi R\,\sigma\cos\theta\); the extra liquid weighs \(\rho g\,\pi R^2h\). Setting them equal:

\[h=\frac{2\sigma\cos\theta}{\rho gR}\]

In words: capillary rise = 2 × surface tension × cos(contact angle) ÷ (density × g × tube radius). Narrower tube, higher rise. \(\theta\gt90^\circ\) makes \(\cos\theta\) negative: a depression.

Reading the formula
\(\sigma\cos\theta\)
the upward part of the skin's pull at the wall, per metre of rim; negative when the skin pulls away (\(\theta\gt90^\circ\)).
\(\rho g\)
how heavy the raised liquid is, per unit volume.
\(R\)
the pull grows with the rim length (\(\propto R\)) but the weight of the column with its area (\(\propto R^2\)), so a narrow tube lifts the liquid higher.

Water in a glass tube of radius 0.5 mm (\(\theta=0\)): \[h=\frac{2(0.0728)}{1000(9.81)(0.0005)}=29.7\u{mm}\] Mercury (\(\sigma\approx0.485\u{N/m}\), \(\theta=135^\circ\), \(\rho=13\,600\u{kg/m^3}\)) in the same tube: \(h=-10.3\u{mm}\). This is why manometer tubes (H2) are made wide: capillarity would spoil the reading in a narrow one.

You can now: define and sketch the contact angle; classify a liquid as wetting or non-wetting; describe water and mercury in a glass tube (rise or depression, and the shape of the meniscus); and estimate a capillary height.

12Vapour pressure and cavitation ~6 min

What is this?

In a closed pot, molecules keep escaping from the liquid into the space above, and some fall back. Once escape and return balance, the escaped molecules push on the lid: that push is the vapour pressure. Heat the liquid and more molecules escape, so the push grows. When it matches the pressure of the surroundings, bubbles of vapour can form inside the liquid itself: it boils.

Definition · vapour pressure

Put some liquid in a closed container with empty space above it. Molecules escape from the liquid into the space (evaporation) and some return (condensation). When the two rates balance, the vapour is in equilibrium with its liquid. The pressure the vapour then exerts is the vapour pressure \(p_v\) of the liquid at that temperature. (The same holds for a solid and its vapour.)

Result · vapour pressure and boiling
  • Vapour pressure increases steadily (monotonically) with temperature.
  • When the temperature rises to the point where the vapour pressure equals the surrounding pressure, the liquid changes phase: it boils.
  • Water boils at 100 °C because that is the temperature at which its vapour pressure equals 101 kPa, standard atmospheric pressure.
  • Water boils at a higher temperature when the surrounding pressure is above 101 kPa (a pressure cooker) and at a lower temperature when it is below (high on a mountain).

In words: a liquid boils when its vapour pressure reaches the pressure around it. Raise the pressure around it and it must get hotter before it boils.

Vapour pressure of water, from steam-table values: \(p_v\) rises slowly at first and then steeply, reaching 101.3 kPa at 100 °C. At 20 °C it is only 2.34 kPa (absolute).

Cavitation

What is this? (cavitation)

Speed a liquid up and its pressure drops. Make it fast enough and the pressure falls to the vapour pressure: the liquid boils at room temperature, without being heated. When the bubbles are carried back into slower, higher-pressure liquid they collapse violently, like tiny hammer blows on the metal nearby.

Definition · cavitation

A general principle of fluid mechanics: the higher the speed of a flow, the lower its pressure (Bernoulli, chapter F3). Cavitation happens when, because of a very high flow speed, the liquid pressure drops below the vapour pressure, and vapour bubbles form in the liquid. The liquid boils not because it is heated, but because it moves so fast.

  • Example: water moving faster than about 15 m/s may cavitate at room temperature.
  • The vapour bubbles are tiny, but they form and implode (collapse inward violently) at a very high frequency.
  • Cavitation is highly undesirable: it makes noise, lowers the efficiency of turbomachines (pumps, turbines, propellers), and causes pitting and corrosion of surfaces such as propeller blades.

A propeller turning fast in a water tunnel trails spiral streaks of bubbles from its blade tips, and a blade surface can end up pitted all over by cavitation damage.

Low pressure at the fast-moving blade tip makes vapour bubbles; carried into higher pressure, they collapse and hammer the surface.

Ready answer · "cause and effect of cavitation" (exam type H1.2)

Cause: where a liquid moves very fast (pump impeller, propeller tip, valve, constriction), its pressure falls; if it falls to the vapour pressure at that temperature, the liquid boils locally and many tiny vapour bubbles form. Effect: when the bubbles are carried into a region of higher pressure they collapse (implode), sending out intense pressure shocks at high frequency; this causes noise and vibration, loss of efficiency of pumps and turbines, and pitting/erosion of the solid surfaces nearby.

Going further · where does "15 m/s" come from?

F3's Bernoulli equation says that, along a flow, \(p+\tfrac12\rho V^2\) stays constant (ignoring height changes and viscosity). Water starting nearly at rest at atmospheric pressure (101.3 kPa absolute) and speeding up to \(V\) has its pressure lowered by \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\). It reaches the vapour pressure (2.34 kPa at 20 °C) when

\[\tfrac12(1000)V^2=101.3-2.34\ \text{kPa}\]

so \(V=\sqrt{2(98\,960)/1000}\approx14\u{m/s}\): the same size as the 15 m/s quoted above.

Watch

"Cavitation is the formation of bubbles in a liquid when the liquid moves at a high speed" (Dec 2024 Q1(k)): true. The bubbles are vapour (the liquid boiling), not air coming out of the liquid. Vapour pressure is an absolute pressure.

You can now: define vapour pressure and use it to explain boiling, the pressure cooker and boiling on a mountain; and state the cause and the effects of cavitation.

13Compressibility, bulk modulus and the Mach number ~6 min

What is this?

Block the end of a syringe and push the plunger. Full of air, it squashes easily; full of water, it hardly moves. The bulk modulus is the fluid's stiffness as a spring: the pressure needed for each fraction of volume lost. Water is a very stiff spring and air a soft one. A push travels through a stiff, light spring faster, which is why the speed of sound depends on stiffness ÷ density.

Definition · bulk modulus of elasticity

Squeeze a fluid and its volume shrinks a little. The bulk modulus of elasticity \(E\) (some books write \(K\)) is the change in pressure divided by the fractional change in volume it causes:

\[E=\frac{\Delta p}{-\Delta V/V}=\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta\rho/\rho}\]

In words: bulk modulus = pressure increase ÷ fractional decrease in volume (= fractional increase in density). The minus sign is there because a pressure increase makes \(\Delta V\) negative, so \(E\) comes out positive.

Reading the formula
\(\Delta p\)
how hard you push the plunger: the pressure rise.
\(-\Delta V/V\)
the fraction of the volume lost; the minus sign makes it positive for a squeeze.
\(\Delta\rho/\rho\)
the same fraction seen as packing: 1% less volume is 1% more density.
\(E\)
pressure needed per fraction squeezed: large for water (stiff), small for air (soft).

A higher bulk modulus means less compressible (stiffer). \(E\) has the dimension of pressure, [ML⁻¹T⁻²], unit Pa (since \(\Delta V/V\) has no units).

why \(-\Delta V/V=\Delta\rho/\rho\) (mass is conserved)

1. Squeezing does not change the mass: \(m=\rho V\) is constant. The same fluid, just packed tighter.

2. For small changes: \(\Delta m=V\Delta\rho+\rho\Delta V=0\). Product rule.

3. Divide by \(\rho V\): \(\dfrac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}=-\dfrac{\Delta V}{V}\). 1% less volume = 1% more density.

  • Air is typically about four orders of magnitude (\(10^4\) times) more compressible than water. Water: \(E\approx2.2\times10^9\u{Pa}\); air at atmospheric pressure: \(E\approx1.4\times10^5\u{Pa}\) (for quick, sound-wave-like squeezing of air, \(E=1.4p\)).
  • Compressibility can be ignored in water flow, except in water hammer (the bang when a tap or valve is shut suddenly and a pressure wave runs back along the pipe).
  • Compressibility matters in high-speed air flow when the Mach number is larger than about 0.3.
Result · speed of sound and Mach number \[c=\sqrt{\frac{E}{\rho}},\qquad \mathrm{Ma}=\frac{V}{c}\]

In words: speed of sound (the speed at which a small pressure disturbance travels through the fluid) = √(bulk modulus ÷ density). Mach number = flow speed ÷ speed of sound. Compressibility is significant only when Ma > 0.3 or so.

Reading the formula
\(E\)
the stiffness of the spring: a stiffer fluid passes a push on faster.
\(\rho\)
the mass to be shoved along: a heavier fluid passes it on more slowly.
\(\mathrm{Ma}=V/c\)
flow speed compared with the speed at which a push travels. At low Ma the pressure changes in the flow squeeze the fluid by only a tiny fraction, so it can be treated as incompressible.
Example · sound speeds and the Ma = 0.3 limit
  1. Water: \(c=\sqrt{2.2\times10^9/1000}=1483\u{m/s}\). Water flows in pipes at a few m/s: Ma ≈ 0.002. Incompressible.
  2. Air at 20 °C: \(E=1.4\times101\,325=1.419\times10^5\u{Pa}\), \(\rho=1.20\u{kg/m^3}\), so \(c=\sqrt{1.419\times10^5/1.20}=344\u{m/s}\).
  3. Ma = 0.3 in air means \(V=0.3(344)=103\u{m/s}\), about 370 km/h. Wind, fans and cars are well below it; aircraft are well above it.
Watch

"The effect of compressibility is always important in air flow" (Dec 2024 Q1(l)): false. Air is very compressible, but it only matters when the flow is fast, Ma > 0.3. Slow air flows (ventilation, wind on buildings) are treated as incompressible.

Exam type H1.4: volume or density change under pressure

Ready answer · bulk-modulus calculations \[\Delta V=-\frac{V\,\Delta p}{E},\qquad \rho_2\approx\rho_1\Big(1+\frac{\Delta p}{E}\Big)\]

In words: volume change = −(original volume × pressure rise ÷ bulk modulus); new density ≈ old density × (1 + pressure rise ÷ bulk modulus). Both assume the change is small (a few per cent), which it always is for liquids.

Example · oil in a hydraulic cylinder

A hydraulic cylinder holds 0.020 m³ of oil (\(E=1.5\times10^9\u{Pa}\), \(\rho=880\u{kg/m^3}\) at atmospheric pressure). The pressure is raised by 20 MPa. Find the change in volume and the new density.

  1. \[\Delta V=-\frac{0.020\times20\times10^6}{1.5\times10^9}\] which is \(-2.667\times10^{-4}\u{m^3}\): the oil shrinks by 0.267 L (1.33%).
  2. New density, from mass conservation: \[\rho_2=\frac{m}{V_2}=\frac{880(0.020)}{0.020-0.000267}\] so \(\rho_2=891.9\u{kg/m^3}\). Squeezing does not add or remove oil: the same mass now sits in a smaller volume.
  3. The short formula gives \(\rho_2\approx880(1+0.01333)=891.7\u{kg/m^3}\): the same to 3 significant figures.
\[\boxed{\Delta V=-0.267\u{L},\quad\rho_2\approx892\u{kg/m^3}}\]

The piston must move further than you might expect: that extra travel is the "springiness" of the oil.

You can now: define the bulk modulus and use it to find a volume or density change; compare the compressibility of air and water; find the speed of sound and the Mach number; and say when compressibility matters.

14The perfect gas law ~3 min

What is this?

A gas is a crowd of molecules flying about and hitting the walls of its container. The pressure is the push of all those hits. Pack in more molecules (higher density) or make them move faster (higher temperature) and the walls are hit harder and more often. The perfect gas law puts these two together.

Definition · ideal (perfect) gas

When the pressure is not too high and the temperature not too low, a gas behaves like an ideal gas: an imaginary substance that obeys the gas law below exactly. Air under normal conditions is an ideal gas (to a very good approximation).

Law · perfect gas law \[p=\rho RT,\qquad R=\frac{R_g}{M_g}\]

\(p\): absolute pressure; \(\rho\): density; \(T\): absolute temperature (K); \(R\): the gas constant of this particular gas; \(R_g\approx8.314\u{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}\): the universal gas constant; \(M_g\): the molar mass of the gas (kg/mol).

In words: pressure = density × gas constant × absolute temperature. It is T2's \(pv=RT\) with the specific volume \(v=1/\rho\).

Reading the formula
\(p\)
the push of the molecules on the walls, counted from a vacuum (absolute), because the molecules do not know about the atmosphere outside.
\(\rho\)
how much gas is packed into each cubic metre: more gas, more hits.
\(T\)
how vigorously the molecules move, counted from absolute zero, so it must be in kelvin.
\(R=R_g/M_g\)
depends on the gas: lighter molecules (small \(M_g\)) mean more molecules in each kilogram, so a larger \(R\).
Example · checking the density of air

Air has molar mass 0.02897 kg/mol. Find its gas constant and its density at 20 °C and 101 kPa.

  1. \(R=8.314/0.02897=287.0\u{J/(kg\,K)}\).
  2. \(T=20+273.15=293.15\u{K}\). The law needs temperature counted from absolute zero, where the molecules would stop pushing.
  3. \[\rho=\frac{p}{RT}=\frac{101\,000}{287.0\times293.15}\] so \(\rho=1.20\u{kg/m^3}\) ✓, the value in Section 3.

At 2 atm (gauge 101 kPa, absolute 202 kPa) and the same temperature the density doubles to 2.40 kg/m³: a gas's density follows its pressure, unlike a liquid's.

Watch

Use absolute pressure and kelvin in \(p=\rho RT\). Putting in a gauge pressure or °C gives nonsense (0 °C would make the density infinite).

You can now: state the perfect gas law with absolute pressure and temperature; find a gas constant \(R\) from the molar mass; and find the density of a gas.

15The physical laws of fluid mechanics ~6 min

What is this?

Think of a bank account. The money in it changes only by deposits and withdrawals; it is never created or destroyed inside. Fluid mechanics keeps the same kind of accounts for any region of fluid you choose: for mass, for momentum and for energy. Each law says: the rate of build-up inside = what comes in − what goes out, plus, for momentum and energy, what the forces or the heat add.

Finally, fluid mechanics applies a handful of physical laws to the fluid. They are developed in F1–F4; here they are only introduced.

Law · conservation of mass

Mass of a species can be neither created nor destroyed, unless there is a source or a sink (such as a chemical reaction). For any region (a "system"):

In words: rate at which mass builds up inside = rate at which mass enters − rate at which mass leaves (across the region's boundary).

For a steady flow nothing builds up, so mass in = mass out: chapter F2's continuity equation.

Law · momentum principle \[F=ma=m\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{\mathrm d(mV)}{\mathrm dt}\]

In words: Newton's second law: net force = rate of change of momentum. For a fluid: the rate of increase of momentum of a small piece of fluid = the net force on it from pressure differences, gravity and viscous stresses.

A fluid particle can accelerate in two ways:

  • local acceleration: the velocity at a fixed point changes with time (a tap being opened);
  • convective acceleration: the velocity changes from place to place, so a particle speeds up as it moves into a faster region, even if the flow is steady (water speeding up in a nozzle).
Laws · first and second laws of thermodynamics

First law: in a process of a closed system, the change in internal energy equals the heat added to the system minus the work done by the system, \(\Delta U=Q-W\). It is conservation of energy, and it applies to all thermodynamic processes (chapter T4).

Second law: it limits which processes can happen (natural processes go in the direction of increasing disorder, not the reverse) and how efficient a heat engine can be. Two classic statements:

  • "No process is possible whose sole result is the transfer of heat from a body of lower temperature to a body of higher temperature." (Clausius: a fridge needs work.)
  • "No process is possible in which the sole result is the absorption of heat from a reservoir and its complete conversion into work." (Kelvin–Planck: no engine is 100% efficient.)

Three ways to analyse a flow

  • Control volume (CV), or integral analysis: draw a region and balance what goes in and out. Only algebraic equations, easy to solve, but it gives only the net change across the CV, not details inside. (This is what F2–F4 do.)
  • Infinitesimal system, or differential analysis: apply the laws to a tiny element; gives differential equations, which may need solving on a computer, but gives the details of the flow at every point and time.
  • Experimental study, or dimensional analysis: find empirical (measured) relations to complement theory and to give data for practical design.

Setting up a problem

  • Governing equations: conservation of mass (continuity); momentum principle (Newton's 2nd law); first law of thermodynamics (conservation of energy).
  • Boundary conditions (what happens at the edges): at a solid surface, no slip (the fluid moves with the surface, Section 6) and zero normal flux (no fluid passes through the wall); at a free surface (liquid open to air), zero shear stress; at an interface between two different fluids, the stresses and velocities are continuous (the same on both sides).
  • Initial conditions (the state at the start) for a flow that changes with time.

To conclude

"Fluid mechanics" involves

  • Fluid as the material: its physical properties (density, viscosity, …) and its rheological behaviour (rheology = how a material flows: its stress–shear rate relation, Newtonian or not).
  • Mechanics as the fundamental principles: static equilibrium (H2–H4); conservation of mass, Newton's second law and energy principles, all in rate form (per second, F1–F4).

You can now: state conservation of mass, the momentum principle and the first and second laws of thermodynamics in words; name the three ways to analyse a flow; and give the boundary conditions at a solid wall, a free surface and an interface.

1 · A student's shaft-in-sleeve answer

"Shaft diameter 60 mm, sleeve 300 mm long, clearance 0.2 mm, \(\mu=0.4\u{Pa\,s}\), speed 0.5 m/s. \(\tau=0.4\times0.5/0.4=0.5\u{Pa}\) (clearance 0.4 mm, both sides). \(A=\pi(0.06)^2/4=2.83\times10^{-3}\u{m^2}\). \(F=1.41\times10^{-3}\u{N}\)."

Hint

Three mistakes: the gap, its units, and the area.

Answer

The gap is the clearance on one side, 0.2 mm = 0.0002 m (not 0.4, and not in mm). The wetted area is the side, \(\pi DL=\pi(0.06)(0.3)=0.05655\u{m^2}\). So \(\tau=0.4(0.5)/0.0002=1000\u{Pa}\) and \(F=1000(0.05655)=56.5\u{N}\), forty thousand times the student's value.

2 · A student's capillary sketch

"Mercury wets glass because its contact angle is large, so it climbs up the tube with a dome-shaped surface."

Hint

Which side of 90° is wetting?

Answer

A large contact angle (135° for mercury on glass) means non-wetting: cohesion beats adhesion, so mercury is depressed in the tube (\(h\lt0\)). The dome shape (meniscus curving up in the middle) is right for mercury; the "climbs" is wrong.

3 · A student's density at depth

"Sea water at the surface 1025 kg/m³, \(E=2.3\times10^9\u{Pa}\), pressure rise 50 MPa. \(\Delta\rho/\rho=E/\Delta p=46\), so the density is \(1025\times46=47\,150\u{kg/m^3}\)."

Hint

Is a 46-fold increase in density believable? Which way up is \(E=\Delta p/(\Delta\rho/\rho)\)?

Answer

\(\Delta\rho/\rho=\Delta p/E\), which is \(50\times10^6/2.3\times10^9=0.0217\), a 2.2% rise: \(\rho=1025(1.0217)=1047\u{kg/m^3}\). Liquids are nearly incompressible; any answer more than a few per cent away from the start is a sign of an inverted ratio.

19Concept checks

1 · Sand and water

Dry sand pours out of a bucket like a liquid. Is it a fluid in the sense of this chapter?

Answer

No. A pile of sand stands at a slope (its angle of repose) without flowing: it resists a shear stress by friction between grains, so it has shear strength. A fluid cannot hold any slope: its surface goes flat, because any shear stress would make it flow.

2 · Two oils

Two Newtonian oils are tested in the same parallel-plate apparatus. Oil A needs 20 N to drag the plate at 0.1 m/s; oil B needs 20 N at 0.4 m/s. Which is more viscous, and by what factor?

Answer

Same stress \(P/A\); oil A gives a quarter of the shear rate \(U/h\). \(\mu=\tau/(U/h)\), so oil A is 4 times more viscous.

3 · Halving the film

A block slides at terminal speed down an oiled slope. The oil film is replaced by one half as thick (same oil). What happens to the terminal speed?

Hint

\(U=W\sin\theta\,h/(\mu A)\).

Answer

It halves: the same driving force must be balanced, and a thinner film gives a steeper velocity gradient (more stress) at the same speed.

4 · Splitting a droplet

A droplet of radius \(R\) is split into 8 equal droplets. What is the radius of each, and how does the pressure excess inside compare with the original?

Answer

Volume ∝ \(R^3\), so each new droplet has radius \(R/2\) (\(8\times(R/2)^3=R^3\)). \(\Delta p=2\sigma/R\) doubles. The total surface area also doubles (\(8\times4\pi(R/2)^2=2\times4\pi R^2\)), so splitting costs surface energy.

5 · Boiling on a mountain

At the top of a mountain the air pressure is 70 kPa. Does water boil above or below 100 °C there? Why does a pressure cooker cook faster?

Answer

Below 100 °C (about 90 °C): the vapour pressure reaches 70 kPa at a lower temperature. A pressure cooker raises the pressure above 101 kPa, so the water stays liquid up to about 120 °C, and food cooks faster at the higher temperature.

6 · Ketchup and paint

Why must you shake a ketchup bottle, and why does good paint not drip from the brush?

Answer

Ketchup is a Bingham plastic (and shear thinning): under its own weight the stress is below its yield stress, so it stays put; a sharp shake exceeds the yield stress and it flows. Paint is shear thinning: brushing (high shear rate) makes it runny, but standing on the brush or wall (low shear rate) it is thick.

7 · Squeezing water

By what fraction must the pressure on water (\(E=2.2\times10^9\u{Pa}\)) be raised to cut its volume by 1%? Compare with atmospheric pressure.

Answer

\(\Delta p=E\times0.01=22\u{MPa}\), about 217 atmospheres. Water is, for almost every purpose, incompressible.

Answer

Newtonian: viscosity is constant, independent of the stress (\(\tau=\mu\,\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\), a straight line through the origin). May 2021 Q1(a), (b)4 + 6 marksH1.4 bulk modulus

Assume the specific weight of seawater at the ocean surface is 10.0 kN/m³, and the bulk modulus of elasticity is \(2.3\times10^9\u{N/m^2}\).

(a) What is the density of seawater at the ocean surface? (4 marks) H1.3

(b) The pressure has increased by 82 MPa at a depth of 8 km in the ocean. What is the density of seawater at such depth? (6 marks)

Hint

(a) \(\rho=\gamma/g\), with \(\gamma\) in N/m³. (b) \(E=\Delta p/(\Delta\rho/\rho)\): find the fractional increase in density.

Answer

(a) \(\rho_1=\dfrac{\gamma}{g}=\dfrac{10.0\times10^3}{9.81}\), so \(\boxed{\rho_1=1019\u{kg/m^3}}\).

(b) \(\dfrac{\Delta\rho}{\rho_1}=\dfrac{\Delta p}{E}=\dfrac{82\times10^6}{2.3\times10^9}=0.03565\).

\[\begin{aligned}\rho_2&=\rho_1\Big(1+\frac{\Delta p}{E}\Big)\\&=1019.4(1.03565)\\&=1055.7\u{kg/m^3}\end{aligned}\] \[\boxed{\rho_2\approx1056\u{kg/m^3}}\]

the bulk-modulus definition is for small changes. Applying it to the volume instead (\(V_2=V_1(1-\Delta p/E)\), then \(\rho_2=\rho_1V_1/V_2\)) gives 1057 kg/m³; the 0.1% difference is within the accuracy of the model, and either working should earn the marks.

A shaft of diameter 100 mm is made to move through a sleeve at a speed of 0.08 m/s under the action of an applied force \(F\). The sleeve is 500 mm long and there is a clearance of 0.5 mm (assume uniform all around) between the shaft and the sleeve. The clearance is filled with oil of viscosity of 0.35 Ns/m². Constant speed: \(F\) equals the viscous force. Wetted area = side of the shaft inside the sleeve; gap = 0.5 mm.

Answer

  4. \(F=\tau A=56\times0.1571\).
\[\boxed{F\approx8.80\u{N}}\]
