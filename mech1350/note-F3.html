The Bernoulli equation

0Where this chapter sits

F2 gave continuity, \(Q=A_1V_1=A_2V_2\): how speed changes with area. This chapter adds how pressure changes with speed and height. Picture each kilogram of flowing water carrying a fixed energy budget, split between three shares: pressure, speed and height. Along a streamline, with no friction and no pump, energy moves between the shares but the total never changes. F4 then adds forces (momentum) and losses.

Sign and unit conventions for this chapter
  • Pressure means gauge pressure (pressure above atmospheric) unless a question says otherwise. So anything open to the air, such as a free jet, the free surface of an open tank or the open end of a pipe, has \(p=0\). A negative gauge pressure means "below atmospheric" (a partial suction).
  • Heights \(z\) are measured upward from a datum (reference level) that you choose; pick the lowest point or a pipe centreline. Only differences of \(z\) matter, so the choice never changes the answer.
  • Number the points along the flow: point 1 upstream, point 2 downstream. Bernoulli is written "point 1 = point 2".
  • Manometer rule (from H2): moving down through a liquid of density \(\rho\) by a height \(h\), pressure rises by \(\rho gh\); moving up, it falls by \(\rho gh\); across a gas column it barely changes (air is about 800 times lighter than water).
  • Data used unless a paper says otherwise: \(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\), \(\rho_\text{water}=1000\u{kg/m^3}\), \(\rho_\text{air}\approx1.2\) to \(1.23\u{kg/m^3}\). Some papers use \(g=9.8\), and some solutions use \(\rho_\text{water}=999\u{kg/m^3}\). Use what the question gives.
  • Specific weight \(\gamma=\rho g\) (N/m³): many solutions write \(p/\gamma\) where this note writes \(p/(\rho g)\). They are the same thing.

1Fun observations: fast air, low pressure ~6 min

What is this?

Picture every kilogram of air or water carrying a fixed energy budget, split between three shares: pressure, speed and height. If nothing adds energy and nothing leaks away, the total stays the same as the fluid moves along. So when the fluid speeds up at the same height, the extra speed is paid for out of the pressure share. That is why fast air has low pressure, and why the slower air nearby, at higher pressure, pushes things towards the fast stream.

Start with experiments you can do with your own breath. They all show one surprising fact, which the rest of the chapter turns into an equation:

Key idea · Bernoulli's principle in words

In words: where a fluid moves faster, its pressure is lower; where it moves slower, its pressure is higher. A body with fast-moving fluid on one side and slow or still fluid on the other is pushed towards the fast side.

Paper lift

One sheet. Hold a sheet of paper by its top edge just below your lips, so it droops forward, and blow across the top of it. The sheet rises. The air you blow moves fast over the top surface, so the pressure there is lower than atmospheric. The still air underneath is at atmospheric pressure, which is higher. The pressure difference times the area of the sheet is a net upward force that lifts the paper.

Two sheets. Hold two sheets vertically a few centimetres apart and blow hard between them. Instead of being blown apart, they move together. The fast air between them is at low pressure; the still outside air, at higher (atmospheric) pressure, pushes both sheets inwards.

Force sketches. Left: one sheet; the pressure on the underside (still air) is larger than on the top (fast air), so the net pressure force \(F\) is upward. Right: two sheets; outside pressure pushes each sheet towards the fast stream between them.

Keep it up: a ball in an air stream

A ping-pong ball can float, steady, in the air stream blowing upward from a pipe (a hair dryer does the same). The explanation: objects in a moving stream of fluid are pulled to the centre of the stream, because the pressure is lower inside the stream than outside. If the ball drifts sideways, the still air on its outer side (higher pressure) pushes it back into the fast stream (lower pressure). Vertically, the upward push of the stream on the ball balances its weight.

A second demonstration: two ping-pong balls hang on threads side by side. Blow between them and they swing together: the air between them is fast and at low pressure, and the normal (atmospheric) pressure outside pushes them inwards. It is the two-sheet experiment again.

Left: ball held in an upward jet; a sideways drift is pushed back by the higher outside pressure. Right: two hanging balls pushed together when air is blown between them.

How does a plane wing work?

What is this? (wing lift)

A wing is shaped and tilted so that it bends the oncoming air downwards as it passes. The air sweeping over the curved top is sped up, so its pressure share drops; the air underneath is slowed a little, so its pressure rises. The two streams do not have to meet again at the back edge: in fact the air over the top gets there first. The pressure difference over the wing area is the lift.

Now think of an airliner. The wing is shaped and tilted so that air flows faster over the top than under the bottom; by the same principle, the pressure on top is lower, and the net upward pressure force is the lift that holds the plane up. Section 5 returns to this with a diagram.

Going further · a caution about these explanations

Bernoulli's equation strictly compares points on the same streamline (Section 2), while the blown stream and the still air below a sheet are different streams. Physicists add that the stream also curves around the paper or wing, and a curved stream needs lower pressure on the inside of the bend. Both pictures agree on the answer (fast, curving flow has low pressure). In the exam, give the standard explanation: fast flow → low pressure → net force towards the fast side.

You can now: explain the paper-lift, two-sheet and floating-ball demonstrations with "fast flow, low pressure, push towards the fast side"; say how a wing gets lift from faster, lower-pressure air over its top; give the standard explanation in the exam even though the full physics has more to it.

2The Bernoulli equation and when it is valid ~8 min

What is this?

Bernoulli's equation is the energy budget written out for each cubic metre of moving fluid: a pressure share, a speed share and a height share. Follow the fluid along one streamline and energy can move from one share to another, but the sum stays fixed. Speed up and the pressure must fall; climb and the pressure must fall too.

What is this? (streamline)

A streamline is like one lane of a motorway: the fluid in it keeps to its lane. The budget is kept lane by lane, which is why Bernoulli only compares two points on the same streamline.

Definition · streamline

A streamline is a line drawn in a flow that is everywhere in the direction of the flow (tangent to the velocity). In steady flow (nothing changes with time) it is also the path that a fluid particle follows. Picture the trail of a tiny drop of dye released into the stream.

Result · the Bernoulli equation \[\begin{aligned}&p+\tfrac12\rho V^2+\rho gh=\text{constant}\\&\text{(along a streamline)}\end{aligned}\]

\(p\): static pressure (the fluid pressure); \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\): dynamic pressure (from the speed \(V\)); \(\rho gh\): hydrostatic pressure (from the height \(h\); \(g\) is the gravitational acceleration).

In words: static pressure + dynamic pressure + hydrostatic pressure stays the same as you follow the fluid along a streamline. If the fluid speeds up, or rises, its pressure must drop to compensate.

Reading the formula
\(p\)
the pressure share: the work the surrounding fluid can do pushing each cubic metre along.
\(\tfrac12\rho V^2\)
the speed share: the kinetic energy of each cubic metre.
\(\rho gh\)
the height share: the potential energy of each cubic metre above the datum.
\(=\text{constant}\)
the total budget: the same at every point of one streamline, though each streamline may have its own value.

Each term is an energy per unit volume (J/m³ = N/m² = Pa): \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\) is kinetic energy per m³, \(\rho gh\) is potential energy per m³, and \(p\) is the work the pressure does per m³ pushing fluid along (Section 3). So Bernoulli is conservation of mechanical energy for a flowing fluid. The equation is named after Daniel Bernoulli (1700–1782).

It is an approximate relation between pressure, velocity and elevation, valid in regions of steady, incompressible flow where net frictional forces are negligible. Despite its simplicity it is one of the most powerful tools in fluid mechanics.

What is this? (the five assumptions)

Each assumption protects the budget. Friction would leak energy away as heat; a pump or fan would add some; heating or cooling would change it; in a changing flow the budget at a point is not settled; and another streamline may start with a different budget. Constant density keeps the pressure share simple. If any assumption fails, the books no longer balance.

Definitions · the five assumptions
  1. Steady flow: at any fixed point, speed and pressure do not change with time.
  2. Inviscid flow (ideal, frictionless fluid): viscosity (internal friction) has no noticeable effect, so no mechanical energy is lost as heat.
  3. Constant density (incompressible flow): \(\rho\) is the same everywhere. True for liquids; fine for gases at low speed (well below the speed of sound, about 100 m/s or less for air).
  4. No shaft work or heat transfer: no pump, fan or turbine between the two points, and no heating or cooling.
  5. Along a streamline: the two points must lie on the same streamline (the constant can differ from one streamline to another).

Picture the streamlines around a wing: Bernoulli is valid in the smooth flow away from the wing, and not valid in the thin layer next to its surface and in the wake behind it. The reason: the equation applies only to inviscid regions of flow; it is typically useful outside boundary layers and wakes, where the motion is governed by pressure and gravity together.

Definitions · boundary layer and wake

A boundary layer is the thin layer of fluid next to a solid surface, where the fluid is slowed by friction from zero speed at the wall (the fluid sticks to the wall) to the full stream speed. A wake is the disturbed, swirling region behind a body. Friction (viscosity) matters in both, so energy is lost and Bernoulli fails there.

Away from the wing (outer streamlines) the flow is effectively frictionless and Bernoulli applies; in the boundary layer on the surface and in the wake it does not. A pipe that rises and narrows shows the three quantities that Bernoulli connects: velocity, elevation and pressure.

Streamlines can be drawn as the paths of particles in a flow, smooth in the free stream and more disturbed near a surface, a reminder that the equation follows the fluid along one streamline.

Ready answer · when does Bernoulli NOT apply? (exam type F3.1)

Check each assumption: unsteady flow (a valve opening, a gust); viscous regions (boundary layers, wakes, long pipes with friction losses, flow through a filter); compressible flow (gas at high speed, say above about 100 m/s); a pump, fan or turbine between the points, or heating; points on different streamlines in a flow that is not uniform. Example (May 2022 Q4, multiple choice): which of "air flow of red wine in a decanter", "lift of an aerofoil", "blood flow in our eyes" and "pulling felt in a subway tunnel" is not a Bernoulli flow? Aerofoil lift and the pull towards a passing train are classic Bernoulli effects; for the other two, run through the five assumptions (is the flow slow and dominated by friction in tiny vessels? is it a swirling, unsteady pour?).

the intended answer to May 2022 Q4 is not certain (blood flow in arteries is a standard Bernoulli example, but the vessels of the eye are tiny and friction-dominated); the exam guide records the decision.

You can now: write Bernoulli's equation and name its three terms; state the five assumptions; say where it fails (boundary layers, wakes, pumps and fans, unsteady or fast gas flow) for a "when does Bernoulli not apply" question.

3Deriving Bernoulli from work and energy ~4 min

What is this?

The derivation is bookkeeping for the budget. Follow a slug of fluid along a pipe. The fluid behind pushes it forward, so pressure pays energy in; the fluid ahead pushes back, so pressure takes some out. With no friction, the net payment must show up as extra speed or extra height of the slug. Write that down, divide by the volume, and Bernoulli's equation appears.

Derive the equation with the work–energy theorem from HKDSE Physics: net work done on a body = change in its mechanical energy. The "body" is a slug of fluid moving along a pipe that rises and changes area.

The fluid between the two green slices moves right. In a short time the left end moves \(x_1\) (cross-section \(A_1\), pressure \(P_1\), speed \(v_1\), height \(h_1\)) and the right end moves \(x_2\) (\(A_2\), \(P_2\), \(v_2\), \(h_2\)). Net effect: a small mass \(m\) has been moved from the low end to the high end.

Work done by pressure

The fluid behind pushes the slug forward with force \(F_1\) through distance \(x_1\); the fluid ahead pushes back with \(F_2\) while the slug moves \(x_2\) forward. The work done on the slug is

\[W=W_1+W_2=F_1x_1-F_2x_2\qquad(1)\]

The second term is negative because \(F_2\) points against the motion. Pressure is force per area, \(F=PA\):

\[\Delta W=P_1A_1x_1-P_2A_2x_2\qquad(2)\]

\(A_1x_1\) is the volume swept at the left end, \(A_2x_2\) the volume at the right end. The fluid is incompressible, so these are the same volume \(V\) (the same fluid that enters must leave):

\[V=A_1x_1=A_2x_2\qquad(3)\] \[\Delta W=(P_1-P_2)V\qquad(4)\]

In words: the net work done by pressure = pressure drop × volume moved.

Here \(V\) is a volume, not a speed; speeds are written \(v\) in this derivation.

Change in mechanical energy

The net change is that mass \(m\) (volume \(V\)) at height \(h_1\) with speed \(v_1\) has been replaced by the same mass at \(h_2\) with speed \(v_2\). Kinetic energy \(K=\tfrac12mv^2\), potential energy \(U=mgh\) (\(h\) = average height of the small mass):

\[\begin{aligned}\Delta E&=E_2-E_1=(U_2+K_2)-(U_1+K_1)\\&=\big(mgh_2+\tfrac12mv_2^2\big)\\&\quad-\big(mgh_1+\tfrac12mv_1^2\big)\qquad(5)\end{aligned}\]

Putting them together

from work–energy to Bernoulli, step by step

1. Work–energy theorem: \(\Delta W=\Delta E\) (Eq. 6). No friction, so all the work by pressure goes into kinetic plus potential energy.

2. Substitute (4) and (5) (Eq. 7):

\[\begin{aligned}(P_1-P_2)V&=\big(mgh_2+\tfrac12mv_2^2\big)\\&\quad-\big(mgh_1+\tfrac12mv_1^2\big)\end{aligned}\]

3. Divide by the volume \(V\), using \(\rho=m/V\) (Eq. 8, 9): mass ÷ volume = density

\[\begin{aligned}P_1-P_2&=\big(\rho gh_2+\tfrac12\rho v_2^2\big)\\&\quad-\big(\rho gh_1+\tfrac12\rho v_1^2\big)\end{aligned}\]

4. Put the point-1 terms on one side and the point-2 terms on the other (Eq. 10):

\[\begin{aligned}&P_1+\rho gh_1+\tfrac12\rho v_1^2\\&=P_2+\rho gh_2+\tfrac12\rho v_2^2\end{aligned}\]

5. Points 1 and 2 were any two points along the flow, so the combination is the same everywhere along it (Eq. 11): If every pair of points has the same total, there is one fixed budget for the whole streamline.

\[P+\rho gh+\tfrac12\rho v^2=\text{constant}\]
Result · Bernoulli between two points (Eq. 10) \[\begin{aligned}&P_1+\rho gh_1+\tfrac12\rho v_1^2\\&=P_2+\rho gh_2+\tfrac12\rho v_2^2\end{aligned}\]

In words: (pressure + potential energy per volume + kinetic energy per volume) at point 1 = the same total at point 2, for two points on one streamline of a steady, frictionless, incompressible flow.

Going further · the analogy

Think of a skateboarder on a ramp: going down she gains speed (kinetic energy) by losing height (potential energy), and the total stays the same if there is no friction. A fluid has a third "energy account", pressure. It can trade between all three: speed up by losing pressure (a nozzle), rise by losing pressure (water pushed up a pipe), or speed up by falling (a jet from a tank).

You can now: derive Bernoulli from the work–energy theorem: net work by pressure \((P_1-P_2)V\), change in kinetic plus potential energy, then divide by the volume; explain why the volume swept at each end is the same.

4Heads: the equation per unit weight ~6 min

What is this?

Heads are the same energy budget, counted per newton of fluid instead of per cubic metre. Energy per newton comes out in metres, so each share becomes a height. The pressure share is how high the pressure could push the fluid up a tube; the speed share is how high the fluid would fly if thrown straight up; the height share is just its height. The total head is the whole budget as one height.

If we further assume the flow is incompressible, Euler's equation can be integrated to get the same result in a second form.

Going further · Euler's equation

Euler's equation is Newton's second law (\(F=ma\)) applied to a tiny fluid particle moving along a streamline, with only pressure and gravity acting (no friction). Integrating it along the streamline with \(\rho\) constant gives exactly Bernoulli's equation. It is the same result as Section 3, reached through forces instead of work.

Dividing Bernoulli's equation by \(\rho g\) (the weight per unit volume) gives every term the unit of length:

Result · Bernoulli in head form \[\frac{p}{\rho g}+z+\frac{V^2}{2g}=\text{constant}\] \[\frac{p_1}{\rho g}+z_1+\frac{V_1^2}{2g}=\frac{p_2}{\rho g}+z_2+\frac{V_2^2}{2g}\]

\(p/(\rho g)\): pressure head; \(z\): elevation (potential) head; \(V^2/(2g)\): velocity head. All in metres.

In words: pressure head + elevation head + velocity head is the same at any two points on the same streamline. This is the form printed on recent papers, \(z+P/(\rho g)+U^2/(2g)=\text{constant}\).

Reading the formula
\(\dfrac{p}{\rho g}\)
pressure head: how high the pressure share could push the fluid up a tube.
\(z\)
elevation head: the height share, measured from your datum.
\(\dfrac{V^2}{2g}\)
velocity head: the speed share, as the height a jet at that speed would climb.
\(=\text{constant}\)
total head: the whole budget as one height. For ideal flow it is a level line, the energy line.
What is this? (piezometric and total head)

Two kinds of tube read the budget. A plain tube on the pipe wall (a piezometer) sees only the pressure and height shares. A tube turned to face the flow stops the fluid at its mouth, so it also catches the speed share and shows the whole budget. The gap between the two levels is the speed share.

Definitions · heads
  • A head is an energy per unit weight of fluid (J/N = m), so it has the dimension of length [L]. It is the height of a column of the flowing fluid that would hold that much energy.
  • Pressure head \(p/(\rho g)\): the height to which the pressure could push the fluid up a tube.
  • Elevation head \(z\): height above the datum.
  • Velocity head \(V^2/(2g)\): the height the fluid would rise to if thrown straight up at speed \(V\) (the same as \(v^2=u^2-2gs\) with final speed 0).
  • Piezometric head = pressure head + elevation head \(=p/(\rho g)+z\): the level the liquid stands at in a piezometer (a plain vertical tube connected to a hole in the pipe wall).
  • Total head = piezometric head + velocity head \(=p/(\rho g)+z+V^2/(2g)\). For ideal steady flow it is constant along a streamline (but may differ between streamlines).

A piezometer (left tube) shows the piezometric head; a tube bent to face the flow (right, a Pitot tube, Section 9) shows the total head. The difference between the two levels is the velocity head \(V^2/2g\). The dashed line joining total heads along a pipe is the energy line; the line joining piezometric heads is the hydraulic grade line. Where the pipe narrows, \(V\) rises, so the hydraulic grade line dips while the energy line stays level (no friction).

Ready answer · "Define energy head. What dimensions does it have?" (Dec 2018 Q3(a), exam type F3.1)

Energy head is the mechanical energy per unit weight of the flowing fluid. Total head \(H=p/(\rho g)+z+V^2/(2g)\) is the sum of pressure head, elevation head and velocity head. Since J/N = N·m/N = m, it has the dimension of length, [L] (unit: m).

Example

Water flows at \(3\u{m/s}\) in a pipe 5 m above the datum, with gauge pressure 200 kPa. Find the three heads, the piezometric head and the total head.

\[\frac{p}{\rho g}=\frac{200\,000}{1000(9.81)}=20.39\u{m}\] \[\frac{V^2}{2g}=\frac{3^2}{2(9.81)}=0.459\u{m},\qquad z=5\u{m}\]

Piezometric head \(=20.39+5=25.39\u{m}\); total head \(=\boxed{25.85\u{m}}\). The velocity head is small: a piezometer would show 25.39 m, a Pitot tube 25.85 m.

Watch

Heads use the density of the flowing fluid. For air (\(\rho\approx1.2\)), 100 Pa is a pressure head of \(100/(1.2\times9.81)=8.5\u{m}\) of air. In gas problems the elevation terms are tiny compared with the others and are usually dropped (Example 2).

You can now: convert a pressure into a pressure head and a speed into a velocity head; define energy head and give its dimension [L]; say what a piezometer and a Pitot tube read and what the gap between their levels means.

5Everyday applications ~8 min

What is this?

Every everyday Bernoulli effect is the same story. Something makes the fluid fast in one place: a narrow gap, a blown stream, a spinning surface. At the same height, the extra speed is paid for out of the pressure share, so the pressure there is low. The slower fluid on the other side, at higher pressure, pushes the body towards the fast side.

Each application below follows the same two-step logic, which is also the marking scheme for "explain" questions:

Ready answer · explaining an everyday effect (exam type F3.2)
  1. Do: Where is the fluid fast? Use continuity: a smaller gap or area means a higher speed (\(A_1V_1=A_2V_2\)); or the fluid is blown, or moves relative to the body. You must find where the budget has moved into speed before you can say where the pressure is low.
  2. Do: Bernoulli: along a streamline (same height), higher speed means lower pressure, \(p+\tfrac12\rho V^2=\text{constant}\). At the same height, a bigger speed share leaves a smaller pressure share.
  3. Do: Force: the higher-pressure side pushes the body towards the lower-pressure side; state the motion that results. The question is always about what the body does, and the net pressure force points from high pressure to low.
  4. Do: Draw a sketch with streamlines (closer together = faster), mark "low p" and "high p", and the force arrow. Crowded streamlines show the fast flow at a glance, and a labelled sketch can earn a mark (May 2025 Q3).

Wing (streamlines crowd over the top), spinning baseball, a constricted artery in the neck, and a burrow (or chimney) with wind blowing across its higher opening.

Aeroplanes

Lift on a wing comes from the different air speeds and pressures on its two surfaces. Streamlines crowd together over the curved top: the air there is faster and at lower pressure; under the wing the air is slower and at higher pressure. The pressure difference over the wing area is the upward lift force.

Baseballs

A ball's path curves because of its spin. Picture a ball (seen from above, heading towards home plate) spinning so that on one side (A) its surface moves along with the air flowing past it, and on the other side (B) against it. The surface drags air along, so the air speeds on the two sides are not equal: faster on one side, slower on the other. The faster side has lower pressure, so the ball is pushed sideways towards it and swerves. (This sideways force on a spinning ball is called the Magnus effect.)

Blood flow

A person with a constricted artery (narrowed, for example by fatty deposits) in the neck may feel a temporary lack of blood to the brain. Follow the arteries from the heart (aorta) through the subclavian and vertebral arteries to the basilar artery that feeds the brain, with a constriction in the left subclavian artery. Blood speeds up to get through the narrow part (continuity) and its pressure drops there (Bernoulli), so less pressure is available to push blood on towards the brain.

Chimneys and burrows

Air flowing across the top helps smoke go up a chimney, and air flowing over several openings provides the circulation an animal needs in an underground burrow. Think of a prairie-dog burrow with two openings: wind blows across the higher, mounded opening faster than across the lower one. The fast air at the top of an opening has lower pressure, so air (or smoke) inside is sucked up and out, and fresh air is drawn in at the other opening.

Ready answer · May 2025 Q1: why does smoke rise faster in a chimney on a windy day? (3 marks)

(1) Wind blows across the top of the chimney at high speed. (2) By Bernoulli (same height), high speed means low pressure at the chimney top, lower than the pressure of the still air in the room at the fireplace. (3) This extra pressure difference sucks the smoke up the chimney faster, on top of the usual rise of hot, less dense gas.

Going further · more exam favourites (May 2025 Q2, Q3; May 2022 Q8)

Thumb over a garden hose. The tap delivers roughly the same flow rate \(Q\). Covering part of the end reduces the exit area \(A\), so by continuity the exit speed \(V=Q/A\) rises; Bernoulli says the pressure in the hose is converted into this extra speed at the exit. A faster jet travels further before gravity brings it down (range grows with speed, like a projectile).

Two boats side by side. Water between two boats moving in parallel is squeezed through a narrow channel, so it flows faster relative to the boats than the water on their outer sides (continuity). Faster water means lower pressure between the hulls (Bernoulli). The higher pressure on the outer sides pushes the boats towards each other, so they risk colliding. (The same effect pulls you towards a passing train or a subway train in a tunnel.)

Bladeless fan. A fan in the base pushes air up into a hollow ring, which blows it out of a thin slit at high speed. The fast jet has low pressure, so it pulls (entrains) the surrounding still air through the ring and around it; the moving air leaving the front is many times the air the motor pumps. 6Torricelli's theorem: jets from tanks ~5 min

What is this?

At the free surface of a big tank the water is almost still and open to the air, so its whole budget sits in the height share. At the hole it is open to the air again but lower down, so the height it lost has become speed. That is why the jet moves as fast as a stone dropped from the surface to the hole. Point the jet upwards and the speed turns back into height: it climbs back towards the surface level.

A large open tank has a small tap (spigot) near its bottom. How fast does the water come out? Take point 2 on the free surface (height \(y_2\)) and point 1 in the jet just outside the tap (height \(y_1\)), on the same streamline.

Open tank, water surface at \(y_2\), jet leaves the tap at \(y_1\).

  • Free surface: open to the air, so \(p_2=0\); the tank is large, so the surface falls very slowly: \(v_2\approx0\).
  • Jet: a free jet is surrounded by air, so its pressure is atmospheric, \(p_1=0\).

Bernoulli between the two points, with both pressures zero and \(v_2=0\):

\[\tfrac12\rho v_1^2+\rho gy_1=\rho gy_2\]
Result · Torricelli's theorem \[v_1=\sqrt{2g(y_2-y_1)}\]

In words: the jet leaves a tank as fast as a stone would be moving after falling freely from the water surface down to the hole. Only the depth of the hole below the surface matters, not the tank's shape.

The reverse is also true: a jet pointing straight up at speed \(V\) rises to height \(V^2/(2g)\) above the nozzle (all its velocity head becomes elevation head). With no losses, a jet from a tank would climb back to the level of the free surface.

a neat formula for the range

Depth of hole below surface \(\Delta y\), height of hole above floor \(y_1\). Speed \(v=\sqrt{2g\,\Delta y}\); fall time \(t=\sqrt{2y_1/g}\).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{range}&=vt=\sqrt{2g\,\Delta y}\sqrt{\frac{2y_1}{g}}\\&=2\sqrt{\Delta y\,y_1}\end{aligned}\]

\(g\) cancels. For a fixed total water depth \(\Delta y+y_1\), the product \(\Delta y\,y_1\) is largest when the two are equal: a hole at half the water depth throws furthest. With 3 m of water, a hole at 1.5 m throws \(2\sqrt{1.5\times1.5}=3.0\u{m}\).

7Flow meters: Venturi, nozzle and orifice ~8 min

What is this?

A Venturi meter is a deliberate squeeze in the pipe. The same flow must pass through the narrow throat, so it goes faster there (continuity). The extra speed share is paid for out of the pressure share, so the pressure at the throat dips. The bigger the flow, the bigger the dip, so measuring the dip measures the flow rate. The gentle widening after the throat slows the water again and gives most of the pressure back.

Many problems on one-dimensional flow of an ideal fluid are solved by using Bernoulli's equation and the continuity equation together. The most useful are flow meters.

Definitions · three meters for measuring discharge in a pipe
  • Venturi meter: a rapidly converging section that speeds the flow up and so lowers the pressure, a short throat (narrowest part), then a gently diverging section (the diffuser) that returns the pipe to its original size. Measuring the pressure difference between the inlet (1) and the throat (2) gives the discharge.
  • Nozzle meter (flow nozzle): essentially a Venturi meter with the converging part replaced by a nozzle inside the pipe, and no diverging part.
  • Orifice meter: the simplest and cheapest: a plate with a sharp-edged hole (orifice), fitted concentrically (centred) in the pipe.

Orifice, nozzle and Venturi meters, pressure taps at (1) upstream and (2) at the narrowest flow. Swirling eddies form behind the orifice plate and the nozzle; the Venturi's gentle diffuser lets the flow slow down smoothly.

The Venturi meter formula

Assume the flow is horizontal, steady, inviscid and incompressible between points (1) and (2), and the velocity is uniform across each section. Picture a Venturi of diameters \(D_1\), \(D_2\) with a U-tube showing a level difference \(h\).

\[\begin{aligned}&p_1+\tfrac12\rho V_1^2=p_2+\tfrac12\rho V_2^2\\&\text{(Bernoulli, } z_1=z_2)\end{aligned}\] \[Q=A_1V_1=A_2V_2\qquad\text{(continuity, }A_2\lt A_1)\]
combining the two into the flow-rate formula

1. Continuity: \(V_1=V_2A_2/A_1\). Same \(Q\) through both sections.

2. Bernoulli rearranged: \(p_1-p_2=\tfrac12\rho(V_2^2-V_1^2)\). The pressure drop pays for the extra kinetic energy.

3. Substitute step 1:

\[p_1-p_2=\tfrac12\rho V_2^2\big[1-(A_2/A_1)^2\big]\]

4. Solve for \(V_2\) and multiply by \(A_2\):

\[V_2=\sqrt{\frac{2(p_1-p_2)}{\rho\,[1-(A_2/A_1)^2]}}\]

so \(Q=A_2V_2\), which is the result below.

Result · theoretical (ideal) flow rate \[Q=A_2\sqrt{\frac{2(p_1-p_2)}{\rho\,[1-(A_2/A_1)^2]}}\]

In words: flow rate = throat area × the speed that the measured pressure drop can produce, corrected for the fluid already moving at the inlet. For a given meter (\(A_1\), \(A_2\) fixed), measure \(p_1-p_2\) and you know \(Q\).

Reading the formula
\(A_2\)
the throat area: flow rate = throat speed × throat area.
\(p_1-p_2\)
the pressure dip: the pressure share spent on extra speed between inlet and throat.
\(\rho\)
the density of the flowing fluid: a lighter fluid gains more speed from the same dip.
\(1-(A_2/A_1)^2\)
a correction for the speed share the fluid already has at the inlet; close to 1 when the throat is much narrower than the inlet.

Real fluids have friction, so the real flow is a little smaller. So include a coefficient of discharge \(C_d\):

Result · actual flow rate \[\begin{aligned}Q_\text{actual}&=C_dQ_\text{ideal}\\&=C_dA_2\sqrt{\frac{2(p_1-p_2)}{\rho\,[1-(A_2/A_1)^2]}}\end{aligned}\]

In words: actual flow = (coefficient of discharge, found by calibration) × ideal flow. \(C_d\) is about 0.95–0.99 for a Venturi, roughly 0.6 for a sharp-edged orifice.

Reading the pressure difference from a manometer

Going further · manometer across a meter, any slope (May 2024 Q1(a))

When the meter is inclined (point 2 a height \(z_2\) above point 1) and a U-tube with a heavier liquid \(\rho_m\) is connected between the two taps, the manometer reading \(H\) gives the piezometric difference, not the pressure difference:

\[(p_1+\rho gz_1)-(p_2+\rho gz_2)=(\rho_m-\rho)gH\]

Bernoulli in the same piezometric form, with continuity, then gives

\[Q_\text{ideal}=A_1\sqrt{\frac{2gH\big(\frac{\rho_m}{\rho}-1\big)}{\big(\frac{A_1}{A_2}\big)^2-1}}\]

In words: the manometer reading already includes the height change, so the slope of the meter drops out; the formula is the same for horizontal, inclined and vertical meters. The full proof is the May 2024 Q1(a) answer in the exam taster.

Example

A horizontal Venturi meter (inlet 100 mm, throat 50 mm) carries water. A mercury (SG 13.6) U-tube between inlet and throat reads \(H=200\u{mm}\); \(C_d=0.98\). Find the discharge.

  1. Pressure difference: \[\begin{aligned}p_1-p_2&=(\rho_m-\rho)gH\\&=12\,600(9.81)(0.2)=24.7\u{kPa}\end{aligned}\] The limbs above the mercury are full of water too, so only the extra density of the mercury counts.
  2. Area ratio \(A_2/A_1=(50/100)^2=0.25\), so \(1-(A_2/A_1)^2=0.9375\).
\[V_2=\sqrt{\frac{2(24\,721)}{1000(0.9375)}}=7.26\u{m/s}\]

\[\begin{aligned}Q_\text{ideal}&=A_2V_2\\&=\tfrac\pi4(0.05)^2(7.26)\\&=0.0143\u{m^3/s}\end{aligned}\] \(Q_\text{actual}=0.98(0.0143)=\boxed{0.0140\u{m^3/s}}\) (14.0 L/s). Inlet speed \(V_1=1.82\u{m/s}\).

Red line: piezometric head (what piezometers along the meter would show); the gap between them is the velocity head. The grey band shows the meter's shape. Going further · advantages and limitations of a Venturi meter (May 2024 Q1(b)(c), exam type F3.5)

Key advantage: the gentle diffuser lets the flow slow down smoothly and recover most of its pressure, so the permanent pressure loss is small (much smaller than an orifice, whose flow leaves the hole as a jet and wastes its energy in eddies). Also: no moving parts, robust, accurate (\(C_d\) close to 1 and well known), suits large flows and dirty fluids (smooth walls, nothing to clog).

Drawbacks with turbulence and separation: the formula assumes steady, uniform, frictionless flow. In turbulent flow the velocity fluctuates and is not uniform across the section, so the readings fluctuate and friction losses grow. If the diffuser angle is too large or the flow too fast, the flow separates from the diffuser wall (it stops following the wall and forms a swirling, reversed-flow region): pressure is not recovered, losses rise sharply, and the pressure at the throat tap may be disturbed. Bernoulli's assumptions then fail, \(C_d\) is no longer the calibrated value, and the measured \(Q\) becomes unreliable. Other drawbacks: Venturis are long and expensive.

9Stagnation point and the Pitot tube ~9 min

What is this?

Put a blunt object in a stream and the fluid heading straight for its nose has to stop. Stopping empties the speed share of the budget into the pressure share, so the pressure at the nose is the highest anywhere on that streamline. The rise in pressure is exactly the speed share the fluid had.

Stagnation point

When a uniform stream flows towards a blunt body (a body with a rounded or flat front, not a sharp edge), the streamlines split: some go over, some under. But the one in the centre, the stagnation streamline, runs straight into the tip of the body and stops. There the velocity is zero: the fluid does not move at this one point.

Definition · stagnation point

A stagnation point is a point in a flow where the fluid is brought to rest (\(V=0\)) by a body in its path, such as the front tip of a blunt body or the open mouth of a tube facing into the flow.

Streamlines around a blunt-nosed body; the centre streamline ends at the stagnation point. A sphere and the tip of a probe each have a stagnation point too.

Stagnation pressure

Apply Bernoulli along the stagnation streamline from a point 1 upstream (pressure \(p_1\), speed \(V_1\)) to the stagnation point 2 (\(V_2=0\)), at the same height (\(z_1=z_2\)):

\[\frac{p_1}{\rho g}+\frac{V_1^2}{2g}=\frac{p_2}{\rho g}\ \Rightarrow\ p_2=p_1+\tfrac12\rho V_1^2\]
Results · dynamic and stagnation pressure \[\text{dynamic pressure}=\tfrac12\rho V_1^2\] \[\text{dynamic head}=\frac{V_1^2}{2g}\] \[\text{stagnation pressure}=p_1+\tfrac12\rho V_1^2\] \[\text{stagnation head}=\frac{p_1}{\rho g}+\frac{V_1^2}{2g}\]

In words: bringing a moving fluid to rest turns all its kinetic energy into pressure. The rise in pressure, \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\), is the dynamic pressure; static pressure + dynamic pressure is the stagnation pressure (or total pressure).

Reading the formula
\(p_1\)
static pressure: the pressure share the moving fluid has; what a side hole or piezometer feels.
\(\tfrac12\rho V_1^2\)
dynamic pressure: the speed share, turned into pressure when the fluid stops.
\(p_1+\tfrac12\rho V_1^2\)
stagnation (total) pressure: the whole budget at that height, felt at the stagnation point.
Ready answer · "Briefly explain the stagnation pressure" (May 2021 Q4, 3 marks; exam type F3.1)

(1) At a stagnation point the flow is brought to rest (\(V=0\)), for example at the nose of a body or the mouth of a Pitot tube. (2) Applying Bernoulli along the streamline (no height change), its kinetic energy is converted into pressure: \(p_0=p+\tfrac12\rho V^2\). (3) The stagnation (total) pressure is the static pressure plus the dynamic pressure \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\); it is the highest pressure on that streamline, and measuring \(p_0-p\) gives the speed \(V=\sqrt{2(p_0-p)/\rho}\).

Example

A car drives at 100 km/h through still air (\(\rho=1.23\u{kg/m^3}\)). How much higher than the surrounding air pressure is the pressure on the front of its bonnet (the stagnation point)?

Seen from the car, air arrives at \(V=100/3.6=27.78\u{m/s}\) and stops at the front. \(\tfrac12\rho V^2=\tfrac12(1.23)(27.78)^2=\boxed{475\u{Pa}}\), about 0.5% of atmospheric pressure.

The Pitot tube

What is this? (Pitot tube)

Hold your hand out of a car window, palm forward: the air that comes to rest against your palm pushes harder than the surrounding air pressure. A Pitot tube measures that extra push. Its mouth faces the flow and stops the fluid, so it feels the whole budget; a side hole or piezometer feels only the pressure share. The difference is the speed share, which gives the speed.

The Pitot tube measures the velocity of flow at any point in a pipe or channel. Principle: where the velocity drops to zero, the pressure rises, because kinetic energy is converted into pressure energy. In its simplest form it is a glass tube bent at a right angle, with its open end facing into the flow.

An open channel flowing at speed \(V\). A straight piezometer tube with its opening flush with the flow (point 1) rises to a height \(h\) above point 1: it shows the static pressure. A Pitot tube bent to face upstream has its mouth at the stagnation point 2 (\(V_2=0\)) and its water column rises to \(H\) above point 2: it shows the stagnation pressure.

the Pitot formula

1. Stagnation: \(p_2=p_1+\tfrac12\rho V_1^2\). Section 9, points 1 and 2 at the same height.

2. Each tube column is a hydrostatic column: \(p_1=\rho gh\), \(p_2=\rho gH\). The liquid in a tube is at rest; pressure = \(\rho g\) × height of liquid above the point.

3. Substitute: \(\rho gH=\rho gh+\tfrac12\rho V^2\).

4. Cancel \(\rho\) and solve: \(V=\sqrt{2g(H-h)}\).

Result · Pitot tube with a piezometer \[V=\sqrt{2g(H-h)}\qquad\text{(ideal)}\] \[V=C_v\sqrt{2g(H-h)}\qquad\text{(real fluid)}\]

In words: the extra height of the liquid in the Pitot tube above that in the piezometer is the velocity head \(V^2/2g\). \(C_v\), the coefficient of velocity, is found by experiment (calibration) and corrects for real-fluid effects; it is close to 1.

Pitot tubes are used to measure the flow velocity in laboratory channels, and a Pitot tube sits under the wing of an aircraft (it gives the plane's airspeed).

Example

In a channel the Pitot column stands 0.15 m higher than the piezometer column. Find the speed, ideal and with \(C_v=0.98\).

\(V=\sqrt{2(9.81)(0.15)}=1.72\u{m/s}\); with \(C_v\): \(0.98(1.716)=\boxed{1.68\u{m/s}}\). The dynamic pressure is \(\tfrac12(1000)(1.716)^2=1.47\u{kPa}\), which is \(\rho g(0.15)\) ✓.

Going further · the Pitot-static tube (May 2023 Q1(b), Dec 2021 Q1)

In a closed pipe or on an aircraft you cannot use open columns. A Pitot-static tube (Prandtl tube) combines both measurements in one probe: two concentric tubes, the inner one open at the nose (facing the flow: stagnation pressure \(p_0\)), the outer one with small holes around its side a few diameters behind the nose (flow passes them parallel: static pressure \(p\)). The two are connected to the two limbs of a differential manometer (U-tube with a heavier liquid \(\rho_m\)) or a pressure transducer:

\[p_0-p=(\rho_m-\rho)gH=\tfrac12\rho V^2\] \[V=\sqrt{\frac{2(\rho_m-\rho)gH}{\rho}}\]

Flow rate in a pipe (Dec 2021 Q1(b)): put the probe on the pipe centreline facing upstream (or traverse it across the pipe), read \(H\), compute \(V\), then \(Q\approx VA\) (strictly, use the average speed: the centreline speed is the largest). Sketch: pipe, probe nose at the stagnation point, side holes, two tubes to a U-tube, labelled \(H\).

Ready answer · Pitot problems (exam type F3.6)
  1. Do: Find the stagnation point (tube mouth facing the flow, nose of a body, centre of a plate struck by a jet): \(V=0\) there. That is where the whole speed share has been turned into pressure.
  2. Do: Bernoulli along the stagnation streamline: \(p_0-p=\tfrac12\rho V^2\) (add \(\rho g\Delta z\) if the points are at different heights). Both points lie on the one streamline that ends at the stagnation point, so they share one budget.
  3. Do: Get \(p_0-p\) from the instrument: column heights (\(\rho g(H-h)\)) or a manometer (\((\rho_m-\rho)gH\), counting each fluid's density separately, e.g. an oil layer). Each column or manometer turns a pressure into a height of its own liquid, so each liquid's density must be counted.
  4. Do: \(V=\sqrt{2(p_0-p)/\rho}\) (times \(C_v\) if given); \(Q=VA\). The difference \(p_0-p\) is exactly the speed share, so it gives \(V\) directly.

10Examples 3–4: Pitot tube and piezometer in pipes ~4 min

What is this?

Both examples read the budget with two tubes. A piezometer shows the pressure and height shares; a Pitot tube facing the flow shows the whole budget. So the excess column in the Pitot tube is the speed share alone, and everything else cancels: the depth of the pipe, unknown levels, even the size of a downstream pipe.

Worked example 3 · velocity measurement by a Pitot tube

A piezometer and a Pitot tube are tapped into a horizontal water pipe to measure static and stagnation (static + dynamic) pressures. The water column heights are: \(h_1=3\u{cm}\) (from the centreline up to the top of the pipe), \(h_2=7\u{cm}\) (pipe top to the level in the piezometer) and \(h_3=12\u{cm}\) (extra height of the column in the Pitot tube). Determine the velocity at the centre of the pipe.

  1. Point 1 on the centreline directly under the piezometer; point 2 at the tip of the Pitot tube, the stagnation point (\(V_2=0\), \(z_1=z_2\)). Steady flow, straight parallel streamlines.
  2. Gauge pressures from the columns: \(p_1=\rho g(h_1+h_2)\), \(p_2=\rho g(h_1+h_2+h_3)\). Each is \(\rho g\) × the height of water standing above the point.
  3. Bernoulli 1→2:
\[\frac{V_1^2}{2g}=\frac{p_2-p_1}{\rho g}=h_3\]

\[\begin{aligned}V_1&=\sqrt{2gh_3}\\&=\sqrt{2(9.81)(0.12)}\\&=\boxed{1.53\u{m/s}}\end{aligned}\]

Discussion: to find the velocity, all we need is the height of the excess fluid column in the Pitot tube (\(h_3\)); \(h_1\) and \(h_2\) cancel.

Worked example 4 · contraction with a piezometer and a Pitot tube

Water flows through the pipe contraction shown (a 0.1 m pipe narrowing to a small pipe of diameter \(D\)). A piezometer is connected to the 0.1 m pipe at section 1; a Pitot tube faces upstream in the small pipe at section 2. The Pitot level is 0.2 m above the piezometer level. Determine the flow rate as a function of the small diameter \(D\).

  1. Bernoulli from point 1 (centreline of the big pipe) to point 2 (tip of the Pitot tube, \(V_2=0\)), \(z_1=z_2\): \(\dfrac{p_1}{\rho g}+\dfrac{V_1^2}{2g}=\dfrac{p_2}{\rho g}\).
  2. Columns: \(p_1/(\rho g)=x\) (piezometer level above the centreline) and \(p_2/(\rho g)=0.2+x\). The unknown \(x\) is the same in both and cancels.
  3. So \(x+V_1^2/(2g)=0.2+x\), i.e. \(V_1=\sqrt{2(9.81)(0.2)}=1.98\u{m/s}\).
  4. \(Q=A_1V_1=\tfrac\pi4(0.1)^2(1.98)\).

\(\boxed{Q=0.0156\u{m^3/s}\ \text{for any }D}\)

Why \(D\) does not matter: the Pitot tube reads the total head, which is the same everywhere along the streamline (no friction), wherever the tube is placed. The piezometer reads the piezometric head at section 1. Their difference is the velocity head at section 1, and section 1's area is known.

You can now: find the speed in a pipe from the excess column in a Pitot tube; explain why the reading does not depend on where the Pitot tube sits along a frictionless streamline.

11Examples 5–6: manometers across an expansion and a riser ~4 min

What is this?

Here the budget is read with manometers instead of open tubes. In Example 5 the pipe widens, so the water slows and gets some pressure share back. In Example 6 the tube at point 2 stops the flow, so it feels the whole budget, while the tap at point 1 feels only pressure and height. The manometer just turns the pressure difference into a level difference; walking through it from one point to the other, as in H2, gives the link.

Worked example 5 · inverted manometer with a light liquid

Water flows steadily, with negligible viscous effects, through a pipe that widens from area 0.05 m² (section 1) to 0.07 m² (section 2). An inverted U-tube manometer connects the two sections; its top is filled with a liquid of density \(600\u{kg/m^3}\) (lighter than water). Determine the manometer reading \(H\) if the flowrate is \(0.5\u{m^3/s}\).

  1. Bernoulli, \(z_1=z_2\): \(p_2-p_1=\tfrac12\rho(V_1^2-V_2^2)\) (1). The pipe widens, so the flow slows and the pressure rises.
  2. Continuity: \(V_1=0.5/0.05=10\u{m/s}\), \(V_2=0.5/0.07=7.14\u{m/s}\).
  3. So \[\begin{aligned}p_2-p_1&=\tfrac12(999)(10^2-7.14^2)\\&=24.5\times10^3\u{N/m^2}\end{aligned}\] (2). This example uses \(\rho_\text{water}=999\).
the manometer equation

Start at point 1 (centreline). Go up a height \(h\) through water to the water–light-liquid interface in the left limb: subtract \(\gamma_w h\). Go up \(H\) through the light liquid to the level of the right-limb interface: subtract \(\gamma_m H\). The light liquid then fills the top bend across to the right limb. Go down through water a height \(h+H\) to point 2: add \(\gamma_w(h+H)\).

\[p_1-\gamma_wh-\gamma_mH+\gamma_w(h+H)=p_2\]

So \[\begin{aligned}p_2-p_1&=(\gamma_w-\gamma_m)H\\&=g(\rho_w-\rho_m)H\end{aligned}\] (3). \(h\) cancels: only the reading \(H\) matters.

Equate (2) and (3): \(24.5\times10^3=9.81(999-600)H\).

\(\boxed{H=6.26\u{m}}\) (with \(\rho_w=1000\): 6.24 m).

The water stands higher on the right (at 2), because the pressure is higher there. A light manometer liquid makes \(\rho_w-\rho_m\) small, which gives a large, easily read \(H\) for a small pressure difference (here, a very large one: a mercury U-tube would read only 0.2 m).

Worked example 6 · riser with an air-filled inverted U-tube (US units)

Water (inviscid, incompressible) flows steadily at \(Q=4\u{ft^3/s}\). It enters along a horizontal 1-ft-diameter pipe (point 1 on its centreline), turns up through a riser, and leaves to the left through a horizontal 0.5-ft-diameter pipe whose centreline is 3 ft higher. An inverted U-tube with air at the top connects a static tap on top of the 1-ft pipe to a tube whose open end faces the oncoming flow inside the upper pipe (point 2). Determine \(h\), the difference between the two water levels in the U-tube.

  1. Point 2 is a stagnation point: \(V_2=0\), \(z_2=3\u{ft}\); \(z_1=0\).
  2. \(V_1=\dfrac{Q}{A_1}=\dfrac{4}{\pi(1)^2/4}=5.09\u{ft/s}\).
  3. Bernoulli (pressure form, \(\gamma=\rho g\)):
\[p_1+\gamma z_1+\tfrac12\rho V_1^2=p_2+\gamma z_2+\tfrac12\rho V_2^2\] \[p_1+\tfrac12(1.94)(5.09)^2=p_2+62.4(3)\]

so \(p_1-p_2=162\u{lb/ft^2}\) (1). US units: water \(\rho=1.94\u{slugs/ft^3}\), \(\gamma=62.4\u{lb/ft^3}\).

the manometer equation

From point 1 go up through water to the right-limb water surface, a height \(\ell+3\u{ft}\): subtract \(\gamma(\ell+3)\). Across the air: no change. Go down the left limb through water a height \(h+\ell\) to point 2: add \(\gamma(h+\ell)\).

\[p_1-\gamma(\ell+3)+\gamma(h+\ell)=p_2\]

so \(p_1=p_2+187-62.4h\). \(62.4\times3=187\); \(\ell\) cancels.

Combine with (1): \(187-62.4h=162\), \(h=25/62.4\).

\(\boxed{h=0.400\u{ft}}\) (≈ 0.12 m); the left (stagnation) side stands higher.

rounding to 187 and 162 lb/ft² gives 0.400 ft; unrounded, \[\begin{aligned}h&=V_1^2/(2g)=5.09^2/(2\times32.2)\\&=0.403\u{ft}\end{aligned}\]

A shortcut: the tap at 1 reads the piezometric head at 1, the tube at 2 reads the total head (the same as at 1). So \(h\) is just the velocity head at section 1, \(V_1^2/2g\), and the 3 ft rise does not matter. This is Example 4's idea again.

You can now: write the manometer equation for an inverted U-tube with a light liquid or air; combine it with Bernoulli and continuity to find a reading; spot when a reading is just the velocity head.

12Tanks and jets: examples 7, 8 and 10 ~5 min

What is this?

All three are tank-and-jet budgets. A tank surface holds its share as height (plus any pressure from an oil layer on top), and a free jet takes it as speed. Example 7 balances inflow and outflow; Example 8 starts with a huge pressure share in almost still water and ends with it all as speed; Example 10 counts the oil layer as extra pressure on the water surface.

Worked example 7 · a tank kept at constant level

Water flows into a large tank at \(0.011\u{m^3/s}\). It leaves through 20 holes in the bottom of the tank, each producing a stream of 10 mm diameter. Determine the equilibrium height \(h\) of water for steady operation.

  1. Steady (level constant): inflow = outflow, \(Q_1=Q_2\) with \(Q_2=20A_2V_2=20\tfrac\pi4d^2V_2\).
  2. Bernoulli from the free surface (1) to a hole exit (2): \(p_1=p_2=0\), \(V_1\approx0\), \(z_1-z_2=h\), so \(V_2=\sqrt{2gh}\) (Torricelli).
  3. \(0.011=20\tfrac\pi4(0.01)^2\sqrt{2(9.81)h}\).
solving for \(h\)

Total hole area \(=20\times\tfrac\pi4(0.01)^2=1.571\times10^{-3}\u{m^2}\).

\(V_2=0.011/1.571\times10^{-3}=7.00\u{m/s}\). Each stream carries one twentieth of the flow.

\(h=V_2^2/(2g)=49.0/19.62=2.50\u{m}\).

\(\boxed{h=2.50\u{m}}\). If the level were lower, the outflow would be less than 0.011 m³/s and the tank would fill up until \(h=2.50\u{m}\); if higher, it would drain down to it.

Worked example 8 · water-jet cutter

Small-diameter, high-pressure liquid jets can cut materials. Neglecting viscous effects, estimate the pressure needed to produce a 0.10 mm diameter water jet with a speed of 700 m/s, and the flowrate.

  1. Point 1 inside the supply, where the water is almost still (\(V_1\approx0\)); point 2 in the jet (\(p_2=0\), \(V_2=700\u{m/s}\)); \(z_1\approx z_2\). In the wide supply the budget is almost all pressure; in the jet it is all speed.
  2. Bernoulli: \[\begin{aligned}p_1&=\tfrac12\rho V_2^2\\&=\tfrac12(999)(700)^2\\&=2.45\times10^{8}\u{Pa}\end{aligned}\]
  3. \[\begin{aligned}Q&=V_2A_2\\&=700\times\tfrac\pi4(10^{-4})^2\\&=5.50\times10^{-6}\u{m^3/s}\end{aligned}\]

\[\boxed{p_1=2.45\times10^{5}\u{kN/m^2}\ (245\u{MPa})}\] \[\boxed{Q=5.50\times10^{-6}\u{m^3/s}}\]

That is about 2400 atmospheres, yet only 5.5 mL of water per second: all the energy is concentrated in a tiny, very fast jet.

Worked example 10 · oil layer on water (= Dec 2018 Q1)

A large open tank contains a 4 m layer of oil (SG 0.7) floating on 2 m of water. A horizontal 0.2 m diameter pipe leaves the tank 1 m above the floor (1 m below the oil–water interface), then turns up and ends in a 0.1 m nozzle whose exit is level with the interface. The flow is steady and inviscid. Determine (a) the height \(h\) the water jet rises, (b) the water velocity in the pipe, (c) the pressure in the horizontal pipe.

Datum \(z=0\) at the oil–water interface (and nozzle exit). Points: 1 on the interface inside the tank (water just below the oil, at rest); 3 at the nozzle exit; 2 at the top of the jet; 4 in the horizontal pipe (\(z_4=-1\u{m}\)).

(a) Bernoulli 1→2: \(z_1=0\), \(V_1=V_2=0\) (the jet stops at its top), \(p_2=0\), \(z_2=h\), and \(p_1\) is the weight of the oil above: \(p_1=\gamma_\text{oil}(4\u{m})\), with \(\gamma_\text{oil}=0.7(9.80)=6.86\u{kN/m^3}\). The oil does not flow; its weight presses on the water like an extra pressure share.

\[\frac{p_1}{\gamma}=z_2\ \Rightarrow\ h=\frac{4(6.86)}{9.80}=\boxed{2.80\u{m}}\]

The oil acts like 2.8 m of extra water on top: \(0.7\times4=2.8\). The jet climbs back to that "equivalent water level".

(b) Jet speed at the nozzle (3): \[\begin{aligned}V_3&=\sqrt{2gh}\\&=\sqrt{2(9.81)(2.80)}=7.41\u{m/s}\end{aligned}\] Continuity, \(V_4A_4=V_3A_3\):

\[V_4=\frac{(0.1)^2}{(0.2)^2}V_3=\tfrac14(7.41)=\boxed{1.85\u{m/s}}\]

(c) Bernoulli 4→2 (\(z_4=-1\u{m}\), \(V_4=1.85\), \(p_2=0\), \(z_2=2.8\u{m}\), \(V_2=0\)):

\[\begin{aligned}&\frac{p_4}{\gamma}-1+\frac{1.85^2}{2(9.81)}=2.8\\&\Rightarrow\ \frac{p_4}{\gamma}=3.63\u{m}\end{aligned}\]

\(p_4=3.63(9.80)=\boxed{35.5\u{kPa}}\).

this uses \(\gamma_w=9.80\u{kN/m^3}\) alongside \(g=9.81\); 3.63 × 9.80 = 35.57, given as 35.5 kPa. With 9.81 throughout, \(p_4=35.6\u{kPa}\). Either is accepted.

May 2021 Q1 is the reverse problem: the jet speed (5 m/s) is given and the oil thickness \(H\) is found (0.394 m, with \(g=9.8\)); pipe speed 1.25 m/s, pipe pressure 31.3 kPa. Solved in the exam guide.

You can now: find the steady water level for a given inflow; find the supply pressure for a given jet speed; handle an oil layer through its pressure at the interface, and find a jet height, pipe speed and pipe pressure (Dec 2018 Q1, May 2021 Q1).

13Examples 9 and 11: wind tunnel and inverted manometer ~4 min

What is this?

In the wind tunnel, still outside air is drawn in and speeds up, so it pays with pressure: the test section is below atmospheric. At the car's nose the air stops again and gets that pressure back. In Example 11 a Pitot tube and a static tap are joined by an inverted U-tube holding a liquid a little lighter than water, which turns a small pressure difference into a large reading.

Worked example 9 · wind tunnel (US units)

Air is drawn into a wind tunnel for testing cars by a fan at the far end. (a) Determine the manometer reading \(h\) when the velocity in the test section is 60 mph. A U-tube below the test section has one limb connected to a wall tap (point 2) and the other open to the air; there is a 1-inch column of oil (SG 0.9) on the water in the open limb. (b) Determine the difference between the stagnation pressure on the front of the car (point 3) and the pressure in the test section.

(a) Point 1 in the still air outside the entrance (\(p_1=0\), \(V_1\approx0\)); point 2 in the test section (\(V_2=60\u{mph}=88\u{ft/s}\)); \(z_1=z_2\). Bernoulli:

\[\begin{aligned}p_2&=-\tfrac12\rho V_2^2=-\tfrac12(0.00238)(88)^2\\&=-9.22\u{lb/ft^2}\end{aligned}\]

The test-section pressure is below atmospheric: the air gained its speed by losing pressure.

the manometer with an oil layer

Start at the water surface in the tap limb (pressure \(p_2\); the air in the tube adds nothing). Go down through the water, across the bottom, and up the open limb to the water–oil interface, which is \(h\) below the tap-limb water level: net change \(+\gamma_wh\) (down by more than up). Then up through 1 in. \(=\tfrac1{12}\u{ft}\) of oil to the open surface: \(-\gamma_\text{oil}(\tfrac1{12})\). The open surface is at \(p=0\).

\[p_2+\gamma_wh-\gamma_\text{oil}\big(\tfrac1{12}\big)=0\]

\(\gamma_\text{oil}=0.9(62.4)=56.2\u{lb/ft^3}\). So \(-9.22+62.4h-56.2/12=0\).

\(\boxed{h=0.223\u{ft}}\) (2.67 in., 68 mm). The water is pulled up on the tunnel side; the oil column makes the reading larger than without it.

(b) Points 2 and 3 on the same streamline at the same height, \(V_3=0\) (stagnation point on the car):

\[p_3-p_2=\tfrac12\rho V_2^2=\boxed{9.22\u{lb/ft^2}}\]

Same question in SI: May 2019 Q2 (100 km/h, 25 mm of oil SG 0.9, air 1.23 kg/m³): \(h=70.9\u{mm}\), \(p_3-p_2=475\u{Pa}\). Solved in the exam guide.

Worked example 11 · inverted U-tube with oil

Determine the flowrate of water through the 0.08 m diameter pipe shown. An inverted U-tube connects a static tap on top of the pipe (point 1) with a Pitot tube facing upstream (tip at point 2). The top of the U-tube holds a manometer liquid of density \(\rho_m=900\u{kg/m^3}\); the reading is \(h=2.5\u{m}\).

  1. Bernoulli 1→2, \(z_1=z_2\), \(V_2=0\): \(V_1=\sqrt{2g\dfrac{p_2-p_1}{\gamma}}\).
  2. Manometer: from 1 up \(\ell\) through water, up \(h\) through the manometer liquid, across, then down \(\ell+h\) through water to 2:
\[p_1-\gamma\ell-\gamma_mh+\gamma(\ell+h)=p_2\]

so \(p_2-p_1=(\gamma-\gamma_m)h\).

\[V_1=\sqrt{2g\Big(1-\frac{\gamma_m}{\gamma}\Big)h}\] \[V_1=\Big[2(9.81)\Big(1-\frac{900}{999}\Big)(2.5)\Big]^{1/2}\]

\(V_1=2.20\u{m/s}\); \[\begin{aligned}Q&=A_1V_1=\tfrac\pi4(0.08)^2(2.20)\\&=\boxed{0.0111\u{m^3/s}}\end{aligned}\]

With \(\rho_m\) close to \(\rho\), a small pressure difference (here 2.4 kPa) gives a large, accurate reading (2.5 m).

You can now: find the suction in a wind-tunnel test section and the manometer reading with an oil layer; find the stagnation pressure rise on a body; find a flow rate from an inverted U-tube with oil.

14Examples 12–13: jets that spread into thin sheets ~4 min

What is this?

A jet that hits a plate or a cone spreads out into a thin sheet. The flow rate stays the same, so the sheet's speed is the flow rate divided by its exit area, and that area is a ring: circumference times thickness. Bernoulli then says how much height or pressure must be traded for that speed.

Worked example 12 · jet on a plate (= May 2019 Q3(a)–(c))

Water flows upward from a 0.01 m diameter pipe as a free jet and strikes a horizontal circular flat plate 0.2 m above the pipe exit. It spreads radially and leaves the edge of the 0.1 m diameter plate as a sheet 0.4 mm thick. The flow is axisymmetric. A tube through the centre of the plate rises to a water level \(H\) above the plate. Determine the flowrate and the manometer reading \(H\).

  1. Point 1 at the pipe exit (\(p_1=0\), \(z_1=0\)); point 2 where the sheet leaves the plate edge (\(p_2=0\), \(z_2=0.2\u{m}\)); both in free jets. Bernoulli:
\[\frac{V_1^2}{2g}=\frac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2\qquad(1)\]
  1. Continuity, \(A_1V_1=A_2V_2\). The sheet leaves through the side of a short cylinder, area \(\pi D_2h\) (circumference × thickness):
\[\begin{aligned}V_1&=\frac{\pi D_2h}{\frac\pi4D_1^2}V_2\\&=\frac{4(0.1)(4\times10^{-4})}{(0.01)^2}V_2=1.6V_2\end{aligned}\]
solving for \(V_2\), \(Q\) and \(H\)

(1): \((1.6V_2)^2=V_2^2+2(9.81)(0.2)\), so \(1.56V_2^2=3.924\) and \(V_2=1.59\u{m/s}\).

\[\begin{aligned}Q&=A_2V_2\\&=\pi(0.1)(4\times10^{-4})(1.59)\\&=2.00\times10^{-4}\u{m^3/s}\end{aligned}\]

For \(H\): point 0 is the stagnation point at the centre of the plate (\(V_0=0\), \(z_0=0.2\u{m}\)), on the centre streamline from point 1 (\(V_1=1.6(1.59)=2.54\u{m/s}\), \(p_1=0\)):

\[\begin{aligned}&\frac{V_1^2}{2g}=\frac{p_0}{\gamma}+z_0\\&\Rightarrow\ H=\frac{p_0}{\gamma}=\frac{V_1^2}{2g}-z_0\end{aligned}\]

\(H=\dfrac{2.54^2}{2(9.81)}-0.2=0.129\u{m}\). The tube shows the stagnation pressure at the plate as a water column.

\(\boxed{Q=2.00\times10^{-4}\u{m^3/s},\quad H=0.129\u{m}}\)

unrounded \(V_1=2.537\u{m/s}\) gives \(H=0.128\u{m}\); 0.129 m uses \(V_1\) rounded to 2.54.

The jet slows as it rises (from 2.54 to 1.59 m/s); the stagnation pressure at the plate holds up a 12.9 cm water column. May 2019 Q3(d) adds the impact force on the plate, which needs the momentum equation (chapter F4).

Worked example 13 · conical plug

A conical plug regulates the air flow from a 0.23 m diameter pipe. The air leaves the edge of the cone (radius 0.20 m) as a sheet of uniform thickness 0.02 m. Viscous effects are negligible; the flowrate is \(0.50\u{m^3/s}\). Determine the pressure within the pipe. (\(\rho_\text{air}=1.23\u{kg/m^3}\).)

  1. Bernoulli from point 1 in the pipe to point 2 in the free jet at the cone edge: \(z_1=z_2\), \(p_2=0\).
  2. \(V_1=\dfrac{0.5}{\frac\pi4(0.23)^2}=12.0\u{m/s}\).
  3. \[\begin{aligned}V_2&=\dfrac{Q}{2\pi Rh}\\&=\dfrac{0.5}{2\pi(0.2)(0.02)}\\&=19.9\u{m/s}\end{aligned}\] The sheet leaves through a ring: circumference \(2\pi R\) times thickness \(h\).
  4. \[\begin{aligned}p_1&=\tfrac12\rho(V_2^2-V_1^2)\\&=\tfrac12(1.23)(19.9^2-12.0^2)\end{aligned}\]

\(\boxed{p_1=155\u{N/m^2}}\) (gauge).

with unrounded speeds (12.03 and 19.89 m/s) the result is 154 N/m²; 155 uses the rounded speeds.

The air must speed up from 12 to 20 m/s to escape through the narrow ring, and only a small pipe pressure (0.15% of an atmosphere) is needed because air is so light.

You can now: find the exit area of a radial sheet; combine it with Bernoulli to get a flow rate, a stagnation reading on a plate, or a supply pressure.

16Common mistakes

✗Using absolute pressure at one point and gauge pressure at the other. ✓Use gauge throughout: free surfaces and free jets are \(p=0\). ✗Setting the speed in a pipe to zero, or forgetting it, when the pipe is not much wider than the jet. ✓Only a large tank's surface has \(V\approx0\); in a pipe, find \(V\) from continuity. ✗Dropping \(z\) in a vertical or inclined pipe with pressure gauges. ✓Keep \(z_2-z_1\) when the data are gauge pressures; it drops out only when a manometer reading is used. ✗Manometer: \(p_1-p_2=\rho_mgH\). ✓\((\rho_m-\rho)gH\): the flowing fluid also fills the limbs. For an inverted U-tube, \((\rho-\rho_m)gH\). ✗Dividing the air-flow pressure difference by \(\rho_\text{water}\) (or the manometer reading by \(\rho_\text{air}\)). ✓Manometer: liquid density gives \(\Delta p\). Bernoulli: the flowing gas's density turns \(\Delta p\) into \(V\). ✗Diameters in \(A=\pi D^2/4\) left in cm or mm; or area ratio taken as \(D_2/D_1\). ✓Metres; \(A_2/A_1=(D_2/D_1)^2\). ✗Applying Bernoulli across a pump or fan, or along a long rough pipe. ✓Shaft work and friction losses break the equation (F4 adds them). ✗Thinking a narrowing has higher pressure "because it is squeezed". ✓Narrow ⇒ faster ⇒ lower pressure (horizontal flow).

17Find the mistake

1 · A student's nozzle speed (May 2025 Q5 set-up)

"The piezometer on the 4.5 cm pipe stands 1.2 m high and the nozzle exit (3 cm) is at the same height and open to the air, so \(V_\text{nozzle}=\sqrt{2g(1.2)}=4.85\u{m/s}\)."

Hint

Is the water in the 4.5 cm pipe at rest?

Answer

The pipe water moves at \(V_\text{pipe}=V_\text{n}(3/4.5)^2=0.444V_\text{n}\), so its velocity head must be kept: \(1.2+\dfrac{V_\text{pipe}^2}{2g}=\dfrac{V_\text{n}^2}{2g}\), giving \(V_\text{n}=5.42\u{m/s}\), about 12% more. Torricelli's \(V=\sqrt{2gh}\) needs \(V\approx0\) at the start point (a large tank).

2 · A student's air duct (Dec 2024 B4(d))

"The manometer gives \(\Delta p=\rho_wg(0.01)=98.1\u{Pa}\), so \(V_1=\sqrt{2(98.1)/1000}=0.443\u{m/s}\)."

Hint

Which fluid is moving at \(V_1\)?

Answer

The water only measures \(\Delta p\); the moving fluid is air, so \(\tfrac12\rho_\text{air}V_1^2=98.1\) and \(V_1=\sqrt{2(98.1)/1.2}=12.8\u{m/s}\). A quick check: air flows in ducts at metres per second to tens of m/s; 0.44 m/s would barely move a 1 cm water column.

3 · A student's Venturi manometer (May 2024 Q1(a) set-up)

"The manometer gives \(p_1-p_2=(\rho_m-\rho)gH\). Bernoulli with point 2 a height \(z_2\) above 1: \(p_1-p_2=\tfrac12\rho(U_2^2-U_1^2)+\rho gz_2\). So \(\tfrac12\rho(U_2^2-U_1^2)=(\rho_m-\rho)gH-\rho gz_2\)."

Hint

Walk through the manometer from point 1 to point 2. Do the limbs have the same length of flowing fluid?

Answer

The right limb holds an extra \(z_2\) of flowing fluid, so the manometer gives the piezometric difference: \((p_1)-(p_2+\rho gz_2)=(\rho_m-\rho)gH\). The \(\rho gz_2\) terms then cancel with Bernoulli's, leaving \(\tfrac12\rho(U_2^2-U_1^2)=(\rho_m-\rho)gH\): the slope does not matter. The student subtracted the height twice.

18Concept checks

1 · A fountain

A fountain must throw water 5 m straight up. What speed must the water leave the nozzle with (no losses)?

Answer

All velocity head becomes elevation head: \(V=\sqrt{2g(5)}=9.90\u{m/s}\).

2 · Halving a pipe

Water flows from a pipe into one of half the diameter. By what factor does the speed change? The velocity head?

Answer

Area falls to \(\tfrac14\), so speed rises ×4 (continuity) and velocity head \(V^2/2g\) rises ×16. If the pipe is horizontal and frictionless, the pressure head falls by exactly that increase.

3 · Two tubes

A piezometer and a Pitot tube sit side by side in the same pipe. Which column stands higher, and what is the difference?

Answer

The Pitot tube (it reads the total head); the difference is the velocity head \(V^2/(2g)\). If the flow stops, the two levels become equal.

4 · Wide or narrow tank?

Two large open tanks, one twice as wide as the other, have the same water depth above identical holes. Which jet is faster?

Answer

Neither: Torricelli's \(V=\sqrt{2g\Delta z}\) depends only on the depth of the hole below the free surface. The wider tank just drains more slowly (its level falls slowly).

5 · Height in an air duct

Why are the \(\rho gz\) terms usually dropped for air ducts but never for water pipes?

Hint

Compare \(\rho gz\) for 1 m of air with the dynamic pressure of air at 20 m/s.

Answer

For air, 1 m of height is \(1.2(9.81)(1)\approx12\u{Pa}\), while \(\tfrac12(1.2)(20)^2=240\u{Pa}\): the height term is small. For water, 1 m is 9810 Pa, comparable with everything else.

6 · Across a fan

Can you apply Bernoulli between a point in front of a fan and a point behind it?

Answer

No: the fan does shaft work on the air, adding energy, so the total head is higher behind it. Apply Bernoulli separately on each side of the fan (as in Example 9, where both points lie upstream of the fan).

May 2021 Q43 markssimilar: Dec 2018 Q3(a), May 2022 Q4F3.1 Bernoulli concepts

Briefly explain the stagnation pressure.

Hint

Where is a stagnation point? Apply Bernoulli along the streamline that ends there.

Answer

At a stagnation point the fluid is brought to rest (\(V=0\)), e.g. the nose of a body or the mouth of a Pitot tube. Bernoulli along that streamline (same height) gives \(p_0=p+\tfrac12\rho V^2\): the kinetic energy is converted into pressure. The stagnation (total) pressure is static pressure plus dynamic pressure, the highest pressure on the streamline; measuring \(p_0-p\) gives the speed \(V=\sqrt{2(p_0-p)/\rho}\).
