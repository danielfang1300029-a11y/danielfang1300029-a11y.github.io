Property, state and process

0Where this chapter sits

This chapter describes what condition a substance is in (its state) and how it changes (a process). One picture runs through it: a pot of water under a heavy lid that slides up freely, so the pressure never changes, heated from below until the water has all turned to steam.

Conventions for this chapter
  • Capital letters are totals, small letters are "per kilogram". \(V\) (m³), \(U\), \(H\) (kJ) belong to the whole system; \(v=V/m\) (m³/kg), \(u=U/m\), \(h=H/m\) (kJ/kg) are specific values. The tables list only specific values; multiply by the mass \(m\) to get totals.
  • Subscripts: \(f\) = saturated liquid, \(g\) = saturated vapour, \(fg\) = "vapour minus liquid" (\(v_{fg}=v_g-v_f\)), \(@T\) = "read at this temperature" (\(h_{f@40^\circ\mathrm C}\)). States are numbered 1, 2, 3 … in the order the process visits them.
  • Changes are final minus initial: \(\Delta V=V_2-V_1\), \(\Delta H=H_2-H_1\). A negative \(\Delta V\) means the water shrank (condensation).
  • Heat added to the water is positive. For heating or cooling at constant pressure the heat equals the enthalpy change, \(Q=m(h_2-h_1)\); when a question asks for the energy "withdrawn" or the "decrease", quote the size (a positive number) and say "removed".
  • Units: tables give \(P\) in kPa (MPa in Tables A-6 and A-7: 1 MPa = 1000 kPa), \(T\) in °C, \(v\) in m³/kg, \(u,h\) in kJ/kg. Since \(1\u{kPa\cdot m^3}=1\u{kJ}\), \(P\times v\) in kPa × m³/kg comes out directly in kJ/kg.
  • Quality \(x\) is a fraction of mass (never of volume), between 0 (all liquid) and 1 (all vapour). It is only defined inside the saturation dome.

1Properties: extensive and intensive ~5 min

What is this?

Think of the pot of water as having a row of gauges: a thermometer, a pressure gauge, a scale for the mass, a mark for the volume. Each reading is a property. It describes the water as it is now, not how it got there. Put a wall down the middle of the pot: the mass and volume of each half are halved, but the thermometer and pressure gauge read the same in both halves. That is the difference between extensive and intensive.

Definition · property

A property is any characteristic of a system in equilibrium (settled, not changing) that you could measure or calculate: pressure \(P\), temperature \(T\), volume \(V\), mass \(m\), energy, density, colour … A property depends only on the condition the system is in now, not on how it got there ("independent of the path").

Analogy: your height is a property: it is the same number whether you grew fast or slowly. "How far you walked today" is not a property of you; it depends on the route. In T4 this is exactly the difference between energy (a property) and heat or work (which depend on the path).

Definitions · extensive and intensive
  • Extensive property: depends on the size (extent) of the system; doubling the system doubles it. Examples: mass, volume, total energy, any mass-dependent property.
  • Intensive property: does not depend on size. Examples: temperature, pressure, age, colour, density, any mass-independent property.

A quick test: take a system with \(m, V, T, P, \rho\) and imagine a partition cutting it into two equal halves. Each half has \(\tfrac12 m\) and \(\tfrac12 V\) (these halved: extensive) but the same \(T\), \(P\) and \(\rho\) (unchanged: intensive).

What is this? (specific volume)

"Specific" means "per kilogram". Divide the volume of the pot by the mass of water in it and you get the space each kilogram takes up. Halve the pot and both the volume and the mass halve, so the ratio stays the same. That is why a per-kilogram value behaves like temperature, not like mass.

Result · specific quantities are intensive \[v=\frac{V}{m}\ \ (\mathrm{m^3/kg}),\qquad \rho=\frac{m}{V}\ \ (\mathrm{kg/m^3})\]

In words: an extensive property divided by the mass is intensive. Specific volume = volume per kilogram; density = kilograms per cubic metre; one is the reciprocal of the other.

The same trick gives \(u=U/m\) and \(h=H/m\): every column of the steam tables is an intensive, per-kilogram value. That is why the tables can be printed once and used for any amount of water.

Example

A 2 L bottle holds 2 kg of water at 20 °C. Then \(v=0.002/2=0.001\u{m^3/kg}\) and \(\rho=1000\u{kg/m^3}\). Pour half into another bottle: each bottle now has 1 kg and 1 L (extensive values halved) but still \(v=0.001\u{m^3/kg}\), \(\rho=1000\u{kg/m^3}\) and 20 °C (intensive values unchanged).

Measurable properties

\(P\), \(V\), \(T\) and \(m\) matter most because they are easy to measure: \(P\) and \(T\) are easily measured intensive properties, \(V\) and \(m\) easily measured extensive ones. Most other properties (internal energy, enthalpy, entropy) cannot be measured directly; they are calculated from \(P\), \(V\), \(T\), \(m\) through relations between properties. The ideal-gas law is one such relation:

\[Pv=RT\]

In words: pressure × specific volume = gas constant × absolute temperature. Know two of \(P,v,T\) and the third follows.

Note that \(P\) and \(T\) are not always independent of one another. The important case is boiling: water at 1 atm boils at 100 °C and at no other temperature, so while it boils, fixing \(P\) also fixes \(T\). This matters for the state principle (next section).

Watch · exam MC (Dec 2022 Q1, May 2022 Q1)

You can now: tell an extensive property from an intensive one with the "cut it in half" test; turn a total into a specific value by dividing by the mass; and name the easily measured properties \(P\), \(V\), \(T\) and \(m\).

2State, the state principle and equilibrium ~7 min

What is this?

The state is the full set of gauge readings at a moment when nothing is changing. The state principle says you do not need every gauge. For a simple substance such as water, two independent readings are enough, and every other value can be looked up. The catch is "independent": while the pot is boiling, the thermometer reading follows from the pressure gauge, so those two count as one.

Definition · state

The state of a system is its condition, described by the set of values of all its properties when nothing is changing. At a given state every property has one fixed value. If any one property changes, the system has moved to a different state:

\[\underbrace{(P_1,v_1,T_1,e_1)}_{\text{state 1}}\ \longrightarrow\ \underbrace{(P_2,v_2,T_2,e_2)}_{\text{state 2}}\]

(\(e\) = specific energy.)

Law · the state principle

The thermodynamic state of a simple system is completely specified by two independent, intensive properties.

In words: for a simple system you need to know just two suitable numbers (such as \(P\) and \(T\), or \(P\) and \(v\)); every other property then has a definite value that can be calculated or read from a table.

The words in the principle each matter:

  • Simple system: one with no effects from external fields: no electrical, magnetic or gravitational effects and no surface tension. A pot of water or a gas in a cylinder counts; a magnetised or charged material, or a tiny droplet where surface tension matters, does not.
  • Intensive: size-free values, so the principle fixes "what kind of condition" without saying how much there is. To get totals you also need the size, usually the mass: \(V=mv\).
  • Independent: one must not be determined by the other. During boiling \(P\) and \(T\) are tied together (\(T=T_\text{sat}(P)\)), so "1 atm and 100 °C" is really only one piece of information: it does not tell you how much has boiled. You then need a second property such as \(v\) or the quality \(x\).

Analogy: a point on a map is fixed by two coordinates (latitude and longitude). "Latitude 22.3° N and 'somewhere in Hong Kong'" is not enough, because the second fact is not independent of the first. The state principle says a simple substance has a two-coordinate "map" of states.

Ready answer · state principle (exam type T2.1, Dec 2024 Q3, Dec 2022 Q3, May 2022 Q3)
  1. Statement: the state of a simple system (no electric, magnetic, gravitational or surface-tension effects) is completely specified by two independent intensive properties.
  2. Relation to the state: once those two are known, the state is fixed and every other property (\(v\), \(u\), \(h\), \(s\), …) has a definite value: it can be calculated or read from property tables. The state changes as soon as one property changes.
  3. "Independent" example: \(T\) and \(P\) are independent for a single-phase substance (superheated steam at 1 MPa and 300 °C is fixed). In a saturated liquid-vapour mixture they are not (\(T=T_\text{sat}\) at that \(P\)), so a second property such as \(v\) or quality \(x\) is needed.

Equilibrium

What is this? (equilibrium)

Put the lid on the pot, turn off the heat and wait. Hot and cold spots even out, the pressure evens out, the amounts of liquid and steam stop changing, and nothing reacts. Only when all four have settled is there one temperature and one pressure to read, so only then does the water have a state.

Definition · thermodynamic equilibrium

A system is in thermodynamic equilibrium when it is in all four of these at once:

  • thermal equilibrium: uniform temperature (no part hotter than another);
  • mechanical equilibrium: uniform pressure (no unbalanced push, nothing about to move);
  • phase equilibrium: the mass of each phase (e.g. ice and liquid water) stays constant;
  • chemical equilibrium: the chemical composition does not change (no reactions going on).

Two pictures help. A closed box with temperatures 20, 23, 30, 35, 40, 42 °C in different places is not in thermal equilibrium; after a while every point reads 32 °C and it is. A block of iron at 150 °C touching copper at 20 °C ends with both at 60 °C.

What is this? (zeroth law)

Dip a thermometer in the pot, then in a cup of tea. If it reads the same in both, the pot and the tea are at the same temperature, even though they never touched. The zeroth law is the rule that makes this comparison valid.

Law · zeroth law of thermodynamics

If two bodies are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body, they are also in thermal equilibrium with each other:

\[T_A=T_B\ \text{and}\ T_B=T_C\ \Longrightarrow\ T_A=T_C\]

In words: if A and B are at the same temperature, and B and C are at the same temperature, then A and C are at the same temperature. It is why a thermometer (body B) works: it can compare A and C without them ever touching.

Watch · exam MC (Dec 2023 Q2)

"Dynamic equilibrium" is not one of the four. Thermodynamic equilibrium = thermal + mechanical + phase + chemical.

You can now: state the state principle and explain its key words (simple, independent, intensive); explain why \(T\) and \(P\) alone do not fix a boiling mixture; list the four kinds of thermodynamic equilibrium; and state the zeroth law.

3Processes, paths and property diagrams ~7 min

What is this?

A process is the trip the water takes from one state to another, such as heating the pot from cold water to steam. The start and finish are the two end states; the path is the route in between. Two different routes can join the same start and finish. A cycle is a round trip that comes back to where it started.

Definitions · process, path, cycle
  • A process is any change of a system from one equilibrium state to another.
  • The path of the process is the series of states the system passes through on the way.
  • A cycle is a process (or chain of processes) whose final state is the same as its initial state.

Picture a graph of thermodynamic state against a process variable. Three different processes (1, 2, 3) all start at state A and finish at state B but follow different curves: same end states, different paths. The second picture is a closed loop that leaves the initial state and returns to it: a cycle.

Ready answer · what defines a process (Dec 2022 Q2)

To define a process you need the initial state, the final state and the process path. Time and "energy input" are not part of the definition.

Property diagrams

What is this? (property diagram)

Plot two gauge readings against each other, pressure against volume say. Each state of the gas is then one dot. As the gas changes, the dot moves and draws a line: that line is the process path.

Example: a gas in a piston–cylinder device (a cylinder closed by a sliding piston, like a bicycle pump with its outlet blocked) is compressed. The piston moves in from position (1) to position (2); the gas goes from \((P_1,V_1)\) to \((P_2,V_2)\), with \(V_2\lt V_1\) and \(P_2\gt P_1\). Plotting pressure against volume gives a property diagram: each point is a state, the curve between them is the process path, and an arrow shows the direction.

Definition · property diagram

A property diagram is a graph whose axes are two properties (e.g. \(P\)–\(V\), \(T\)–\(v\), \(P\)–\(v\), \(P\)–\(T\)). By the state principle two independent intensive properties fix the state, so each point is one state and a process is a line.

Ready answer · sketching iso-processes (exam type T2.2, Dec 2024 Q4)
  1. Do: Ask "which axis is the constant property?" A property that is held fixed cannot change, so the dot cannot move along that axis.
  2. Do: A process holding an axis property constant is a straight line perpendicular to that axis: The dot is free to move only along the other axis, so the path is a line straight across.
    • isobaric on \(P\)–\(V\): horizontal line at that \(P\) (arrow to the right for expansion, left for compression);
    • isothermal on \(P\)–\(T\): vertical line at that \(T\) (arrow up if pressure rises, down if it falls);
    • isochoric on \(P\)–\(V\): vertical line; isothermal on \(T\)–\(v\): horizontal line; isobaric on \(P\)–\(T\): horizontal line.
  3. Do: Label the axes, the initial and final states, and the arrow. The line alone does not say where the trip starts or which way it goes; the arrow does.

Quasi-equilibrium processes

What is this? (quasi-equilibrium)

Squeeze or heat the gas gently and it has time to even out at every moment, so it always has one pressure and one temperature to read. Do it violently and different parts are out of step, so there is no single reading and no dot to plot.

Definition · quasi-equilibrium (quasi-static) process

A process that stays infinitesimally close to equilibrium at every moment. It is slow enough that the system can even itself out internally, so that properties in one part never change faster than in other parts.

Push a piston into gas quickly and the molecules pile up just under the piston, like a traffic jam of particles: the pressure is not uniform, the gas is not in equilibrium, and no single \(P\) describes it. Push slowly and the molecules stay approximately uniformly spread: at each instant the gas has a single \(P\) and \(T\). Only a quasi-equilibrium process can be drawn as a continuous line on a property diagram, because only then is each point on the path a real equilibrium state.

Going further · why engineers like quasi-equilibrium

Real processes are never perfectly slow, but quasi-equilibrium processes are easy to calculate and are the best a device can do (they deliver the most work in an expansion and need the least in a compression). The past papers use the phrase "quasi-static" for the same idea (Dec 2024 Q7).

You can now: define process, path and cycle; say what defines a process (initial state, final state, path); draw an iso-process as a horizontal or vertical line on the right diagram; and explain why only a quasi-equilibrium process can be drawn as a line.

4Phases and pure substances ~4 min

What is this?

A phase is one uniform kind of stuff: all ice, all liquid water, or all steam. A pure substance has the same chemical make-up in every bit of it, even if some is liquid and some is steam. The water and the steam in the pot are both H₂O, so the pot stays a pure substance all the way through boiling, and one set of steam tables describes it.

Definition · phase

A phase is a quantity of matter that is homogeneous (the same all through) in both chemical composition and physical structure: all solid, or all liquid, or all vapour (gas). A system can contain one phase or more. Water has three: solid (ice), liquid (water) and vapour (steam).

Definition · pure substance

A pure substance has a homogeneous and invariable chemical composition: the same chemical make-up everywhere, and it does not change. It may exist in more than one phase.

Examples:

  1. water (in any of its solid, liquid and vapour phases);
  2. a mixture of liquid water and water vapour (both are H₂O);
  3. carbon dioxide, CO₂;
  4. nitrogen, N₂;
  5. mixtures of gases such as air, as long as there is no change of phase.

More cases: pure gas N₂ (pure); gaseous air (pure); vapour over liquid water (pure); oil floating on water (not pure: two different chemicals, and they stay separate); liquid air under vapour air (not pure).

Why is liquid air with gaseous air not pure, although gaseous air is? Air is mainly nitrogen and oxygen, which condense at different temperatures (oxygen at −183 °C, nitrogen at −196 °C at 1 atm). When air is partly liquefied, the liquid is richer in oxygen than the gas above it, so the composition is not the same everywhere. Liquid water and steam are both exactly H₂O, so that mixture is pure.

Ready answer · is it a pure substance? (May 2022 Q2)

Pure: liquid nitrogen; a mixture of liquid water and water vapour. Not pure: salt water (composition can vary, salt and water are different chemicals); a mixture of liquefied and gaseous air (the two phases have different compositions); water with cooking oil (two different substances that do not mix).

Going further · salt water

Salt water is the same throughout once stirred, so it looks homogeneous, but its composition is not invariable: the salt fraction can be anything, and boiling changes it (the vapour is pure water, leaving saltier liquid behind). It therefore does not count as a pure substance, and the steam tables for water do not apply to it.

You can now: define a phase and a pure substance; and decide whether a mixture (water and steam, air, liquid and gaseous air, oil and water, salt water) is pure, with the reason.

5Heating water at constant pressure ~6 min

What is this?

This is the picture for the rest of the chapter: a pot of water under a heavy lid that slides up freely, so the pressure inside never changes. Turn on the heat. First the cold water warms and swells very slightly. Then it reaches its boiling point and starts making steam, and the temperature stops rising until the last drop has gone. Then the steam itself gets hotter and keeps expanding.

Here is the experiment that the rest of the chapter is built on. Water at 20 °C and 1 atm sits in a piston–cylinder device. The piston is free to slide and carries a fixed weight, so the pressure inside stays at 1 atm whatever the volume. Heat is added from below. What happens to the temperature \(T\) and the specific volume \(v\)? It passes through five states.

Definitions · the five states
  • State 1, compressed liquid (also called subcooled liquid): a liquid that is not about to vaporize (20 °C at 1 atm). Process 1–2: \(T\) and \(v\) both increase (water expands slightly when heated).
  • State 2, saturated liquid: a liquid that is about to vaporize; the water has reached the temperature at which it begins to boil, the saturation temperature (100 °C at 1 atm). Any more heat makes vapour.
  • State 3, saturated liquid-vapour mixture: liquid and vapour coexist in equilibrium. During process 2–3–4 both temperature and pressure stay constant (100 °C, 1 atm) while the heat turns liquid into vapour; \(v\) increases enormously because vapour takes up about 1600 times the space of the liquid.
  • State 4, saturated vapour: vaporization is complete; only vapour is left, and it is a vapour that is about to condense (remove any heat and drops form).
  • State 5, superheated vapour: continue heating at constant pressure and the temperature rises above the saturation temperature (for example 300 °C) and the volume keeps increasing. This vapour is not about to condense.
What is this? (saturation temperature)

The saturation temperature is the boiling point at the pressure the lid holds. Put a heavier weight on the lid and the water boils at a higher temperature, but for any one weight there is exactly one boiling point.

Definitions · saturation temperature and pressure

The saturation pressure (or saturation temperature) is the pressure (temperature) at which the liquid and vapour phases coexist in equilibrium. Every point on the flat line 2–4 has the same temperature and pressure, \(T_\text{sat}\) and \(P_\text{sat}\). In everyday words, \(T_\text{sat}\) is the boiling point at that pressure.

Plot the five states on axes of temperature against specific volume (a T-v diagram): the path rises steeply from 1 to 2 (compressed liquid), runs flat from 2 through 3 to 4 (saturated mixture, constant \(T\)), then rises again from 4 to 5 (superheated vapour). States 2, 3 and 4 are saturation states.

Schematic T-v diagram of states 1–5, at 1 atm. The horizontal axis is stretched so the liquid states are visible: in reality \(v_1\approx0.001\u{m^3/kg}\) and \(v_4\approx1.67\u{m^3/kg}\).

A molecular picture helps. In the liquid, molecules are packed closely and slide past each other; in the vapour they are far apart and fly about freely. The heat added along 2–4 goes into pulling the molecules apart (breaking the attractions between them), not into making them faster, which is why the temperature does not rise while water boils.

Analogy: a kettle of boiling water on full power stays at 100 °C until it boils dry. All the extra heat is "spent" on turning liquid into steam. Only after the last drop has gone can the steam get hotter.

Naming states with subscripts

A subscript names the state a property belongs to: \(X_A\) is property \(X\) at state A; \(X_f\) at the saturated liquid state (f from the German flüssig, "liquid"); \(X_g\) at the saturated vapour state (g for gas); \(X_{fg}=X_g-X_f\) the difference between them. So at 1 atm, \(v_f\) is the specific volume at state 2 and \(v_g\) at state 4.

Watch

"Saturated" does not mean "a mixture". A saturated liquid (\(x=0\)) and a saturated vapour (\(x=1\)) are both saturation states, at the two ends of the flat line. "Compressed liquid", "saturated liquid", "saturated liquid-vapour mixture", "saturated vapour" and "superheated vapour" are the five region names that the exam asks you to label.

You can now: name the five states of water heated at constant pressure and say what happens to \(T\) and \(v\) in each stage; explain why the temperature stays flat while water boils; and use the subscripts \(f\), \(g\) and \(fg\).

6The saturation dome on the T-v diagram ~6 min

What is this?

Repeat the pot experiment with a heavier lid, then a heavier one again. Each time the water boils at a higher temperature, and the boiling stretch is shorter. Mark where every boiling stretch starts and ends and join the marks: they trace a dome. Under the dome the pot holds liquid and steam together; to its left there is only liquid; to its right only steam.

Now repeat the heating experiment at other constant pressures: 0.01, 0.1, 1, 8, 15, 22.09 and 25 MPa. Each gives the same shape of curve, but:

  • the higher the pressure, the higher the boiling temperature (the flat part sits higher): 99.61 °C at 100 kPa, 179.88 °C at 1000 kPa;
  • the higher the pressure, the shorter the flat part: the liquid at boiling is a little less dense (\(v_f\) grows) and the vapour much denser (\(v_g\) shrinks);
  • at the critical pressure the flat part shrinks to a single point, and above it (25 MPa) there is no flat part at all.
Definitions · saturation lines, critical point, dome
  • Joining all the saturated liquid states (the left ends of the flat lines) gives the saturated liquid line.
  • Joining all the saturated vapour states (the right ends) gives the saturated vapour line.
  • The two lines meet at the critical point. For water: \(T_\text{cr}=373.95\) °C, \(P_\text{cr}=22.064\u{MPa}\), \(v_\text{cr}=0.003106\u{m^3/kg}\).
  • Together they form the steam dome (saturation dome). The region under the dome is called the saturated liquid-vapour mixture region, the wet region, the two-phase region or just the saturation region.

The dome divides the T-v diagram into three regions: compressed liquid to the left of the saturated liquid line, saturated liquid-vapour mixture under the dome, superheated vapour to the right of the saturated vapour line.

The steam tables give the critical point as 373.95 °C, 22.064 MPa, \(v=0.003106\u{m^3/kg}\). Use these values (older sources round the critical pressure to 22.09 MPa).

Above the critical point

What is this? (supercritical fluid)

Make the lid heavy enough (above the critical pressure) and the pot never boils at all. There is no moment when bubbles appear and the temperature pauses: the water just thins out smoothly, from liquid-like to steam-like.

At temperatures and pressures above the critical point, the change from liquid to vapour is no longer distinct: there is no boiling, no bubbles and no flat part. The substance is a supercritical fluid. It is neither a liquid nor a vapour but shares characteristics of both: it can be as dense as a liquid yet fills its container like a gas.

A classic demonstration uses carbon dioxide in a sealed, thick-walled window cell at 28.8 °C. A line across the cell marks where the liquid CO₂ starts: below it liquid, above it vapour.

As the cell warms past CO₂'s critical temperature (31.0 °C, at 7.38 MPa) the meniscus between liquid and vapour fades and disappears, and the cell holds one uniform supercritical fluid; on cooling, a fog of droplets forms and the meniscus reappears.

Going further · supercritical fluids in use

Supercritical CO₂ dissolves caffeine and is used to decaffeinate coffee; supercritical water is used in the most efficient coal power stations (boilers running above 22.064 MPa, where water turns into steam without ever boiling).

You can now: draw the T-v dome with the saturated liquid line, saturated vapour line and critical point; name the three regions; quote the critical point of water; and describe a supercritical fluid.

7The P-v diagram; vaporization versus condensation ~6 min

What is this?

You can make the pot boil without heating it. Stand it in a bath that holds its temperature fixed and let the lid rise slowly so the pressure falls. When the pressure reaches the saturation pressure for that temperature, the water starts to boil. Condensation is the same journey run backwards: take heat away, or push the pressure up.

Water can also be made to boil without heating it: keep the temperature constant and reduce the pressure. Start with compressed liquid at high pressure and let the piston rise slowly while the cylinder sits in a bath that holds \(T\) fixed. The pressure falls; when it reaches \(P_\text{sat}\) at that temperature the water starts to boil; it stays at \(P_\text{sat}\) until it is all vapour; then the pressure falls further and the vapour becomes superheated.

Plotted on a P-v diagram (pressure against specific volume) the isotherm falls steeply through the compressed-liquid region, runs horizontally across the dome at \(P_\text{sat}\), then falls gently through the superheated region. The dome has the same shape and the same names as on the T-v diagram, but the constant-temperature lines now go downwards from left to right; a higher temperature (\(T_2\gt T_1\)) gives a higher line.

A second demonstration puts a beaker of water under a bell jar. Room-temperature water in a vacuum chamber starts to boil vigorously as the air is pumped out, once the pressure falls to about 2–3 kPa (the saturation pressure at 20–25 °C: 2.3392 kPa at 20 °C, Table A-4).

Going further · boiling point and altitude

On a high mountain the air pressure is lower, so water boils below 100 °C (about 86 °C at 60 kPa, on the summit of a 4000 m mountain): food cooks slowly. A pressure cooker does the opposite: at about 2 atm water boils at about 120 °C (Table A-4: \(P_\text{sat}=198.67\u{kPa}\) at 120 °C), so food cooks faster. Past papers ask both (May 2023 Q5 is a pressure cooker).

Vaporization versus condensation

Running each process backwards turns vapour into liquid (condensation). The key point: the process path for vaporization and condensation of water is the same; only the direction of the path differs.

8Enthalpy and the work PV ~5 min

What is this?

The steam in the pot stores energy in its molecules: its internal energy. But to exist at all it has had to push the heavy lid up to make room for itself, and that took work too. Enthalpy adds the two: the energy inside plus the energy spent making room. When the pot is heated at constant pressure, the heat you supply pays for both, so it equals the rise in enthalpy.

The property tables (Tables A-4 to A-8) give, besides \(T\), \(P\) and \(v\), the specific internal energy \(u\), specific enthalpy \(h\) and specific entropy \(s\) of water. Internal energy is the energy stored in the molecules (their motion and the attractions between them); entropy is introduced in T4. Enthalpy is new here.

Definition · enthalpy \[H=U+PV,\qquad h=u+Pv\]

In words: enthalpy = internal energy + pressure × volume. Per kilogram: specific enthalpy = specific internal energy + pressure × specific volume. Unit of \(h\): kJ/kg.

Reading the formula
\(u\)
energy stored in the molecules of one kilogram (their motion and the pull between them).
\(P\)
the push of the lid on each square metre of water.
\(v\)
the room one kilogram takes up.
\(Pv\)
the work done pushing the lid back to make that room, starting from nothing (see below).
\(h\)
the full energy cost of one kilogram being there: what it holds plus the room it has made.

Enthalpy is a convenient grouping of the internal energy and the work needed to make room for the system. It is useful for calculating the energy that must be added or withdrawn to change a system from one state to another. The \(PV\) part comes from work.

Work in terms of \(P\) and \(V\)

Work done by a constant force \(F\) moving its point of action a distance \(s\) along the force is \(W=Fs\). For gas pushing a piston of area \(A\) a distance \(s\): the force is \(F=PA\) and the swept volume is \(\Delta V=As\), so

\[W=Fs=\frac{F}{A}(As)=P\,\Delta V\]

In words: at constant pressure, the work done in expanding = pressure × increase in volume.

So \(PV\) is the work needed to push the surroundings back to make room for a system of volume \(V\), growing it from nothing at pressure \(P\). Enthalpy = the energy inside the system (\(U\)) + the energy spent making room for it (\(PV\)).

Result · heat at constant pressure equals the enthalpy change \[Q=\Delta H=m(h_2-h_1)\quad(\text{constant }P)\]

In words: when water is heated or cooled at constant pressure, the heat added (or removed) equals mass × change in specific enthalpy. Part of the heat raises \(U\); the rest is the work \(P\Delta V\) done pushing the piston up, and \(h\) counts both.

Reading the formula
\(Q\)
heat put into the pot (positive) or taken out (negative).
\(h_2-h_1\)
change per kilogram in stored energy plus room made, from start to finish.
\(m\)
turns the per-kilogram change into the change for the whole pot.
constant \(P\)
the lid weight never changes; without that the work is not \(P\Delta V\) and the formula fails.
why \(Q=\Delta H\) at constant pressure (uses T4's first law)

1. Energy conservation for the water (first law, chapter T4): heat in = increase in internal energy + work done by the water, \(Q=\Delta U+W\).

2. At constant pressure the work is \(W=P\Delta V=P(V_2-V_1)\).

3. So

\[\begin{aligned}Q&=(U_2-U_1)+P(V_2-V_1)\\&=(U_2+PV_2)-(U_1+PV_1)\end{aligned}\]

\(P\) is the same at both states, so it can be split between them

4. Each bracket is \(H=U+PV\), so \(Q=H_2-H_1=m(h_2-h_1)\). This is why Examples 3 and 4 find the energy transferred as \(\Delta H\).

Example · checking \(h=u+Pv\) in the table

Saturated vapour at 100 kPa (Table A-5): \(u_g=2505.6\u{kJ/kg}\), \(v_g=1.6941\u{m^3/kg}\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}h_g&=u_g+Pv_g\\&=2505.6+100(1.6941)\\&=2675.0\u{kJ/kg}\end{aligned}\]

which is exactly the table's \(h_g\). (kPa × m³/kg = kJ/kg.) Of the 2257.5 kJ/kg needed to boil 1 kg at 100 kPa, \(P v_{fg}=100(1.6931)=169.3\u{kJ}\) is the work of pushing back the atmosphere; the rest raises \(u\).

The reverse use is handy too: if a table does not list \(u\), get it from \(u=h-Pv\).

You can now: define enthalpy as \(h=u+Pv\) and explain the \(Pv\) part as the work of making room; derive \(W=P\Delta V\); use \(Q=m(h_2-h_1)\) for heating or cooling at constant pressure; and check \(h=u+Pv\) against a table row.

9Saturated water tables A-4 and A-5 ~7 min

What is this?

The saturated tables are a record of the boiling stretch for every lid weight. Each row is one pressure (or one boiling temperature) and lists the two ends of the flat line: water just about to boil (\(f\)) and steam that has just finished boiling (\(g\)). Because boiling ties the temperature to the pressure, the same rows can be listed by either one, which is why there are two tables.

Under the dome \(T\) and \(P\) are tied together (each pressure has one boiling temperature), so the saturation data can be listed against either one:

  • Table A-4, the temperature table: \(T\) is the independent property (first column, °C); the second column is \(P_\text{sat}\) at that temperature (kPa).
  • Table A-5, the pressure table: \(P\) is the independent property (first column, kPa); the second column is \(T_\text{sat}\) at that pressure (°C).

The two contain the same information, and often only one is given. Both end at the critical point: Table A-4's last row is 373.95 °C, Table A-5's last row is 22 064 kPa. The same tables are appended to the exam papers.

The "fg" columns and the latent heat

What is this? (fg columns)

Going from the \(f\) end of the flat line to the \(g\) end means boiling one kilogram completely. The \(fg\) columns are the change over that trip. \(h_{fg}\) is the heat the pot must receive to turn one kilogram of just-boiling water into steam.

Result · "fg" differences \[\begin{gathered}u_{fg}=u_g-u_f,\quad h_{fg}=h_g-h_f,\\ s_{fg}=s_g-s_f\end{gathered}\]

In words: an "fg" value is the saturated-vapour value minus the saturated-liquid value at the same temperature (or pressure): the change from one end of the flat line to the other.

Definition · enthalpy of vaporization \(h_{fg}\)

\(h_{fg}\) is the enthalpy of vaporization (or latent heat of vaporization): the energy needed to vaporize 1 kg of saturated liquid at a given temperature or pressure. It decreases as the temperature or pressure increases, and becomes zero at the critical point (where liquid and vapour become identical).

What is this?

11Quality and the lever rule ~6 min

What is this?

Stop the pot halfway through boiling. Some water is still liquid at the bottom and the rest is steam above it, both at the boiling temperature. The quality is the fraction of the mass already turned to steam: 0 when boiling has just begun, 1 when the last drop has gone. Temperature and pressure do not change during boiling, so the quality is the extra reading that says how far through the boiling the pot is.

At state 3 (a mixture) \(T\) and \(P\) are both fixed at saturation values, but that does not say how much has boiled. To locate state 3 on the flat line we need one more property.

Definition · quality (dryness fraction) \(x\) \[x=\frac{m_\text{saturated vapour}}{m_\text{total}}=\frac{m_g}{m_f+m_g}\]

In words: quality = mass of vapour ÷ total mass of liquid plus vapour. \(x=0\) for saturated liquid, \(x=1\) for saturated vapour, \(0\le x\le 1\) in between. (Quality is sometimes loosely called "wetness"; strictly the wetness is \(1-x\), the liquid fraction.)

Picture a cylinder containing saturated vapour (\(v_g\)) on top of saturated liquid (\(v_f\)). On average it behaves like one uniform "saturated liquid-vapour mixture" with an average specific volume \(v_\text{av}\).

Result · properties of a mixture \[v=(1-x)v_f+x\,v_g=v_f+x\,v_{fg}\] \[y=y_f+x\,y_{fg},\qquad y=v,\ u,\ h\ \text{or}\ s\]

In words: a mixture's specific property = liquid value + quality × (vapour value − liquid value). It is a mass-weighted average: a fraction \(1-x\) of the mass has \(y_f\), a fraction \(x\) has \(y_g\).

Reading the formula
\(y_f\)
the value if none of the water had boiled yet: the liquid end of the flat line.
\(y_{fg}\)
the full change from boiling all of it.
\(x\,y_{fg}\)
the part of that change already made, because a fraction \(x\) has boiled.
\(y\)
the average per kilogram over everything in the pot, liquid and steam together.
deriving \(v=v_f+x\,v_{fg}\)

1. The volumes and masses of the two phases add: \(V=V_f+V_g\), \(m=m_f+m_g\).

2. Each volume is mass × specific volume: \(V=mv\), \(V_f=m_fv_f\), \(V_g=m_gv_g\). So \(mv=m_fv_f+m_gv_g\).

3. Divide by \(m\): \(v=\dfrac{m_f}{m}v_f+\dfrac{m_g}{m}v_g\).

4. By definition \(m_g/m=x\), and \(m_f/m=(m-m_g)/m=1-x\).

5. So \(v=(1-x)v_f+xv_g\), and expanding, \(v=v_f+x(v_g-v_f)=v_f+xv_{fg}\). the form used most often

6. Nothing here was special to volume: for any extensive property \(Y\) with \(y=Y/m\), the same steps give \(y=y_f+x\,y_{fg}\).

Result · the lever rule \[x=\frac{y-y_f}{y_{fg}}\qquad\text{e.g.}\quad x=\frac{v-v_f}{v_{fg}}\]

In words: quality = (how far the state is from the liquid end) ÷ (length of the whole flat line). On the diagram, with A on the saturated liquid line, B the state and C on the saturated vapour line, \(x=AB/AC\).

Reading the formula
\(y-y_f\)
how far the pot has got past the start of boiling.
\(y_{fg}\)
the length of the whole boiling stretch.
\(x\)
the ratio: the fraction of the way through boiling, which is the fraction of the mass already steam.

Analogy: a seesaw. The state B is the pivot between a liquid end and a vapour end; the closer B is to C (vapour), the more of the mass must be vapour.

Example · quality from \(v\)

A rigid 1 m³ tank holds 2 kg of water at 100 kPa. Find the quality and the mass of vapour.

  1. Do: \(v=V/m=1/2=0.5\u{m^3/kg}\). The tables are per kilogram, so turn the tank into "room per kilogram" before comparing.
  2. Do: Table A-5 at 100 kPa: \(v_f=0.001043\), \(v_g=1.6941\). Since \(v_f\lt0.5\lt v_g\), it is a mixture, at \(T_\text{sat}=99.61\) °C. The value lies between the "just started boiling" and "just finished boiling" ends, so the water is partway through boiling.
  3. Do: \(x=\dfrac{0.5-0.001043}{1.6931}=\boxed{0.2947}\); vapour mass \(m_g=xm=0.589\u{kg}\). Distance past the liquid end ÷ length of the whole flat line = fraction already turned to steam (the lever rule).

Only 29% of the mass is vapour, yet the vapour fills \(0.589\times1.6941=0.998\u{m^3}\): 99.8% of the tank. Mass fraction and volume fraction are very different things.

Try it · mass fraction (quality) versus volume fraction

Ready answer · mass and quality from volumes (exam type T2.3)

When a question gives the volumes of liquid and vapour (or "the vapour takes up 95% of the volume"):

  1. Do: Split the volume: \(V_f\) and \(V_g\). Liquid and steam take up very different room per kilogram, so each part must be converted on its own.
  2. Do: Masses: \(m_f=V_f/v_f\), \(m_g=V_g/v_g\) (saturation table at the given \(P\) or \(T\)). Volume ÷ room per kilogram = number of kilograms.
  3. Do: \(m=m_f+m_g\) and \(x=m_g/m\). Quality is by definition the steam's share of the mass, not of the volume.

Never write \(x=V_g/V\).

You can now: define quality as a mass fraction; find \(v\), \(u\), \(h\) of a mixture from \(y=y_f+xy_{fg}\); find \(x\) from a given \(v\) with the lever rule; and find the masses and quality when the volumes of liquid and vapour are given.

12Choosing the right table ~5 min

What is this?

Before reading any table, find where the water is on the pot's journey: still liquid, boiling, or steam being heated further. The saturation values at the given pressure are the two milestones: where boiling starts (\(f\)) and where it ends (\(g\)). Compare your number with them and you know the stretch; each stretch has its own table.

Every steam question starts by deciding which region the state is in, because that decides which table to use. Compare the given data with the saturation values. For example, given \(P\) and \(v\), ask three questions at that pressure:

Test (at the given \(P\))RegionWhat to do
\(v\lt v_f\)?compressed liquidTable A-7, or \(y\approx y_{f@T}\) from Table A-4
\(v_f\lt v\lt v_g\)?saturated mixtureTable A-4 or A-5: \(T=T_\text{sat}\); find \(x=(v-v_f)/v_{fg}\), then \(u,h,s\) from \(y=y_f+xy_{fg}\)
\(v_g\lt v\)?superheated vapourTable A-6, block at that \(P\)

The method, step by step:

  1. Do: At the given pressure (or temperature), read the saturation values from Table A-4 or A-5: \(T_\text{sat}\), and the \(f\) and \(g\) values of the property you were given. These are the two milestones for this lid weight: where boiling starts and where it ends.
  2. Do: Compare the given value with them, as in the table above. Below \(f\): boiling has not started (compressed liquid). Between \(f\) and \(g\): partway through boiling (mixture). Above \(g\): boiling has finished and the steam is being heated (superheated).
  3. Do: For a mixture, find the quality \(x=(v-v_f)/v_{fg}\) next, then every other property from \(y=y_f+xy_{fg}\). Under the dome \(T\) and \(P\) are already known; the quality is the one reading still missing.
  4. Do: If the value falls between two rows of Table A-6 or A-7, interpolate (box below). The tables only print every few degrees; your state usually lies between two printed ones.

The same three questions work with \(u\), \(h\) or \(s\) in place of \(v\) (e.g. "is \(h\lt h_f\)?"), and with temperature:

Ready answer · locating the region (exam type T2.3)
  • Given \(P\) and \(T\): find \(T_\text{sat}\) at \(P\) (Table A-5). \(T\lt T_\text{sat}\): compressed liquid. \(T\gt T_\text{sat}\): superheated vapour. \(T=T_\text{sat}\): saturated, and you need one more property (\(x\), \(v\), …).
  • Given \(P\) (or \(T\)) and \(v\), \(u\), \(h\) or \(s\): compare with the \(f\) and \(g\) values as in the table above.
  • Given a word: "saturated liquid" (\(x=0\)), "saturated vapour" (\(x=1\)), "mixture" / "wet steam" / "liquid and vapour in equilibrium" / "half the mass has vaporized" (saturated, \(0\lt x\lt1\)) all mean the saturation table; \(T=T_\text{sat}\) or \(P=P_\text{sat}\).
Example · three states

Find the region and the temperature or specific enthalpy.

  1. 120 °C, 500 kPa. \(T_\text{sat}@500\u{kPa}=151.83\) °C, and \(120\lt151.83\): compressed liquid. \(h\approx h_{f@120^\circ\mathrm C}=503.81\u{kJ/kg}\), \(v\approx0.001060\u{m^3/kg}\). Squeezing liquid barely changes it, so it is read at its own temperature, 120 °C, not at 500 kPa (section 10).

Going further · linear interpolation in a table

When a value falls between two rows, assume the property changes in a straight line between them.

  1. Do: Find the two rows that bracket the given value. On the pot's superheated stretch at this pressure, your state lies between these two recorded states.
  2. Do: Work out how far along the gap the given value sits, as a fraction (given − lower) ÷ (upper − lower). This says how much of the change between the two rows the steam has already made.
  3. Do: Add the same fraction of the gap in the other column to the lower row's value. Over a short stretch the curve is nearly straight, so equal fractions of one property go with equal fractions of the other.

For state 3: \(T\) goes from 179.88 to 200 °C while \(v\) goes from 0.19437 to 0.20602:

\[\begin{aligned}T&\approx179.88+\frac{0.2-0.19437}{0.20602-0.19437}(20.12)\\&=179.88+0.483(20.12)\\&=189.6\ ^\circ\mathrm C\end{aligned}\]

The exact (IAPWS) value is 189.5 °C, so straight-line interpolation is good to a few tenths of a degree. Some exam parts say "find the closest value available in the table" (Dec 2024 Q7(d)): then just pick the nearest row and say so.

You can now: decide the region of a state from \(P\) and \(T\), from \(P\) and \(v\) (or \(u\), \(h\), \(s\)), or from words; pick Table A-4/A-5, A-6 or A-7 to match; and interpolate linearly between two table rows.

13Examples 1–4 ~5 min

What is this?

All four examples are the pot at some point of its journey. Example 1 is water just about to boil; Example 2 is steam that has just finished boiling; Example 3 is the whole boiling stretch; Example 4 starts as liquid and stops halfway through boiling. Place each state on the journey and the table row follows.

Worked example 1 · pressure of saturated liquid in a tank

A rigid tank contains 50 kg of saturated liquid water at 90 °C. Determine the pressure in the tank and the volume of the tank.

  1. "Saturated" means the water is at a saturation state, so the pressure is the saturation pressure at 90 °C (Table A-4, row 90 °C): \(P=P_{\text{sat}@90^\circ\mathrm C}=\boxed{70.183\u{kPa}}\).
  2. "Liquid" means \(x=0\), so \(v=v_{f@90^\circ\mathrm C}=0.001036\u{m^3/kg}\) (same row).
  3. \(V=mv=(50)(0.001036)\), so \(V=\boxed{0.0518\u{m^3}}\) (about 52 litres).

On a T-v diagram the state sits on the saturated liquid line at 90 °C.

Worked example 2 · temperature of saturated vapour in a cylinder

A piston–cylinder device contains 0.05 m³ of saturated water vapour at 350 kPa. Determine the temperature and the mass of the vapour inside the cylinder.

  1. Saturated vapour at 350 kPa: \(T=T_{\text{sat}@350\u{kPa}}=\boxed{138.86\ ^\circ\mathrm C}\) (Table A-5, row 350 kPa).
  2. \(v=v_{g@350\u{kPa}}=0.524\u{m^3/kg}\) (the table prints 0.52422).
  3. \(m=V/v=0.05/0.524=\boxed{0.0954\u{kg}}\).
Worked example 3 · volume and energy change during evaporation

A mass of 200 g of saturated liquid water is completely vaporized at a constant pressure of 100 kPa. Determine (a) the volume change and (b) the amount of energy transferred to the water.

(a) The water goes from saturated liquid (\(v_f\)) to saturated vapour (\(v_g\)) along the flat line, so each kilogram grows by \(v_{fg}\). Table A-5 at 100 kPa:

\[\begin{aligned}v_{fg}&=v_g-v_f\\&=1.6941-0.001043\\&=1.6931\u{m^3/kg}\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}\Delta V&=mv_{fg}=(0.2)(1.6931)\\&=\boxed{0.3386\u{m^3}}\end{aligned}\]

(b) The energy to vaporize 1 kg at a given pressure is the enthalpy of vaporization, \(h_{fg}=2257.5\u{kJ/kg}\) at 100 kPa, so The water runs the whole boiling stretch, from the \(f\) end to the \(g\) end, at constant pressure, so the heat is the full \(h_{fg}\) per kilogram.

\[\Delta H=mh_{fg}=(0.2)(2257.5)=\boxed{451.5\u{kJ}}\]

In words: volume change = mass × \(v_{fg}\); energy = mass × \(h_{fg}\). 200 mL of water becomes 339 litres of steam.

Worked example 4 · heating at a constant pressure

A piston–cylinder device contains 1 kg of compressed liquid water at 300 °C and 10 MPa. The water is heated at constant pressure until one-half of the mass vaporizes. (a) Determine the volume change of the water. (b) What is the remaining energy that we need to add to the water to complete the vaporization?

(a) State 1 is a compressed liquid (\(300\lt T_\text{sat}=311.00\) °C at 10 MPa). From Table A-7 at 10 MPa, 300 °C: \(v_1=0.001398\u{m^3/kg}\). Above 5 MPa the shortcut \(y\approx y_{f@T}\) is less accurate, so the compressed-liquid table is used when it is given.

State 2 is a saturated mixture at 10 MPa with \(x=0.5\). Table A-5 at 10 MPa: \(v_f=0.001452\), \(v_g=0.018028\u{m^3/kg}\).

\[\begin{aligned}v_2&=v_f+x(v_g-v_f)\\&=0.001452+0.5(0.016576)\\&=0.00974\u{m^3/kg}\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}\Delta V&=m(v_2-v_1)\\&=1(0.00974-0.001398)\\&=\boxed{0.0083\u{m^3}}\end{aligned}\]

(b) To finish vaporizing, the water must go from state 2 to saturated vapour (\(x=1\)), so the energy is \(m(h_g-h_2)\). Table A-5 at 10 MPa: \(h_f=1407.8\), \(h_g=2725.5\u{kJ/kg}\).

\[\begin{aligned}h_2&=h_f+x(h_g-h_f)\\&=1407.8+0.5(1317.7)\\&=2066.65\u{kJ/kg}\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}\Delta H&=m(h_g-h_2)\\&=1(2725.5-2066.65)\\&=\boxed{658.85\u{kJ}}\end{aligned}\]
a faster route for (b), and a check with the compressed-liquid shortcut

The remaining vapour fraction is \(1-x=0.5\), and each kilogram of it needs \(h_{fg}\): \(\Delta H=m(1-x)h_{fg}\), which is \(1(0.5)(1317.6)=658.8\u{kJ}\). \(h_g-h_2=h_g-h_f-xh_{fg}=(1-x)h_{fg}\) (Working with \(h_g-h_f=1317.7\) instead of the printed \(h_{fg}=1317.6\) differs only by rounding.)

Without Table A-7, \(v_1\approx v_{f@300^\circ\mathrm C}=0.001404\u{m^3/kg}\) (Table A-4): \(\Delta V=0.00974-0.001404\), or \(0.00834\u{m^3}\), the same to 2 significant figures. At 10 MPa (above 5 MPa) the table value is the better one.

With the unrounded \(v_2=0.009740\): \(\Delta V=0.008342\u{m^3}\) (8.3 litres).

Ready answer · volume change and energy at constant pressure (exam type T2.4)
  1. Do: Find each state's region (section 12) and its \(v\) and \(h\): mixture \(y=y_f+xy_{fg}\); superheated: Table A-6; compressed liquid: Table A-7 or \(y_{f@T}\). The start and the finish can sit on different stretches of the pot's journey, and each stretch has its own table.
  2. Do: \(\Delta V=m(v_2-v_1)\). \(v\) is per kilogram, so the whole pot changes by the mass times the change per kilogram.
  3. Do: Energy: \(Q=\Delta H=m(h_2-h_1)\). To complete vaporization from quality \(x\): \(m(1-x)h_{fg}\). To complete condensation from quality \(x\): \(mxh_{fg}\) removed. At constant pressure the heat pays for the energy stored and the lid pushed up, which is the enthalpy change (section 8); to finish boiling, only the fraction \(1-x\) still liquid needs \(h_{fg}\).
  4. Do: State the sign in words: "added to" or "removed from" the water; "increase" or "decrease" in volume. A bare number does not say which way the energy went.

You can now: find the pressure or temperature of a saturated state from one table row; find a mass from a volume; find the volume change and energy for complete vaporization; and find the energy still needed to finish boiling from a given quality.

15Common mistakes

✗Quality from volumes: "the vapour takes 95% of the volume, so \(x=0.95\)". ✓\(x\) is a mass fraction: \(m_g=V_g/v_g\), \(m_f=V_f/v_f\), \(x=m_g/(m_f+m_g)\). ✗Reading a compressed liquid at the saturation row of its pressure. ✓Use the row of its temperature: \(y\approx y_{f@T}\) (or Table A-7). ✗Drawing the T-v isobar sloping inside the dome, or drawing it above the critical point for a subcritical pressure. ✓Inside the dome it is horizontal (\(T=T_\text{sat}\)) and below the critical point. ✗On a P-v diagram, drawing the isotherm rising to the right. ✓It falls (steeply in the liquid, flat across the dome, gently in the vapour). ✗Using \(h_g\) (or \(h_{fg}\)) for the energy when the start state is a mixture or superheated. ✓\(Q=m(h_2-h_1)\) with each \(h\) found for its own state. ✗Mixing kPa and MPa: \(P\times v\) with \(P\) in MPa. ✓kPa × m³/kg = kJ/kg; 5 MPa = 5000 kPa. ✗Calling a saturated vapour "superheated" because it is hot. ✓Superheated means \(T\gt T_\text{sat}\) at that pressure. ✗"Temperature and pressure fix the state" for boiling water. ✓In the mixture they are not independent; add \(x\) or \(v\).

"A 2 m³ boiler at 5 MPa holds a saturated mixture, vapour taking 95% of the volume. Then \(x=0.95\), \(v=0.001286+0.95(0.038162)=0.03754\), so \(m=2/0.03754=53.3\u{kg}\)."

Is 95% a fraction of the mass or of the volume?

Answer

What temperature is saturated liquid at 10 MPa?

Answer

Saturated liquid at 10 MPa is at 311 °C, a very different state. Use the 40 °C row: \(v_1\approx v_{f@40^\circ\mathrm C}=0.001008\) (Table A-7 at 10 MPa, 40 °C: 0.0010035), so \(V_1\approx0.00502\u{m^3}\).

"0.8 kg of superheated vapour at 1 MPa, 300 °C is cooled until one third of the mass condenses. Energy removed so far \(=m\,h_{fg}/3=0.8(2014.6)/3=537\u{kJ}\)."

17Concept checks

1 · Extensive or intensive?

Classify: kinetic energy of a moving car, its speed, specific enthalpy of the coolant, volume of the fuel tank, density of the fuel.

Answer

Extensive: kinetic energy (\(\tfrac12mV^2\) doubles with mass), tank volume. Intensive: speed, specific enthalpy, density.

2 · Enough information?

Water at 200 kPa: (a) with \(T=150\) °C; (b) with \(T=120.21\) °C (\(=T_\text{sat}\) at 200 kPa). Is the state fixed in each case?

Answer

(a) Yes: \(150\gt T_\text{sat}\), superheated vapour, and \(P,T\) are independent there. (b) No: at saturation \(T\) follows from \(P\), so only one independent property is known; the water could be anything from saturated liquid to saturated vapour. A quality or \(v\) is needed.

3 · Which way does it go?

A sealed rigid tank holds a wet mixture at 100 °C with \(x=0.5\). It is heated. Will it end as saturated vapour or as saturated liquid?

Hint

Rigid, sealed: \(v\) is constant. Compare \(v\) with the critical value 0.003106 m³/kg.

Answer

\(v=0.001043+0.5(1.6710)\), so \(v=0.836\u{m^3/kg}\), far bigger than \(v_\text{cr}\). A vertical line at this \(v\) meets the saturated vapour line: it ends as saturated vapour, then superheated. (A tank with \(v\lt v_\text{cr}\) would instead fill with liquid.)

4 · Latent heat and pressure

Is it cheaper in energy per kilogram to boil water at 100 kPa or at 10 MPa (starting from saturated liquid each time)? Why does the difference vanish at 22.064 MPa?

Answer

At 10 MPa: \(h_{fg}=1317.6\) versus 2257.5 kJ/kg at 100 kPa, so less energy per kilogram (but the liquid must first be heated to 311 °C). At the critical point liquid and vapour are identical, so \(h_{fg}=0\).

5 · Quality bounds

Someone computes \(x=(v-v_f)/v_{fg}=1.3\). What does it tell you?

Answer

\(v\gt v_g\): the state is superheated, not a mixture, and the quality formula does not apply. Use Table A-6. (A negative result would mean compressed liquid.)

6 · Equilibrium

A cup of hot coffee sits in a room. Which kinds of equilibrium does it lack?

Answer

Thermal (it is hotter than the room and not uniform inside) and phase (water keeps evaporating). It is in mechanical equilibrium (pressure uniform at atmospheric), and chemical equilibrium only approximately.

Dec 2024 Q34 markssame: Dec 2022 Q3, May 2022 Q3 (5 marks)T2.1 property & state vocabulary

Explain what the state principle is and explain its relationship with the thermodynamic state of a simple system.

Hint

Three marker points: the statement (how many properties, of what kind), what a "simple" system is, and what "fixed state" means for the other properties.

Answer

State principle: the thermodynamic state of a simple system is completely specified by two independent, intensive properties.

Simple system: one with no effects of external fields (electrical, magnetic, gravitational) or surface tension.

Relation to the state: once two independent intensive properties are known, the state is fixed, and every other property (\(v,u,h,s,\ldots\)) has a definite value that can be calculated or read from tables; changing any property changes the state. "Independent" matters: in a saturated mixture \(T\) and \(P\) are linked (\(T=T_\text{sat}(P)\)), so a second property such as quality \(x\) or \(v\) is needed.
