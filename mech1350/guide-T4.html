Decision procedure · two tanks joined by a valve (T4.1)
  1. Convert. Every temperature to kelvin (+273.15), every pressure to kPa (1 bar = 100 kPa). Cue: a temperature that looks wrong (Dec 2021 printed "428 C") is a misprint: state your reading.
  2. Find \(R\). \(R=c_p-c_v\) from the table given, or \(R_u/M\). (For the final pressure alone \(R\) cancels.)
  3. Volumes. \(V_i=m_iRT_i/P_i\) for each tank; rigid tanks: \(V_\text{tot}=V_1+V_2\) stays the same after mixing.
  4. Final pressure. \(P_f=m_\text{tot}RT_f/V_\text{tot}\), converted back to bar if asked.
  5. Energy. Rigid: \(W=0\), so \(Q=\Delta U=\sum m_ic_v(T_f-T_i)\), each portion from its own \(T_i\). Positive = received, negative = lost; check with the stem ("receiving energy" means \(Q\gt0\)).
Answer skeleton · cycle on diagrams (T4.3, May 2019 Q3, 14 marks)

Diagram to draw: the reading rules below, then one \(p\)-\(V\) diagram per cycle with A, B, C, arrows and each leg named.

Marker points: (1) each leg named (isobaric / isochoric / isothermal) with the reason from the given graph; (2) correct \(p\)-\(V\) shape: horizontal, vertical, and a hyperbola \(pV=\)const for the isotherm; (3) arrows and the direction of the cycle (clockwise = net work out); (4) for each leg: heat absorbed or rejected, with why (\(Q=\Delta U+W\)); (5) a table with columns \(V\), \(T\), \(W\), \(\Delta U\), \(Q\); (6) whole cycle: \(\Delta U=0\), \(Q_\text{net}=W_\text{net}\).

Model answer in the May 2019 Q3 card.

Answer skeleton · first law versus second law (T4.4, May 2025 Q4, 5 marks)

Diagram to draw (optional, earns the example point): hot coffee in a cool room. Heat leaving the coffee happens; heat flowing back into it never does, although both conserve energy.

Marker points (one each): (1) first law = conservation of energy, \(Q-W=\Delta U\); (2) second law = processes have a direction (heat flows hot → cold by itself); (3) first law is about the quantity of energy, second about its quality (ability to do work); (4) the first law alone cannot say whether a process can happen: it must satisfy both; (5) the second law brings in entropy: \(S_\text{gen}\ge0\), \(=0\) reversible, \(\gt0\) irreversible, \(\lt0\) impossible.

2T4.1 · Ideal-gas law with an energy balance (rigid tanks)

\(V_i=m_iRT_i/P_i\) → \(P_f=m_\text{tot}RT_f/V_\text{tot}\) → \(Q=\sum m_ic_v(T_f-T_i)\), \(W=0\). Learn it: note-T4 §1 ideal gas · §2 R, cp, cv · §10 rigid tanks

Ready answer · final pressure of two mixed tanks \[P_f=\frac{(m_1+m_2)\,T_f}{\dfrac{m_1T_1}{P_1}+\dfrac{m_2T_2}{P_2}}\]

In words: final pressure = total mass × final temperature, divided by the sum of each tank's "mass × temperature / pressure"; \(R\) cancels, temperatures in kelvin.

Dec 2021 Q2(a)(b)8 + 9 marksnumerical

Two tanks are connected by a valve. One tank contains 2 kg of carbon monoxide gas at 77 °C and 0.7 bar. The other tank holds 8 kg of the same gas at 27 °C and 1.2 bar. The valve is opened and the gases are allowed to mix while receiving energy by heat transfer from the surroundings. The final equilibrium temperature is 428 C. Using the ideal gas model with constant \(C_p\), determine

Appended Table A-20 (ideal-gas specific heats), carbon monoxide: \(c_p=1.040\), \(c_v=0.744\) at 300 K; \(c_p=1.043\), \(c_v=0.746\) at 350 K, in kJ/(kg·K).

(a) the final equilibrium pressure, in bars. (8 marks)

Hint

Type T4.1. Is "428 C" plausible for two tanks near room temperature? Find each tank's volume first; kelvin and kPa.

Solution
  1. Read "428 C" as \(T_f=42\) °C (see the flag). In kelvin: \(T_1=350.15\), \(T_2=300.15\), \(T_f=315.15\) K. Pressures: \(P_1=70\), \(P_2=120\) kPa.
  2. \(R=c_p-c_v=1.040-0.744\) \(=0.296\u{kJ/(kg\,K)}\) (or \(R_u/M=8.314/28.01=0.2968\)).
  3. Tank volumes from \(V=mRT/P\):
\[V_1=\frac{2(0.296)(350.15)}{70}=2.961\u{m^3}\] \[V_2=\frac{8(0.296)(300.15)}{120}=5.923\u{m^3}\]
  1. Rigid tanks: \(V_\text{tot}=8.884\u{m^3}\), \(m_\text{tot}=10\) kg.
\[\begin{aligned}P_f&=\frac{10(0.296)(315.15)}{8.884}\\&=105.0\u{kPa}\end{aligned}\] \[\boxed{P_f\approx1.05\u{bar}}\]

Marker note: most marks are for the two volumes with kelvin and kPa; \(R\) cancels, so either value of \(R\) gives 1.05 bar.

"428 C" is read as 42 °C: the degree sign was printed as "8" (the source textbook problem, Moran & Shapiro, has 42 °C, and 428 °C would need a huge heat input for gases "receiving energy" near room temperature). Taken literally, 428 °C gives \(P_f=233.6\u{kPa}\approx2.34\u{bar}\).

(b) the heat transfer for the process, in kJ. (9 marks)

Hint

Type T4.1. Rigid tanks: what is \(W\)? Then \(\Delta U\) of each gas portion from its own starting temperature.

Solution
  1. System = all the gas in both tanks. The tanks are rigid, so \(W=0\) and the first law gives \(Q=\Delta U\).
  2. Ideal gas, constant specific heat: \(\Delta U=\sum m_ic_v(T_f-T_i)\). Take \(c_v=0.745\u{kJ/(kg\,K)}\) (Table A-20 between 300 and 350 K).
  3. Temperature changes (same in °C and K): tank 1: \(42-77=-35\); tank 2: \(42-27=+15\).
\[\begin{aligned}Q&=0.745\,[2(-35)+8(15)]\\&=0.745\,(-70+120)\\&=37.25\u{kJ}\end{aligned}\] \[\boxed{Q\approx+37.3\u{kJ}\ \text{(received)}}\]

Marker note: using \(c_p\) (as the stem's "constant \(C_p\)" invites) gives 52.1 kJ: for a rigid tank only \(c_v\) is right.

"constant \(C_p\)" is read as "constant specific heats"; the rigid-tank energy balance needs \(c_v\). With \(c_v=0.744\): 37.2 kJ. Check: \(Q=0\) would need \(T_f\) = the mass-weighted mean 37 °C, so 42 °C does require heat in, as the stem says. Literal 428 °C: \(Q\approx2.91\times10^{3}\u{kJ}\).

3T4.2 · First-law energy balance

\(Q-W=\Delta U=mc_v\Delta T\); rigid vessel: \(Q=m(u_2-u_1)\), time = \(Q\)/power; mixing: energy in = energy out. Learn it: note-T4 §4 specific heats · §5 heat and work · §7 first law

Ready answer · final temperature after heat in and work out \[T_2=T_1+\frac{Q-W}{mc_v}\]

In words: final temperature = initial temperature + (heat added − work done by the gas) / (mass × \(c_v\)). kJ / (kg × kJ/(kg·K)) = K.

Dec 2024 Q64 marksnumericalsame template: Dec 2023 Q6

Consider a combustion engine with 2 kg of air at a temperature of 500 K initially. 200 kJ of heat is added to the air, while the piston of the engine expands such that the air performs 150 kJ of work. What is the final temperature of the air? Given that the specific heat at constant volume \(C_v=0.733\u{kJ/kg\cdot K}\) and \(\Delta U=mC_v\Delta T\).

Hint

Type T4.2. Signs: heat added is \(+Q\); work performed by the air is \(+W\). How much energy stays in the air?

Solution
  1. First law: \(\Delta U=Q-W\) \(=200-150=50\u{kJ}\).
  2. \(\Delta T=\dfrac{\Delta U}{mC_v}=\dfrac{50}{2(0.733)}\) \(=34.11\u{K}\).
  3. \(T_2=500+34.11\).
\[\boxed{T_2\approx534\u{K}\ (534.1\u{K})}\]

Marker note: adding the work (\(Q+W=350\)) gives 739 K, the most common lost mark.

Dec 2023 Q65 marksnumericalsame template: Dec 2024 Q6

Consider a combustion engine with 1 kg of air at a temperature of 500 K initially. 100 kJ of heat is added to the air, while the piston of the engine expands such that the air performs 75 kJ of work. What is the final temperature of the air? Given that the specific heat at constant volume \(C_v=0.733\u{kJ/kg\cdot K}\) and \(\Delta U=mC_v\Delta T\).

Hint

Type T4.2. As Dec 2024 Q6 with half the mass and half the energies.

Solution
  1. \(\Delta U=Q-W=100-75=25\u{kJ}\).
  2. \(\Delta T=\dfrac{25}{1(0.733)}=34.11\u{K}\).
\[\boxed{T_2\approx534\u{K}}\]

Same answer as Dec 2024: mass and both energies are all halved, so \(\Delta T\) is unchanged.

May 2023 Q4(iii)2 marksMCpart (i): T3.5part (ii): T4.4

For the preparation of warm water in a shower, an insulated mixing chamber receives hot water with mass flow rate \(\dot m_1\) at temperature \(T_1\) and cold water with mass flow rate \(\dot m_2\) at \(T_2\). A single stream of warm water exits at the faucet with mass flow rate \(\dot m_3\) and temperature \(T_3\). Assuming the heat capacity of water is constant \(c\), the exit temperature is:

  • (a) \(T_3=(T_1+T_2)/2\)
  • (b) \(T_3=(\dot m_1T_1+\dot m_2T_2)/\dot m_3\)
  • (c) \(T_3=c(T_1-T_2)\)
  • (d) \(T_3=(\dot m_1T_1-\dot m_2T_2)/\dot m_3\)

Beyond the handouts (steady-flow mixing; solvable as "heat lost = heat gained")

Hint

Type T4.2. Insulated, no work, steady: energy carried in each second = energy carried out each second.

Solution
  1. Mass balance: \(\dot m_3=\dot m_1+\dot m_2\).
  2. Energy balance per second (no heat loss, no work), measuring energy as \(cT\) per kg:
\[\dot m_1cT_1+\dot m_2cT_2=\dot m_3cT_3\]
  1. Divide by \(\dot m_3c\). Check the others: (a) is only true when \(\dot m_1=\dot m_2\); (c) has units of \(c\times\)K, not K; (d) has the wrong sign.
\[\boxed{\text{(b) }T_3=\frac{\dot m_1T_1+\dot m_2T_2}{\dot m_3}}\]
May 2023 Q5(a)3 marksnumericalparts (b)(c): T2.3, (d): T2.4

Pressure cookers operate at higher-than-atmospheric pressures, which allows higher temperatures and significantly shorter cooking times to be achieved. You are interested in designing an industrial pressure cooker with a total volume of 40 L (\(V=0.04\u{m^3}\)) and cooking temperature of \(T_\text{sat}=150\) °C. To regulate the pressure, a mass on top will 'rock' (open) when the desired operating pressure is reached, venting some steam and closing again. This process happens cyclically during cooking at moderate frequency. 30 kg (approximately 8 gallons) of liquid H₂O are filled into an electric pressure cooker from the faucet at \(T_1=20\) °C and \(P_1=P_\text{atm}=1\times10^5\) Pa.

The paper appends Moran Table A-2 (saturated water, temperature table): \(u_f=83.95\) kJ/kg at 20 °C; \(u_f=631.68\), \(u_g=2559.5\) kJ/kg, \(P_\text{sat}=4.758\) bar at 150 °C.

(a) Assume the cooker is properly insulated so no heat loss is involved before steam vapor is released, let use the change of internal energy to find the total energy needed to heat up water from the ambient state (\(T_1\) and \(P_1\) specified above) to cooking temperature of \(T_\text{sat}=150\) °C. If the electrical power required by the cooker is 18 kW, estimate how long it takes for steam generation to commence. (3 marks)

Hint

Type T4.2. Closed, rigid, insulated: \(Q=\Delta U\). Liquid water: read \(u\) as \(u_f\) at each temperature.

Solution
  1. The cooker is closed and rigid (\(W=0\)) and insulated, so the electrical energy all goes into the water: \(E=\Delta U=m(u_2-u_1)\).
  2. State 1, liquid at 20 °C: \(u_1\approx u_f(20\,^\circ\mathrm C)=83.95\) kJ/kg (a liquid's \(u\) depends almost only on \(T\)).
  3. State 2, saturated liquid at 150 °C: \(u_2\approx u_f=631.68\) kJ/kg.
\[\begin{aligned}\Delta U&=30(631.68-83.95)\\&=16\,432\u{kJ}\approx16.4\u{MJ}\end{aligned}\] \[t=\frac{\Delta U}{\text{power}}=\frac{16\,432}{18}=913\u{s}\] \[\boxed{t\approx913\u{s}\approx15\u{min}}\]

Marker note: 18 kW = 18 kJ/s; dividing kJ by kW gives seconds directly.

\(u\) of the liquid is taken as \(u_f\) at its temperature (values from the paper's Moran table; IAPWS-IF97 gives 83.91 and 631.73, \(\Delta U=16\,435\) kJ). Strictly, at 150 °C a tiny amount has evaporated to fill the space (quality \(x\approx6.2\times10^{-4}\), part (c)), which raises \(\Delta U\) to 16 468 kJ and \(t\) to 915 s; the shortcut \(mc\Delta T\) with \(c=4.18\) gives 906 s. All round to about 15 minutes.

For each pV diagram, classify different cycle part of the processes and explain the characteristics of their heat flow. Hint

Type T4.3. Extend A→B: does it pass through the origin? On \(V\)-\(T\) that means \(V/T\) constant; on \(p\)-\(T\), \(p/T\) constant. Horizontal and vertical lines hold one quantity fixed. Skeleton in At a glance.

Solution
  1. Cycle (a), \(V\)-\(T\). A→B lies on a line through the origin: \(V\propto T\), so \(p=mRT/V\) is constant: isobaric expansion. B→C horizontal: \(V\) constant, \(T\) falls: isochoric cooling. C→A vertical: \(T\) constant, \(V\) falls: isothermal compression.
  2. Cycle (b), \(p\)-\(T\). A→B through the origin: \(p\propto T\), so \(V\) is constant: isochoric heating. B→C vertical: \(T\) constant, \(p\) falls: isothermal expansion. C→A horizontal: \(p\) constant, \(T\) falls: isobaric compression (cooling).
  3. Redraw on \(p\)-\(V\): isobars horizontal, isochores vertical, isotherms the curve \(pV=\)const. Both cycles run clockwise, so \(W_\text{net}\gt0\).

5T4.4 · Second-law & reversibility concepts

First law: energy conserved (quantity). Second law: direction and quality, \(S_\text{gen}\ge0\) (\(=0\) reversible). Learn it: note-T4 §7 first law · §11 towards the second law · §12 entropy

Ready answer · the two lists

Entropy generated: \(S_\text{gen}=0\) reversible · \(S_\text{gen}\gt0\) irreversible (every real process) · \(S_\text{gen}\lt0\) impossible. Energy is conserved in all of them.

What stays constant: boiling or condensing at a fixed pressure: \(P\) and \(T\) · reversible adiabatic (isentropic): \(s\) · gas through a valve (throttling, Beyond the handouts): \(h\), and for an ideal gas also \(T\) · rigid tank: \(V\) · cycle: every property returns, \(\Delta U=0\).

May 2025 Q13 marksMC, select all

Which of the following processes can lead to vaporization of liquid water? Select ALL correct answers.

  • (a) Heating at constant pressure
  • (b) Increase volume at constant temperature
  • (c) Decrease volume at constant temperature
  • (d) Stir the water vigorously at constant pressure
Hint

Type T4.4. Liquid vaporises when its temperature reaches \(T_\text{sat}\) at its pressure, or its pressure falls to \(P_\text{sat}\) at its temperature. Which options do that? Is stirring a way to add energy?

Solution
  1. (a) Heating at constant \(P\) raises \(T\) to \(T_\text{sat}(P)\): the water boils. Yes.
  2. (b) Expanding at constant \(T\) lowers the pressure; once it reaches \(P_\text{sat}(T)\) the liquid vaporises (at 20 °C, below 2.34 kPa). Yes.
  3. (c) Compressing at constant \(T\) raises the pressure: the water stays compressed liquid. No.
  4. (d) Stirring is work done on the water (handout slide 19, "stirring" as the work route to the same \(\Delta U\) as heating); \(U\) and \(T\) rise until it boils at that pressure. Yes.
\[\boxed{\text{(a), (b), (d)}}\]

(d) is read as sustained vigorous stirring, as in the handout's "boiling versus stirring" slide: work raises \(U\) like heat does. If the examiner meant stirring merely mixes, (d) would be excluded; (a) and (b) are certain.

Dec 2023 Q13 marksMC, more than one answer

Which of the following requirement is necessary for a reversible thermodynamic process (can be more than one answer)?

  • (a) Total energy is conserved.
  • (b) Internal energy is conserved.
  • (c) Entropy generation is zero.
  • (d) Entropy generation is positive.
  • (e) Entropy generation is negative.
Hint

Type T4.4. Which condition must every process meet? Which extra one does "reversible" add? Can a reversible process change \(U\)?

Solution
  1. (a) The first law holds for every process, so a reversible one must conserve total energy too: necessary.
  2. (b) Not necessary: a slow reversible compression raises \(U\) and \(T\).
  3. (c) Reversible means no entropy generated: \(S_\text{gen}=0\). Necessary.
  4. (d) \(S_\text{gen}\gt0\) describes irreversible processes; (e) \(S_\text{gen}\lt0\) is impossible.
\[\boxed{\text{(a) and (c)}}\]

decision: answer (a) and (c). (a) is a necessary condition of every process, and the stem says "can be more than one answer". If the examiner wanted only the condition that distinguishes reversible processes, the key would be (c) alone; (c) is certain either way.

May 2023 Q4(ii)3 marksshort answerpart (i): T3.5part (iii): T4.2

For the following processes, what thermodynamic property (or properties) would you expect to remain essentially constant?

  • (a) Saturated liquid water enters a boiler; saturated vapor (steam) exits.
  • (b) Air at 300 K, 1 bar, enters a reversible adiabatic compressor; air at 500 K exits.
  • (c) An ideal gas is expanded adiabatically through a valve just opened.

Beyond the handouts ((c) throttling is not in the 2026 handouts; (a) and (b) are)

Hint

Type T4.4. (a) What is fixed while water boils? (b) Reversible + adiabatic = no entropy generated and none carried by heat. (c) No heat, no work, no speed change.

Solution
  1. (a) A boiler works at (nearly) constant pressure, and a saturated mixture at fixed \(P\) has fixed \(T=T_\text{sat}\): pressure and temperature stay constant (\(v\), \(u\), \(h\), \(s\) all rise).
  2. (b) Adiabatic: no heat, so no entropy carried in; reversible: \(S_\text{gen}=0\). So entropy \(s\) is constant (isentropic), although \(T\) and \(P\) rise.
  3. (c) Through a valve: no heat, no work, negligible change in speed, so the flow energy balance gives constant enthalpy \(h\); for an ideal gas \(h\) depends only on \(T\), so the temperature is constant too. (\(P\) falls, \(s\) rises: throttling is irreversible.)
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{(a) }P,\ T\quad\text{(b) }s\\&\text{(c) }h\ (\text{ideal gas: also }T)\end{aligned}}\]

6Traps across T4

Traps
  • °C or bar in \(PV=mRT\). Kelvin and kPa only: 0.7 bar = 70 kPa, 77 °C = 350.15 K.
  • \(Q+W\) instead of \(Q-W\). Work done by the gas leaves it: Dec 2024 Q6 gives 739 K instead of 534 K.
  • \(c_p\) for a rigid tank. \(\Delta U=mc_v\Delta T\); \(c_p\) belongs to \(\Delta h\) (Dec 2021: 52.1 kJ instead of 37.3 kJ).
  • Rigid means \(W=0\), not \(Q=0\). Heat may still cross the wall; it all becomes \(\Delta U\).
  • One temperature for both tanks. Each portion changes from its own \(T_i\) to \(T_f\).
  • Misread diagrams. Through the origin: \(V\)-\(T\) → constant \(p\); \(p\)-\(T\) → constant \(V\). Vertical on either → constant \(T\).
  • Cycle energy. \(\Delta U_\text{cycle}=0\), so \(Q_\text{net}=W_\text{net}\); clockwise on \(p\)-\(V\) means net work out.
  • Reversible ≠ adiabatic, ≠ "\(U\) constant". Reversible means \(S_\text{gen}=0\); energy is conserved in every process.
  • kJ ÷ kW = s. Heating time is energy / power; do not divide by 1000 again.
