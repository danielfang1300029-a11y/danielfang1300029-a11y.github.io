0Where this chapter sits

F2 conserved mass and F3 conserved energy; this chapter adds the third conservation law, conservation of momentum, for the forces flowing fluids exert, plus drag, losses and open channels. It is the last chapter of the course. Three pictures run through it: a fire hose kicking back, a jet of water hitting a plate, and your hand pushed back out of a car window.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 A moving stream carries momentum with it, like a fire hose throwing water forward. Momentum per second, \(\dot mV=\rho AV^2\), is measured in newtons.
  2. §2 Draw a box and keep a momentum account: net force on the fluid = momentum out − momentum in, per second. The recipe for every force question
  3. §3 A jet stopped dead by a square plate pushes with its whole momentum flux.
  4. §4 A tilted plate stops only the part of the jet aimed into it; a vane that turns the jet right round pushes twice as hard.
  5. §5 An upward jet slows like a thrown ball, so use its speed where it arrives.
  6. §6 In pipes the pressure forces usually beat the momentum terms. A bend is pushed outwards, a nozzle forwards.
  7. §7 A scale under a tank also has to stop the falling jet.
  8. §8 Drag is the push back on your hand out of a car window; lift is the push across the wind.
  9. §9 Drag grows as \(V^2\), so the power to beat it grows as \(V^3\).
  10. §10 A wing lifts until the air can no longer follow its top surface: then it stalls. Flaps let it fly slower.
  11. §11 Head loss is the energy friction turns into heat, written as a height.
  12. §12 A channel can carry the same flow deep and slow or shallow and fast.

You need: momentum and \(P=Fv\) from HKDSE Physics, continuity (F2) and Bernoulli (F3). In the exam: asked in 9 of 11 past sittings (Dec 2018 to May 2025), usually one 6–10 mark force question in Section C. See the F4 exam guide.

Sign convention for this chapter
  • Pick axes first and draw them on your sketch: \(x\) to the right and \(y\) up unless the question gives others. Every velocity, force and momentum flux is then a component with a sign: a jet moving left has \(V_x=-20\u{m/s}\).
  • In the momentum equation \(\sum F\) means the forces on the fluid inside the control volume. The force on the plate, bend or tank is equal and opposite (Newton's third law). Say clearly which one you are giving.
  • Pressures are gauge pressures (above atmospheric). The atmosphere pushes on every side of the control volume equally, so it cancels; using gauge pressure removes it in one step. A free jet (a jet in open air) has gauge pressure 0.
  • A pressure force at an inlet or outlet always pushes into the control volume, with size \(pA\).
  • Flow is steady (nothing changes with time), incompressible (constant density) and uniform across each inlet and outlet (one speed per section), as in every past paper.
  • Colours in the sketches: teal = flow and momentum flux \(\dot mV\); orange = pressure force \(pA\); purple = weight; red = the support (anchoring) force; green dashed = control-volume boundary.

1Momentum and momentum flux Going further ~4 min

What is this?

Turn on a fire hose and it shoves back in the firefighter's hands. Every second the hose throws a heavy stream of water forward, and to get water moving something has to push it; the water pushes back just as hard. Momentum flux measures how much forward motion the stream carries away each second. It is also the push a wall would feel if the stream hit it and stopped dead.

From HKDSE Physics: the momentum of a body is mass × velocity, \(mV\), a vector. Newton's second law says the net force on a body equals its rate of change of momentum, \(F=\mathrm d(mV)/\mathrm dt\), which is \(ma\) when \(m\) is fixed. This is the linear momentum relation; in components, \(F_x=ma_x\), \(F_y=ma_y\), \(F_z=ma_z\).

For a fluid we do not follow one lump of water. We watch a fixed region, the control volume (F2), and ask how much momentum flows in and out of it every second.

Definition · mass flow rate and momentum flux

Through a section of area \(A\) where fluid of density \(\rho\) crosses at speed \(V\):

  • the mass flow rate is \(\dot m=\rho AV\) (kg of fluid crossing per second);
  • the momentum flux is \(\dot m\vec V=\rho AV\,\vec V\): the momentum carried across the section per second. Its direction is the direction of the flow there; its size is \(\rho AV^2\).
Result · momentum flux of a jet \[\dot mV=\rho AV^2\]

In words: momentum per second = (mass per second) × velocity = density × area × speed squared. Unit: (kg/s)(m/s) = kg·m/s² = N, the same as a force.

Reading the formula
\(\rho AV\)
the kilograms of water leaving the hose each second, \(\dot m\).
the second \(V\)
the momentum each of those kilograms carries away, per kilogram.
\(V^2\)
faster water means more kilograms per second and more momentum in each one, hence the square.
unit N
momentum per second is a force: the push needed to start the stream, or to stop it.
why the momentum flux is \(\rho AV^2\)

1. In a short time \(\Delta t\) the fluid crossing the section fills a cylinder of length \(V\Delta t\) and area \(A\). Every particle moves \(V\Delta t\).

2. Its mass is \(\rho AV\Delta t\), so the mass per second is \(\rho AV\). This is \(\dot m\), as in F2's continuity equation.

3. Each kilogram carries momentum \(V\), so the momentum crossing in \(\Delta t\) is \(\rho AV\Delta t\times V\). Momentum = mass × velocity.

4. Divide by \(\Delta t\): momentum per second \(=\rho AV^2=\dot mV\).

Analogy: a stream of tennis balls fired from a machine. If 10 balls of 0.06 kg leave each second at 20 m/s, the machine pushes out \(10\times0.06\times20=12\) kg·m/s of momentum every second. A wall that stops them all must push back with 12 N. A water jet is the same idea with a continuous stream instead of balls.

Example

A garden-hose jet: diameter 15 mm, speed 12 m/s, water. Find \(\dot m\) and the momentum flux.

  1. \(A=\tfrac{\pi}{4}(0.015)^2=1.767\times10^{-4}\u{m^2}\).
  2. \(\dot m=\rho AV\) \(=1000(1.767\times10^{-4})(12)\) \(=2.121\u{kg/s}\).
  3. \(\dot mV=2.121\times12=\boxed{25.4\u{N}}\): the force needed to stop this jet dead (Section 3).
Watch

Momentum flux goes as \(V^2\): double the speed through the same nozzle and \(\dot m\) doubles and each kilogram carries twice the momentum, so the force from the jet is four times as large.

You can now: compute \(\dot m\) and the momentum flux \(\rho AV^2\) of a jet, in newtons; explain why the force of a jet goes as \(V^2\).

2The control-volume momentum equation Going further ~5 min

What is this?

Draw an imaginary box round the fire hose nozzle and keep a momentum account, like a bank account. Water carrying momentum comes in through one side (a deposit) and goes out through another (a withdrawal). In steady flow the balance inside the box never changes, so the forces on the water in the box must pay for exactly the difference, out minus in, every second.

For a steady flow the momentum inside a fixed control volume (CV) does not change with time. So the net force on the fluid in the CV only has to account for the difference between the momentum that flows out each second and the momentum that flows in.

Law · linear momentum equation for a steady control volume \[\sum\vec F=\sum_\text{out}\dot m\vec V-\sum_\text{in}\dot m\vec V\]

In components (one equation per axis):

\[\sum F_x=\sum_\text{out}\dot mV_x-\sum_\text{in}\dot mV_x\] \[\sum F_y=\sum_\text{out}\dot mV_y-\sum_\text{in}\dot mV_y\]

In words: net force on the fluid in the CV = momentum flowing out per second − momentum flowing in per second, worked out separately in \(x\) and in \(y\).

Reading the formula
\(\sum\vec F\)
every real push on the water inside the box: pressure at the cuts, weight, and the support holding the device.
\(\sum_\text{out}\dot m\vec V\)
momentum carried out of the box each second: the withdrawals.
\(\sum_\text{in}\dot m\vec V\)
momentum carried in each second: the deposits.
out − in
steady flow keeps the balance in the box fixed, so the forces must supply exactly the difference.
one equation per axis
each direction has its own account: sideways momentum can never pay for forward momentum.
where this comes from (Newton's second law for a moving lump of fluid)

1. Take the lump of fluid that is inside the CV at time \(t\). Newton's second law applies to it: net force = rate of change of its momentum. The lump is a fixed set of particles, a "system" in F2's language.

2. A moment \(\Delta t\) later the lump has shifted: a little of it has left through the outlets, and new fluid (not part of the lump) has entered through the inlets.

3. Lump's momentum at \(t+\Delta t\) = (momentum inside the CV) + (momentum of the bit that left) − (momentum of the new bit that entered). Book-keeping: the CV now holds new fluid that is not part of the lump.

4. Steady flow: momentum inside the CV is the same at both times. So the change of the lump's momentum is (left) − (entered) \(=\sum_\text{out}\dot m\vec V\Delta t-\sum_\text{in}\dot m\vec V\Delta t\).

5. Divide by \(\Delta t\): \(\sum\vec F=\sum_\text{out}\dot m\vec V-\sum_\text{in}\dot m\vec V\). This is the same "budget" idea as the continuity equation, with momentum instead of mass.

Which forces go into \(\sum F\)?

What is this? (the forces)

These are the real pushes on the water in the box, as on any free-body diagram: the hand or bolts holding the nozzle, the water pressure at each cut, and gravity. The momentum carried through the cuts is not a push; it goes on the other side of the account.

Draw the CV boundary and list every force that acts on the fluid inside it, exactly as in a free-body diagram:

  1. Pressure forces \(pA\) at each inlet and outlet where the pressure is not atmospheric, pointing into the CV. Free jets: \(p=0\) gauge, no force.
  2. The support force from whatever the CV cuts through: the bolts or hand holding a plate or bend, the scale under a tank. Usually this is the unknown: give it a symbol (\(R_x\), \(R_y\) or \(F_A\)) and a guessed direction; a negative answer means the other way.
  3. Weights (of the fluid and the device inside the CV), downward, when the question gives them or they matter (tanks, vertical jets). Often negligible for air jets.
  4. Viscous (friction) forces on the fluid from walls are part of the support force when the CV includes the wall. On a "smooth" or "frictionless" plate the plate can only push normal to its surface.

A general control volume. Inflow at section 1, outflow at section 2. The momentum fluxes (teal) are not forces, but they enter the equation like forces: "out minus in". The forces (orange pressures, purple weight, red support) act on the fluid inside the dashed boundary.

Ready answer · the momentum recipe (every F4.1 and F4.2 question)
  1. Do: Sketch the device, choose the CV (cut perpendicular to the flow at each inlet and outlet; enclose the plate, bend or tank), draw axes. Cutting square to the flow gives each crossing one speed and one pressure, so each deposit or withdrawal is a single \(\dot mV\) and each pressure force a single \(pA\).
  2. Do: Continuity first: \(\dot m=\rho A_1V_1=\rho A_2V_2\) (or the sum of outlets = inlet). Bernoulli (F3) if a pressure or speed is missing. Every momentum term needs \(\dot m\) and a speed, and every pressure force needs a pressure, so find them before the account can be written.
  3. Do: FBD of the CV: pressure forces \(pA\) into the CV, weight, unknown support force \(R_x,R_y\). These are the only real pushes on the water in the box: one side of the account.
  4. Do: Write \(\sum F_x=\sum_\text{out}\dot mV_x-\sum_\text{in}\dot mV_x\) and the same for \(y\); keep signs of velocity components. Each axis is its own account, and the sign of a component says whether that momentum leaves going forwards or backwards.
  5. Do: Solve for \(R_x,R_y\); give size \(\sqrt{R_x^2+R_y^2}\) and direction. State whether it is the force on the fluid or on the device (opposite). The equation gives the push on the water; by Newton's third law the device feels the same push the other way, and the question may want either.
Watch

The "out minus in" side is not a force on anything: never draw \(\dot mV\) arrows on an FBD and then add them as forces as well. Put forces on one side and momentum fluxes on the other.

You can now: write the steady momentum equation for each axis, with signed velocity components; list every force on the fluid in a control volume; apply the five-step recipe to any jet, bend or tank question.

3Jets on flat plates Going further ~6 min

What is this?

Aim a hose square at a wall. The water arrives fast, splashes out sideways along the wall and leaves with no forward motion at all. Every second the wall destroys the jet's whole forward momentum, so it must push the water back with a force equal to the momentum flux. The water pushes the wall forward just as hard.

Definition · free jet

A free jet is a stream of fluid moving through open air (or a liquid jet in air) with nothing around it. Its pressure is atmospheric, so its gauge pressure is zero everywhere along it. That is why jets in these questions have no \(pA\) forces.

A jet hitting a plate square-on

A horizontal jet (area \(A\), speed \(V\)) strikes a flat plate held at right angles to it. The water spreads out sideways along the plate and leaves the edges parallel to the plate. Choose a CV that encloses the plate and cuts the jet upstream and the sheet of water at the edges.

Left: the jet, the plate and the CV. Right: the free-body sketch of the CV. Only the support force \(R_x\) acts in \(x\): the jet and the outgoing sheets are at atmospheric pressure, and weight acts in \(y\) only.

  1. Do: In: momentum flux \(\dot mV\) in the \(+x\) direction. Out: the sheets move along the plate (\(\pm y\)), so their \(x\)-velocity is zero. The water is only steered sideways, so by the time it leaves all its forward momentum is gone.
  2. Do: \(\sum F_x=R_x=(\text{out})-(\text{in})=0-\dot mV\). The jet and sheets are free jets with no pressure force, and weight acts in \(y\), so the support is the only push in \(x\).
  3. Do: So \(R_x=-\dot mV\): the plate pushes the fluid backwards (to the left) with \(\dot mV\). The minus sign means upstream: the plate holds the water back, and the water pushes the plate downstream just as hard.
Result · jet striking a stationary plate normally \[F=\dot mV=\rho AV^2\]

In words: the force of a jet on a plate square to it = the momentum flux of the jet, because all of its forward momentum is destroyed. The jet pushes the plate downstream; the support must push upstream with the same force.

Example

A water jet of diameter 50 mm and speed 20 m/s strikes a fixed flat plate normally. Find the force needed to hold the plate.

  1. \(A=\tfrac\pi4(0.05)^2=1.963\times10^{-3}\u{m^2}\); \(\dot m=1000(1.963\times10^{-3})(20)\) \(=39.27\u{kg/s}\).
  2. \(F=\dot mV=39.27\times20=\boxed{785\u{N}}\), applied to the plate against the jet (upstream).

About the weight of an 80 kg person: fire-hose jets knock people over for this reason.

Fact · the speed along a smooth plate does not change

Follow a streamline from the jet onto the plate and out to the edge. The pressure is atmospheric at both ends, and the height hardly changes, so Bernoulli (F3) gives the same speed \(V\) leaving as arriving (if friction is ignored). Past papers state this as "the velocity magnitude remains constant as the air flows over the plate" (May 2025). Only the direction of the velocity changes, and that is what needs a force.

A plate with a hole: part of the jet passes through

What is this? (a plate with a hole)

Now drill a hole in the wall. The middle of the jet passes straight through and keeps its forward momentum, so the wall does not have to stop it. Only the ring of the jet that is splashed sideways pushes on the plate.

If the plate has a hole in the middle, a smaller jet (area \(A_2\)) carries on through it at the same speed. Only the part of the jet that is turned sideways loses its forward momentum.

  1. Do: Continuity: \(\dot m_1=\dot m_2+\dot m_\text{side}\), with \(\dot m_1=\rho A_1V\), \(\dot m_2=\rho A_2V\). Only the water turned sideways is stopped, so split the flow first.
  2. Do: \(x\)-momentum: \(R_x=\dot m_2V-\dot m_1V\) (the sideways sheets have no \(x\)-velocity). The jet through the hole leaves with all its forward momentum, so it costs the plate nothing.
  3. Do: So the force on the plate is \(\dot m_1V-\dot m_2V=\rho V^2(A_1-A_2)\). Only the blocked part of the jet, area \(A_1-A_2\), pushes on the plate.
Example

A 60 mm water jet at 15 m/s hits a plate square-on; a 20 mm hole in the plate lets a 20 mm jet through at 15 m/s. Find the horizontal force to hold the plate.

\[\begin{aligned}F&=\rho V^2\,\tfrac\pi4\big(D_1^2-D_2^2\big)\\&=1000(15^2)\tfrac\pi4(0.06^2-0.02^2)\\&=\boxed{565\u{N}}\end{aligned}\]

Dec 2018 Q2 (8 marks) is this question with an air jet: see the exam guide, F4.1.

Watch

Use the jet's own area for \(\dot m\), not the plate's area. And \(\rho\) is the density of the jet fluid: an air jet (1.23 kg/m³) gives forces about 800 times smaller than a water jet of the same size and speed.

You can now: find the force of a jet on a square plate, with or without a hole; explain why a free jet has no pressure force and why the speed along a smooth plate does not change.

4Inclined plates and curved vanes Going further ~6 min

A jet on an inclined plate

What is this?

Spray a hose at a tilted window. The water is not stopped; it slides off along the glass, and most of it carries on the way the jet was heading. Split the jet's motion into a part aimed into the glass and a part along it. A smooth plate can only push straight out of its surface, so it stops only the first part; the second part has to leave in the two streams.

Now the plate makes an angle \(\theta\) with the jet. The jet divides: a mass flow \(\dot m_2\) runs one way along the plate and \(\dot m_3\) the other way. The plate is smooth, so both leave at the jet speed \(V\). The trick is to use axes along the plate (\(s\)) and normal to it (\(n\)).

Try it · a jet on an inclined plate

Starts at the May 2025 Q6 numbers (air, 30 mm, 40 m/s, 30°). The arrow widths along the plate show the split \(\dot m_2:\dot m_3\). At \(\theta=90°\) the plate is square to the jet: equal split, \(F_A=\dot mV\).

  1. Do: Along the plate (\(s\)). A smooth plate cannot push along itself, so \(\sum F_s=0\). The jet brings in \(\dot mV\cos\theta\) along \(+s\); \(\dot m_2\) leaves with \(+V\), \(\dot m_3\) with \(-V\): \[0=\dot m_2V-\dot m_3V-\dot mV\cos\theta\] The smooth plate cannot grip the water, so the jet's along-plate momentum must leave in the two streams, and more water goes the way the jet leans.
  2. Do: Continuity: \(\dot m_2+\dot m_3=\dot m\). Two unknown flows need a second equation: no water is lost.
  3. Do: Normal to the plate (\(n\)). Only the support pushes along \(n\). The outflows have no \(n\)-velocity; the jet brings \(\dot mV\sin\theta\) into the plate. So the plate must push back with \(\dot mV\sin\theta\). The part of the jet aimed into the plate is destroyed, as on the square plate of Section 3; that is all the plate has to stop.
Result · smooth inclined plate (angle \(\theta\) between jet and plate) \[F_A=\dot mV\sin\theta\quad\text{(normal to the plate)}\] \[\frac{\dot m_2}{\dot m}=\frac{1+\cos\theta}{2},\qquad\frac{\dot m_3}{\dot m}=\frac{1-\cos\theta}{2}\]

In words: the plate only has to cancel the part of the jet's momentum that points into it, \(\dot mV\sin\theta\). The flow along the plate splits so that the "forward" share (\(\dot m_2\), the side the jet leans towards) is bigger: their difference carries exactly the jet's along-plate momentum \(\dot mV\cos\theta\).

Reading the formula
\(\dot mV\sin\theta\)
the part of the jet's momentum flux aimed straight into the plate: the only part the plate stops.
\(\dfrac{1+\cos\theta}{2}\)
the share of the water that runs on the way the jet leans (\(\dot m_2\)).
\(\dfrac{1-\cos\theta}{2}\)
the share sent back the other way (\(\dot m_3\)).
\(\theta=90°\)
a square plate: nothing to lean towards, so the split is half and half and the force is the whole \(\dot mV\).
solving for the split, step by step

1. From step 1, divide by \(V\): \(\dot m_2-\dot m_3=\dot m\cos\theta\). Same speed \(V\) everywhere cancels.

2. Add continuity \(\dot m_2+\dot m_3=\dot m\): \(2\dot m_2=\dot m(1+\cos\theta)\). Two equations, two unknowns.

3. Subtract instead: \(2\dot m_3=\dot m(1-\cos\theta)\).

4. Check: \(\theta=90°\) gives \(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\) (square-on plate, equal split); \(\theta\to0\) gives \(1,0\) (the jet just slides along the plate, no force). Limits match common sense.

Example

A 25 mm water jet at 12 m/s strikes a smooth flat plate inclined at 60° to the jet. Find the normal force needed to hold the plate and how the flow divides.

  1. \(\dot m=1000\cdot\tfrac\pi4(0.025)^2(12)\) \(=5.890\u{kg/s}\); \(\dot mV=70.69\u{N}\).
  2. \(F_A=\dot mV\sin60°\) \(=70.69(0.8660)=\boxed{61.2\u{N}}\), normal to the plate, pushing towards the jet side.
  3. \(\dot m_2/\dot m=(1+0.5)/2=\boxed{0.75}\) (along the plate, in the direction the jet leans), \(\dot m_3/\dot m=\boxed{0.25}\).

If the question wants horizontal and vertical components, resolve: with the jet along \(x\), \(F_x=F_A\sin\theta=53.0\u{N}\) and \(F_y=F_A\cos\theta=30.6\u{N}\).

A curved vane that turns the jet

What is this? (a curved vane)

A curved vane does more than stop the jet: it can send it back the way it came. Think of a ball that bounces straight back off a wall: it pushes the wall twice as hard as a lump of clay that sticks. A vane that turns the jet right round pushes twice as hard as a flat plate.

A vane is a curved blade (as in a turbine) that turns a jet through an angle \(\beta\) without stopping it. On a smooth vane the speed stays \(V\). Jet in along \(+x\); out at angle \(\beta\) measured from the \(+x\) direction.

  1. Do: Out velocity components: \(V\cos\beta\) and \(V\sin\beta\). In: \(V\) and 0. A smooth vane changes only the direction of the water, so only the components change, not the speed.
  2. Do: \(R_x=\dot mV\cos\beta-\dot mV\) \(=-\dot mV(1-\cos\beta)\). This is the forward momentum the jet loses each second; at \(\beta=180°\) it leaves backwards and loses twice its forward momentum, like the bouncing ball.
  3. Do: \(R_y=\dot mV\sin\beta-0=\dot mV\sin\beta\). The jet leaves with sideways momentum it did not have, so the vane must push it sideways, and the vane is pushed the opposite way.
Result · force of a jet on a fixed smooth vane (deflection \(\beta\)) \[F_x=\dot mV(1-\cos\beta),\qquad F_y=\dot mV\sin\beta\]

In words: the vane is pushed back along the jet by \(\dot mV(1-\cos\beta)\) and sideways, away from the direction the jet is turned, by \(\dot mV\sin\beta\). Turning the jet right round (\(\beta=180°\)) gives the largest push, \(2\dot mV\): twice the force of a flat plate.

Example

A jet with \(\dot m=5\u{kg/s}\) and \(V=20\u{m/s}\) is turned through 150° by a fixed smooth vane. Find the force on the vane.

\[\begin{aligned}F_x&=100(1-\cos150°)=186.6\u{N}\\F_y&=100\sin150°=50.0\u{N}\\|F|&=\sqrt{186.6^2+50^2}=\boxed{193\u{N}}\end{aligned}\]

\(F_y\) points opposite to the sideways velocity given to the jet.

Watch

Check which angle the question gives. "Plate inclined at 30° to the horizontal" with a horizontal jet means \(\theta=30°\) between jet and plate, so \(F_A=\dot mV\sin30°\). "Vane deflects the jet by 30°" means \(\beta=30°\), so \(F_x=\dot mV(1-\cos30°)\). Draw it.

You can now: find the normal force and the flow split for a jet on a smooth inclined plate; find both force components on a vane that turns a jet through \(\beta\); tell the plate angle \(\theta\) from the deflection \(\beta\).

5Vertical jets: gravity changes the speed Going further ~5 min

What is this?

Point the hose straight up. The water slows as it rises, exactly like a ball thrown upward. A plate held above meets the same kilograms per second as leave the nozzle, but each one arrives slower, so the push is smaller than it would be at the nozzle. Use the speed the water has when it arrives.

A jet pointing up slows down as it rises, like a ball thrown upward. Its pressure stays atmospheric, so Bernoulli (F3) between the nozzle (speed \(V_0\)) and height \(h\) gives

\[\frac{V_0^2}{2g}=\frac{V^2}{2g}+h\quad\Longrightarrow\quad V^2=V_0^2-2gh\]

In words: the jet loses speed exactly as a thrown stone would (the HKDSE equation \(v^2=u^2-2as\)). Continuity, \(A_0V_0=AV\), then says the jet gets wider as it slows.

Which speed goes into \(\dot mV\)?

The force on a plate (or cup) at height \(h\) comes from the momentum the jet still has when it arrives, so use the speed \(V\) at the plate, not the nozzle speed \(V_0\). The mass flow \(\dot m=\rho A_0V_0\) is the same at every height.

Two equally good control volumes for a vertical jet hitting a plate. Small CV (left): cut the jet just under the plate, where it moves at \(V\); weight inside is negligible. Large CV (right): cut at the nozzle, where it moves at \(V_0\); now the weight \(W_\text{jet}\) of the water column is inside and must be included.

Example

A water jet leaves a 20 mm nozzle vertically upward at 8 m/s and strikes a horizontal plate 2 m above the nozzle; the water leaves the plate horizontally. Find the force on the plate (\(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\)).

  1. \(\dot m=1000\cdot\tfrac\pi4(0.02)^2(8)=2.513\u{kg/s}\).
  2. Speed at the plate: \(V=\sqrt{8^2-2(9.81)(2)}\) \(=\sqrt{24.76}=4.976\u{m/s}\).

the large CV gives the same answer once the jet's weight is included

6Pipe bends, nozzles and reducers Going further ~9 min

What is this?

Lay a fire hose in a loop and start the pump: the loop tries to straighten. Two things push a bend outwards. The water at high pressure pushes on its walls, and the bend has to turn the water's momentum round the corner. In a pipe the pressure part is usually much the bigger, and the bolts holding a bend must resist both.

Inside a pipe the water is usually at a pressure well above atmospheric, so the pressure forces \(pA\) at the cut sections are often much bigger than the momentum fluxes. A bend, nozzle or reducing section is held by flanges and bolts (or a support); the momentum equation gives the force those bolts must carry.

Definition · bend, reducer, nozzle, elbow

A bend (or elbow) changes the direction of a pipe; a reducer (contraction, "convergent section") makes it narrower; a reducing elbow does both; a nozzle is a reducer at the end of a pipe that makes a fast free jet. A flange is the bolted ring joining two pipe pieces.

The general bend

Take a horizontal bend: water enters at section 1 along \(+x\) (pressure \(p_1\), area \(A_1\), speed \(V_1\)) and leaves at section 2 turned through angle \(\theta\) (here turning towards \(-y\), as in the past paper), with \(p_2,A_2,V_2\). Let \(\vec R=(R_x,R_y)\) be the force of the bend on the water.

Try it · forces on a reducing bend (water, \(A_1=0.01\u{m^2}\), horizontal)

\(p_2\) is found from Bernoulli (no losses, no height change); if it comes out negative the bend would need a suction outlet, which the readout points out. The red arrow is the force the support must apply to hold the bend; it equals \(\vec R\), because the water pushes the bend with \(-\vec R\) and the support has to cancel that. It starts at the May 2024 Q3 elbow with the frictionless inlet pressure 120 kPa (outlet to the atmosphere, \(p_2=0\)). Set \(\theta=0\): a straight reducer, pushed downstream.

  1. Do: Forces on the water in the CV: \(p_1A_1\) pushing in at section 1 (along \(+x\)); \(p_2A_2\) pushing in at section 2 (opposite to the outflow direction); \(\vec R\) from the bend; weight in the vertical direction only (not in this horizontal plane). These are the real pushes on the water in the box; in a pipe the two pressure forces are usually the largest.
  2. Do: Outflow direction at 2: \((\cos\theta,-\sin\theta)\). This gives the signs of the outgoing velocity components, and the outlet pressure force points the opposite way.
  3. Do: \(x\): \(p_1A_1-p_2A_2\cos\theta+R_x\) \(=\dot m(V_2\cos\theta-V_1)\). Forces on the left, momentum out minus in on the right, for the \(x\) account only.
  4. Do: \(y\): \(p_2A_2\sin\theta+R_y=\dot m(-V_2\sin\theta)\). The water arrives with no \(y\)-momentum and leaves with \(-\dot mV_2\sin\theta\), so the bend must push it towards \(-y\).
Result · force of a horizontal bend on the water \[\begin{aligned}R_x&=\dot m(V_2\cos\theta-V_1)\\&\quad-p_1A_1+p_2A_2\cos\theta\end{aligned}\] \[R_y=-\dot mV_2\sin\theta-p_2A_2\sin\theta\]

In words: support force on the water = (momentum out − momentum in) − (pressure forces). The water pushes the bend with \(-\vec R\): outwards, away from the inside of the turn and along the incoming flow. The support must push the bend with \(+\vec R\) to hold it.

Reading the formula
\(\dot m(V_2\cos\theta-V_1)\), \(-\dot mV_2\sin\theta\)
momentum out minus momentum in: what it takes to turn the water round the corner (and speed it up in a reducer).
\(-p_1A_1\)
the inlet pressure already pushes the water forward along \(+x\); the bend does not have to supply that part, so it is subtracted.
\(+p_2A_2\cos\theta\), \(-p_2A_2\sin\theta\)
the outlet pressure pushes back into the box, against the outflow; the bend has to make up for it.
\(\vec R\)
the push of the bend on the water. The water pushes the bend with \(-\vec R\), outwards: why the looped hose tries to straighten.
Example · a 90° bend

Water flows at 0.05 m³/s through a horizontal 90° bend in a 150 mm pipe. The gauge pressure is 100 kPa at both ends (losses ignored). Find the force needed to hold the bend.

  2. \(\theta=90°\): \(R_x=\dot m(0-V)-p_1A\) \(=-141.5-1767=-1909\u{N}\).

  4. Size \(\sqrt{1909^2+1909^2}=\boxed{2700\u{N}}\), at 45°. On the water it points back and down; the bend is pushed forward (\(+x\)) and up (\(+y\)), so the support pushes the bend back and down.

A nozzle (or convergent duct): pushed downstream

What is this? (a nozzle)

A nozzle speeds the water up, so you might expect it to be pulled backwards. But the water reaching it from the hose is at high pressure, and that pressure pushes forward on the narrowing walls more than enough to speed the water up. So the nozzle is pushed forward, off the hose, and the coupling must hold it on. The firefighter still feels a kick back: the hose wall behind the nozzle is stretched by the same pressure and pulls back harder, so hose and nozzle together are pushed back by the momentum the water gains.

A nozzle on the end of a hose (or a convergent section ending in a free jet) speeds the flow up, so the water gains forward momentum. Yet the nozzle is pushed off the hose, downstream. The reason is the inlet pressure.

Example · a hose nozzle

  3. \(x\)-momentum: \(p_1A_1+R_x=\dot m(V_2-V_1)\).
  4. \(R_x=6(16.98)-509.3=-407.5\u{N}\): the nozzle pushes the water backwards. The inlet pressure pushes forward with 509 N, but speeding the water up needs only 102 N, so the nozzle must hold back the rest.
  5. So the water pushes the nozzle forwards (downstream) with \(\boxed{408\u{N}}\): the coupling to the hose must hold it on, in tension.

7Tanks on scales Going further ~4 min

What is this?

Put a jug on a kitchen scale and run a tap into it. While the stream is falling in, the scale reads more than the weight of the jug and the water inside. On top of holding up the weight, the scale has to stop the falling stream's downward momentum every second. Water leaving sideways does not change the reading, because it carries no up-or-down momentum.

A tank sits on a scale while water pours in from above and runs out of side openings. The scale reads the upward force \(N\) it must supply. It is more than the weight of the tank and the water: the scale also has to stop the downward momentum of the incoming jet.

The CV surrounds the tank and all the water in it, cutting the jets at the openings. Forces: the scale's push \(N\) (up), the weights of tank and water (down). Momentum: in at the top going down; out at the sides going sideways.

  1. Do: Axes: \(y\) up. In: \(\dot m_1\) with \(V_y=-V_1\) (downward). Out: \(\dot m_2,\dot m_3\) horizontal, \(V_y=0\). Only vertical momentum affects what the scale reads.
  2. Do: \(y\)-momentum: \(N-W_\text{tank}-W_\text{water}\) \(=0-\dot m_1(-V_1)=\dot m_1V_1\). Stopping the falling jet needs an extra upward push each second, and only the scale can give it.
  3. Do: Free jets: no pressure forces at the openings. Every opening is at atmospheric pressure, so no \(pA\) term enters.
Result · tank on a scale, jet in from above, outflow sideways \[N=W_\text{tank}+W_\text{water}+\dot m_1V_1\]

In words: scale reading = weight of the tank + weight of the water inside + the momentum flux of the jet arriving from above. Equal and opposite side jets cancel in \(x\); an outflow going down would reduce the reading by its own \(\dot mV\), like a small rocket.

Reading the formula
\(W_\text{tank}+W_\text{water}\)
what the scale would read with the tap off and the water still.
\(\dot m_1V_1\)
the extra push needed every second to stop the downward momentum of the jet arriving from above.
side outflows
they move horizontally, so they carry no vertical momentum and add nothing to the reading.
Example

A tank weighing 50 N when empty stands on a scale. Its inside cross-section is 0.2 m² and the water depth stays at 0.5 m. Water enters through the top as a vertical jet of area 0.005 m² at 4 m/s and leaves through a horizontal side pipe. Find the scale reading (\(g=9.81\)).

  1. Water: \(W=\rho gV\) \(=1000(9.81)(0.2\times0.5)=981\u{N}\). Here \(V\) is the volume of water in the tank, inside area × depth, not a speed.
  2. Jet: \(\dot m_1=\rho A_1V_1\) \(=1000(0.005)(4)=20\u{kg/s}\); \(\dot m_1V_1=80\u{N}\).
  3. \(N=50+981+80=\boxed{1111\u{N}}\).

8Drag and lift Going further ~7 min

What is this?

Hold your hand out of a car window. With your palm facing forward the wind shoves it back hard; turn it edge-on and the push almost disappears. Tilt its front edge up a little and your hand rises. The push back, along the wind, is drag; the push across the wind is lift. Both come from the air pressing and rubbing on the surface of your hand.

When air or water flows past a body (or the body moves through it, which is the same thing seen from the body), the fluid pushes on every bit of the body's surface. Adding all those pushes gives one resultant force. We split it into two parts relative to the oncoming flow.

Definition · drag and lift (May 2019 Q1(h), (i))
  • Drag \(F_D\): the component of the fluid's resultant force on a body parallel to the direction of the oncoming flow (the relative velocity). It resists the motion.
  • Lift \(F_L\): the component perpendicular to the oncoming flow. On a wing it holds the aircraft up; on a racing car's upside-down wing it pushes the car down.

The fluid's pressure and friction on the surface add up to one resultant force; drag is its part along the flow and lift its part across it. Lift does not have to be "upward": it is defined by the flow direction, not by gravity.

Where drag comes from

What is this? (friction and pressure drag)

Edge-on, your hand feels mainly the air rubbing along its skin: friction drag. Palm forward, the air piles up against your palm at high pressure while a low-pressure swirl forms behind it. That pressure difference is pressure drag, and it is far bigger.

Definition · friction drag and pressure drag
  • Friction (skin-friction) drag: viscous shear stress of the fluid dragging along the surface. Because of the no-slip condition (F1: fluid touching a wall has the wall's velocity) a thin boundary layer forms, in which the speed rises from zero at the surface to the outside speed. The shear in it pulls the body backwards. It is large for long, thin bodies with much surface area (a flat plate along the flow, a ship's hull) and is reduced by a smooth surface.
  • Pressure (form) drag: the pressure on the front of the body is high (near the stagnation point, F3) but the pressure behind it is low. The difference, acting on the frontal area, pushes the body back. It is large for bluff (blunt) bodies.
What is this? (separation and wake)

Behind your palm the air cannot curl neatly round to fill the space. It breaks away at the edges and leaves a swirling, low-pressure pocket, like the swirl behind a rock in a stream. The wider that pocket, the harder the push back.

Definition · flow separation, wake and vortex shedding

Around the back of a blunt body the flow would have to slow down against rising pressure. The slow fluid in the boundary layer cannot do this: it stops and separates from the surface. Behind the separation points is a wake: a region of swirling eddies at low pressure. A wide wake means large pressure drag. Behind a cylinder the eddies peel off alternately from the top and bottom, a pattern called vortex shedding (a "Kármán vortex street"). It makes the force on the body fluctuate (wires "sing" in the wind; chimneys and bridges can vibrate) and adds drag.

Top: a streamlined body (long tapering tail) lets the flow follow its surface almost to the end: thin wake, mostly friction drag. Bottom: a bluff body (cylinder, flat plate across the flow) makes the flow separate early: wide wake with shed vortices, mostly pressure drag.

Drag and lift coefficients

What is this? (drag coefficient)

Drive twice as fast and the push on your hand becomes about four times as strong. A bigger hand feels more, and a palm feels more than an edge. The drag equation writes this as three factors: the strength of the wind, the area facing it, and a number for the shape.

Experiments show that both forces grow with the dynamic pressure \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\) of F3 and with the size of the body. Engineers therefore write:

Result · drag and lift equations \[F_D=C_D\cdot\tfrac12\rho V^2\cdot A\] \[F_L=C_L\cdot\tfrac12\rho V^2\cdot A\]

In words: force = (a dimensionless coefficient for the shape) × (dynamic pressure of the oncoming flow) × (a reference area). \(V\) is the speed of the body relative to the fluid.

Reading the formula
\(\tfrac12\rho V^2\)
the dynamic pressure (F3): how hard the wind presses on a palm that brings it to rest.
\(A\)
how much of the body meets the wind: the reference area that goes with the quoted coefficient.
\(C_D\), \(C_L\)
a shape score, found by experiment: high for a palm facing the wind, low for an edge or a fish shape.
\(V\)
speed relative to the air, squared: twice as fast, four times the force.
Definition · drag coefficient and reference area

The drag coefficient \(C_D\) (no units) measures how "draggy" a shape is, found by experiment. It is always quoted with a reference area: for blunt bodies, cars, balls and airships the frontal area (the area of the shadow the body would cast on a screen facing the flow; for a round body of maximum diameter \(D\), \(A=\pi D^2/4\)); for wings, the planform area (wing area seen from above).

9Drag force and propulsion power Going further ~3 min

What is this?

To keep your hand still out of the window, your arm must keep pushing it forward against the wind, and the car carries that push forward through the air. Pushing a force through a distance every second costs power. Drive twice as fast and the push is four times as large while it travels twice as far each second: eight times the power.

To move a body steadily through a fluid, the engine must push it forward with a thrust equal to the drag. Power is force × speed (HKDSE: \(P=Fv\)).

Result · power to overcome drag \[P=F_DV=C_D\cdot\tfrac12\rho A\cdot V^3\]

In words: propulsion power = drag force × speed. Since drag goes as \(V^2\), power goes as \(V^3\): twice the speed needs eight times the power.

Example · a car on the motorway

A car has \(C_D=0.30\) and frontal area 2.2 m². Find the drag and the power needed against drag at 100 km/h and at 120 km/h in air of density 1.23 kg/m³.

  1. Convert: \(100\u{km/h}=100/3.6=27.78\u{m/s}\).
  2. \(F_D=0.30(\tfrac12)(1.23)(27.78^2)(2.2)\) \(=313\u{N}\).
  3. \(P=F_DV=313\times27.78=8.70\u{kW}\).
  4. At 120 km/h: \(P\) scales with \(V^3\): \(8.70(120/100)^3=\boxed{15.0\u{kW}}\) (and \(F_D=451\u{N}\)). \(C_D\), \(\rho\) and \(A\) are unchanged, so only the speed ratio matters; scaling saves recomputing.

Driving 20% faster costs 73% more power against air drag.

10Aerofoils: lift, stall and flaps Going further ~7 min

What is this?

Your tilted hand out of the car window is a crude wing: tilt its front edge up, the air is thrown downward off it, and your hand is pushed up. A real wing does the same job far better. Its rounded front and sharp back let the air follow both surfaces smoothly, so it makes a large lift for a small drag.

Definition · aerofoil words
  • Aerofoil (airfoil): the cross-section shape of a wing, rounded at the front and sharp at the back.
  • Leading edge (front) and trailing edge (back); the straight line between them is the chord, of length \(c\).
  • Camber: how curved the aerofoil is (the bulge of its middle line above the chord).
  • Angle of attack \(\alpha\): the angle between the chord and the oncoming air.

Why a wing lifts

Air flowing over the curved upper surface speeds up, so (Bernoulli, F3) its pressure falls below atmospheric; under the wing the air is slowed slightly and its pressure rises. The pressure difference, over the whole wing area, pushes the wing up. The same thing seen through momentum: the wing turns the air stream downward (downwash), so by the momentum equation the air pushes the wing up. Both views are correct descriptions of the same flow.

The lift coefficient \(C_L\) rises almost in proportion to \(\alpha\) (about 0.1 per degree for a thin wing) up to a maximum \(C_{L,\max}\) at the stall angle, typically 12°–16°. Drag rises slowly at first and then steeply.

Typical shape of \(C_L\) and \(C_D\) against angle of attack for a wing section (not data for a particular aerofoil).

Stall (Dec 2018 Q3(b))

What is this? (stall)

Tilt your hand further and further. At first it rises harder; then the upward pull suddenly fades, your hand shakes and the wind just shoves it back. The air can no longer follow the curve of the top surface and breaks away from it. A wing tipped beyond its stall angle does the same.

Definition · stall

An aerofoil stalls when the angle of attack is too large: the air flowing over the upper surface can no longer follow its curve, and the flow separates from the upper surface near the leading edge. A wide turbulent wake of eddies covers the upper surface, so the low pressure there (the "suction" that gives most of the lift) is lost.

Left: normal flight, small \(\alpha\): the flow stays attached to both surfaces and leaves smoothly from the trailing edge. Right: stalled, large \(\alpha\): the flow separates from the upper surface and a wide wake forms.

Ready answer · stall: what happens and the consequence
  1. Cause: angle of attack increased beyond the stall (critical) angle, often because the aircraft flies too slowly and the pilot raises the nose to keep enough lift.
  2. Flow: the boundary layer on the upper surface separates near the leading edge; a large turbulent wake covers the upper surface (sketch the two flows).
  3. Consequence: lift drops suddenly and sharply, drag rises sharply; the aircraft loses height and may buffet or lose control (the controls sit in separated flow). Dangerous near the ground at take-off and landing.
  4. Recovery: reduce the angle of attack (nose down) and gain speed so the flow re-attaches.

Flaps (Dec 2018 Q3(c))

What is this? (flaps)

Curl your fingertips down at the back of your hand and it gives more lift at the same speed. Flaps do this to a wing: they make it more curved, so it can hold the aircraft up at a lower speed without stalling.

Definition · flaps

Flaps are hinged sections at the trailing edge of a wing that can be lowered (and often slid backwards). Lowering them increases the camber (and with sliding flaps the wing area), so the wing gives more lift at the same speed and a higher \(C_{L,\max}\). The aircraft can then fly slowly without stalling: lower take-off and landing speeds and shorter runways. Flaps also add drag, which helps the aircraft slow down and descend steeply on approach. In cruise they are retracted to keep drag low. (Slats at the leading edge do a similar job.)

Example · what flaps buy

A light aircraft of mass 1000 kg has a wing area of 15 m². \(C_{L,\max}=1.5\) with flaps up and 2.4 with flaps down. Find the stall speed (the lowest speed at which lift can still equal weight) both ways, in air of 1.23 kg/m³.

  1. Level flight at the slowest speed: \(W=C_{L,\max}\tfrac12\rho V^2A\).
  2. So \(V_\text{stall}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2W}{\rho AC_{L,\max}}}\), with \(W=9810\u{N}\). Below this speed even the largest lift coefficient the wing can reach gives less lift than the weight.
  3. Flaps up: \(\sqrt{19620/(1.23\cdot15\cdot1.5)}=26.6\u{m/s}\) (96 km/h).
  4. Flaps down: \(\sqrt{19620/(1.23\cdot15\cdot2.4)}=\boxed{21.0\u{m/s}}\) (76 km/h).

The landing run needed grows roughly as \(V^2\), so flaps cut it by about 37.5% (the ratio \(1.5/2.4=0.625\)).

Options were vortex shedding, smooth surface, high drag, high lift. A plane wants high lift and low drag; a smooth surface keeps friction drag low; vortex shedding means separated, unsteady flow (more drag, vibration). So (b) and (d).

11Head loss: energy lost in real flows Going further ~5 min

What is this?

Picture a child on a playground slide. Without friction she would reach the bottom at the speed her height allows; with friction she arrives slower, and the missing energy has warmed the slide and her clothes. Water in a pipe is the same: friction and eddies turn some of its energy into a tiny warming of the water. Head loss is that lost energy, written as the height of water it is worth.

F3's Bernoulli equation assumes an ideal (frictionless) fluid: the total head \(\tfrac{p}{\rho g}+\tfrac{V^2}{2g}+z\) stays the same along a streamline. In a real fluid, viscosity causes friction at the walls (the no-slip condition, F1) and turbulent eddies inside the flow. Some mechanical energy (pressure, kinetic and potential energy) is turned into heat (internal energy, a tiny temperature rise) and cannot be got back as pressure, speed or height. In internal flows such as pipes, viscous effects cause substantial losses. Flow-measuring devices (F3) allow for them with a discharge coefficient \(C_d\) and a velocity coefficient \(C_v\).

Definition · head loss (May 2019 Q1(g))

The head loss \(h_L\) between two sections of a flow is the mechanical energy per unit weight of fluid that is converted into heat by friction (viscous and turbulent effects) between them. It is a length, in metres: the drop in total head from the upstream to the downstream section.

Result · energy equation with losses (from section 1 to section 2, downstream) \[\frac{p_1}{\rho g}+\frac{V_1^2}{2g}+z_1=\frac{p_2}{\rho g}+\frac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2+h_L\]

In words: total head upstream = total head downstream + the head lost to friction in between. With \(h_L=0\) it is Bernoulli. The power lost is \(\rho gQh_L\) (weight flow per second × head lost).

Reading the formula
left side
the total head at section 1: pressure, speed and height energy per unit weight of water, in metres.
first three terms on the right
the same total head at section 2, downstream.
\(h_L\)
the head friction turned into heat between 1 and 2: the height the child on the slide lost without gaining speed for it.
\(h_L=0\)
no friction: Bernoulli (F3).

A tank drains through a pipe to a free jet. The energy line (total head, teal) would be level for ideal flow; in a real pipe it slopes down along the pipe and drops a little at the entrance. The fall in the line is the head loss.

Example

Water drains from a large open tank through a 50 mm pipe that ends as a free jet 10 m below the water surface. The measured jet speed is 12 m/s. Find the head loss and the power lost to friction.

  1. Section 1 = tank surface (\(p=0\), \(V\approx0\), \(z=10\u{m}\)); section 2 = jet (\(p=0\), \(V=12\), \(z=0\)).
  2. \(10=\dfrac{12^2}{2(9.81)}+h_L\), so \(h_L=10-7.34=\boxed{2.66\u{m}}\). (Ideal flow would give 14.0 m/s.)
  3. \(Q=\tfrac\pi4(0.05)^2(12)=0.02356\u{m^3/s}\).
  4. Power lost \(=\rho gQh_L\) \(=1000(9.81)(0.02356)(2.66)\) \(=\boxed{615\u{W}}\), turned into heat in the water.
Beyond the past papers · what head loss depends on (for reference)

Head loss is usually split into major losses, friction along straight pipe, \(h_L=f\,\dfrac{L}{D}\,\dfrac{V^2}{2g}\) (Darcy–Weisbach; \(f\) is a friction factor from a chart), and minor losses at bends, valves, entrances and sudden expansions, \(h_L=K\,V^2/(2g)\). Both grow roughly with \(V^2\). No past MECH1350/ENGG1350 paper has asked for these formulas; know the definition and the energy equation.

You can now: define head loss in exam words; use the energy equation with \(h_L\) between two sections; find the power lost, \(\rho gQh_L\).

12Open-channel flow: specific energy and critical depth Going further ~6 min

What is this?

Watch a river run under a sluice gate. Upstream it is deep and slow; just after the gate it is shallow and fast, yet the same water passes each second with almost the same energy. Open-channel flow is about this trade between depth and speed. A dropped pebble tells the two kinds apart: on the slow side its ripples spread upstream, on the fast side they are all swept downstream.

Definition · open channel

An open channel carries liquid with a free surface open to the atmosphere: rivers, canals, drains, gutters, spillways. Gravity drives the flow (a pipe flowing full is driven by pressure instead). For a rectangular channel of width \(b\) with water depth \(y\) and flow rate \(Q\), continuity gives the mean speed

\[V=\frac{Q}{by}.\]

Specific energy

Definition and result · specific energy

The specific energy \(E_s\) is the total head measured from the channel bed: depth (the pressure head plus height of the surface above the bed) plus velocity head.

\[E_s=y+\frac{V^2}{2g}=y+\frac{Q^2}{2gb^2y^2}\]

In words: specific energy = depth + velocity head, in metres. For a given flow rate, a deep slow flow and a shallow fast flow can have the same \(E_s\).

Reading the formula
\(y\)
the depth: energy stored as the height of the surface above the bed.
\(\dfrac{V^2}{2g}\)
energy stored as speed (velocity head).
\(\dfrac{Q^2}{2gb^2y^2}\)
the same velocity head written with the depth: shallower water must run faster to carry the same \(Q\), so this term grows.

Plot \(E_s\) against \(y\) for a fixed \(Q\): very deep flow is slow, so \(E_s\approx y\); very shallow flow is fast, so the velocity head blows up. In between, \(E_s\) has a minimum. Every larger value of \(E_s\) is reached at two depths, the alternate depths.

What is this? (critical depth)

Lower the river's energy and its deep slow depth and its shallow fast depth move towards each other. At the lowest energy that can still carry the flow they meet: that single depth is the critical depth. It is also the depth at which a ripple just stands still against the flow.

Result · critical depth (rectangular channel) \[y_c=\left(\frac{Q^2}{gb^2}\right)^{1/3},\qquad E_{s,\min}=\tfrac32y_c\]

In words: the critical depth is the depth at which a given flow has the least specific energy. Past papers print this formula (Dec 2021).

where \(y_c\) and \(E_{s,\min}\) come from

1. Minimum of \(E_s(y)\): set \(\dfrac{\mathrm dE_s}{\mathrm dy}=1-\dfrac{Q^2}{gb^2y^3}=0\). Differentiate \(y+\tfrac{Q^2}{2gb^2}y^{-2}\).

2. So \(y^3=\dfrac{Q^2}{gb^2}\), i.e. \(y_c=(Q^2/gb^2)^{1/3}\).

3. At \(y_c\): \(\dfrac{Q^2}{2gb^2y_c^2}=\dfrac{y_c^3}{2y_c^2}=\dfrac{y_c}{2}\), so \(E_{s,\min}=y_c+\tfrac12y_c=\tfrac32y_c\). Use \(Q^2/(gb^2)=y_c^3\).

4. Also \(V_c^2=Q^2/(b^2y_c^2)=gy_c\), so at critical depth \(V_c=\sqrt{gy_c}\): the Froude number below equals 1.

Definition · Froude number; subcritical and supercritical flow \[\mathrm{Fr}=\frac{V}{\sqrt{gy}}\]
  • Subcritical (tranquil): \(y\gt y_c\), \(\mathrm{Fr}\lt1\): deep and slow (a lowland river).
  • Critical: \(y=y_c\), \(\mathrm{Fr}=1\).
  • Supercritical (rapid): \(y\lt y_c\), \(\mathrm{Fr}\gt1\): shallow and fast (water rushing down a spillway).

Meaning: \(\sqrt{gy}\) is the speed of a small surface wave in water of depth \(y\). In subcritical flow a ripple can travel upstream; in supercritical flow every ripple is swept downstream, much as a supersonic jet outruns its own sound.

Try it · specific-energy diagram of a rectangular channel

The curve is \(E_s(y)\) for the chosen \(Q\) and \(b\); the dashed line is \(E_s=y\) (no velocity head). The red dot is your depth; the green dot is its alternate depth (same \(E_s\)); the nose of the curve is the critical depth. Starts at the example below.

Alternate depth

If something makes the flow change character (subcritical ↔ supercritical) without losing energy, for example flowing under a sluice gate, the new depth is the other depth with the same \(E_s\). Rearranged, it is a root of the cubic

\[y^3-E_sy^2+\frac{Q^2}{2gb^2}=0.\]

The cubic has three roots: the depth you started with, the alternate depth, and a negative root (no physical meaning). Find the alternate one with a calculator's equation solver or by trial (try values on the other side of \(y_c\)).

Example

Water flows at 3 m³/s in a 2 m wide rectangular channel, 1.2 m deep. Is the flow subcritical or supercritical? Find the alternate depth (\(g=9.81\)).

  1. \(V=\dfrac{3}{2(1.2)}=1.25\u{m/s}\); \(\mathrm{Fr}=\dfrac{1.25}{\sqrt{9.81(1.2)}}=0.364\).
  2. \(y_c=\left(\dfrac{3^2}{9.81(2^2)}\right)^{1/3}=0.612\u{m}\). Since \(y=1.2\gt y_c\) (and \(\mathrm{Fr}\lt1\)): subcritical.
  3. \(E_s=1.2+\dfrac{1.25^2}{2(9.81)}\) \(=1.2+0.0796=1.280\u{m}\).
  4. Alternate depth: solve \(y+\dfrac{0.1147}{y^2}=1.280\) for \(y\lt0.612\): The alternate depth is on the other side of \(y_c\) from the 1.2 m we started with.
trial values for the alternate depth

\(Q^2/(2gb^2)\) \(=9/(2\cdot9.81\cdot4)=0.1147\u{m^3}\).

\(y=0.30\): \(0.30+0.1147/0.09=1.574\) too big: go deeper

\(y=0.35\): \(0.35+0.1147/0.1225=1.286\) slightly too big

\(y=0.352\): \(0.352+0.1147/0.1239=1.278\) slightly too small: the root is between

Root: \(y=0.3515\u{m}\) (a calculator's solver gives 0.35151).

\(\boxed{y_\text{alt}\approx0.352\u{m}}\), supercritical: \(V=3/(2\times0.3515)=4.27\u{m/s}\), \(\mathrm{Fr}=2.30\).

Watch

Use the flow rate \(Q\) (m³/s) and the full width \(b\) in \(y_c\). Compare \(y\) with \(y_c\) to classify the flow and quote \(\mathrm{Fr}\) as a check; both must agree. The alternate depth is on the other side of \(y_c\).

You can now: find the speed and specific energy of a rectangular channel flow; classify it with \(y_c\) or \(\mathrm{Fr}\); find the alternate depth with the same \(E_s\).

14Common mistakes

✗Leaving out the pressure forces \(pA\) at the inlet of a bend or nozzle. ✓Inside a pipe the gauge pressure is not zero: \(p_1A_1\) pushes into the CV and is often ten times the momentum terms. ✗Adding pressure forces at a free jet or an outlet to the atmosphere. ✓Free jets are at atmospheric pressure: \(p=0\) gauge, no force. ✗Writing "in − out" or losing the sign of a velocity component. ✓Always "out − in", each \(V\) as a signed component on your axes. ✗Giving the force on the fluid when the question asks for the force on the plate (or the reverse). ✓The momentum equation gives the force on the fluid; the fluid's force on the device is opposite. "Force to hold it" = the support's force. ✗Using the plate's area, or the nozzle speed at the plate of a vertical jet. ✓\(\dot m=\rho A_\text{jet}V\); for a vertical jet, \(V^2=V_0^2-2gh\) at the plate. ✗Putting km/h into \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\). ✓Divide by 3.6 first. ✗Frontal area of an airship taken as length × diameter. ✓"Based on the frontal area": \(A=\pi D^2/4\) with the maximum diameter. ✗"Stall" described as the engine stopping. ✓Aerodynamic stall: flow separates from the upper surface at high angle of attack; lift drops. ✗Classifying open-channel flow by speed alone ("3 m/s is fast"). ✓Compare \(y\) with \(y_c\) (or \(\mathrm{Fr}\) with 1). ✗Giving the negative root, or the original depth, as the alternate depth. ✓The alternate depth is the other positive root, on the other side of \(y_c\).

16Concept checks

1 · Flat plate or cup?

The same jet hits (i) a flat plate square-on and (ii) a smooth cup that turns it right round. Compare the forces.

Answer

(i) \(\dot mV\); (ii) \(2\dot mV\). The cup sends the water back at \(V\), so the momentum change is \(\dot mV-(-\dot mV)=2\dot mV\): twice as much.

2 · Double the speed

A jet from a fixed nozzle doubles its speed. By what factor does the force on a square plate change? The drag on a car at double the speed? The power against drag?

Answer

Jet force \(\rho AV^2\): ×4. Drag \(\propto V^2\): ×4. Power \(\propto V^3\): ×8.

3 · Which way is the nozzle pushed?

The water speeds up through a nozzle, gaining forward momentum, so the nozzle must push it forward. Why is the nozzle nevertheless pushed downstream off the hose?

Hint

List every force on the water in the nozzle.

Answer

The inlet pressure force \(p_1A_1\) pushes the water forward more than enough to supply its momentum gain. The nozzle must hold the water back with the difference, so (third law) the water pushes the nozzle forward, downstream.

4 · Tank with a downward outlet

The tank of Section 7 has its side outlet replaced by an outlet in the floor that sends the water straight down at speed \(V_2\) (it falls past the scale). Does the scale reading go up or down, and by how much?

Answer

Down by \(\dot mV_2\): \(N-W=\dot m(-V_2)-\dot m(-V_1)\), so \(N=W+\dot mV_1-\dot mV_2\). The downward outflow acts like a rocket pushing the tank up.

5 · Lift and drag on a car

A racing car has an upside-down wing at the back that pushes the car down. Is that force lift or drag? Why might the team want it?

Answer

Lift: it is perpendicular to the oncoming air (lift is defined by the flow direction, not "up"). It presses the tyres onto the road for more grip in corners, at the cost of extra drag.

6 · Head loss and Bernoulli

A long pipe of constant diameter runs horizontally. The speed is the same at both ends. If there were no head loss, how would the pressures at the ends compare? With a head loss of 3 m of water?

Answer

Same \(V\) and \(z\): with \(h_L=0\), \(p_1=p_2\). With \(h_L=3\u{m}\), \(p_1-p_2=\rho gh_L\) \(=1000(9.81)(3)=29.4\u{kPa}\): friction is paid for by a pressure drop along the pipe.

7 · Which side of critical?

A shallow, fast stream rushes down a spillway into a deep, slow river. Which flow is supercritical? Which has \(\mathrm{Fr}\gt1\)?

Answer

The spillway flow: shallow and fast, \(y\lt y_c\), \(\mathrm{Fr}\gt1\). The river is subcritical. (Where they meet, a hydraulic jump forms: a sudden rise from the supercritical to the subcritical depth.)

\(y=0.69\): 0.7168 (just too big); \(y=0.689\): 0.7158 (too small); root \(\boxed{y\approx0.690\u{m}}\), subcritical (\(V=0.725\u{m/s}\), \(\mathrm{Fr}=0.28\)).
