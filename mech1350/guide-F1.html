Decision procedure · the Reynolds-number question (F1.3)
  1. List the data in SI. mm → m; L/min → m³/s (÷ 60 000); mm²/s → m²/s (× 10⁻⁶); cP → Pa·s (× 10⁻³). Cue: "kg/m⁻³" or "Pa·S" are misprints for kg/m³ and Pa·s.
  2. Which diameter? "Diameter of the passage/pipe" is the inner diameter. "OD × wall": \(D=\text{OD}-2t\).
  3. Which velocity? "Average velocity" or "flows at 10 m/s": use it. A flow rate: \(V=Q/A\), \(A=\pi D^2/4\) with the inner \(D\).
  4. Which formula? \(\rho\) and \(\mu\) given: \(\rho VD/\mu\). \(\nu\) given: \(VD/\nu\). Only SG: \(\rho=\text{SG}\times1000\).
  5. Classify against 2000 and 4000 and write the word (laminar / transitional / turbulent). Check: Re has no units.
Decision procedure · viscosity-unit multiple choice (F1.2)
  1. Base units. Write every option in kg, m, s: Pa·s = N·s/m² = kg/(m·s). Anything else (W = N·m/s, kg/s, m²/s) is not dynamic viscosity.
  2. Compute in SI (\(\mu=\rho\nu\), Pa·s), then convert the one number into each option's unit: × 1000 for mPa·s or cP, × 10⁶ for μPa·s.
  3. Name the distractors: a factor of 10 (cP vs P), \(\nu/\text{SG}\) instead of \(\rho\nu\).
Answer skeleton · laminar versus turbulent in words (F1.4, e.g. May 2023 Q1(a), 6 marks)

Diagram to draw: Reynolds's apparatus (tank, smooth bell-mouth entrance, glass pipe, dye needle on the axis, valve) and three dye streaks as the flow rate rises: straight, wavy, broken up.

Marker points (about one mark each): (1) laminar: particles move in smooth, parallel layers; dye stays a thin straight line; (2) turbulent: particles move haphazardly in all directions, eddies; dye spreads across the pipe; (3) mixing: none (laminar) versus strong (turbulent); (4) velocity at a point steady (laminar) versus fluctuating, three-dimensional (turbulent); (5) \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho VD/\mu\) = inertia ÷ viscous forces; (6) regimes: \(\mathrm{Re}\lt2000\) laminar, 2000–4000 transitional, \(\gt4000\) turbulent; turbulent flow has higher friction losses and a flatter velocity profile.

Model answer (exam length) is in the May 2023 Q1(a) card; the channel sketches in the May 2022 Q7 card.

Answer skeleton · "briefly explain the term" (F1.1, 1 mark each)

Marker points: (1) what property the word is about (time, position, viscosity, density, path); (2) the test in one sentence ("at a fixed point… does not change with time"); (3) a one-line example. Two sentences per term are enough; a formula helps where there is one (\(\partial V/\partial t=0\), \(M=V/c\)).

Model answers are in the May 2019 Q1 card.

2F1.1 · Flow classification & terminology

Name the property and the test: time at a point (steady), position (uniform), viscosity neglected (ideal/inviscid), orderly layers or mixing (laminar/turbulent), \(M=V/c\) (compressibility). Learn it: note-F1 §3 steady · §4 uniform · §5 inviscid · §6 Mach · §8 turbulent · §10 streamline, pathline

Ready answer · one-line definitions
TermDefinition
Steady / unsteadyproperties at a fixed point do not / do change with time
Uniform / non-uniformvelocity is / is not the same at every point at a given instant
Ideal (inviscid)viscosity neglected: no shear stress, no friction loss
Laminar / turbulentsmooth parallel layers, no mixing / random 3-D eddies, strong mixing
Compressibledensity changes matter; gas with \(M=V/c\gt0.3\)

May 2022 Q53 marksMC

Which of the following is true about turbulence?

  • (a) Flow is static.
  • (b) Velocity is not time dependent.
  • (c) Velocity is uniform.
  • (d) Flow contains three-dimensional velocity.
Hint

Type F1.1. Picture the dye breaking up in Reynolds's pipe and test each option against it.

Solution
  1. (a) False: turbulence is a state of moving fluid.
  2. (b) False: in turbulent flow the velocity at a point fluctuates with time about its mean.
  3. (c) False: eddies make the velocity vary from point to point.
  4. (d) True: particles move haphazardly in all directions, so the velocity has components along \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), even in a straight pipe.

\(\boxed{\text{(d)}}\)

May 2019 Q1(a)(b)(d)(e)(f)1 mark each(c): F2.2; (g)–(i): F4older format

Take density of water = 1,000 kg/m³, density of air = 1.23 kg/m³, acceleration due to gravity = 9.81 m/s². Briefly explain the following terms in the context of fluid flow. (9 marks for nine terms)

(a) Streamline (1 mark) Beyond the handouts

Hint

Type F1.1. A snapshot of the flow: how is the line related to the velocity arrows?

Solution
  1. A streamline is a line drawn in the flow at one instant that is tangent to the velocity vector at every point on it.
  2. So no fluid crosses a streamline; where streamlines crowd together the flow is faster.

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{tangent to the velocity}\\&\text{everywhere, at one instant}\end{aligned}}\]

1 mark per term assumed: the paper prints only "(9 marks)" for the nine terms.

(b) Pathline (1 mark) Beyond the handouts

Hint

Type F1.1. Follow one particle with a long-exposure camera.

Solution
  1. A pathline is the actual path traced by one fluid particle over a period of time (the handout's "particle path").
  2. In steady flow pathlines and streamlines coincide; in unsteady flow they differ.

\(\boxed{\text{path of a single particle over time}}\)

(d) Unsteady flow (1 mark)

Hint

Type F1.1. Stand at one fixed point and watch.

Solution
  1. Flow in which the properties (velocity, pressure, density) at a given point change with time: \(\partial V/\partial t\ne0\).
  2. Example: a bath emptying through its plughole; flow while a valve is being opened.

\(\boxed{\text{changes with time at a point}}\)

(e) Non-uniform flow (1 mark)

Hint

Type F1.1. Freeze time and compare two points.

Solution
  1. Flow in which the velocity (size or direction) changes from point to point at a given instant: \(\partial V/\partial s\ne0\) along the flow.
  2. Example: flow through a tapering nozzle or round a pipe bend.

\(\boxed{\text{velocity changes with position}}\)

(f) Ideal flow (1 mark)

Hint

Type F1.1. Which fluid property is switched off?

Solution
  1. Flow of a fluid treated as inviscid (zero viscosity, and usually incompressible): no shear stresses, so no friction and no energy loss.
  2. Used outside thin boundary layers and in the Bernoulli equation.

\(\boxed{\text{inviscid: no friction, no losses}}\)

Marker note: each mark needs the defining test, not just the opposite word ("unsteady = not steady" earns nothing).

ENGG1205 2015–16 Sem 1 Q1(d)3 marksAlso: ENGG1205 2012–13 Q1(f), 2 marksolder course

Take density of water = 1,000 kg/m³, acceleration due to gravity = 9.81 m/s². Name and define the number that determines the significance of compressibility in fluid flow.

Hint

Type F1.1. Compare the flow speed with the speed at which pressure waves travel.

Solution
  1. Name: the Mach number.
  2. Definition: \(M=V/c\), the flow speed divided by the speed of sound in the fluid.
  3. Use: \(M\lt0.3\) (air below about 100 m/s): density changes stay within about 3%, flow treated as incompressible; \(M\gt0.3\): compressibility must be included.

\(\boxed{M=\dfrac{V}{c}}\)

The printed ENGG1205 solution gives the same answer ("the Mach number, the ratio of velocity of flow to sound speed in fluid") for both sittings.

3F1.2 · Viscosity units & conversions

Everything to kg/(m·s); \(\mu=\rho\nu\), \(\rho=\text{SG}\times1000\); 1 cP = 1 mPa·s. Learn it: note-F1 §2 viscosity and units · note-H1 §5 viscosity

Ready answer · viscosity units \[\begin{aligned}1\u{Pa\,s}&=1\u{N\,s/m^2}=1\u{kg/(m\,s)}\\1\u{cP}&=10^{-3}\u{Pa\,s}=1\u{mPa\,s}\\\mu&=\rho\nu\end{aligned}\]

In words: dynamic viscosity = density × kinematic viscosity; a centipoise is a millipascal-second.

May 2022 Q13 marksMCnext: May 2022 Q2

Which is not a possible unit of dynamic viscosity?

  • (a) mPa.s
  • (b) centipoise
  • (c) Nm/s
  • (d) kg/m.s

Beyond the handouts poise is not in the 2026 handouts.

Hint

Type F1.2. Write Pa·s in kg, m and s; then do the same for each option.

Solution
  1. From \(\tau=\mu\,\Delta u/\Delta y\): \(\mu\) has units Pa ÷ (1/s) = Pa·s.
  2. Pa·s = N·s/m² = (kg·m/s²)(s)/m² = kg/(m·s). So (d) is a unit, and (a) mPa·s is too.
  3. (b) centipoise: 1 cP = 1 mPa·s, a unit of dynamic viscosity.
  4. (c) N·m/s = J/s = W, a unit of power. (Read as N/(m/s) it would be kg/s, still not viscosity.)

\(\boxed{\text{(c) Nm/s}}\)

May 2022 Q23 marksMC

What is the dynamic viscosity of a liquid C? Given the specific gravity of liquid C is 0.96 and kinematic viscosity of 610 mm²/s.

  • (a) 586 mPa.s
  • (b) 58.6 cP
  • (c) 63500 μPa.s
  • (d) 635 cP

Beyond the handouts needs \(\mu=\rho\nu\) (H1) and the centipoise.

Hint

Type F1.2. \(\rho\) from SG, \(\nu\) in m²/s, multiply; then convert to each option's unit.

Solution
  1. \(\rho=0.96\times1000=960\u{kg/m^3}\); \(\nu=610\times10^{-6}\u{m^2/s}\).
  2. \(\mu=\rho\nu=960(610\times10^{-6})\) \(=0.5856\u{Pa\,s}\).
  3. In the options' units: \(585.6\u{mPa\,s}\) \(=585.6\u{cP}\) \(=5.856\times10^{5}\u{\mu Pa\,s}\).
  4. Distractors: (b) 58.6 cP is 10 times too small; (c) is 63.5 mPa·s; (d) 635 = 610/0.96, the wrong \(\nu/\text{SG}\).

\(\boxed{\text{(a) }586\text{ mPa·s}}\)

4F1.3 · Reynolds number: compute & classify

SI units → inner \(D\) → \(V=Q/A\) → \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho VD/\mu=VD/\nu\) → compare with 2000 and 4000. Learn it: note-F1 §9 Reynolds number · §8 laminar and turbulent

Ready answer · Reynolds number and the verdict \[\mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho VD}{\mu}=\frac{VD}{\nu}=\frac{4Q}{\pi\nu D}\]

\(\mathrm{Re}\lt2000\) laminar; 2000–4000 transitional; \(\gt4000\) turbulent.

In words: Reynolds number = density × mean velocity × inner diameter ÷ viscosity; small means viscosity wins (laminar), large means inertia wins (turbulent).

May 2025 Q44 marks

Oil (\(\rho\): density 900 kg/m³; \(\mu\): viscosity 0.2 Pa·s) flows through a 0.15 m diameter pipe at 10 m/s. Calculate the Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}=\dfrac{\rho VD}{\mu}\) and classify the flow (Laminar/Turbulent/Transitional flow).

Hint

Type F1.3. Everything is already in SI; substitute and compare with 4000.

Solution
  1. \(\rho=900\u{kg/m^3}\), \(V=10\u{m/s}\), \(D=0.15\u{m}\), \(\mu=0.2\u{Pa\,s}\).
  2. Substitute:
\[\mathrm{Re}=\frac{900(10)(0.15)}{0.2}=6750\]
  1. \(6750\gt4000\).

\(\boxed{\mathrm{Re}=6750:\ \text{turbulent}}\)

Marker note: the classification mark needs the comparison written out ("6750 > 4000, so turbulent"), not just the word.

Dec 2023 Q35 marksAlso: Dec 2022 Q3 (same wording and numbers)

Determine whether the flow is laminar or turbulent if glycerin at 25 °C flows in a circular passage within a fabricated chemical processing device. The diameter of the passage is 150 mm. The average velocity of flow is 3.6 m/s. The density and viscosity of glycerin is 1258 kg/m⁻³ and \(9.6\times10^{-1}\) Pa·S.

Hint

Type F1.3. mm → m; \(\rho\) and \(\mu\) given, so use \(\rho VD/\mu\).

Solution
  1. \(D=150\u{mm}=0.150\u{m}\); \(\mu=0.96\u{Pa\,s}\); \(\rho=1258\u{kg/m^3}\).
  2. Substitute:
\[\begin{aligned}\mathrm{Re}&=\frac{1258(3.6)(0.150)}{0.96}\\&=707.6\end{aligned}\]
  1. \(708\lt2000\).

\(\boxed{\mathrm{Re}\approx708:\ \text{laminar}}\)

Glycerin is about a thousand times as viscous as water, which keeps even this fast, wide flow laminar.

the paper prints "1258 kg/m⁻³" and "Pa·S": read as 1258 kg/m³ and 0.96 Pa·s (any other reading makes Re dimensioned).

Dec 2023 Q46 marksAlso: Dec 2022 Q4 (same wording and numbers)

Determine whether the flow is laminar or turbulent if water at 70 °C flows in a hydraulic copper tube with a 32 mm OD x 2.0 mm wall. The flow rate is 285 L/min. The kinematic viscosity of water is \(4.11\times10^{-7}\u{m^2/s}\).

Hint

Type F1.3. OD = outside diameter: the water fills only the inside. Convert L/min, then \(V=Q/A\).

Solution
  1. Inner diameter \(D=32-2(2.0)=28\u{mm}\) \(=0.028\u{m}\).
  2. \(Q=285/60\,000\) \(=4.75\times10^{-3}\u{m^3/s}\).
  3. \(A=\pi(0.028)^2/4\) \(=6.158\times10^{-4}\u{m^2}\).
  4. \(V=Q/A=7.714\u{m/s}\).
  5. \(\nu\) is given, so use \(VD/\nu\):
\[\begin{aligned}\mathrm{Re}&=\frac{7.714(0.028)}{4.11\times10^{-7}}\\&=5.26\times10^{5}\end{aligned}\]

\(\boxed{\mathrm{Re}\approx5.26\times10^{5}\gt4000:\ \text{turbulent}}\)

Check with the shortcut: \(\mathrm{Re}=4Q/(\pi\nu D)\), same value. With the outside diameter (32 mm) by mistake: \(V=5.91\) m/s and \(\mathrm{Re}=4.60\times10^5\): same verdict here, but marks lost.

Marker note: the 6 marks are spread over inner diameter, \(Q\) in m³/s, \(V\), Re and the verdict; show each line.

5F1.4 · Laminar vs turbulent: Reynolds-experiment reasoning

Dye streak straight / wavy / broken up; orderly layers versus mixing eddies; Re = inertia ÷ viscous; transition at a fixed Re ≈ 2000, so \(Q_\text{crit}\propto\nu\). Learn it: note-F1 §8 Reynolds's experiment · §9 Reynolds number

Ready answer · onset of turbulence with a different fluid \[\begin{aligned}\mathrm{Re}_\text{crit}&=\frac{V_\text{crit}D}{\nu}\approx2000\\Q_\text{crit}&=\frac{2000\,\pi\nu D}{4}\end{aligned}\]

In words: in the same pipe, the flow rate at which turbulence starts is proportional to the kinematic viscosity: a more viscous fluid needs a faster flow.

May 2023 Q1(a)6 marksexplainpart (b): F3.6

Bernoulli equation \(z+\dfrac{P}{\rho g}+\dfrac{U^2}{2g}=\text{constant}\), where \(z\) is the height, \(P\) the pressure, \(\rho\) the fluid density, \(U\) the flow speed and \(g\) (= 9.81 m s⁻²) the gravitational acceleration. Density of water \(\rho\) = 1,000 kg m⁻³.

(a) State the key differences between laminar and turbulence flows. Use flow visualization in a pipe at different Reynolds numbers as an example. (6 marks)

Hint

Type F1.4 (written). Draw Reynolds's dye experiment at three flow rates, then contrast the two regimes point by point. Skeleton in At a glance.

Solution

Draw the sketch of the answer skeleton:

  1. Experiment. Water flows from a large tank through a smooth bell-mouth into a glass pipe; a thin stream of dye is injected on the axis; a valve sets the flow rate, i.e. the Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho VD/\mu\).
  2. Low Re (\(\lt2000\)), laminar. The dye stays a thin, sharp, straight line: particles move in smooth parallel layers along the pipe, with no mixing between layers.
  3. 2000–4000, transitional. The dye line starts to wave and break up in occasional bursts.
  4. High Re (\(\gt4000\)), turbulent. Soon after injection the dye breaks into eddies and colours the whole pipe: particles move haphazardly in all directions; strong mixing.
  5. Velocity. Laminar: steady at a point, parabolic profile. Turbulent: fluctuates in time, has three components, flatter profile with steep gradients at the wall, so higher friction losses.
  6. Why. Re = inertial ÷ viscous forces. At low Re viscosity damps any disturbance; at high Re inertia makes disturbances grow into eddies.

Marker note: the visualisation (sketch of the three streaks with their Re ranges) is asked for explicitly; a table of differences alone loses those marks.

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{laminar: layers, no mixing,}\\&\quad\mathrm{Re}\lt2000\\&\text{turbulent: eddies, mixing,}\\&\quad\mathrm{Re}\gt4000\end{aligned}}\]
May 2022 Q7(a)(b)(c)2 + 1 + 3 markssketch / explain

(a) Make sketches of laminar and turbulent flows in a water channel. (2 marks)

Hint

Type F1.4. Two labelled sketches of the same channel: parallel lines versus eddies; add a velocity profile to each.

Solution
  1. Laminar: straight, parallel, evenly spaced streamlines; velocity grows smoothly from zero at the bed (curved profile).
  2. Turbulent: swirling eddies of different sizes, paths crossing and mixing; a flatter mean profile, steep close to the bed.

\(\boxed{\text{layers vs eddies, labelled}}\)

(b) In Reynolds's experiment, if water is replaced by corn oil, will the onset of turbulence take place at a lower or higher flow rate? (1 mark)

Hint

Type F1.4. Turbulence starts at a fixed Re. Which property of corn oil is very different from water's?

Solution
  1. Corn oil is much more viscous, so for the same Re ≈ 2000 it needs a larger velocity.

\(\boxed{\text{at a higher flow rate}}\)

(c) Explain with your knowledge on fluid properties, dimensionless number and turbulent flow. (3 marks)

Hint

Type F1.4. One sentence for each of the three things named: the property, the number, the transition.

Solution
  1. Fluid property: corn oil's viscosity is tens of times water's, so its kinematic viscosity \(\nu=\mu/\rho\) is much larger (typically \(\nu\approx5.4\times10^{-5}\) m²/s against \(1.0\times10^{-6}\) m²/s, about 54 times).
  2. Dimensionless number: \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho VD/\mu=VD/\nu\) is the ratio of inertial to viscous forces.
  3. Turbulent flow: transition starts when Re reaches about 2000, when inertia overcomes viscous damping. In the same pipe \(V_\text{crit}=2000\nu/D\) and \(Q_\text{crit}=V_\text{crit}\pi D^2/4\), both proportional to \(\nu\): about 54 times larger for corn oil (in a 20 mm pipe: 0.031 L/s for water, about 1.7 L/s for oil).

\(\boxed{Q_\text{crit}\propto\nu:\ \text{higher for corn oil}}\)

the paper gives no corn-oil properties; typical room-temperature values (\(\mu\approx0.05\) Pa·s, \(\rho\approx920\) kg/m³) are used only to show the size of the effect. The answer needs only the direction.

6Traps across F1

Traps
  • Outside diameter. "32 mm OD × 2.0 mm wall" means \(D=28\) mm, not 32 mm or 30 mm.
  • L/min left unconverted. 285 L/min is \(4.75\times10^{-3}\) m³/s (÷ 60 000), not 4.75 or 0.285.
  • mm²/s. 610 mm²/s is \(6.1\times10^{-4}\) m²/s (× 10⁻⁶, not × 10⁻³).
  • \(\nu/\text{SG}\) instead of \(\rho\nu\). Gives 635 "cP": exactly the May 2022 distractor.
  • Steady versus uniform. Steady: no change with time at a point; uniform: no change with position.
  • Verdict without comparison. Write "6750 > 4000, so turbulent"; between 2000 and 4000 the answer is "transitional".
  • Viscosity the wrong way round. More viscous ⇒ smaller Re ⇒ turbulence needs a higher velocity and flow rate.
  • Turbulent "velocity is constant". It fluctuates in time and has three components (May 2022 Q5).
  • Opposite-word definitions. "Non-uniform = not uniform" earns nothing: state the test.

Check yourself: the F1 concept checks and find-the-mistake exercises test exactly these traps; the common mistakes list explains each one.
