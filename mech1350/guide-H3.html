Expect one 10–20 mark gate or curved-surface question: the force on each wetted face, where it acts, then moments about the hinge (often after a pressure sketch). Practise H3.4 and H3.5 first, then H3.3.

Do

H3.5Curved surface: horizontal & vertical componentsappeared 21 times · last Dec 2024

A block on a 15° slope, water in a gap behind it and seeping under it; "will the object slide?"
Do
\(W\), thrust on the back, uplift under the base; \(N=W\cos\alpha-U\); compare \(F_\text{back}+W\sin\alpha\) with \(\mu N\).
Watch
Uplift reduces the normal force and so the friction; depth at B uses \(\cos\alpha\) (the back face is tilted).
Decision procedure · gate in equilibrium (H3.4)
  1. Draw the gate alone. Mark the hinge, every wetted face (both sides!), the stop/applied force, the weight if given. Cue: "the back is exposed to the atmosphere" means no force there (gauge pressure).
  2. One force per wetted face. Plane face: \(F=\rho g h_cA\) at its CP (H3.2, or H3.3 with a surcharge). Horizontal face: uniform \(p\), acts at the face's centre. Curved face: \(F_H\), \(F_V\) (H3.5).
  3. Lines of action. Plane: the CP, written as a distance from the hinge along the gate. Circular arc: the resultant passes through the centre — if the hinge is the centre, the arc gives no moment.
  4. Moments about the hinge. Lever arm = perpendicular distance; a horizontal force uses the vertical distance, a vertical force the horizontal one. Sum clockwise = sum anticlockwise.
  5. Answer what is asked: force at the stop (direction!), moment with its sense, weight, or a limiting hinge height (the gate opens when the resultant's line crosses the hinge).
  6. Check: the answer must have the sense that keeps the gate shut; if it comes out negative, the gate is already held by the water.
Decision procedure · vertical force on a curved surface (H3.5)
  1. Where is the fluid? Above the surface (water lies on it) → real fluid column, \(F_V\) down. Below it (surface is a roof over the water) → imaginary column, \(F_V\) up.
  2. Draw the column: vertical lines from each end of the curve up to the free-surface level (the level, even if a wall is in the way). Its area = rectangle ± quarter circle / sector / segment.
  3. Mixed arc (bulging into the water): split at the lowest/highest point; the net is the difference of the two columns, often just the segment.
  4. \(F_V=\rho g\times\text{area}\times\text{width}\); then \(R\) and \(\phi\); for a circle draw \(R\) through the centre.
Answer skeleton · "sketch the pressure distribution" (H3.1)

Diagram to draw: the wetted surface; arrows perpendicular to it, pointing at it; the outline (envelope) of the arrow tips straight between corners; every corner and interface labelled with its pressure.

Marker points: (1) arrows normal to the surface and towards it; (2) zero at a free surface, \(p_0\) under a gas; (3) linear in vertical depth; (4) a kink where the liquid changes (steeper in the denser one); (5) values at top, interface and bottom with units; (6) if asked, the resultant drawn at the CP, below the centroid.

Model answer: the Dec 2023 Q2 card (two layers on a sloping wall).

2H3.1 · Pressure distribution diagrams

Walk down from the surface: \(p=p_0+\sum\rho g\,\Delta h\) (vertical depth); arrows normal to the surface, pointing at it; kink at each interface. Learn it: note-H3 §2 pressure diagrams · §10 gas & layers

Ready answer · pressure at a corner \[p=p_\text{gas}+\rho_1 g h_1+\rho_2 g h_2+\dots\]

In words: pressure at a point = pressure on the top surface + one "\(\rho g\times\) thickness" for every layer above the point, measured vertically.

Dec 2023 Q25 marksAlso: ENGG1205 2012–13 Q1(i)

Sketch the distribution of hydrostatic pressure acting on an inclined wall of a tank containing oil and water, as is shown in Figure B2. Calculate the pressures at the points \(A\) and \(B\). (Paper: density of water = 1000 kg/m³, \(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\).)

Sketch the distribution; calculate \(p_A\) and \(p_B\). (5 marks)

Hint

Type H3.1. Two layers: work out the pressure at the interface first, then add the water layer. Depth is vertical, whatever the wall's slope.

Solution
  1. O is at the free surface: \(p_O=0\) (gauge).
  2. A is under 1 m of oil (\(\rho=800\u{kg/m^3}\)): \(p_A=800(9.81)(1)\) \(=7848\u{Pa}\).
  3. B is a further 1 m down in water: \(p_B=7848+1000(9.81)(1)\) \(=17\,658\u{Pa}\).
  4. Sketch: arrows perpendicular to the 45° wall, pointing at it (outwards from the liquid). Straight from 0 at O to 7.85 kPa at A; steeper from A to B (9.81 kPa per metre of depth in water against 7.85 in oil).
\[\boxed{p_A\approx7.85\u{kPa},\quad p_B\approx17.66\u{kPa}}\]

Marker note: the kink at A and the arrows normal to the sloping wall carry the sketch marks; horizontal arrows on a 45° wall lose them.

ENGG1205 2012–13 printed \(p_A=7.85\), \(p_B=17.66\u{kPa}\): agrees.

Figure B2: a vertical wall of a water tank with a circular window. Along the wall: 2 m from the free surface to the top of the window, window diameter 3 m, 5 m from the bottom of the window to the tank bottom (depth 10 m). Specific weight of water 9.81 kN/m³.

(b) Sketch the pressure distribution on the circular window, and indicate the values of highest and lowest pressures. Draw the resultant force on the same diagram. (4 marks)

Hint

Type H3.1. The window spans depths 2 m to 5 m. The resultant comes from part (a).

Solution

Dec 2018 Q2(a)4 marksparts (b)(c): H3.3older format

A sealed container with a dimension of 1 m × 1 m × 1 m is half filled with water (Figure B2). The pressure of the air above the water is 4 kPa. The specific weight of water is 9.81 kN/m³ and the second moment of area about the centroidal axis for a rectangle is \(I_{xx,c}=\dfrac{bh^3}{12}\).

(a) Determine the pressures at the top surface of the water and the bottom of the water. Sketch the pressure variation along one container wall. (4 marks)

Hint

Type H3.1. The air presses on the water surface; the air's own weight is negligible.

Solution
  1. Top surface of the water: the air pressure, \(p=4\u{kPa}\).
  2. Bottom: \(p=4+9.81(0.5)\) \(=8.905\u{kPa}\).
  3. Wall: upper half (air) a uniform 4 kPa; lower half (water) from 4 kPa rising linearly to 8.905 kPa: a rectangle on top of a trapezoid.
\[\boxed{p_\text{top}=4\u{kPa},\ p_\text{bottom}\approx8.91\u{kPa}}\]

Marker note: the 4 kPa strip over the air half of the wall is the mark most often missed.

"Pressure of the air … 4 kPa" read as gauge (the outside is atmospheric); the air's weight is neglected, so its pressure is the same on the whole upper half.

3H3.2 · Plane surface: force and centre of pressure

\(F_R=\rho g h_cA\) at the CP, \(y_R=y_c+I_c/(y_cA)\) along the plane; rectangle from the surface: \(\tfrac23\) of its wetted length. Learn it: note-H3 §5 size of the force · §7 second moment · §8 centre of pressure · §9 no formulas needed

Ready answer · rectangle with its top edge at the free surface \[F=\tfrac12\rho g h\,(bL),\qquad y_R=\tfrac23L\]

In words: average pressure (half the bottom pressure) times the wetted area; it acts two thirds of the way down the wetted length (one third up from the bottom), whatever the slope.

Dec 2024 Q1(n)1 marktrue / false

State true (T) or false (F): For hydrostatic force acting on a plane surface, the center of pressure is always below the center of area.

Hint

Type H3.2. Look at the \(\sin^2\theta\) in \(h_R-h_c\). Is there a plane for which it vanishes?

Solution
  1. \(h_R-h_c=\dfrac{I_c\sin^2\theta}{h_cA}\): positive for any tilted or vertical plane.
  2. For a horizontal plane \(\theta=0\): the pressure is uniform and the CP is the centroid. So "always below" fails.
\[\boxed{\text{False}}\]

No official answer. "Always" is read strictly: the horizontal plane (handout slide 37) is the exception, so False. If the examiner had only non-horizontal planes in mind, True would be accepted; write the one-line reason either way.

May 2023 Q2(a)(b)6 + 6 markspart (c): H3.4

There is a self-opening rectangular flat gate that rotates around a hinge, as shown in the figure below. The gate width is 6 m. The gate is in equilibrium when the water level on the right side of the gate is \(h_1=0.8\u{m}\) and the water depth on the left side is \(h_2=0.5\u{m}\). \(\alpha=45^\circ\), and the specific weight of water is 9.81 kN/m³.

(a) Find the forces exerted on the gate by the water from each side of the gate. (6 marks)

Hint

Type H3.2. Each side is a rectangle starting at its own free surface: wetted length = depth / sin 45°.

Solution
  1. Right side: wetted length \(L_1=0.8/\sin45^\circ=1.131\u{m}\), area \(6(1.131)=6.788\u{m^2}\), \(h_c=0.4\u{m}\).
  2. \(F_1=9.81(0.4)(6.788)=26.64\u{kN}\), normal to the gate, pushing it towards the left side.
  3. Left side: \(L_2=0.5/\sin45^\circ=0.7071\u{m}\), area \(4.243\u{m^2}\), \(h_c=0.25\u{m}\).
  4. \(F_2=9.81(0.25)(4.243)=10.41\u{kN}\), normal to the gate, pushing it towards the right side.
\[\boxed{F_1\approx26.64\u{kN},\quad F_2\approx10.41\u{kN}}\]

Marker note: \(h_c\) is the vertical depth (0.4 m), but the area uses the sloping length (1.131 m).

(b) Find the locations of the center of pressure exerted by the water from each side of the gate. \(I_{xx,c}=\dfrac{bl^3}{12}\); \(y_{cp}=\bar y+\dfrac{I_{xx,c}}{\bar yA}\). (6 marks)

Hint

Type H3.2. Measure \(y\) along the gate from each side's own free surface; \(\bar y=L/2\).

Solution
  1. Right: \(\bar y=0.5657\u{m}\); \(\dfrac{I}{\bar yA}=\dfrac{L_1}{6}=0.1886\u{m}\); \(y_{cp}=0.7542\u{m}\) from the right surface.
  2. That is \(1.131-0.754=0.3771\u{m}\) up the gate from its bottom end (height 0.267 m).

  4. That is 0.2357 m up the gate from its bottom end (height 0.167 m).
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{right: }0.754\text{ m along gate}\\&\quad(0.377\text{ m from bottom})\\&\text{left: }0.471\text{ m along gate}\\&\quad(0.236\text{ m from bottom})\end{aligned}}\]

Determine the magnitude and the location of the resultant force on a circular window that is under water, as shown in Figure B2 (vertical tank wall; 2 m from the free surface to the top of the window; window diameter 3 m; 5 m below the window to the bottom). 6H3.5 · Curved surfaces: horizontal and vertical components

\(F_H\) = force on the vertical projection; \(F_V\) = weight of the (real or imaginary) fluid above, up to the free-surface level; \(R\) through the arc centre. Use the vertical-force procedure in §1. Learn it: note-H3 §12 curved surfaces · §13 water underneath · §14 circular arcs

Ready answer · the three lines for any curved part \[\begin{aligned}F_H&=\rho g h_c A_\text{proj}\\F_V&=\rho g\,(\text{area above})\,b\\R&=\sqrt{F_H^2+F_V^2},\ \phi=\tan^{-1}\frac{F_V}{F_H}\end{aligned}\]

In words: horizontal = as if the curve were flattened onto a vertical wall; vertical = the weight of the column of fluid that sits (or would sit) on it; for a circular arc the resultant points at the centre.

7H3.6 · Structure on a slope: sliding check

Weight, thrust on the back face, uplift under the base, normal reaction \(N=W\cos\alpha-U\), friction \(\mu N\); slides if the driving force exceeds \(\mu N\). Only in the older course. Learn it: note-H3 §16 block on a slope

Ready answer · sliding check \[\begin{aligned}\text{driving}&=F_\text{back}+W\sin\alpha\\\text{resisting}&=\mu\,(W\cos\alpha-U)\end{aligned}\]

In words: push down the slope = water thrust on the back + the downhill part of the weight; grip = friction coefficient × (the part of the weight pressing into the slope, minus the uplift). Safe if grip ≥ push.

8Traps across H3

Traps
  • Sloping length for depth. \(h_c\) and pressures use vertical depth; areas and \(y\) use the length along the plane (May 2023: 0.4 m vs 1.131 m).
  • CP at the centroid. It is \(I_c/(y_cA)\) lower; only a horizontal plane has CP = centroid (Dec 2024 Q1(n) is False).
  • CP formula from the wrong origin. \(y\) starts at the (real or equivalent) free surface, never at the top of the gate (May 2022 says so).
  • Losing the vacuum sign. Mercury higher on the tank side means negative gauge pressure: the oil level drops to 2.14 m, not rises (May 2024).
  • \(F_V\) the wrong way. Water under the surface pushes up (imaginary column); a bulging arc nets the segment, upward (May 2021).
  • Stopping \(F_V\) at the wall. The column always runs to the free-surface level, even where a wall or arm is in the way (Dec 2024: 4 + π/4).
  • Lever arms along the force. The arm is the perpendicular distance from the hinge; a horizontal force at B uses B's height, not its distance along the gate.
  • Forgetting a face. Both sides of a gate, and a horizontal leg under water (ENGG1205 2012–13: 137.3 kN on AB) all enter the moment sum.
  • No sense stated. Moments need "clockwise / anticlockwise as drawn"; forces need a direction (towards the oil, upward).
