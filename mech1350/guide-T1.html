One type, one test. Learn the card and the ready answer; the skeleton below covers a written "which system is it?" part if one is ever set.

Looks like
"Which of the following should be considered as an open system? Select ALL correct answers": jet engine, piston–cylinder, wind turbine, thermometer.
Do
For each device ask only "does mass cross the boundary while it works?" Yes → open. No, but heat or work crosses → closed. Nothing → isolated.
Watch
Heat, work and electric current are energy, not mass; a moving piston is still closed. More than one option can be right.
Answer skeleton · "is this an open, closed or isolated system? explain"

Diagram to draw: the device with a dashed boundary; straight arrows where matter crosses, wavy arrows where heat or work crosses.

Marker points: (1) state where the boundary is; (2) list what crosses: mass (name the stream) and energy (heat, work, electricity); (3) conclude with the definition: "mass crosses, so open (control volume)" or "only energy crosses, so closed (control mass)"; (4) if asked, say whether it is at steady state (nothing inside changes with time).

2T1.1 · System types (isolated / closed / open)

Does mass cross the boundary? Yes → open; only energy → closed; nothing → isolated. Learn it: note-T1 §8 thermodynamic systems · §9 wind-turbine example

Ready answer · the one-question test
SystemMass crosses?Energy crosses?Usual exam examples
Open (control volume)yesyesjet engine, wind turbine, turbine, pump, compressor, nozzle, running car engine

In words: a flow of matter makes a system open; heat, work or electricity alone never do.

May 2025 Q33 marksMC, select all

Which of the following(s) should be considered as an open system for thermodynamic calculation? Select ALL correct answers.

  • (a) A jet engine
  • (b) A piston-cylinder device
  • (c) A wind turbine
  • (d) A liquid-in-glass thermometer
Hint

Type T1.1. For each device, picture it working and ask whether any matter (air, fuel, gas, liquid) flows across its boundary.

Solution
  1. (a) Jet engine: air flows in at the front, fuel is injected, hot gas leaves at the back. Mass crosses: open.
  2. (b) Piston–cylinder device: the gas is trapped under the piston. Heat crosses the walls and work is done as the piston moves, but no mass crosses: closed.
  3. (c) Wind turbine: air flows through the rotor; electricity leaves through the cable. Mass crosses: open.
  4. (d) Liquid-in-glass thermometer: the liquid is sealed in the glass; only heat crosses: closed.

\(\boxed{\text{(a) and (c)}}\)

Marker note: no marking scheme is published; give one line of reason per option ("air flows through", "gas trapped, only heat and work") in case part marks are given.

Reading used: (b) is the sealed piston–cylinder of handout slide 38 (no valves mentioned), so closed. A piston–cylinder with open intake or exhaust valves, as in a running car engine, would be open.

Practice 13 marksMC, select allWritten for these notes

Which of the following should be considered as a closed system? Select ALL correct answers.

  • (a) A sealed rigid tank of air heated by a flame
  • (b) A car engine running on a test bench
  • (c) A smartphone battery being charged
  • (d) A water pump running in a pipeline
  • (e) A sealed balloon warmed in the sun, so that it expands
Hint

Type T1.1. Closed means energy may cross but mass may not. A boundary that moves is still allowed.

Solution
  1. (a) Heat enters, no air leaves: closed.
  2. (b) Air and fuel in, exhaust out: open.
  3. (c) Electric current is energy (electrical work), not mass: closed.
  4. (d) Water flows in and out: open.
  5. (e) The boundary (the rubber) moves outward and the gas does work on the air around it, but no gas escapes: closed.

\(\boxed{\text{(a), (c), (e)}}\)

Practice 24 marksexplainWritten for these notes

A household refrigerator runs with its door shut.

(a) Take the whole refrigerator (cabinet, food and the sealed refrigerant circuit) as the system. Is it open, closed or isolated? Name every interaction with the surroundings. (3 marks)

Hint

Type T1.1 (written). The refrigerant circulates, but does it ever leave the refrigerator? Then list each energy flow through the dashed boundary.

Solution
  1. Boundary: the outer surface of the refrigerator (dashed line (a)).
  2. Mass: the refrigerant moves round a sealed loop inside the boundary, and the door is shut, so no mass crosses.
  3. Energy: electrical work enters through the cable; heat leaves from the coils at the back; a little heat leaks in through the walls and door seals.
  4. Only energy crosses, so the refrigerator is a closed system (control mass).
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{closed: work in,}\\&\text{heat in and out, no mass}\end{aligned}}\]

Marker note: one mark each for the boundary, the list of interactions and the conclusion with its reason.

(b) Now take only the compressor as the system. What type is it? (1 mark)

Hint

Type T1.1. Draw the boundary tightly around the compressor and follow the refrigerant.

Solution
  1. Refrigerant vapour flows into the compressor and out again (dashed line (b)): mass crosses; electrical work also enters.

\(\boxed{\text{open system (control volume)}}\)

The same machine gives a different answer for a different boundary: always say where your boundary is.

Practice 34 marksWritten for these notes

A mercury U-tube manometer (\(\rho=13\,600\u{kg/m^3}\), \(g=9.81\u{m/s^2}\)) is connected to a gas pipe. The local atmospheric pressure is 100 kPa.

(a) The mercury on the pipe side stands 15 cm lower than on the open side. Find the gas pressure as gauge (kPa and psi) and absolute (kPa and bar). (3 marks)

Hint

Gauge pressure is the \(\rho gL\) of the column. The gas side is lower, so the gas pushes harder than the air.

Solution
  1. Column: \(\rho gL=13\,600(9.81)(0.15)\) \(=20\,012\u{Pa}\), so the gauge pressure is 20.01 kPa.
  2. In psi: \(20\,012/6894.76=2.90\u{psi}\).
  3. Absolute: \(p=100+20.01=120.01\u{kPa}\), that is \(1.200\u{bar}\).
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}p_\text{gauge}&=20.0\u{kPa}=2.90\u{psi}\\p_\text{abs}&=120.0\u{kPa}=1.200\u{bar}\end{aligned}}\]

(b) Another day the mercury on the pipe side stands 15 cm higher. Find the absolute pressure and the vacuum reading. (1 mark)

Hint

Same column height, but now the air pushes harder than the gas.

Solution
  1. \(p_\text{abs}=100-20.01=79.99\u{kPa}\); the vacuum reading is the column, 20.01 kPa.
\[\boxed{p_\text{abs}\approx80.0\u{kPa},\ p_\text{vac}\approx20.0\u{kPa}}\]
Practice 44 marksWritten for these notes

An air-conditioner is rated at 12 000 Btu/h of cooling (1 Btu = 1.0551 kJ). It runs at this rate for 6 hours and cools a room from 32 °C to 24 °C.

(a) Express the cooling rate in kW and the heat removed in 6 h in kWh and MJ. (2 marks)

Hint

Btu/h is a rate (power). Convert Btu to J and hours to seconds; energy = power × time.

Solution
  1. \(12\,000\times1055.1=1.266\times10^{7}\u{J}\) each hour.
  2. Rate: \(1.266\times10^{7}/3600=3517\u{W}\), that is 3.517 kW.
  3. 6 h: \(3.517\times6=21.10\u{kWh}\); \(21.10\times3.6=75.97\u{MJ}\).
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}\dot Q&\approx3.52\u{kW}\\Q&\approx21.1\u{kWh}\approx76.0\u{MJ}\end{aligned}}\]

Marker note: this is heat removed, not electricity used; the electrical input is smaller (T4 explains why).

(b) Give the final room temperature in K and °F, and the temperature drop in K and °F. (2 marks)

Hint

A temperature needs the offset (+273.15, +32); a difference does not.

Solution
  1. \(24+273.15=297.15\u{K}\); \(1.8(24)+32=75.2\ ^\circ\mathrm F\).
  2. Drop: \(32-24=8\ ^\circ\mathrm C\) \(=8\u{K}\) \(=1.8\times8=14.4\ ^\circ\mathrm F\).
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}T&=297.15\u{K}=75.2\ ^\circ\mathrm F\\\Delta T&=8\u{K}=14.4\ ^\circ\mathrm F\end{aligned}}\]

4Traps across T1

Traps
  • "Energy crosses, so it is open." Only mass makes a system open; heat and work cross closed systems all the time.
  • Electric current counted as mass. A charging battery or the wind turbine's cable carries energy, not matter.
  • Moving boundary called open. A piston or an expanding balloon changes the volume, not the mass: still closed.
  • No boundary stated. The fridge is closed, its compressor open: say where your boundary is.
  • Only one box ticked. "Select ALL" questions usually have two or more right options.
  • Gauge used as absolute. Add \(p_\text{atm}\) to a gauge reading; subtract a vacuum reading from it.
  • Offset added to a difference. A drop of 8 °C is 8 K and 14.4 °F, never 281.15 K or 46.4 °F.
  • Rate confused with amount. kW and Btu/h are rates; kWh, MJ and Btu are amounts of energy.

Check yourself: the T1 concept checks and find-the-mistake exercises test exactly these traps; the common mistakes list explains each one.
