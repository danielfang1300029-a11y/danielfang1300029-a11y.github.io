Introduction to fluid dynamics

0Where this chapter sits

H1–H4 studied fluids at rest; this chapter starts Section C, fluids in motion. Picture a river: it can look the same every time you visit or surge after a storm, and its water can slide by smoothly like honey or churn like a tap at full blast. This chapter gives the words for sorting such flows and the two numbers (Mach and Reynolds) that decide them. F2 then adds conservation of mass (continuity), F3 the Bernoulli equation, and F4 further past-paper topics.

Chapter on one page

  2. §2 Viscosity is a fluid's internal friction: honey resists layers sliding over each other, water hardly does. \(\tau=\mu\,\Delta u/\Delta y\).
  3. §3 Six yes/no questions sort every flow. Steady: one spot of the river looks the same every visit.

  7. §7 Why a rocket nozzle narrows, then widens. Optional on first read

  9. §9 The Reynolds number is the score of inertia against viscosity: below 2000 laminar, above 4000 turbulent. Most predictable F1 marks
  10. §10 Section C treats a pipe as one-dimensional: one mean speed per cross-section. Streamlines are the lanes of the flow.
  11. §11 Flow visualisation: dye, smoke, tufts and particles make a flow visible.

Sign and symbol conventions for this chapter
  • Coordinates. \(x, y, z\) are Cartesian axes; the velocity components along them are \(u, v, w\), positive in the \(+x, +y, +z\) directions. Time is \(t\).
  • Shear flows. \(y\) is measured from the wall into the fluid, and \(u\) is the velocity parallel to the wall. A shear stress \(\tau\) is reported as a size (positive); its direction is found from the physics (the fluid drags a slower neighbour forwards and a faster neighbour backwards).
  • Pipe flow. \(V\) (also written \(\bar V\)) is the mean velocity over the cross-section, \(V=Q/A\), and \(d\) or \(D\) is the inner diameter, the one the fluid actually fills.
  • Units. SI throughout: lengths in m (convert mm and cm first), flow rates in m³/s (1 L = 10⁻³ m³), viscosity in Pa·s. Dimensionless numbers (\(\mathrm{Re}\), \(M\)) have no unit, which is a useful check.
  • Temperatures inside a speed-of-sound formula must be absolute (kelvin).

1What fluid mechanics studies ~5 min

What is this?

A fluid is anything that keeps flowing for as long as you push it sideways. Push sideways on the top of a rubber eraser: it leans over a little, then stops and holds. Push sideways on the surface of honey or water and it keeps sliding for as long as you push. Water, air, honey and ketchup are all fluids; what makes them different is how hard they resist that sliding.

Why do different fluids flow differently?

Picture three scenes: water running from a tap in a smooth glassy stream; a close-up of a water stream broken into a rough, bubbly column; and two hands squeezing a bottle of tomato ketchup. Water pours at once; ketchup sits in the bottle until you squeeze or shake it, then suddenly flows. The property behind the difference is viscosity (Section 2), and the change from a smooth to a rough stream is the change from laminar to turbulent flow (Section 8). The whole chapter answers this question.

How do ships move and aeroplanes fly?

Think of a large cruise ship at sea and an airliner above the clouds. Both move by pushing on a fluid and being pushed back: the propeller throws water (or the jet engine throws air) backwards, and the reaction drives the vehicle forwards; the wings are shaped so that the air pushes them up (lift), while the water or air resists the motion (drag). Predicting these forces is a central job of fluid mechanics (lift and drag are treated in F3 and F4).

Definition · fluid mechanics

Fluid mechanics is the branch of physics that studies how fluids (liquids and gases) behave, at rest and in motion, and how they interact with forces and with other physical systems. A fluid is a substance that keeps deforming (flowing) for as long as a shear force acts on it, however small; a solid instead deforms a fixed amount and stops. Fluid statics (hydrostatics, Section B) studies fluids at rest; fluid dynamics (Section C) studies fluids in motion.

The quantities that describe a fluid fall into four groups:

GroupQuestion it answersQuantities
Primary physical quantitiesWhat state is the fluid in?density \(\rho\), pressure \(p\), temperature \(T\)
Kinematic propertiesHow does the fluid move?velocity, flow rate
Dynamic propertiesWhat forces act?viscosity, shear stress, surface tension, drag and lift forces
Energy and conservation quantitiesHow does energy change?specific weight (\(\gamma=\rho g\), weight per unit volume), …

("Kinematic" means describing motion without asking about its cause; "dynamic" means involving forces.) Fluid mechanics is built on principles you already know: Newtonian mechanics (\(F=ma\) applied to bits of fluid), thermodynamics (T1–T4) and three conservation laws: mass (F2's continuity equation), momentum (F4) and energy (F3's Bernoulli equation is an energy statement).

Engineering applications

Fluid mechanics turns up in at least eight application areas:

  • Vehicle aerodynamics: a racing car with air streaming over it (shaping to cut drag and create downforce).
  • Water supply and distribution systems: a city with its network of underground pipes (pipe sizing, pumps, pressure losses).
  • Aircraft design and propulsion: a flying vehicle with rotors (lift, thrust, drag).
  • Energy engineering: wind turbines (extracting power from moving air; also hydro turbines and power-plant cooling).
  • High-speed transportation: a high-speed train (drag, pressure waves in tunnels).
  • Electronics cooling and thermal management: a circuit board with a copper heat sink and fans (forced convection, T3).
  • Weather and environmental systems: a satellite image of a hurricane (atmosphere and ocean flows, pollution spread).
  • Biomedical devices: the heart and blood vessels (blood flow, artificial valves, stents).

You can now: say what fluid mechanics studies and what makes a substance a fluid; sort fluid quantities into the four groups; name the three conservation laws Section C is built on.

2Viscosity: Newtonian and non-Newtonian fluids ~10 min

What is this?

Drag a spoon slowly across the top of a jar of honey. The honey touching the spoon moves with it, the honey at the bottom stays put, and every layer in between slides over the one beneath it, like the cards of a deck pushed sideways. Viscosity is how hard the fluid resists this layer-on-layer sliding: honey a lot, water very little. For an ordinary fluid, the faster the layers slide past each other, the harder you have to push.

Put a thin layer of fluid between two flat plates. Hold the bottom plate still and pull the top plate sideways at a steady speed \(U\). The fluid touching each plate moves with that plate (it "sticks" to it: the no-slip condition, Section 5), so the fluid velocity \(u\) rises from 0 at the bottom to \(U\) at the top. Each layer slides over the one below it, like the cards in a deck pushed sideways. This sliding is called shearing, and the fluid resists it with a shear stress \(\tau\).

Definitions · shear stress, rate of strain, viscosity
  • Shear stress \(\tau\): the tangential (sliding) force per unit area between neighbouring layers of fluid, or between the fluid and a wall. Unit Pa = N/m².
  • Rate of strain (rate of shear deformation) \(\Delta\theta/\Delta t\): how fast a small fluid element is being distorted, measured by how fast its originally right angle closes up. Unit rad/s, i.e. 1/s.
  • Viscosity \(\mu\) (dynamic viscosity): the fluid's resistance to being sheared, "internal friction". Honey and engine oil have high viscosity; water and air low.
Law · Newtonian fluid \[\tau=\mu\frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t}=\mu\frac{\Delta u}{\Delta y}\] \[\mu\ne\text{function}\Big(\frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t}\Big)\]

In words: shear stress = viscosity × rate of strain, and the rate of strain equals the velocity gradient (change in velocity per unit distance across the flow). For a Newtonian fluid the viscosity is a constant of the fluid (at a given temperature): it does not depend on how fast the fluid is sheared, so \(\tau\) is directly proportional to the rate of strain.

Reading the formula
\(\tau\)
the sideways drag one layer feels from its neighbour, per square metre: what you feel through the spoon.
\(\Delta u\)
how much faster the upper layer moves than the lower one.
\(\Delta y\)
the distance between the two layers.
\(\Delta u/\Delta y\)
how violently the layers slide past each other: a big speed difference across a thin gap is fierce shearing.
\(\mu\)
the fluid's stickiness: large for honey, small for water. For a Newtonian fluid it is a fixed number, so twice the sliding rate means twice the drag.
why the rate of strain equals the velocity gradient \(\Delta u/\Delta y\)

1. Take a small rectangle of fluid of height \(\Delta y\). Its bottom edge moves at \(u\), its top edge at \(u+\Delta u\). The velocity changes across the layer.

2. In a short time \(\Delta t\) the top edge moves \(\Delta u\,\Delta t\) further than the bottom edge, so the vertical sides tilt. Relative displacement = relative speed × time.

3. The tilt angle is \(\Delta\theta\approx\tan\Delta\theta=\Delta u\,\Delta t/\Delta y\). For small angles (in radians) \(\tan\theta\approx\theta\).

4. Divide by \(\Delta t\): \(\Delta\theta/\Delta t=\Delta u/\Delta y\). The rate at which the right angle closes equals the velocity gradient.

5. In the limit of a very thin layer this is the derivative \(\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\); Newton's law of viscosity is usually written \(\tau=\mu\,\mathrm du/\mathrm dy\).

What is this? (non-Newtonian fluids)

For water or honey the stickiness is fixed: shear it twice as fast and it pushes back twice as hard. Some fluids change their stickiness as you shear them. Ketchup gets runnier when you shake it; cornflour paste goes stiff when you punch it; toothpaste will not move at all until you squeeze hard enough.

Definition · Newtonian and non-Newtonian fluids

A Newtonian fluid has shear stress directly proportional to rate of strain (straight line through the origin on a \(\tau\)–rate graph). Water, air, petrol, most oils and glycerin are Newtonian.

A non-Newtonian fluid has shear stress that varies with rate of strain in a non-linear way. We still write \(\tau=\mu\,\Delta\theta/\Delta t\), but now the "viscosity" depends on the rate of strain:

\[\mu=\text{function}\Big(\frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t}\Big)\]

(this is called the apparent viscosity: the slope from the origin to a point on the curve).

Plot \(\tau\) (up) against \(\Delta\theta/\Delta t\) (across) for four fluid types:

  • Newtonian fluid: a straight line through the origin; its slope is \(\mu\).
  • Shear-thinning: a curve through the origin that rises steeply at first and then flattens: the faster you shear it, the thinner (less viscous) it gets. Examples: ketchup, paint, blood, shampoo. Shaking or squeezing a ketchup bottle shears it, and it runs.
  • Shear-thickening: a curve through the origin that starts flat and bends upwards: the faster you shear it, the thicker it gets. Example: cornflour mixed with a little water ("oobleck"), which you can poke slowly but which goes hard if you punch it.
  • Bingham plastic: a straight line that does not start at the origin: it needs a minimum stress (the yield stress) before it flows at all, then behaves like a Newtonian fluid. Examples: toothpaste, mayonnaise, drilling mud. Toothpaste stays on the brush until you squeeze hard enough.

Try it · shear stress against rate of strain

The thick curve is \(\tau=\tau_y+K(\Delta\theta/\Delta t)^n\) (a standard power-law model; arbitrary units); the grey curves are the four basic types for comparison. \(n=1\), \(\tau_y=0\): Newtonian. \(n\lt 1\): shear-thinning. \(n\gt 1\): shear-thickening. \(\tau_y\gt 0\), \(n=1\): Bingham plastic. The readout gives the apparent viscosity \(\tau/(\Delta\theta/\Delta t)\) at a rate of 2.

Example · a plate dragged over oil

A flat plate of area 0.5 m² slides at a steady 0.4 m/s over a 2 mm film of oil (\(\mu=0.25\u{Pa\,s}\), Newtonian) on a fixed table. Assume the velocity in the film rises in a straight line from 0 at the table to 0.4 m/s at the plate. Find the shear stress, the force needed to keep the plate moving and the power used.

Free-body diagram of the plate: pull \(F\) forwards, viscous drag \(\tau A\) backwards from the oil, weight \(W\) down and the oil's pressure force \(N\) up. Steady speed means no acceleration, so the forces balance.

  1. Velocity gradient: \(\Delta u/\Delta y=0.4/0.002=200\u{s^{-1}}\).
  2. Shear stress: \(\tau=\mu\,\Delta u/\Delta y=0.25(200)=50\u{Pa}\).
  3. Horizontal balance on the plate: \(F=\tau A=50(0.5)=\boxed{25\u{N}}\). The plate moves at a steady speed, so your pull must exactly cancel the oil's drag on its whole underside.
  4. Power = force × speed \(=25(0.4)=\boxed{10\u{W}}\), all turned into heat in the oil.

Units of viscosity and kinematic viscosity

What is this? (kinematic viscosity)

Dynamic viscosity \(\mu\) says how sticky a fluid is. Kinematic viscosity \(\nu\) divides that stickiness by the density, so it says how sticky the fluid is for its weight. A light fluid has little mass to slow down, so the same stickiness smooths out its motion more quickly. That is why air, far less sticky than water, has the larger kinematic viscosity.

From \(\tau=\mu\,\Delta u/\Delta y\), \(\mu=\tau\div(\Delta u/\Delta y)\), so its unit is Pa ÷ (1/s) = Pa·s. Viscosity and \(\nu=\mu/\rho\) are first met in H1 (Theme 2). Section C asks you to convert between their many units:

Result · kinematic viscosity \[\nu=\frac{\mu}{\rho}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\mu=\rho\,\nu\]

In words: kinematic viscosity = dynamic viscosity ÷ density. To go back, multiply by density, with \(\rho=\mathrm{SG}\times1000\u{kg/m^3}\).

Going further · viscosity unit conversions (exam type F1.2)
QuantitySI unit and equivalentsOlder (CGS) units
dynamic viscosity \(\mu\)Pa·s = N·s/m² = kg/(m·s)poise: 1 P = 1 g/(cm·s) = 0.1 Pa·s; 1 cP = 1 mPa·s
kinematic viscosity \(\nu\)m²/sstokes: 1 St = 1 cm²/s = 10⁻⁴ m²/s; 1 cSt = 1 mm²/s = 10⁻⁶ m²/s

Handy anchors: water at 20 °C has \(\mu\approx1.0\u{mPa\,s}=1\u{cP}\) and \(\nu\approx1.0\times10^{-6}\u{m^2/s}=1\u{cSt}\). Air at 20 °C has \(\mu\approx1.8\times10^{-5}\u{Pa\,s}\) and \(\nu\approx1.5\times10^{-5}\u{m^2/s}\): air's \(\mu\) is 55 times smaller than water's, but because air is so light its \(\nu\) is 15 times larger.

To test whether a unit can be a viscosity, reduce it to kg, m and s: it must come out as kg/(m·s). For example N·m/s = (kg·m/s²)·m/s = kg·m²/s³, which is a power (watt), not a viscosity.

Watch

Convert \(\nu\) given in mm²/s by multiplying by \(10^{-6}\) (1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m²), not \(10^{-3}\). And go from \(\nu\) to \(\mu\) by multiplying by \(\rho\), never dividing by SG.

You can now: state Newton's law of viscosity and read a \(\tau\)–rate-of-strain graph; tell Newtonian, shear-thinning, shear-thickening and Bingham fluids apart, with an example of each; find the shear stress and drag force on a plate over a thin film; convert between Pa·s, cP, m²/s and cSt.

3Classifying flows; steady and unsteady flow ~5 min

What is this?

Stand on a bridge and watch one spot of a river. If the water there moves the same way every time you visit, the flow is steady, even though it is different water each time and even though the water near the bank is slower than in the middle. After a storm the river rises and surges, and the same spot looks different from one minute to the next: the flow is unsteady. Steady and unsteady are only one of six ways to sort a flow; each way asks one simple question about it.

Real flows are complicated, so engineers first sort a flow into types; each type allows certain simplifications. There are six pairs, and each pair is one yes/no question about the flow:

PairThe questionSection
steady / unsteadyDo the properties at a fixed point change with time?3
uniform / non-uniformDoes the velocity change from place to place at one instant?4
laminar / turbulentDo the fluid layers slide smoothly, or mix chaotically?8–9
viscous / inviscid ("inviscous")Does viscosity matter for the flow?5
compressible / incompressibleDoes the density change noticeably?6
one-, two-, three-dimensionalHow many velocity components (and directions of variation) matter?10

The pairs are independent: one flow can be, for example, steady, non-uniform, turbulent, viscous and incompressible all at once (water in a tapering garden-hose nozzle). Exam answers should name the property each word is about.

How to classify a flow (exam type F1.1), one question per pair:

  1. Do: Fix one point and ask whether its readings change with time: steady or unsteady. This is visiting the same spot of the river on different days.
  2. Do: Freeze one instant and compare different points: uniform or non-uniform. This is one photograph of the whole river: is every part flowing the same way at the same speed?
  3. Do: Ask whether the layers slide smoothly or mix: laminar or turbulent (decide with \(\mathrm{Re}\), Section 9). Smooth sheets like pouring honey, or churning like a tap at full blast.
  4. Do: Ask whether viscosity matters where you are looking: viscous or inviscid. Friction only matters where neighbouring layers move at very different speeds, which happens next to walls.
  5. Do: Ask whether the density changes: liquids (except sudden stops such as water hammer) and gases with \(M\lt 0.3\) are incompressible. A fluid is squeezed only when it is pushed hard or fast compared with how quickly pressure travels through it.
  6. Do: Count the velocity components that matter: one-, two- or three-dimensional. Each component you can drop makes the model simpler to solve by hand.
Definition · steady and unsteady flow

Steady flow: the flow properties (velocity, pressure, density) at a given point do not change with time.

Unsteady flow: the flow properties at a given point change with time.

The test is always "stand at one fixed point and watch": if the readings there stay the same, the flow is steady, even if they differ from those at another point. Examples:

  • Steady: water pouring smoothly from a hose held still (top left); an aircraft cruising at constant speed (top right). Seen from the aircraft, the air flows past the wings in the same pattern every second.
  • Unsteady: water gushing and splashing from a drain outlet in a wall (bottom left), its flow surging; wind gusts over vehicles on a road (bottom right): the wind speed at a point rises and falls from moment to moment.
Examples
  • A tap left running at a fixed setting: steady. Opening or closing the tap: unsteady while the flow changes.
  • A bath emptying through its plughole: unsteady, because the water level and so the outflow speed fall with time.
  • Water in a pipe fed from a huge reservoir with a constant level: steady.
  • Blood in an artery: unsteady (pulsing with every heartbeat).
Watch

Turbulent flow is strictly unsteady: the velocity at a point flickers all the time (Section 8). Engineers still call a turbulent pipe flow "steady" when its time-averaged values do not change. In a definition question, say "at a fixed point" and "with time"; do not confuse steady (time) with uniform (position).

You can now: list the six pairs of flow types and the question behind each; define steady and unsteady flow using "at a fixed point" and "with time"; classify everyday flows as steady or unsteady.

4Uniform and non-uniform flow ~3 min

What is this?

Now take one photograph of the whole river at a single instant. In a straight canal of constant width the water moves at the same speed in the same direction all along it: uniform flow. Where the river squeezes through a narrow gorge, the water there runs faster than upstream and bends with the banks: non-uniform flow. Steady asks about time at one spot; uniform asks about place at one instant.

Definition · uniform and non-uniform flow

Uniform flow: the flow velocity has the same magnitude and direction at every point in the flow; the flow conditions do not change with position.

Non-uniform flow: at a given instant, the velocity is not the same at every point.

Picture two channels drawn with velocity arrows. In a straight channel of constant width, every arrow is the same length and points the same way: uniform. In a channel that narrows from left to right (a converging duct), the arrows get longer towards the narrow end and tilt to follow the sloping walls: non-uniform. The same amount of fluid must squeeze through a smaller gap, so it speeds up (this is F2's continuity equation).

Ready answer · the four combinations
Uniform (same everywhere)Non-uniform (changes with position)
Steady (no change with time)constant flow rate in a long straight pipe of constant diameter (velocity taken as the section mean)constant flow rate through a tapering nozzle or a pipe bend
Unsteady (changes with time)flow speeding up in a long straight pipe as a valve is openeda flood wave travelling down a river; the flow in a nozzle while the pump starts
Watch

Strictly, real fluid flowing in a pipe is never perfectly uniform: it is zero at the wall (no slip, Section 5). "Uniform" in pipe problems means the mean velocity is the same along the pipe, which is the one-dimensional picture of Section 10.

You can now: define uniform and non-uniform flow; give an example of each of the four steady/unsteady and uniform/non-uniform combinations; explain why "uniform" in a pipe refers to the mean velocity.

5Viscous and inviscid flow ~6 min

What is this?

Think of wind blowing over a field. High up, the air sweeps along freely; right at the ground it is held still, and only a thin layer of air near the grass feels the drag. Around a wing this thin layer is the boundary layer, and outside it the air flows as if it had no viscosity at all: inviscid flow. Inside a pipe the walls surround the fluid on every side, so the slowed layers fill the pipe and viscosity matters everywhere: viscous flow.

Definition · inviscid and viscous flow

An inviscid flow is one in which viscous effects do not significantly influence the flow, so they are neglected. If the shear stresses are small and act over such small areas that they do not noticeably change the flow field, the flow can be treated as inviscid.

In a viscous flow the effects of viscosity are important and cannot be ignored.

An inviscid fluid is an idealisation (every real fluid has some viscosity). Past papers also call it an ideal flow or a "frictionless" flow: a flow with no viscosity, hence no internal friction and no energy lost to friction (F3's Bernoulli equation assumes exactly this).

External flows and the boundary layer

Experiments show that the main class of flows that can be modelled as inviscid is external flows: flows of an unbounded fluid outside a body, such as air around a wing, a car or a building. Any viscous effects are confined to a thin layer next to the body's surface, called the boundary layer.

Definition · boundary layer

The boundary layer is the thin layer of fluid next to a solid surface in which the velocity rises from zero at the surface (no slip) to almost the full outside velocity. Inside it, velocity gradients are large, so viscous shear stresses matter; outside it, the flow can be treated as inviscid.

Picture the external flow around an airfoil: streamlines curve smoothly around a wing section, and the flow away from the wing is inviscid. A thin boundary layer hugs the wing surface; magnified, it has an edge, and small velocity arrows inside it grow from zero at the surface. Other external flows include red blood cells streaming around a body inside a blood vessel, and a paraglider in the open air.

Internal flows are viscous

Viscous flows include the broad class of internal flows, where the fluid is enclosed by walls: flows in pipes, hydraulic machines, conduits (closed ducts) and open channels (rivers, canals). In such flows viscous effects cause large "losses" (mechanical energy turned into heat by friction), which is why huge amounts of energy must be spent pumping oil and gas along pipelines. The losses come from the no-slip condition and the shear stresses it creates.

What is this? (no-slip)

Fluid touching a solid sticks to it. That is why a car's bonnet is still dusty after a motorway drive: the air right at the surface moves with the car, so it never blows the dust off. Moving away from the surface, the fluid speed has to climb from zero, and that climb is where the friction comes from.

Definition · no-slip condition

A real (viscous) fluid in contact with a solid surface moves with the surface: at a stationary wall its velocity is zero. The velocity therefore has to rise from zero at the wall to its larger value away from it, and this velocity gradient produces shear stresses \(\tau=\mu\,\Delta u/\Delta y\).

For viscous internal flow (a) in a pipe and (b) between two parallel plates, the velocity profiles look alike: arrows that are zero at the walls and longest in the middle, forming a rounded (parabolic) profile \(u(r)\) across the pipe radius \(r\), and \(u(y)\) across the gap between plates.

Going further · the laminar pipe profile

For laminar flow in a round pipe of radius \(R\) the profile is exactly a parabola, \(u=u_\text{max}(1-r^2/R^2)\), and the mean velocity is half the centre-line velocity: \(\bar V=u_\text{max}/2\). This is why the Reynolds number uses the section-mean velocity \(\bar V=Q/A\), not the maximum. A turbulent profile is much flatter in the middle and steeper at the wall.

You can now: define viscous and inviscid flow; explain why external flows are inviscid outside a thin boundary layer while internal flows are viscous; state the no-slip condition and sketch the velocity profile in a pipe and between plates.

6Compressible and incompressible flow; the Mach number ~7 min

What is this?

Put your finger over the end of a syringe full of air and push the plunger: the air squashes easily. Do the same with water and the plunger barely moves. That is compressibility. A flowing gas is only squashed if it is pushed faster than it can get out of the way. Pressure changes travel through the gas at the speed of sound, so when the flow is slow compared with sound, the gas ahead feels the push coming and moves aside, and its density hardly changes.

Definition · compressible and incompressible flow

A flow is incompressible if the density of every bit of fluid stays (practically) constant. It is compressible if the density changes noticeably because of pressure changes in the flow.

The key points:

  • All fluids are compressible, even water: their density changes when the pressure changes.
  • Under steady conditions with small pressure changes, the analysis can usually be simplified by treating the flow as incompressible, with constant density.
  • Liquids are hard to compress, so under most steady conditions they are treated as incompressible. But in some unsteady conditions very large pressure differences can occur, and compressibility must be included even for liquids. Example: water hammer.
  • Gases are very easy to compress; in high-speed flow they must be treated as compressible, taking the pressure-driven density changes into account.
Definition · water hammer

Water hammer is the pressure surge (and banging noise) when a liquid flowing in a pipe is stopped suddenly, for example by a quick-closing tap or valve. The moving column cannot stop all at once; a pressure wave travels back along the pipe at the speed of sound in the liquid, and the pipe briefly sees a very high pressure. Because the wave depends on the liquid being slightly compressible, this is a compressible, unsteady liquid flow.

Going further · how big is water hammer?

The Joukowsky estimate is \(\Delta p=\rho c\,\Delta V\), with \(c\approx1480\u{m/s}\) for water. Stopping water flowing at 2 m/s instantly: \(\Delta p=1000(1480)(2)\approx2.96\u{MPa}\), the pressure at the bottom of a 300 m column of water. This is why long pipelines close their valves slowly.

Low-speed gas flows and the Mach number

What is this? (Mach number)

The Mach number compares the flow's speed with the speed at which pressure changes travel through it, the speed of sound. At \(M=0.1\) the push travels ten times faster than the flow, so the gas has plenty of warning and simply flows aside. Near \(M=1\) there is no warning, and the gas piles up and is squeezed.

Low-speed gas flows, such as ordinary winds in the atmosphere, are also treated as incompressible. The test uses the Mach number.

Definition · Mach number \[M=\frac{V}{c}\]

In words: Mach number = speed of the flow (or of the object through the fluid) ÷ speed of sound in that fluid. \(V\) is the gas speed and \(c\) is the speed of sound.

Ready answer · when is a gas flow incompressible?
  • If \(M\lt 0.3\): density varies by at most about 3%, and the flow is assumed incompressible. For standard air this means speeds below about 100 m/s.
  • If \(M\gt 0.3\): the density variations affect the flow, and compressibility effects must be accounted for.

Old-course answer (ENGG1205): "the Mach number \(Ma=V/c\), flow velocity divided by the speed of sound in the fluid, determines the significance of compressibility."

The speed of sound is the speed at which a small pressure disturbance travels through the fluid. When \(M\) is small, pressure news travels far ahead of the flow, so the fluid "gets out of the way" smoothly without being squeezed. When \(M\) approaches 1, the fluid has no warning and is compressed.

Going further · speed of sound in a gas, and "about 100 m/s"

For an ideal gas \(c=\sqrt{\gamma RT}\), with \(\gamma=1.4\) and \(R=287\u{J/(kg\,K)}\) for air, and \(T\) in kelvin. Standard air (15 °C = 288.15 K):

\[\begin{aligned}c&=\sqrt{1.4(287)(288.15)}\\&=340.3\u{m/s}\end{aligned}\]

so \(M=0.3\) is \(0.3(340.3)=102\u{m/s}\): "about 100 m/s". In water \(c\approx1480\u{m/s}\), so ordinary liquid flows have tiny Mach numbers.

"At most 3%" is the usual rule-of-thumb figure. The isentropic formula gives a density difference of about 4.4% between moving air at \(M=0.3\) and the same air brought to rest (see the slider below); books quote "a few per cent", 2–5%, depending on what they compare. For exams quote the rule: \(M\lt 0.3\) means incompressible.

Examples · compressible or not?
  1. A car at 30 m/s in air at 15 °C: \(M=30/340.3=0.088\lt 0.3\): incompressible.
  2. An airliner cruising at 250 m/s at high altitude where the air is −50 °C (223.15 K): \(c=\sqrt{1.4(287)(223.15)}=299.5\u{m/s}\), so \(M=250/299.5=0.835\gt 0.3\): compressible. Cold air carries sound more slowly, so the speed of sound must be worked out at the cruising temperature, not at 15 °C.
  3. Water at 20 m/s in a pipe: \(M\approx20/1480=0.014\): incompressible (unless the flow is stopped suddenly: water hammer).

7Why does a rocket nozzle first narrow and then expand? ~4 min

What is this?

Squeeze the end of a garden hose and the water speeds up: less room, the same flow, so faster water. Gas slower than sound behaves the same way, so a rocket nozzle first narrows to speed the gas up. But once the gas is faster than sound, every gain in speed makes it expand so much that it needs more room, not less. So beyond the narrowest point the nozzle must widen to let the gas keep accelerating.

A rocket nozzle has a converging section (getting narrower), a narrowest point called the throat, and a diverging section (getting wider), coloured green to red from top to bottom. Arrows show the gas flowing down through it. Beside it, three curves are plotted against position along the nozzle (position runs downwards):

  • the area ratio \(A/A^*\) (area ÷ throat area): falls to 1 at the throat, then rises;
  • the Mach number \(M\): rises all the way, with \(M\lt 1\) above the throat and \(M\gt 1\) below it; it equals 1 exactly at the throat;
  • the pressure ratio \(p/p_0\) (pressure ÷ pressure in the combustion chamber): falls all the way.

Compare two cases:

Incompressible flowSupersonic compressible flow in an expanding nozzle
\(\rho AV=\text{constant}\) with \(\rho\) fixed, so \(A\uparrow\ \Rightarrow\ V\downarrow\): a wider pipe means slower flow (the garden-hose rule).\(A\uparrow,\ \rho\downarrow,\ V\uparrow\): in the widening part the gas expands so much that its density falls faster than the area grows, and it speeds up.
Result · area–velocity relation \[\frac{\mathrm dA}{A}=(M^2-1)\frac{\mathrm dV}{V}\]

In words: the fractional change in area = \((M^2-1)\) × the fractional change in velocity. Below the speed of sound (\(M\lt 1\)) the bracket is negative, so to speed the gas up (\(\mathrm dV\gt 0\)) the area must shrink. Above it (\(M\gt 1\)) the bracket is positive, so to keep speeding up the area must grow. At \(M=1\) the bracket is zero: that can only happen where \(\mathrm dA=0\), at the throat.

Reading the formula
\(\mathrm dA/A\)
the fractional change in nozzle area as you move along it: negative where it narrows, positive where it widens.
\(\mathrm dV/V\)
the fractional change in gas speed: positive all the way down a rocket nozzle.
\(M^2-1\)
the switch: negative below the speed of sound (the garden-hose rule, narrow to speed up), positive above it (widen to speed up).
\(M=1\)
the switch is at zero, so the area must stop changing there: the throat.

That is the answer to the question in the heading: the gas leaves the combustion chamber slowly (subsonic), so the nozzle first narrows to accelerate it up to the speed of sound at the throat; after that it must widen to accelerate it further to supersonic exhaust speeds, which gives more thrust. (This converging–diverging shape is called a de Laval nozzle.) For \(M\) near 0 the relation becomes \(\mathrm dA/A=-\mathrm dV/V\), the incompressible rule.

where \(\mathrm dA/A=(M^2-1)\,\mathrm dV/V\) comes from

1. Mass conservation: \(\rho AV=\) constant. Take logs and differentiate: \(\dfrac{\mathrm d\rho}{\rho}+\dfrac{\mathrm dA}{A}+\dfrac{\mathrm dV}{V}=0\). \(\ln\rho+\ln A+\ln V=\) constant.

2. Momentum along the flow, friction neglected (Euler's equation): \(\mathrm dp=-\rho V\,\mathrm dV\). A pressure drop pushes the gas faster (the idea behind Bernoulli, F3).

3. Speed of sound: \(\mathrm dp=c^2\,\mathrm d\rho\). This is the definition of \(c\) for a small, fast (isentropic) disturbance.

4. Combine 2 and 3: \(\dfrac{\mathrm d\rho}{\rho}=-\dfrac{V\,\mathrm dV}{c^2}=-M^2\dfrac{\mathrm dV}{V}\). \(V^2/c^2=M^2\).

5. Put into 1: \(\dfrac{\mathrm dA}{A}=-\dfrac{\mathrm d\rho}{\rho}-\dfrac{\mathrm dV}{V}=(M^2-1)\dfrac{\mathrm dV}{V}\).

Numbers: at \(M=0.5\), \(\mathrm dA/A=-0.75\,\mathrm dV/V\) (a 1% speed-up needs 0.75% less area); at \(M=2\), \(\mathrm dA/A=+3\,\mathrm dV/V\) (a 1% speed-up needs 3% more area).

Going further · scope

8Laminar and turbulent flow; Reynolds's experiment ~6 min

What is this?

Pour honey from a spoon: it falls in a smooth, glassy ribbon, every layer sliding neatly beside the next. That is laminar flow. Now open a tap at full blast: the stream turns rough, white and noisy, with water swirling from one side of the stream to the other. That is turbulent flow. The same water can do both: a gently opened tap gives a glassy stream too.

Definition · laminar and turbulent flow

Laminar flow: all fluid layers move parallel to each other and do not cross one another ("lamina" = a thin sheet).

Turbulent flow: fluid layers cross each other and do not move parallel; the fluid mixes in irregular swirls called eddies.

Draw two pipes. In the laminar pipe, straight parallel arrows run the length of the pipe. In the turbulent pipe, the inside is filled with tangled loops and curls. A tap stream is the standard demonstration: a gently opened tap gives a clear, glassy, smooth stream (laminar); opened wide, the stream becomes rough, cloudy and noisy (turbulent), the two streams of Section 1.

Reynolds's dye experiment

What is this? (the dye experiment)

Reynolds made the difference visible with a thin thread of dye in a glass pipe. In smooth flow the thread stays a straight line, because no water crosses from one layer to another. Once the flow turns turbulent, the swirls tear the thread apart and the whole pipe turns coloured, like a drop of ink stirred into a glass of water.

In 1883 Osborne Reynolds ran water from a tank through a glass pipe with a smooth, well-rounded entrance (so the entrance itself does not stir the water) and injected a thin streak of dye into the middle of the flow through a fine tube. The flow rate \(Q=VA\) is set by a valve. The set-up: pipe of diameter \(D\), dye reservoir above, dye streak running along the pipe. As the flow rate rises, the dye shows three pictures:

  • Laminar (top, low flow rate): the dye stays a thin, sharp, straight line all along the pipe.
  • Transitional (middle): the line starts to wobble and wave, with occasional bursts.
  • Turbulent (bottom, high flow rate): soon after the injection point the dye breaks up into irregular curls and spreads across the whole pipe, colouring the water.

What the experiment shows:

  • Laminar flow happens when the flow is sufficiently slow, turbulent when it is sufficiently fast.
  • In laminar flow the fluid particles move in a very orderly way, all in straight lines parallel to the pipe wall; there is essentially no mixing of neighbouring particles. (The dye spreads only by slow molecular diffusion.)
  • In turbulent flow mixing is very significant: fluid particles move haphazardly in all directions.
  • So it is impossible to trace the motion of individual particles in turbulent flow.

9The Reynolds number ~8 min

What is this?

Why is honey laminar but a tap at full blast turbulent? Inside every flow there is a tug of war. The fluid's momentum (its inertia) carries any small wobble onwards and lets it grow into a swirl; viscosity smooths the wobble out. The Reynolds number is the score: inertia divided by viscosity. Honey is so sticky that viscosity wins; fast water in a wide pipe carries so much momentum that inertia wins.

Whether a flow is laminar or turbulent depends not on the speed alone but on one combination of speed, size and fluid properties, the Reynolds number.

Definition · Reynolds number \[\mathrm{Re}\equiv\frac{\rho\bar Vd}{\mu}=\frac{\bar Vd}{\nu}\]

\(\rho\) = density, \(\mu\) = viscosity, \(\bar V\) = section-mean velocity, \(d\) = (inner) diameter of the pipe; \(\nu=\mu/\rho\).

In words: Reynolds number = density × mean velocity × pipe diameter ÷ viscosity (or mean velocity × diameter ÷ kinematic viscosity). It has no units.

Reading the formula
\(\rho\)
a denser fluid carries more momentum, so wobbles keep going: pushes towards turbulence.
\(\bar V\)
faster flow, more momentum: pushes towards turbulence (the tap at full blast).
\(d\)
a wider pipe spreads the speed differences over a larger distance, so the viscous stresses that damp wobbles are weaker: pushes towards turbulence.
\(\mu\) on the bottom
a stickier fluid damps wobbles harder: pushes towards laminar (the honey).
no units
a pure score, so the same 2000 and 4000 limits work for any fluid in any pipe.
Result · pipe-flow regimes, found by experiment \[\mathrm{Re}\ \begin{cases}\lt 2000 & \text{laminar flow}\\ 2000\text{ to }4000 & \text{transitional flow}\\ \gt 4000 & \text{turbulent flow}\end{cases}\]

What the Reynolds number means

\(\mathrm{Re}\) compares two effects on a bit of fluid. Inertia (the fluid's tendency to keep moving, to overshoot and swirl) grows with \(\rho V^2\). Viscosity (internal friction that smooths out disturbances) gives stresses of size \(\mu V/d\). Their ratio:

\[\frac{\text{inertial}}{\text{viscous}}\sim\frac{\rho V^2}{\mu V/d}=\frac{\rho Vd}{\mu}=\mathrm{Re}\]

In words: Re = inertial forces ÷ viscous forces. Small Re: viscosity wins, any wobble is damped out, the flow stays laminar. Large Re: inertia wins, small disturbances grow into eddies, the flow becomes turbulent.

So the flow becomes turbulent more easily with a faster flow, a bigger pipe, a denser fluid, or a less viscous fluid. Honey in a straw is laminar; water in a water main is turbulent.

checking that Re has no units

\(\rho\): kg/m³; \(V\): m/s; \(d\): m; \(\mu\): Pa·s = kg/(m·s).

Numerator \(\rho Vd\): (kg/m³)(m/s)(m) = kg/(m·s). m·m/m³ = 1/m.

Divide by \(\mu\) in kg/(m·s): the units cancel completely. If your Re ends up with a unit, a quantity was not in SI.

Ready answer · Reynolds-number questions (exam type F1.3)
  1. Do: Convert everything to SI: mm → m, L/min → m³/s (÷ 1000 ÷ 60), mm²/s → m²/s (× 10⁻⁶), cP → Pa·s (× 10⁻³). Re is a pure number only if the units cancel; one unconverted mm leaves the score off by a factor of 1000.
  2. Do: Find the inner diameter: for a tube "OD × wall", \(d=\text{OD}-2\times\text{wall}\). The tug of war happens in the fluid, and the fluid only fills the inside of the tube.
  3. Do: If a flow rate is given, mean velocity \(\bar V=Q/A\) with \(A=\pi d^2/4\). Re needs the typical speed of the fluid, and \(Q/A\) spreads the flow evenly over the cross-section.
  4. Do: \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho\bar Vd/\mu\), or \(\bar Vd/\nu\) if \(\nu\) is given. This is the score: inertia divided by viscosity.
  5. Do: Compare with 2000 and 4000 and state the regime in words. The limits come from Reynolds-type experiments: below 2000 viscosity damps every wobble, above 4000 inertia wins.

Shortcut when \(Q\) is given: \(\mathrm{Re}=\dfrac{4\rho Q}{\pi\mu d}=\dfrac{4Q}{\pi\nu d}\).

\(\mathrm{Re}=708\lt 2000\), so the flow is laminar. Glycerin is about a thousand times more viscous than water, which keeps even this fast, wide flow laminar.

10One-, two- and three-dimensional flow ~7 min

What is this?

In general fluid moves in all three directions at once, but often one or two of those motions are tiny and can be dropped. Think of traffic on a long straight motorway: cars mostly move along the road, a little sideways when they change lane, and never up. Knowing the speed along the road tells you nearly everything. Water in a long straight pipe is the same, and that one-dimensional model, one mean speed per cross-section, is the one Section C uses.

In general a fluid flow is three-dimensional and depends on position and time. The velocity at position \(\vec r=(x,y,z)\) at time \(t\) is a vector with three components:

\[\vec V=\vec V(\vec r,t)\] \[\vec V=u(\vec r,t)\,\mathbf i+v(\vec r,t)\,\mathbf j+w(\vec r,t)\,\mathbf k\]

In words: the velocity is made of a part \(u\) along \(x\), a part \(v\) along \(y\) and a part \(w\) along \(z\); each part may depend on where you are and when you look. \(\mathbf i\), \(\mathbf j\), \(\mathbf k\) are unit vectors (length-1 arrows) along the \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) axes.

Reading the formula
\(\vec V\)
the velocity arrow of the fluid at one place and one moment.
\(u\,\mathbf i\)
the part of the arrow along \(x\): on the motorway, the speed along the road.
\(v\,\mathbf j,\ w\,\mathbf k\)
the parts along \(y\) and \(z\): sideways and up. Drop the ones that are tiny to get a 2-D or 1-D flow.
\((\vec r,t)\) in each part
each part may change from place to place (non-uniform) and from moment to moment (unsteady).

The earlier definitions in symbols: the flow is uniform if \(u, v, w\) do not depend on the position \((x,y,z)\), and steady if they do not depend on the time \(t\).

Two-dimensional flow

In many engineering applications the flow can be treated as two-dimensional: one velocity component (say \(w\)) is zero or much smaller than the other two, and the flow varies essentially only in two directions (\(x\) and \(y\)). The velocity reduces to

\[\vec V=u\,\mathbf i+v\,\mathbf j\]

with \(u, v\) functions of \((x,y)\) (and possibly \(t\)). Example: air flowing over the middle part of a long wing, where every slice along the span looks the same.

One-dimensional flow

It is sometimes possible to simplify further by neglecting two of the velocity components, leaving a one-dimensional flow field:

\[\vec V=u\,\mathbf i\]

where \(u\) may still vary across the section of flow. Typical examples are fully developed flows (flows whose velocity profile no longer changes along the pipe) in long uniform pipes and open channels. One-dimensional problems need only elementary analysis and can usually be solved by hand. In Section C this is the standard model: one mean velocity per cross-section, \(\bar V=Q/A\).

Picture a pipe that narrows partway along: the uniform velocity arrows \(V_1\) across the wide part become longer arrows \(V_2\) across the narrow part. Each section is described by a single velocity, so the flow is treated as one-dimensional (along the pipe), even though it is non-uniform (it changes from section 1 to section 2).

Three-dimensional flow and particle paths

A flow is three-dimensional if all three velocity components are equally important. Such problems are the most complex and the hardest to solve. Picture \(x, y, z\) axes and a curved line, the particle path: a small fluid particle A is at position \(\vec r_A(t)\) at time \(t\) and has moved along the curve to \(\vec r_A(t+\delta t)\) at time \(t+\delta t\).

Going further · streamline, pathline, streakline (exam type F1.1)

Streamlines (F3) and particle paths need precise definitions. May 2019 Q1 asked for both.

What is this? (streamline)

Streamlines are the lanes of traffic in a flow. Each lane follows the direction the fluid is heading at that instant, and fluid never changes lane, so none crosses a streamline. Each lane carries a fixed amount of fluid, so where the lanes are squeezed closer together, that fluid must move faster to get through. Lanes are drawn at one instant; a pathline is instead the route one particular car actually drove.

  • Pathline: the actual path traced out by one fluid particle over a period of time (the "particle path" of Section 10). A long-exposure photo of one floating light.
  • Streamline: a line drawn in the flow at one instant that is everywhere tangent to the velocity. No fluid crosses a streamline; closely spaced streamlines mean fast flow.
  • Streakline: the line formed at one instant by all the particles that have passed through one fixed point, such as the dye line in Reynolds's experiment or smoke from a chimney.

In steady flow the three coincide. In unsteady flow they differ.

You can now: write the velocity as \(u\mathbf i+v\mathbf j+w\mathbf k\) and state uniform and steady in those terms; say when a flow can be treated as two- or one-dimensional; define pathline, streamline and streakline.

11Flow visualisation ~2 min

What is this?

Air and water are mostly invisible, so their motion has to be made visible. Flow visualisation is like scattering leaves on a river to watch the current: you add something you can see, such as dye, smoke, tufts or tiny particles, and it traces out the lanes, the swirls and the slow layers near walls.

Flow visualisation means making an invisible flow visible: dye in water (as in Reynolds's experiment), smoke in a wind tunnel, small tufts of wool taped to a car, or tiny particles lit by a laser sheet. The patterns show streamlines and streaklines, boundary layers, laminar and turbulent regions and where the flow separates from a body.

Look at the length in the Reynolds-number formula.

Answer

\(d\) is the diameter: \(\mathrm{Re}=900(10)(0.15)/0.2\) \(=6750\gt 4000\), so the flow is turbulent. Using the radius halves \(\mathrm{Re}\) and gives the wrong regime.

3 · A student's viscosity conversion

"SG = 0.96, \(\nu=610\u{mm^2/s}\). \(\mu=\nu/\mathrm{SG}=610/0.96=635\), so \(\mu=635\u{cP}\)."

Hint

What must \(\nu\) be multiplied by to get \(\mu\), and what are its units?

Answer

\(\mu=\rho\nu\), with \(\rho=960\u{kg/m^3}\) and \(\nu=610\times10^{-6}\u{m^2/s}\): \(\mu=0.5856\u{Pa\,s}\) \(=586\u{mPa\,s}=586\u{cP}\). Dividing by SG is wrong both physically and in units (the answer would still be in mm²/s).

15Concept checks

1 · Classify the flow

A large tank empties through a short tapered nozzle at its base. Is the flow in the nozzle steady or unsteady? Uniform or non-uniform?

Answer

Unsteady: the water level falls, so the outflow speed at a fixed point in the nozzle decreases with time. Non-uniform: the nozzle narrows, so the velocity changes from place to place. (If the tank is kept topped up, the flow becomes steady but stays non-uniform.)

2 · Bigger pipe, same flow rate

Water flows at a fixed flow rate \(Q\). The pipe diameter is doubled. What happens to the mean velocity and to \(\mathrm{Re}\)?

Hint

\(\mathrm{Re}=4Q/(\pi\nu d)\).

Answer

The area is 4 times larger, so \(\bar V\) falls to a quarter. \(\mathrm{Re}=\bar Vd/\nu\) changes by \(\tfrac14\times2=\tfrac12\): it halves. At fixed \(Q\), a bigger pipe is more likely to be laminar.

3 · Air versus water

Air and water flow through identical pipes at the same mean speed at 20 °C. Which has the larger Reynolds number?

Answer

Water. \(\mathrm{Re}=\bar Vd/\nu\) and water's \(\nu\) (\(1.0\times10^{-6}\)) is 15 times smaller than air's (\(1.5\times10^{-5}\u{m^2/s}\)), so water's Re is 15 times larger, even though air's \(\mu\) is smaller. Compare \(\nu\), not \(\mu\).

4 · A fan and a fighter jet

Can the air blown by a desk fan (5 m/s) be treated as incompressible? The air over a fighter jet at 600 m/s?

Answer

Fan: \(M\approx5/340=0.015\lt 0.3\): incompressible. Jet: \(M\approx600/340=1.8\gt 0.3\) (supersonic): compressible.

5 · Ketchup and toothpaste

Why does ketchup flow once the bottle is shaken, and why does toothpaste stay on the brush?

Answer

Ketchup is shear-thinning: shaking shears it fast, its apparent viscosity drops and it runs. Toothpaste is (close to) a Bingham plastic: the small stress from its own weight on the brush is below its yield stress, so it does not flow at all.

6 · Why inviscid outside the boundary layer?

Air is viscous. Why can the flow around a wing be treated as inviscid?

Answer

Viscous stress is \(\mu\times\) velocity gradient. Away from the wing the velocity changes only gradually and air's \(\mu\) is tiny, so the shear stresses are negligible. Large gradients exist only in the thin boundary layer at the surface (no slip), so viscous effects are confined there.

Oil (\(\rho\): density 900 kg/m³; \(\mu\): viscosity 0.2 Pa·s) flows through a 0.15 m diameter pipe at 10 m/s. Calculate the Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho VD/\mu\) and classify the flow (Laminar/Turbulent/Transitional flow).

Dec 2023 Q46 markssame: Dec 2022 Q4F1.3 Reynolds number: compute & classify

Determine whether the flow is laminar or turbulent if water at 70 °C flows in a hydraulic copper tube with a 32 mm OD × 2.0 mm wall. The flow rate is 285 L/min. The kinematic viscosity of water is \(4.11\times10^{-7}\u{m^2/s}\).

Hint

OD = outside diameter. Find the inner diameter, convert L/min to m³/s, get \(V=Q/A\), then \(\mathrm{Re}=Vd/\nu\).

Answer

1. Inner diameter \(d=32-2(2.0)=28\u{mm}=0.028\u{m}\).

2. \(Q=285/(1000\times60)\) \(=4.75\times10^{-3}\u{m^3/s}\);

\(A=\pi(0.028)^2/4\) \(=6.158\times10^{-4}\u{m^2}\).

3. \(V=Q/A=7.71\u{m/s}\).

\[\mathrm{Re}=\frac{Vd}{\nu}=\frac{7.714(0.028)}{4.11\times10^{-7}}\]

\(\boxed{\mathrm{Re}\approx5.26\times10^{5}\gt 4000:\ \text{turbulent}}\)

May 2022 Q23 marksalso: May 2022 Q1 (units)F1.2 Viscosity units & conversions

What is the dynamic viscosity of a liquid C? Given the specific gravity of liquid C is 0.96 and kinematic viscosity of 610 mm²/s.

(a) 586 mPa.s   (b) 58.6 cP   (c) 63500 μPa.s   (d) 635 cP

\(\mu=\rho\nu\); \(\rho=0.96\times1000\); 1 mm²/s = 10⁻⁶ m²/s; 1 cP = 1 mPa·s.

Answer

\(\mu=960\times610\times10^{-6}\) \(=0.5856\u{Pa\,s}\) \(=586\u{mPa\,s}\).

\(\boxed{\text{(a) }586\text{ mPa·s}}\)

May 2022 Q53 marksF1.1 Flow classification & terminology

Which of the following is true about turbulence?

(a) Flow is static. (b) Velocity is not time dependent. (c) Velocity is uniform. (d) Flow contains three-dimensional velocity.

Hint

Picture the dye in the turbulent pipe of Reynolds's experiment: how do the particles move?

Answer

\(\boxed{\text{(d)}}\) Turbulent particles move haphazardly in all directions, so the velocity has components in all three directions. (a) the fluid is moving; (b) the velocity at a point fluctuates with time; (c) it varies from point to point (eddies).

May 2022 Q7(b)(c)1 + 3 marksF1.4 Laminar vs turbulent: Reynolds-experiment reasoning

(a) Make sketches of laminar and turbulent flows in a water channel. (2 marks; see the ready answer in Section 8)

(b) In Reynolds's experiment, if water is replaced by corn oil, will the onset of turbulence take place at a lower or higher flow rate?

(c) Explain with your knowledge on fluid properties, dimensionless number and turbulent flow.

Hint

Which property of corn oil is very different from water's? Where does it appear in \(\mathrm{Re}\)? Turbulence starts at a fixed \(\mathrm{Re}\).

Answer

(b) At a higher flow rate.

(c) Fluid property: corn oil's viscosity is much higher than water's (tens of times), so its kinematic viscosity \(\nu=\mu/\rho\) is much higher. Dimensionless number: \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho\bar Vd/\mu=\bar Vd/\nu\) measures inertial forces against viscous forces. Turbulent flow: transition begins at a fixed value, \(\mathrm{Re}\approx2000\), when inertia overcomes viscous damping of disturbances. In the same pipe this needs \(\bar V=2000\nu/d\), so a larger \(\nu\) needs a proportionally larger velocity and flow rate \(Q=\bar V\pi d^2/4\).
