Decision procedure · any manometer question (H2.2)
  1. Read the picture. Mark every free surface and every interface between two fluids with a number. Open to the air: \(p=0\). Cue: "Air at atmospheric pressure" by a limb.
  2. Sign first. Which mercury level is lower? The side that pushes the mercury down has the higher pressure. Mercury higher on the tank side: the tank is below atmospheric.
  3. Choose the start: the open mercury surface (0) or a known pressure from an earlier part.
  4. Walk to the unknown: down \(\Delta z\) through a fluid adds \(\gamma\Delta z\), up subtracts; sideways in one fluid: nothing; air or gas: nothing.
  5. Cross a U-bend only at a level with the same fluid on both sides (the lower mercury surface).
  6. Two manometers or an unknown height (May 2023 (b), May 2022): write one walk per manometer or per state and solve them together. An unknown column found from the first state is reused in the second.
  7. Reading changes: one level drops \(x\), the other rises \(x\); the reading becomes \(\Delta h+2x\); the system-fluid column above the falling level grows by \(x\).
  8. Check: the sign matches step 2; pressures in kPa; a level or height must come out positive and fit the drawing.
Answer skeleton · "what does a manometer measure / advantages" (H2.1, May 2025 Q1, 6 marks)

Diagram to draw: a simple U-tube (open limb, reading \(h\)) next to a well-type inclined manometer (wide well, sloping tube at \(\theta\), reading \(L\) along the tube, vertical rise \(L\sin\theta\)).

Marker points (about one or two marks each): (1) it measures pressure at a point relative to the atmosphere (gauge, can be negative) or the pressure difference between two points; (2) by balancing a liquid column: \(\Delta p=\rho_mgh\); (3) inclined: reading along the tube \(L=h/\sin\theta\), magnified by \(1/\sin\theta\): more sensitive, small pressures read accurately; (4) wide well: its level hardly moves, so only one reading (less reading error, scale can be marked in pressure units); (5) compact, suits low pressures such as air ducts.

Model answer (exam length) is in the May 2025 Q1 card.

2H2.1 · Pressure & manometer concepts

Pressure: normal compressive stress, scalar, isotropic, point function; equal at one level only in one continuous fluid; a manometer turns a column height into a pressure. Learn it: note-H2 §2 pressure as a stress · §3 Pascal's law · §7 same level, same fluid · §13 piezometer & U-tube · §15 inclined manometer

Ready answer · properties of pressure
  • Normal compressive stress: force per unit area acting perpendicular to, and pushing on, a surface (\(p=F_n/A\)).
  • Scalar: a size only; the pressure force on a surface is the vector, always perpendicular and inward.
  • Isotropic (Pascal's law): at a point it is the same in all directions.
  • Point function: it can vary from point to point (with depth), \(p=\rho gz+p_0\).
  • Same level rule: equal pressures at two points on one level only if they are joined by the same continuous fluid at rest.
May 2025 Q16 marksexplain

What fluid properties does the U-tube manometer measure? A variation of the U-tube manometer is the well-tube inclined manometer. Describe at least two advantages of it over the standard U-tube manometer.

Beyond the handouts (the inclined manometer is not in the 2026 handouts; the U-tube is)

Hint

Type H2.1 (written). A U-tube turns a height into what? For the inclined version compare the length you read with the vertical rise, and count how many levels you must read. Skeleton in At a glance.

Solution

Draw the two sketches of the answer skeleton, then write:

  1. What it measures. The pressure of a fluid at a point relative to the atmosphere (gauge pressure, positive or negative) when one limb is open, or the pressure difference between two points when both limbs are connected (differential manometer).
  2. How. The pressure is balanced by a column of manometer liquid of known density: \(\Delta p=\rho_mgh\) (minus any column of the system fluid), found by walking through the fluids.
  3. Advantage 1: sensitivity. The tube slopes at a small angle \(\theta\), so a vertical rise \(h\) gives a reading \(L=h/\sin\theta\) along the tube: magnified 3.9 times at 15°, 5.8 times at 10°. Small pressures (a few hundred Pa) are read accurately.
  4. Advantage 2: one reading. The well is much wider than the tube, so its level hardly falls (\(\Delta p=\rho_mgL(\sin\theta+a/A)\), \(a/A\) small). Only the tube is read, not two levels, so there is less reading error, and the scale can be marked directly in pressure units.
  5. Advantage 3 (extra). Compact; with a light gauge oil it suits low pressures such as air ducts and wind tunnels.

Marker note: name both quantities (pressure and pressure difference) for the first marks; each advantage needs its reason (\(1/\sin\theta\) magnification; wide well, single reading).

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{pressure / pressure difference;}\\&\text{inclined: more sensitive,}\\&\text{one reading only}\end{aligned}}\]

Reading used: "fluid properties" read as pressure (gauge) and pressure difference; a manometer gives nothing else directly. The well-type inclined manometer is beyond the handouts (taught in note-H2 §15).

Dec 2024 Q1(m)1 marktrue/falsesame idea: ENGG1205 2015–16 Q1(e)

State true (T) or false (F) for each of the following statements. (16 statements, 16 marks.)

(m) Pressure is an isotropic quantity. (1 mark)

Hint

Type H2.1. What does "isotropic" mean, and what did the fluid wedge (Pascal's law) prove?

Solution
  1. "Isotropic" means the same in every direction.
  2. Pascal's law (force balance on a tiny wedge of still fluid): the pressure at a point is the same on faces of every orientation.

\(\boxed{\text{True}}\)

Reading used: 1 mark per statement (16 marks for 16 statements, as printed).

ENGG1205 2015–16 Sem 1 Q1(e)2 marksstate a lawolder course

(Take density of water = 1,000 kg/m³, acceleration due to gravity = 9.81 m/s².) State Pascal's law about pressure at a point.

Hint

Type H2.1. One sentence about direction.

Solution
  1. Consider a tiny wedge of fluid at rest: no shear, so only pressure forces and weight act; as the wedge shrinks to a point, the weight becomes negligible and the pressures on all faces must be equal.
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{pressure at a point is the}\\&\text{same in all directions}\end{aligned}}\]

ENGG1205 printed solution: "The pressure at a point is independent of direction." Same answer.

ENGG1205 2012–13 Q1(h)1 markyes/no + reasonolder course

Is it always true that the hydrostatic pressure at one point is the same as that at another point if the two points are at the same level?

Hint

Type H2.1. Think of the two limbs of a mercury U-tube just above the lower mercury surface.

Solution
  1. The rule \(p_\text{below}-p_\text{above}=\rho g\Delta z\) holds along a path through one fluid. Two points on one level in different fluids (water on one side of a U-tube, mercury on the other), or in two unconnected tanks, can have different pressures.
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\text{No: only if joined by the}\\&\text{same continuous fluid}\end{aligned}}\]

ENGG1205 printed solution: "No, it is not always true, because the two points may not be connected by the same fluid." Same answer.

3H2.2 · Manometer chain (walk through the fluid columns)

Start at a known pressure; down adds \(\gamma\Delta z\), up subtracts; cross only in one fluid; gas adds nothing; a changed reading grows by \(2x\). Learn it: note-H2 §7 same level, same fluid · §8 pressure in a gas · §13 U-tube · §14 differential manometer · §15 manometer chains

Ready answer · the chain equation \[p_\text{start}+\sum_\text{down}\gamma_i\Delta z_i-\sum_\text{up}\gamma_i\Delta z_i=p_\text{end}\]

In words: pressure where you start, plus every column you go down, minus every column you go up, equals the pressure where you finish. Each column uses its own \(\gamma\).

Reading change (equal limbs): write the walk for both states; the falling level drops \(x\), the other rises \(x\):

\[\Delta h_\text{new}=\Delta h+2x\]
May 2024 Q1(a)5 marksparts (b), (c): H3.3, H3.4

A closed oil tank and an open water tank are separated by a rectangular flat gate \(AB\), which rotates around hinge \(A\), as shown in the figure below. The closed tank is equipped with a mercury manometer to measure the air pressure inside it. The reading of the mercury manometer is: \(h_1=5\u{cm}\). The height of the oil in the closed tank is \(h_2=3\u{m}\). The height of the gate \(AB\) is the same as the height of the water, which is \(h_3=0.8\u{m}\). The width of the gate \(b=1\u{m}\). The specific weights of mercury, water, and the oil are 133.7 kN/m³, 9.81 kN/m³, and 7.8 kN/m³, respectively.

(a) What is the air pressure (gage) inside the oil tank? (5 marks)

Hint

Type H2.2. Which limb has the higher mercury? Start at the open limb and walk to the tank air; the air pipe adds nothing.

Solution
  1. Sign. The mercury stands \(h_1\) higher in the limb joined to the tank, so the tank air pushes less than the atmosphere: expect a negative gauge pressure.
  2. Start at the open mercury surface: \(p=0\). Cross the bend to the same level in the tank-side limb (same mercury): still 0.
  3. Go up \(h_1=0.05\u{m}\) of mercury to the tank-side surface: subtract \(\gamma_\text{Hg}h_1\).
  4. Through the air in the pipe into the tank: no change.
\[p_\text{air}=0-133.7(0.05)=-6.685\u{kPa}\] \[\boxed{p_\text{air}\approx-6.69\u{kPa}\ \text{(gauge)}}\]

Marker note: keep the minus sign: part (b) turns it into an oil head \(-6.685/7.8=-0.857\u{m}\), so the equivalent open oil level is \(3-0.857=2.14\u{m}\) (H3.3).

Reading used: the mercury in the tank-side limb is higher (paper figure, RESOLVED), so the air is below atmospheric. The paper's \(\gamma_\text{Hg}=133.7\u{kN/m^3}\) is used, not \(13.6\times9.81=133.4\).

May 2023 Q1(a)(b)5 + 8 marks

A pressure vessel is an enclosed container designed to store liquids and gases at specified pressures. The pressure vessel shown in the figure below is designed to store water and air. The vessel is equipped with two mercury manometers to measure the air pressure inside the vessel and the water level. The readings of the mercury manometers are given: \(h_1=3\u{cm}\), \(h_2=30\u{cm}\), and \(h_3=31\u{cm}\). The specific weight of water is 9.81 kN/m³, and the specific gravity of mercury is 13.6. The air pressure in this vessel can be assumed to be the same everywhere.

(a) What is the air pressure (gage) \(p_0\) inside the vessel? (5 marks)

Hint

Type H2.2. Use the right manometer only: it is joined to the air by air. Which mercury level is higher?

Solution
  1. \(\gamma_\text{Hg}=13.6(9.81)=133.4\u{kN/m^3}\).
  2. Sign. The open limb's mercury is \(h_1\) higher, so the vessel air pushes the mercury down: \(p_0\) is above atmospheric.
  3. Start at the open mercury surface (0); go down \(h_1=0.03\u{m}\) of mercury to the level of the vessel-side surface: add \(\gamma_\text{Hg}h_1\). That surface touches the air, and the air pipe adds nothing.
\[p_0=133.4(0.03)=4.003\u{kPa}\] \[\boxed{p_0\approx4.00\u{kPa}\ \text{(gauge)}}\]

(b) What is the water level \(H\) inside the vessel? (8 marks)

Hint

Type H2.2. Left manometer: walk from its open mercury down to the connected-limb mercury, then up through the water (\(h_2\), then \(H\)) to the water surface, where the pressure is \(p_0\) from (a).

Solution
  1. Start at the left open mercury surface (0); go down \(h_3=0.31\u{m}\) of mercury to the level of the connected-limb surface: \(p=133.4(0.31)\) \(=41.36\u{kPa}\).
  2. From there go up through water: \(h_2=0.30\u{m}\) to the vessel floor, then \(H\) to the water surface: subtract \(\gamma_w(h_2+H)\).
  3. The water surface is at the air pressure \(p_0=4.003\u{kPa}\):
\[\begin{aligned}41.36-9.81(0.30+H)&=4.003\\0.30+H&=3.808\u{m}\end{aligned}\]

In symbols: \(H=\dfrac{\gamma_\text{Hg}(h_3-h_1)}{\gamma_w}-h_2\) \(=13.6(0.28)-0.30\).

\[\boxed{H\approx3.51\u{m}}\]

Marker note: write the whole chain in symbols first, then substitute; marks go for each column with its own \(\gamma\) and the correct up/down sign.

Reading used: \(h_2\) is the water column from the vessel floor down to the connected-limb mercury; \(h_3\) is how much higher the open-limb mercury stands (paper figure, RESOLVED).

May 2022 Q113 marksnumerical + sketch

A conical container is connected to a U-shaped mercury differential pressure gauge at point \(C\). Injecting water below and up to point \(C\) while keeping the conical container empty, the differential pressure gauge reading is \(\Delta h=0.2\u{m}\). When the conical container is also full of water, what is the reading of the differential pressure gauge? Draw the setup when the conical container is full and clearly indicate how the liquid levels change. The specific gravity of mercury is 13.6.

Hint

Type H2.2 (reading change). The water column \(y\) from C down to the mercury is not given: find it from the first state. Then write the balance again with each mercury level moved by \(x\).

Solution
  1. Empty cone. Water stands exactly at C, so \(p_C=0\). Balance at the right-limb mercury level: \(\gamma_wy=\gamma_\text{Hg}(0.2)\), so \(y=13.6(0.2)=2.72\u{m}\).
  2. Full cone. 3 m of water above C: \(p_C=9.81(3)\) \(=29.43\u{kPa}\). Only the depth counts, not the cone's shape or its 3 m diameter.
  3. The higher pressure pushes the right mercury level down \(x\); the open left level rises \(x\). Water column above the new right level: \(3+2.72+x\); reading \(0.2+2x\).
  4. Balance at the new right-limb mercury level (divide by \(\gamma_w\)):
\[\begin{aligned}3+2.72+x&=13.6(0.2+2x)\\5.72+x&=2.72+27.2x\\x&=3/26.2=0.1145\u{m}\end{aligned}\] \[\boxed{\Delta h_\text{new}=0.2+2(0.1145)\approx0.429\u{m}}\]

Sketch (as asked): the right (water-side) mercury level falls 0.1145 m, the left (open) level rises 0.1145 m; the water column below C lengthens by 0.1145 m.

Marker note: the sketch with both level changes marked carries marks; the short cut \(0.2+3/13.6=0.421\u{m}\) ignores the \(2x\) and the extra \(x\) of water and loses the method marks.

Reading used: left limb open and its mercury higher; water from C down to the right mercury; C-to-mercury distance found from the first state (RESOLVED). The paper gives no \(g\); 9.81 m/s² is used (it cancels).

ENGG1205 2017–18 Sem 1 Q1(a)10 markspart (b): H3.4older course

(Take density of water = 1,000 kg/m³, acceleration due to gravity = 9.81 m/s².) For the tank shown in Figure C1(a), find the pressure of air inside the tank and the pressure gauge reading if the mercury manometer reading \(H=0.12\u{m}\). Relative density of mercury = 13.6.

Figure C1(a): closed tank, air over water; the pressure gauge on the left wall is 4 m below the water surface; a pipe from the air space leads to a mercury U-tube whose right limb is open; the mercury is \(H\) higher in the tank-side limb.

Hint

Type H2.2. Two walks: open mercury → tank air; then tank air → down 4 m of water to the gauge.

Solution
  1. Sign. Mercury higher on the tank side: the air is below atmospheric.
  2. From the open mercury surface (0), across the bend, up \(H\) of mercury, then air (no change):
\[\begin{aligned}p_\text{air}&=-13.6(1000)(9.81)(0.12)\\&=-16.01\u{kPa}\end{aligned}\]
  1. From the air surface go down 4 m of water to the gauge: \(9.81(4)=39.24\u{kPa}\).
\[p_\text{gauge}=-16.01+39.24=23.23\u{kPa}\] \[\boxed{\begin{aligned}p_\text{air}&\approx-16.0\u{kPa}\\p_\text{gauge}&\approx23.2\u{kPa}\end{aligned}}\]

ENGG1205 printed solution: \(P_\text{air}=-16\u{kPa}\) (exact −16.01 kPa, rounded in print) and gauge reading 23.23 kPa. Same answers.

ENGG1205 2015–16 Sem 1 Q2(a)6 markspart (b): H3.5older course

(Take density of water = 1,000 kg/m³, acceleration due to gravity = 9.81 m/s².) Determine the elevation difference, \(\Delta h\), between the water levels in the open tanks shown in Figure C2(a). The fluid in the inverted manometer has a relative density of 0.9.

Figure C2(a): two open tanks; the left water level is 1 m above the floor and \(\Delta h\) above the right one. An inverted U-tube joins them: water fills the left leg to the top of the bend; the SG 0.90 liquid fills the bend and the top 0.4 m of the right leg; water below it. Labels A, B, C, D, \(x\) as in the printed solution.

Hint

Type H2.2. A and B are at one level in the SG 0.9 liquid, so \(p_A=p_B\). Walk down each leg to its tank's free surface (0); the unknown \(x\) cancels.

Solution
  1. \(p_A=p_B\): same level, joined through the SG 0.9 liquid across the bend.
  2. Left leg, A down \(0.4+x\) of water to C (level with the left surface): \(p_A+\gamma_w(0.4+x)=0\).
  3. Right leg, B down 0.4 m of SG 0.9 liquid, then \(x+\Delta h\) of water to D (right surface): \(p_B+0.9\gamma_w(0.4)+\gamma_w(x+\Delta h)=0\).
  4. Subtract (step 3 minus step 2), divide by \(\gamma_w\): \(0.36+\Delta h-0.4=0\).
\[\boxed{\Delta h=0.4(1-0.9)=0.04\u{m}}\]

Marker note: a gauge liquid lighter than water in an inverted U-tube magnifies the difference: 0.4 m of reading for 4 cm of level difference.

ENGG1205 printed solution: \(\Delta h=0.4-0.9\times0.4=0.04\u{m}\). Same answer.

Reading used: left leg water to the top of the bend, SG 0.9 liquid in the bend and the top 0.4 m of the right leg (paper figure, RESOLVED).

4Traps across H2

Traps
  • Up adds? Down adds \(\gamma\Delta z\), up subtracts. Check: the side that pushes the mercury down has the higher pressure.
  • Dropping a minus sign. Mercury higher on the tank side gives negative gauge pressure (May 2024: −6.69 kPa; ENGG1205: −16.0 kPa).
  • Same level, different fluid. Cross a U-bend only where the same fluid is on both sides (ENGG1205 2012–13 Q1(h)).
  • Wrong \(\gamma\). Each column its own: mercury 13.6 × 9.81 = 133.4 kN/m³, or the paper's printed 133.7.
  • Counting air. Gas columns in tanks and pipes add nothing.
  • cm left in cm. \(h_1=3\u{cm}=0.03\u{m}\).
  • Reading change without \(x\). Each level moves \(x\), the reading \(2x\), the system column \(x\) (May 2022: 0.429 m, not 0.421 m).
  • Container shape. Only the depth above C matters, not the cone's volume or diameter.
  • Adding 101.3 kPa. "Pressure" means gauge unless "absolute" is asked.
  • Pressure as a vector. Pressure is a scalar (isotropic); the pressure force has a direction.
