1The scalar product ~9 min

What is this?

Picture a till receipt. Each line is a quantity times a price, and the total adds the lines up. A scalar product does the same with two vectors: multiply matching entries and add, and one number comes out. Swapping the two lists gives the same total (SP1). Splitting a basket into two baskets splits the bill into two bills (SP2). Doubling every quantity doubles the bill (SP3).

In HKDSE M2 the dot product of \(\mathbf a=(a_1,a_2,a_3)\) and \(\mathbf b=(b_1,b_2,b_3)\) is \(a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3\), a single number. Slide 2 does not start from a formula. It lists the rules that any sensible "product of two vectors giving a number" must obey, and calls anything that obeys them a scalar product. Then every result proved from the rules holds for all of them at once.

Definition: scalar product (slide 2)

Let \(V\) be a vector space over a field \(K\). A scalar product or a dot product on \(V\) is the association of a scalar to any pair of elements \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\) of \(V\), denoted by \(\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle\) or \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v\), satisfying the following properties:

  • SP1, commutative: \(\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle=\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf u\rangle\) for all \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\in V\).
  • SP2, distributive: \(\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v+\mathbf w\rangle=\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle+\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf w\rangle\) for all \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\in V\).
  • SP3, factorization: \(\langle\alpha\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle=\langle\mathbf u,\alpha\mathbf v\rangle=\alpha\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle\) for all \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\in V\), \(\alpha\in K\).

The slide's title writes this as a map \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\times V\to K\): it takes a pair of vectors (an element of \(V\times V\)) and returns a number (an element of \(K\)).

In words: a scalar product eats two vectors and returns one number. The order of the two vectors does not matter, and it expands over sums and lets numbers be pulled out, exactly like ordinary multiplication.

Words used on the slide:

  • A vector space \(V\) is a set of objects ("vectors") that can be added and multiplied by numbers, with the usual rules. \(\mathbb R^n\) from L2 is one; Example 2 below uses another, a space of functions.
  • A field \(K\) is the set of numbers you may multiply vectors by. In this course \(K=\mathbb R\), the real numbers, throughout.
  • The symbol \(\forall\) means "for all", and \(\in\) means "belongs to".
Beyond the handouts: the fourth rule

Textbooks add one more rule, and slide 7 uses it without saying so:

SP4, positive: \(\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle\ge0\) for every \(\mathbf v\), and \(\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle=0\) only when \(\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\).

Without it, "the length \(\sqrt{\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle}\)" in section 2 could be the square root of a negative number. Both examples below satisfy SP4: a sum of squares \(a_1^2+\cdots+a_n^2\), and an integral \(\int_0^1 f(t)^2\,dt\) of a square, are never negative, and are zero only if every \(a_k\) (or \(f\) itself) is zero. A scalar product with all four rules is often called an inner product; past papers use "scalar product" and "inner product" for the same thing.

Two consequences of SP1 to SP3 used all the time: the product also expands over sums on the left, \(\langle\mathbf u+\mathbf v,\mathbf w\rangle=\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf w\rangle+\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf w\rangle\) (use SP1 to swap, SP2, then swap back), and \(\langle\mathbf 0,\mathbf v\rangle=0\) (take \(\alpha=0\) in SP3).

Example 1: the dot product on ℝⁿ (slides 3 and 4)

Example 1 (slide 3)

Let \(V=\mathbb R^n\), \(\mathbf a=(a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n)\), \(\mathbf b=(b_1,b_2,\dots,b_n)\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b&=\sum_{k=1}^n a_kb_k\\ &=a_1b_1+a_2b_2+\cdots+a_nb_n \end{aligned} \]

is a scalar product. The slide checks the three rules for all \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b\in V\) (with \(\mathbf c=(c_1,\dots,c_n)\) a third vector):

SP1:

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle\mathbf a,\mathbf b\rangle&=\sum_{k=1}^n a_kb_k=\sum_{k=1}^n b_ka_k\\ &=\langle\mathbf b,\mathbf a\rangle \end{aligned} \]

SP2:

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle\mathbf a,\mathbf b+\mathbf c\rangle&=\sum_{k=1}^n a_k(b_k+c_k)\\ &=\sum_{k=1}^n a_kb_k+\sum_{k=1}^n a_kc_k\\ &=\langle\mathbf a,\mathbf b\rangle+\langle\mathbf a,\mathbf c\rangle \end{aligned} \]

SP3:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{k=1}^n(\alpha a_k)b_k&=\sum_{k=1}^n a_k(\alpha b_k)\\ &=\alpha\sum_{k=1}^n a_kb_k \end{aligned} \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \langle\alpha\mathbf a,\mathbf b\rangle=\langle\mathbf a,\alpha\mathbf b\rangle=\alpha\langle\mathbf a,\mathbf b\rangle \]

Thus \(\langle\mathbf a,\mathbf b\rangle=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\sum_{k=1}^n a_kb_k\) is a scalar product on \(V\).

In words: multiply matching entries and add. Each rule holds because it holds for ordinary numbers in every term of the sum.

Example 1.1 (slide 4)

\(V=\mathbb R^3\), \(\mathbf a=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf b=\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\\1\end{bmatrix}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b&=\sum_{k=1}^3a_kb_k=\mathbf a^T\mathbf b\\ &=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&-1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\\1\end{bmatrix}\\ &=(1)(2)+(2)(-1)+(-1)(1)\\ &=-1 \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf b\cdot\mathbf a&=\mathbf b^T\mathbf a=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{bmatrix}\\ &=(2)(1)+(-1)(2)+(1)(-1)\\ &=-1 \end{aligned} \]

The two agree, as SP1 says they must.

The dot product as a matrix product (slide 4) \[ \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\mathbf a^T\mathbf b \]

for column vectors \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b\in\mathbb R^n\). \(\mathbf a^T\) (the transpose) is a \(1\times n\) row, \(\mathbf b\) is \(n\times1\), and the product is \(1\times1\): a single number, by the row-column rule.

In words: lay \(\mathbf a\) on its side and multiply it into \(\mathbf b\). This form is what makes least squares (section 7) a matrix calculation.

Example 2: a scalar product of functions (slides 5 and 6)

What is this? (functions)

A function on [0, 1] is like a list with one entry for every instant t, as if a thermometer were read continuously. To multiply matching entries and add, multiply the two values at each instant and add over all the instants. With infinitely many instants, adding becomes integrating. The receipt is now infinitely long, but the three rules still hold.

Functions can be added (\((f+g)(t)=f(t)+g(t)\)) and multiplied by numbers, so they form a vector space. The sum \(\sum a_kb_k\) "multiply matching entries and add" has a natural version for functions: multiply the values \(f(t)g(t)\) at each point and "add them all up", which for a continuous range of \(t\) means integrate.

Example 2 (slide 5)

Let \(V\) be the vector space of continuous real-valued functions on the interval \([0,1]\). Then

\[ \langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1 f(t)\,g(t)\,dt\qquad\forall f,g\in V \]
Reading the formula
\(f(t)\,g(t)\)
the two "entries" at one instant \(t\), multiplied, like one line of the receipt.
\(\int_0^1\cdots\,dt\)
adds up all those products over every instant from 0 to 1: the total of the receipt.
\(\langle f,g\rangle\)
one number, exactly as for vectors: large and positive when \(f\) and \(g\) are mostly big together with the same sign.

is a scalar product. The slide checks the rules for all \(f,g\in V\) (and \(h\) a third function):

SP1:

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle f,g\rangle&=\int_0^1 f(t)g(t)\,dt\\ &=\int_0^1 g(t)f(t)\,dt=\langle g,f\rangle \end{aligned} \]

SP2:

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle f,g+h\rangle&=\int_0^1 f(t)\big(g(t)+h(t)\big)\,dt\\ &=\int_0^1 f(t)g(t)\,dt\\ &\quad+\int_0^1 f(t)h(t)\,dt\\ &=\langle f,g\rangle+\langle f,h\rangle \end{aligned} \]

SP3:

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^1\alpha f(t)g(t)\,dt&=\int_0^1 f(t)\big(\alpha g(t)\big)\,dt\\ &=\alpha\int_0^1 f(t)g(t)\,dt \end{aligned} \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \langle\alpha f,g\rangle=\langle f,\alpha g\rangle=\alpha\langle f,g\rangle \]

Thus \(\langle f,g\rangle\) is a scalar product on \(V\).

In words: the "dot product" of two functions is the integral of their product. Each rule is a familiar rule of integrals: \(\int(F+G)=\int F+\int G\) and \(\int\alpha F=\alpha\int F\).

Example 2.1 (slide 6)

In Example 2, let \(f(t)=t\) and \(g(t)=1-t\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle f,g\rangle&=\int_0^1 f(t)g(t)\,dt=\int_0^1 t(1-t)\,dt\\ &=\int_0^1(t-t^2)\,dt=\left[\frac{t^2}2-\frac{t^3}3\right]_0^1\\ &=\frac12-\frac13=\frac16, \end{aligned} \]

and \(\langle g,f\rangle=\frac16\) as well (SP1).

The scalar product of two functions on [0, 1]

f(t)g(t)f(t)g(t), whose integral is ⟨f, g⟩

The green curve is the product \(f(t)g(t)\); \(\langle f,g\rangle\) is the signed area under it from 0 to 1 (area below the axis counts as negative). The last two pairs give 0: those functions are "perpendicular" (section 4), and they reappear in section 8.

You can now: check that a proposed product is a scalar product by testing SP1 to SP3; compute \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\sum a_kb_k=\mathbf a^T\mathbf b\); compute \(\langle f,g\rangle\) as the integral of \(f(t)g(t)\) over \([0,1]\).

2Length and unit vectors ~3 min

What is this?

Length is how long the stick is. In the plane you find it with Pythagoras: square each entry, add, and take the square root, which is exactly \(\sqrt{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v}\). A unit vector is the same stick cut to length 1 without turning it, like a compass needle: it only says which way. Using the scalar product of functions, the same rule gives a function a length too.

In \(\mathbb R^2\), Pythagoras gives the length of \((v_1,v_2)\) as \(\sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2}\), and that is \(\sqrt{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v}\). Slide 7 turns this into the definition of length for any scalar product.

Definition: length or norm (slide 7)

The length (or norm) of a vector is defined as

\[ \|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle}\ \ge0, \]

and \(\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\) if and only if \(\|\mathbf v\|=0\).

  • \(\mathbf u\in V\) is a unit vector if \(\|\mathbf u\|=1\).
  • If \(\mathbf v\in V\) and \(\mathbf v\ne\mathbf 0\), then \(\dfrac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|}\) is a unit vector.

In words: the length of a vector is the square root of its scalar product with itself. Dividing a non-zero vector by its own length keeps its direction and makes its length exactly 1; this is called normalizing the vector.

Why is \(\mathbf v/\|\mathbf v\|\) a unit vector? Write \(c=\frac1{\|\mathbf v\|}\). By SP3 twice, \(\langle c\mathbf v,c\mathbf v\rangle=c^2\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle=\frac{\|\mathbf v\|^2}{\|\mathbf v\|^2}=1\). The same calculation shows \(\|c\mathbf v\|=|c|\,\|\mathbf v\|\) for any number \(c\): stretching a vector by \(c\) stretches its length by \(|c|\).

The notation \(\|\mathbf v\|\) (double bars) is used for the norm so that it is not confused with \(|x|\) for a number; HKDSE M2 writes \(|\mathbf v|\) for the same thing in \(\mathbb R^3\), and some past papers do too.

Example 3 (slide 8)

Let \(\mathbf v=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{bmatrix}\). Find the norm of \(\mathbf v\) and a unit vector \(\mathbf u\) in the same direction as \(\mathbf v\).

Solution. The norm of \(\mathbf v\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf v\|&=\sqrt{\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle}\\ &=\sqrt{1^2+2^2+(-1)^2}=\sqrt6, \end{aligned} \] \[ \mathbf u=\frac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|}=\frac1{\sqrt6}\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Check: \(\|\mathbf u\|^2=\frac16(1+4+1)=1\ \checkmark\)

Example 4 (slide 9)

In Example 2.1 (\(f(t)=t\), \(g(t)=1-t\) on \([0,1]\)), find the norm of \(f\) and of \(g\).

Solution.

\[ \begin{aligned} \|f\|&=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle}=\sqrt{\int_0^1 f^2(t)\,dt}\\ &=\sqrt{\int_0^1t^2\,dt}=\sqrt{\left[\frac{t^3}3\right]_0^1}=\frac1{\sqrt3}. \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} \|g\|&=\sqrt{\langle g,g\rangle}=\sqrt{\int_0^1 g^2(t)\,dt}\\ &=\sqrt{\int_0^1(1-t)^2\,dt}\\ &=\sqrt{\left[-\frac{(1-t)^3}3\right]_{t=0}^{t=1}}\\ &=\sqrt{0-\left(-\frac13\right)}=\frac1{\sqrt3}. \end{aligned} \]

Slide 9 also offers a second way to write the same evaluation, using \(1-t\) as the variable: \(\sqrt{\left[\frac{(1-t)^3}3\right]_{1-t=0}^{1-t=1}}\). As \(t\) goes from 0 to 1, \(1-t\) goes from 1 down to 0, and swapping the limits absorbs the minus sign: \(\frac{1^3}3-\frac{0^3}3=\frac13\).

(Here \(f^2(t)\) means \(\big(f(t)\big)^2\).) A unit "vector" in the direction of \(f\) is \(f/\|f\|=\sqrt3\,t\): indeed \(\int_0^1 3t^2\,dt=1\).

For \(-\frac{(1-t)^3}{3}\): differentiate it and you get \(-\frac{3(1-t)^2\cdot(-1)}{3}=(1-t)^2\), the integrand. The minus sign comes from the chain rule (the inside \(1-t\) has derivative \(-1\)). Expanding \((1-t)^2=1-2t+t^2\) and integrating term by term gives \(1-1+\frac13=\frac13\) too.

You can now: find the norm of a vector in \(\mathbb R^n\) and of a function on \([0,1]\); normalize a non-zero vector or function to length 1; use \(\|c\mathbf v\|=|c|\,\|\mathbf v\|\).

3Cauchy–Schwarz, angles, triangle inequality ~6 min

What is this?

Hold a stick \(\mathbf v\) above the line through \(\mathbf u\), with the light falling at right angles to that line. The shadow of the stick on the line is never longer than the stick itself, and it is the full length only when the stick lies along the line. Cauchy–Schwarz says exactly this: \(|\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v|\) is \(\|\mathbf u\|\) times the shadow's length, so it is at most \(\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\). The ratio of shadow to stick is \(\cos\theta\), and that is how the angle is defined.

Slide 10 states two inequalities that every scalar product obeys. The first one lets us define the angle between two vectors, even in \(\mathbb R^{10}\) or between two functions.

Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (slide 10) \[ |\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v|\le\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\| \]

In words: the size of the scalar product of two vectors is never more than the product of their lengths. It equals the product of the lengths exactly when one vector is a multiple of the other (they lie on one line).

The slide states this without proof. Here is the short standard one; it uses only the rules of section 1 and the HKDSE fact that a quadratic that is never negative has discriminant \(\le0\).

Beyond the handouts: proof of Cauchy–Schwarz

If \(\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\) both sides are 0. Otherwise, for every real number \(x\), the length of \(\mathbf u-x\mathbf v\) squared is \(\ge0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} 0&\le\|\mathbf u-x\mathbf v\|^2\\ &=\langle\mathbf u-x\mathbf v,\mathbf u-x\mathbf v\rangle\\ &=\|\mathbf v\|^2x^2-2\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle x+\|\mathbf u\|^2. \end{aligned} \]

(Expand with SP2 and SP3 as you would \((a-xb)^2\), and use SP1 to combine the two middle terms.) This is a quadratic in \(x\) with positive leading coefficient that is never negative, so it has at most one real root and its discriminant is \(\le0\):

\[ 4\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle^2-4\|\mathbf u\|^2\|\mathbf v\|^2\le0 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ |\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle|\le\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|. \]

Equality means the quadratic touches 0 at some \(x\), that is \(\mathbf u=x\mathbf v\).

The angle between two vectors (slide 10)

Cauchy–Schwarz says \(-1\le\dfrac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|}\le1\) for non-zero \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\). Every number in \([-1,1]\) is \(\cos\theta\) for exactly one \(\theta\) between \(0\) and \(\pi\), so the following definition makes sense.

Definition: angle (slide 10)

With Cauchy–Schwarz, one can define the angle \(\theta\) between two non-zero vectors by

\[ \cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|},\qquad 0\le\theta\le\pi. \]
Reading the formula
\(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v\)
\(\|\mathbf u\|\) times the signed length of the shadow of \(\mathbf v\) on the line through \(\mathbf u\).
\(\div\|\mathbf u\|\)
removes the factor \(\|\mathbf u\|\), leaving just the shadow length.
\(\div\|\mathbf v\|\)
turns the shadow into a fraction of the stick: between \(-1\) and 1 by Cauchy–Schwarz.
a negative value
the shadow points backwards along \(\mathbf u\): the angle is more than \(90^\circ\).

In words: the scalar product divided by both lengths is the cosine of the angle between the vectors. This is HKDSE's \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|\cos\theta\), turned round to define \(\theta\).

Note on slide 10. The slide writes \(\cos\theta=|\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v|/(\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|)\le1\), with absolute-value bars on the top. With the bars, \(\cos\theta\) could never be negative, so an obtuse angle (one more than \(90^\circ\)) could never come out. The standard definition, used here and in the past papers (for example May 2013 Q2(a)(ii), "the cosine of the angle between them"), has no bars: \(\cos\theta=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v/(\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|)\). The bars belong to Cauchy–Schwarz, which says this fraction lies between \(-1\) and \(1\).

Example (slide 4's vectors and slide 9's functions)

Vectors. \(\mathbf a=(1,2,-1)\), \(\mathbf b=(2,-1,1)\): \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=-1\) (slide 4), \(\|\mathbf a\|=\|\mathbf b\|=\sqrt6\). So

\[ \cos\theta=\frac{-1}{\sqrt6\sqrt6}=-\frac16, \]

an angle a little more than \(90^\circ\). Cauchy–Schwarz: \(|-1|\le\sqrt6\cdot\sqrt6=6\ \checkmark\)

Functions. \(f(t)=t\), \(g(t)=1-t\): \(\langle f,g\rangle=\frac16\) (slide 6), \(\|f\|=\|g\|=\frac1{\sqrt3}\) (slide 9). Cauchy–Schwarz: \(\frac16\le\frac1{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac1{\sqrt3}=\frac13\ \checkmark\), and \(\cos\theta=\frac{1/6}{1/3}=\frac12\): the "angle" between these two functions is \(60^\circ\).

The triangle inequality (slide 10)

What is this? (triangle inequality)

Walking straight from start to finish is never longer than walking there via a corner. The two legs are \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\), and the straight path is \(\mathbf u+\mathbf v\). The only way to tie is if the corner already lies on the straight path, with both legs pointing the same way.

Triangle inequality (slide 10) \[ \|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|\le\|\mathbf u\|+\|\mathbf v\| \]

Proof (slide 10):

\[ \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|^2&=\langle\mathbf u+\mathbf v,\mathbf u+\mathbf v\rangle\\ &=\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf u\rangle+2\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle+\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle\\ &\le\|\mathbf u\|^2+2\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|+\|\mathbf v\|^2\\ &=(\|\mathbf u\|+\|\mathbf v\|)^2 \end{aligned} \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|\le\|\mathbf u\|+\|\mathbf v\|. \]

In words: one side of a triangle is never longer than the other two sides together: going straight from start to finish is never longer than going via a corner.

The step marked \(\le\) is Cauchy–Schwarz: \(\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle\le|\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle|\le\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|\). The last step takes square roots, which is allowed because both sides are \(\ge0\). The slide calls it the "triangular inequality"; the usual name is the triangle inequality.

Left: the angle \(\theta\) between \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\). Right: \(\mathbf u+\mathbf v\) is the third side of a triangle with sides \(\|\mathbf u\|\) and \(\|\mathbf v\|\), so it is no longer than their sum.

You can now: state Cauchy–Schwarz and prove it with the discriminant of a quadratic; find the angle between two vectors or two functions from \(\cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|}\); prove the triangle inequality by expanding \(\|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|^2\).

4Orthogonality and orthonormal bases ~9 min

What is this?

With the light falling at right angles to the line through \(\mathbf u\), a stick that is perpendicular to \(\mathbf u\) casts no shadow on that line: its shadow shrinks to a single point. So "perpendicular" can be tested with a number: the scalar product is 0. That test still works in \(\mathbb R^{10}\) or for functions, where no right angle can be drawn.

In \(\mathbb R^2\) and \(\mathbb R^3\), perpendicular vectors are exactly those with \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=0\) (\(\cos90^\circ=0\)). Slide 11 makes this the definition in every space with a scalar product.

Definition: orthogonal (slide 11)

Let \(V\) be a vector space with a scalar product. We define elements \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\) of \(V\) to be orthogonal or perpendicular, written \(\mathbf u\perp\mathbf v\), if

\[ \langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle=0. \]

In words: two vectors are perpendicular when their scalar product is zero. "Orthogonal" is the general word; in \(\mathbb R^2\) and \(\mathbb R^3\) it is the usual right angle.

By this definition the zero vector is orthogonal to every vector (\(\langle\mathbf 0,\mathbf v\rangle=0\)), and it is the only vector orthogonal to itself (SP4). Two functions can be orthogonal too: section 1's figure shows \(\langle 1,\,t-\tfrac12\rangle=\int_0^1(t-\tfrac12)\,dt=0\).

Example (slide 11)

\(V=\mathbb R^3\), \(\mathbf a=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf b=\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\\0\end{bmatrix}\). Show that \(\mathbf a\) and \(\mathbf b\) are orthogonal.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b&=\sum_{k=1}^3a_kb_k\\ &=(1)(2)+(2)(-1)+(-1)(0)=0. \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(\mathbf a\) and \(\mathbf b\) are orthogonal.

What is this? (orthonormal basis)

Think of the corner of a room: three edges meeting at right angles, each marked with a ruler one unit long. To read off any vector's coordinates, measure its shadow on each ruler. No equations need solving, because the rulers do not interfere with each other. A basis that is merely independent is like rulers at odd angles: the coordinates are still there, but you must solve a system to find them.

Definition: orthogonal and orthonormal basis (slide 12)

Let \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots,\mathbf v_n\}\) be a basis of \(V\). It is an orthogonal basis if

\[ \langle\mathbf v_i,\mathbf v_j\rangle=0\quad\forall i\ne j. \]

If \(\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots,\mathbf v_n\) are also unit vectors, it is an orthonormal basis. For a finite-dimensional vector space \(V\) with a scalar product, an orthogonal basis can always be found.

In words: orthogonal basis = every two different basis vectors are perpendicular. Orthonormal = perpendicular and each of length 1 ("ortho" for perpendicular, "normal" for normalized). Every space we meet has one; section 8's Gram–Schmidt process is how to build it.

In one line: \(\{\mathbf u_1,\dots,\mathbf u_n\}\) is orthonormal when \(\langle\mathbf u_i,\mathbf u_j\rangle=0\) for \(i\ne j\) and \(=1\) for \(i=j\).

Example (slide 12) \[ \left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\} \]

is an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb R^3\): each pair has dot product 0 and each vector has length 1.

\[ \left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\} \]

is also a basis of \(\mathbb R^3\) but not an orthogonal basis.

Why (the slide does not show it): it is a basis because the three vectors are independent (the matrix with these columns has determinant \(-2\ne0\), L4; or row reduce, L2). It is not orthogonal because \((1,1,0)\cdot(1,0,1)=1\ne0\).

Beyond the handouts: why orthonormal bases are so useful

The past papers (type L7.4) ask for these three facts with proof. Let \(\{\mathbf u_1,\dots,\mathbf u_n\}\) be orthonormal.

1. They are linearly independent. Suppose \(c_1\mathbf u_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf u_n=\mathbf 0\). Take the scalar product of both sides with \(\mathbf u_i\). Every term \(c_j\langle\mathbf u_j,\mathbf u_i\rangle\) with \(j\ne i\) is 0 and \(\langle\mathbf u_i,\mathbf u_i\rangle=1\), so \(c_i=\langle\mathbf 0,\mathbf u_i\rangle=0\). This holds for every \(i\), so all weights are 0: independent. (The same proof works for an orthogonal set of non-zero vectors, dividing by \(\langle\mathbf u_i,\mathbf u_i\rangle\ne0\).)

2. Coordinates are just scalar products. If \(\mathbf v=\alpha_1\mathbf u_1+\cdots+\alpha_n\mathbf u_n\), the same trick (scalar product with \(\mathbf u_i\)) gives

\[ \alpha_i=\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf u_i\rangle. \]

So the weights are fixed by \(\mathbf v\): the representation is unique. No row reduction is needed to find them.

3. Length from coordinates. Expanding \(\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle\) with all the cross terms zero:

\[ \|\mathbf v\|^2=\alpha_1^2+\alpha_2^2+\cdots+\alpha_n^2. \]

In words: in an orthonormal basis, each coordinate is "how much of \(\mathbf v\) points along \(\mathbf u_i\)", and Pythagoras holds for the coordinates exactly as for \(x,y,z\).

Example. Section 8 builds the orthonormal basis \(\mathbf u_1=\frac1{\sqrt{14}}(1,2,3)\), \(\mathbf u_2=\frac1{\sqrt{21}}(4,1,-2)\), \(\mathbf u_3=\frac1{\sqrt6}(-1,2,-1)\). For \(\mathbf v=(2,5,7)\):

\[ \alpha_1=\frac{2+10+21}{\sqrt{14}}=\frac{33}{\sqrt{14}}, \] \[ \alpha_2=\frac{8+5-14}{\sqrt{21}}=-\frac1{\sqrt{21}}, \] \[ \alpha_3=\frac{-2+10-7}{\sqrt6}=\frac1{\sqrt6}, \] \[ \begin{aligned} \alpha_1^2+\alpha_2^2+\alpha_3^2&=\frac{1089}{14}+\frac1{21}+\frac16\\ &=\frac{3267+2+7}{42}=78\\ &=2^2+5^2+7^2\ \checkmark \end{aligned} \]

If only some of the \(\mathbf u_i\) are used, the sum of squares can only be smaller: \(\sum_{i=1}^r\alpha_i^2\le\|\mathbf v\|^2\) for \(r\le n\) (this is called Bessel's inequality).

Beyond the handouts: vectors perpendicular to given vectors

Many questions ask for all vectors \(\mathbf x\) orthogonal to some given vectors \(\mathbf a_1,\dots,\mathbf a_m\) (the set of them is called the orthogonal complement). Each condition \(\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf x=0\) is one linear equation, so:

  1. Do: Write the given vectors as the rows of a matrix \(A\). Then "\(\mathbf x\) is orthogonal to all of them" is \(A\mathbf x=\mathbf 0\) (row \(i\) of \(A\mathbf x\) is \(\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf x\), L3). "No shadow on each given vector" becomes one linear equation per vector, all handled at once.
  2. Do: Solve \(A\mathbf x=\mathbf 0\) by row reduction, with free variables as in L1. The free variables sweep out every direction that casts no shadow on any of the given vectors; there are usually infinitely many.
  3. Do: If a unit vector is asked for, divide by the length. If an orthogonal or orthonormal basis of the solutions is asked for, apply Gram–Schmidt (section 8) to the basic solutions. Row reduction gives directions that are perpendicular to the given vectors but not, in general, to each other.

Example. Vectors perpendicular to both \((1,1,0)\) and \((0,1,1)\): \(x_1+x_2=0\), \(x_2+x_3=0\). With \(x_3=s\) free, \(x_2=-s\), \(x_1=s\): \(\mathbf x=s(1,-1,1)\). Check: \(1-1+0=0\), \(0-1+1=0\ \checkmark\). The unit vectors are \(\pm\frac1{\sqrt3}(1,-1,1)\).

Beyond the handouts: orthogonal matrices

A square matrix \(U\) whose columns form an orthonormal basis is called an orthogonal matrix (older papers say "orthonormal matrix"). Entry \((i,j)\) of \(U^TU\) is (column \(i\))\(\cdot\)(column \(j\)), so the definition says exactly

\[ U^TU=I,\qquad\text{that is}\qquad U^{-1}=U^T. \]

Two facts the past papers ask you to prove:

  • \(\det U=\pm1\): from \(U^TU=I\) and the determinant rules \(\det(AB)=\det A\det B\), \(\det A^T=\det A\) (L4), \((\det U)^2=\det I=1\).
  • \(U\) keeps lengths: \[ \begin{aligned} \|U\mathbf x\|^2&=(U\mathbf x)^T(U\mathbf x)\\ &=\mathbf x^TU^TU\mathbf x\\ &=\mathbf x^T\mathbf x=\|\mathbf x\|^2, \end{aligned} \] using \((AB)^T=B^TA^T\) (L3).

Orthogonal matrices appear in L6, where a symmetric matrix is diagonalized with one.

The cross product in \(\mathbb R^3\). Older papers (May 2013) ask for \(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\). It is a vector (not a number) defined only for vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\):

To remember it, expand a "determinant" along its first row, whose entries are the unit vectors \(\mathbf i=(1,0,0)\), \(\mathbf j=(0,1,0)\), \(\mathbf k=(0,0,1)\) (cofactor expansion, L4):

  • \(\|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\|=\|\mathbf a\|\|\mathbf b\|\sin\theta\), the area of the parallelogram on \(\mathbf a\) and \(\mathbf b\).

And

5Pythagoras and the parallelogram law ~3 min

What is this?

Walk 3 m east, then 4 m north. You end up 5 m from where you started, because the two legs meet at a right angle. Pythagoras for vectors says the same, with "right angle" meaning scalar product 0, so it also holds in \(\mathbb R^n\) and for functions. The parallelogram law needs no right angle: the cross term appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign, and cancels.

Both results on slide 13 are proved the same way: write the squared length as a scalar product and expand it like \((a+b)^2\).

Pythagoras theorem (slide 13)

If \(\mathbf u\perp\mathbf v\), then

\[ \|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|^2=\|\mathbf u\|^2+\|\mathbf v\|^2. \]

Proof (slide 13):

\[ \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|^2&=\langle\mathbf u+\mathbf v,\mathbf u+\mathbf v\rangle\\ &=\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf u\rangle+2\underbrace{\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle}_{=0\ \because\ \mathbf u\perp\mathbf v}+\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle\\ &=\|\mathbf u\|^2+\|\mathbf v\|^2. \end{aligned} \]

In words: when two vectors are perpendicular, the square on their sum equals the sum of their squares, as in a right-angled triangle with legs \(\mathbf u\), \(\mathbf v\) and hypotenuse \(\mathbf u+\mathbf v\).

The expansion in the middle line uses SP2 (and its left-hand version) to get four terms \(\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf u\rangle+\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle+\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf u\rangle+\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle\), then SP1 to combine the two middle ones. The symbol \(\because\) means "because".

Exercise (slide 13): verify Pythagoras for \(\mathbf u=(3,4)\) and \(\mathbf v=(-4,3)\)

First, they are perpendicular: \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=3(-4)+4(3)=0\).

\(\mathbf u+\mathbf v=(-1,7)\), so \(\|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|^2=(-1)^2+7^2=50\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf u\|^2+\|\mathbf v\|^2&=(9+16)+(16+9)\\ &=25+25=50\ \checkmark \end{aligned} \]
Parallelogram law (slide 13) \[ \|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|^2+\|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|^2=2\|\mathbf u\|^2+2\|\mathbf v\|^2 \]

Proof (slide 13):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|^2+\|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|^2\\ &=\langle\mathbf u+\mathbf v,\mathbf u+\mathbf v\rangle+\langle\mathbf u-\mathbf v,\mathbf u-\mathbf v\rangle\\ &=\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf u\rangle+2\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle+\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle\\ &\quad+\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf u\rangle-2\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle+\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle\\ &=2\|\mathbf u\|^2+2\|\mathbf v\|^2. \end{aligned} \]

In words: in any parallelogram, the squares of the two diagonals add up to the squares of all four sides. The \(\pm2\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle\) terms cancel, so no right angle is needed.

The parallelogram on \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\). Its diagonals are \(\mathbf u+\mathbf v\) and \(\mathbf u-\mathbf v\) (from the tip of \(\mathbf v\) to the tip of \(\mathbf u\)). If \(\mathbf u\perp\mathbf v\) it is a rectangle and both diagonals have the same length.

For slide 13's pair: \(\mathbf u-\mathbf v=(7,1)\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} &\|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|^2+\|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|^2\\ &=50+50=100\\ &=2(25)+2(25)\ \checkmark \end{aligned} \]

A related fact asked in an old paper (May 2013 Q2(b)): if \(\|\mathbf u\|=\|\mathbf v\|\) then \((\mathbf u+\mathbf v)\perp(\mathbf u-\mathbf v)\). Expand: \(\langle\mathbf u+\mathbf v,\mathbf u-\mathbf v\rangle=\|\mathbf u\|^2-\|\mathbf v\|^2=0\). In pictures: the diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular.

6Projection of v along u ~6 min

What is this?

Think of the line through \(\mathbf u\) as the floor, with the sun straight overhead. Hold the stick \(\mathbf v\) above it. Its shadow on the floor is the projection \(\alpha\mathbf u\). The rest of the stick, from the tip of the shadow up to the tip of \(\mathbf v\), is \(\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\), and it points straight up, at a right angle to the floor. That right angle is the only condition needed to find \(\alpha\).

Shine a light straight down onto the line through \(\mathbf u\): the shadow of \(\mathbf v\) on that line is its projection. The shadow is some multiple \(\alpha\mathbf u\), and what is left over, \(\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\), must stand at a right angle to the line. Slide 14 uses exactly this to find \(\alpha\).

Projection and Fourier coefficient (slide 14)

For \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\in V\), \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\ne\mathbf 0\), there exists a unique value \(\alpha\) such that \(\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf u\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf u\perp(\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u)&\Rightarrow\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\rangle=0\\ &\Rightarrow\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle-\alpha\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf u\rangle=0 \end{aligned} \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \text{Fourier's coefficient}\quad \alpha=\frac{\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle}{\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf u\rangle}. \]

We call \(\alpha\mathbf u\) the projection of \(\mathbf v\) along \(\mathbf u\).

In words: the projection of \(\mathbf v\) onto \(\mathbf u\) is \(\dfrac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}\,\mathbf u\): the dot product of the two, divided by the squared length of the one you project onto, times that vector.

Reading the formula
\(\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle\)
\(\|\mathbf u\|\) times the signed length of the shadow.
\(\div\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf u\rangle=\|\mathbf u\|^2\)
one \(\|\mathbf u\|\) cancels the one in the top, leaving the shadow length; the other turns that length into "how many copies of \(\mathbf u\)", since \(\alpha\mathbf u\) has length \(|\alpha|\,\|\mathbf u\|\).
\(\alpha\mathbf u\)
the shadow itself, as a vector lying along the floor line.
\(\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\)
the upright part, from the shadow's tip to the stick's tip: perpendicular to \(\mathbf u\).

Why is \(\alpha\) unique? The last equation is linear in \(\alpha\), and \(\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf u\rangle=\|\mathbf u\|^2\ne0\) because \(\mathbf u\ne\mathbf 0\), so there is exactly one solution. (The slide's condition \(\mathbf v\ne\mathbf 0\) is not needed: for \(\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\) the formula gives \(\alpha=0\).)

So every \(\mathbf v\) splits into two perpendicular pieces:

\[ \mathbf v=\underbrace{\alpha\mathbf u}_{\text{along }\mathbf u}+\underbrace{(\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u)}_{\perp\ \mathbf u}. \]

Past papers call \(\alpha\mathbf u\) the component of \(\mathbf v\) along (or in the direction of) \(\mathbf u\), and \(\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\) the component of \(\mathbf v\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf u\). Some older papers mean by "component of \(\mathbf v\) in the direction of \(\mathbf u\)" the signed length \(\dfrac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|}\) of the shadow instead; if it is unclear, give both.

Slide 14: the projection of v along u

uvprojection αuv − αu (perpendicular part)

Start: slide 14's \(\mathbf z=(4,3)\) on \(\mathbf u=(3,4)\). Set \(\mathbf v=(-4,3)\) to see the first part of slide 14: the projection shrinks to the zero vector. The green piece always meets the line through \(\mathbf u\) at a right angle, and it is the shortest way from the tip of \(\mathbf v\) to that line.

Example (slide 14)

Find the projection of \(\mathbf v=(-4,3)\) on \(\mathbf u=(3,4)\) and the projection of \(\mathbf z=(4,3)\) on \(\mathbf u=(3,4)\).

  1. The projection of \(\mathbf v\) on \(\mathbf u\) is \((0,0)\), as \(\mathbf v\perp\mathbf u\) (\(3(-4)+4(3)=0\)), so that \(\alpha=0\). A stick standing at right angles to the floor line casts its whole shadow onto a single point.
  2. To find the projection of \(\mathbf z\) on \(\mathbf u\): \[ \alpha=\frac{\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf z\rangle}{\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf u\rangle}=\frac{3(4)+4(3)}{3(3)+4(4)}=\frac{24}{25}. \] The projection of \(\mathbf z\) on \(\mathbf u\) is \[ \begin{aligned} \alpha\mathbf u&=\frac{24}{25}(3,4)=\left(\frac{72}{25},\frac{96}{25}\right)\\ &=(2.88,\,3.84). \end{aligned} \]

Going one step further (not on the slide): the perpendicular part is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf z-\alpha\mathbf u&=\left(4-\frac{72}{25},\ 3-\frac{96}{25}\right)\\ &=\left(\frac{28}{25},-\frac{21}{25}\right)=\frac7{25}(4,-3). \end{aligned} \]

Check it is perpendicular to \(\mathbf u\): \(\frac7{25}\big(3(4)+4(-3)\big)=0\ \checkmark\). Its length, \(\frac7{25}\cdot5=\frac75\), is the distance from the point \((4,3)\) to the line through the origin in the direction \((3,4)\).

Trap: which vector goes on the bottom

Projecting \(\mathbf v\) onto \(\mathbf u\) divides by \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u\) and multiplies \(\mathbf u\). Projecting \(\mathbf u\) onto \(\mathbf v\) is a different vector, \(\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v}\mathbf v\), along the other line. Also, the answer is a vector; \(\alpha\) alone is only its coefficient.

Beyond the handouts: the perpendicular part is the shortest distance

For any other multiple \(c\mathbf u\), the vector \(\mathbf v-c\mathbf u=(\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u)+(\alpha-c)\mathbf u\) is a sum of two perpendicular pieces, so by Pythagoras (section 5)

\[ \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf v-c\mathbf u\|^2&=\|\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\|^2+(\alpha-c)^2\|\mathbf u\|^2\\ &\ge\|\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\|^2. \end{aligned} \]

So \(\alpha\mathbf u\) is the point of the line through \(\mathbf u\) closest to \(\mathbf v\), and \(\|\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\|\) is the distance from \(\mathbf v\) to that line. "Closest point = foot of the perpendicular" is the whole idea behind least squares, next.

A useful formula for the length of the perpendicular part, again by Pythagoras:

\[ \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\|^2&=\|\mathbf v\|^2-\alpha^2\|\mathbf u\|^2\\ &=\|\mathbf v\|^2-\dfrac{(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)^2}{\|\mathbf u\|^2}. \end{aligned} \]

For \(\mathbf z\): \(25-\frac{24^2}{25}=\frac{49}{25}\), length \(\frac75\ \checkmark\)

You can now: compute the projection \(\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}\mathbf u\) and the perpendicular component \(\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\); find the distance from a point to a line through the origin; avoid dividing by \(\|\mathbf u\|\) instead of \(\|\mathbf u\|^2\).

7Least squares: the best-fit line ~9 min

What is this?

Picture a floor made of every point you can reach: all the combinations \(X\mathbf a\) of the columns of \(X\). The data vector \(\mathbf y\) hangs in the air above the floor, out of reach, because no line passes through all the points. The best you can do is stand on the floor directly below it. There the error \(\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*\) sticks straight up out of the floor, at right angles to every direction along it, and by Pythagoras every other spot on the floor is further from \(\mathbf y\). Here the "floor" is a plane inside \(\mathbb R^5\), but the right angle works the same way.

Measured data never lie exactly on a straight line. Five points give five equations for the two unknowns of a line \(y=a_0+a_1x\), and in general no line passes through all five: the system has no solution (L1). Slide 15 asks for the next best thing: the line that comes closest, in the sense that the sum of the squared vertical errors is as small as possible. Hence "least squares".

Problem (slide 15)

Find the best approximating line (\(y\) approximated by \(a_0+a_1x\)) for the data:

\(x\)13467
\(y\)12345

Setting it up as vectors (slide 15)

Put the five measured \(y\) values in one vector, and the five values the line predicts at \(x=1,3,4,6,7\) in another:

\[ \mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\\4\\5\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \text{approximation}=\begin{bmatrix}a_0+a_1(1)\\a_0+a_1(3)\\a_0+a_1(4)\\a_0+a_1(6)\\a_0+a_1(7)\end{bmatrix} \] \[ =\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&3\\1&4\\1&6\\1&7\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a_0\\a_1\end{bmatrix}=X\mathbf a. \]

Here \(X\) is the \(5\times2\) matrix (a column of 1s, then the \(x\) values) and \(\mathbf a=\begin{bmatrix}a_0\\a_1\end{bmatrix}\) holds the two unknowns. Denote the best \(\mathbf a\) by \(\mathbf a^*\).

The error vector \(\mathbf y-X\mathbf a\) holds the five vertical gaps between the data and the line, and its squared length \(\|\mathbf y-X\mathbf a\|^2\) is the sum of their squares. So "least squares" means: make \(\|\mathbf y-X\mathbf a\|\) as small as possible.

The key idea: closest means perpendicular

As \(\mathbf a\) varies, \(X\mathbf a=a_0(\text{column }1)+a_1(\text{column }2)\) runs over all linear combinations of the two columns of \(X\): a plane through the origin inside \(\mathbb R^5\). We want the point \(X\mathbf a^*\) of that plane closest to \(\mathbf y\). Section 6 showed that, for a line, the closest point is the foot of the perpendicular. The same holds for a plane: the error \(\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*\) must be perpendicular to every vector \(X\mathbf a\) of the plane. Reason: if \(\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*\) is perpendicular to the plane, then for any other point \(X\mathbf a\), Pythagoras gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf y-X\mathbf a\|^2&=\|\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*\|^2\\ &\quad+\|X\mathbf a^*-X\mathbf a\|^2\\ &\ge\|\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*\|^2. \end{aligned} \]
What is this? (normal equations)

Straight up out of the floor means at right angles to every direction along the floor. The floor is spanned by the two columns of \(X\), so it is enough to ask that the error has scalar product 0 with each column: two conditions for two unknowns. The rows of \(X^T\) are those columns, so both conditions together read \(X^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*)=\mathbf 0\).

Derivation of the normal equations (slide 15) \[ (\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*)\perp X\mathbf a\ \ \forall\mathbf a \] \[ \Rightarrow\ (\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*)\cdot X\mathbf a=0 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ (X\mathbf a)^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*)=0 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf a^T\big[X^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*)\big]=0\ \ \forall\mathbf a \] \[ \Rightarrow\ X^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*)=\mathbf 0 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ (X^TX)\,\mathbf a^*=X^T\mathbf y. \]

In words: the best \(\mathbf a^*\) solves the square system \(X^TX\mathbf a^*=X^T\mathbf y\), the normal equations: multiply the unsolvable system \(X\mathbf a=\mathbf y\) on the left by \(X^T\), and solve that instead.

Reading the formula
\(X^T\mathbf y\)
the scalar products of the data \(\mathbf y\) with each floor direction (each column of \(X\)).
\(X^TX\,\mathbf a^*\)
the same scalar products for the reachable point \(X\mathbf a^*\) on the floor.
"\(=\)"
\(\mathbf y\) and \(X\mathbf a^*\) cast the same shadows on the floor directions, so their difference, the error, casts none: it sticks straight up.
\(\mathbf a^*\)
the recipe (how much of each column) for the point of the floor directly below \(\mathbf y\).

The steps, one by one:

  • The dot product is \(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q=\mathbf p^T\mathbf q\) (section 1), and \(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q=\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf p\), so \((\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*)\cdot X\mathbf a=(X\mathbf a)^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*)\).
  • \((X\mathbf a)^T=\mathbf a^TX^T\), since a transpose reverses a product (L3).
  • Write \(\mathbf r=X^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*)\), a vector with 2 entries. \(\mathbf a^T\mathbf r=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf r=0\) for every \(\mathbf a\); choosing \(\mathbf a=\mathbf r\) gives \(\|\mathbf r\|^2=0\), so \(\mathbf r=\mathbf 0\). A vector with no shadow on any direction, its own direction included, can only be the zero vector.
  • Expanding \(X^T\mathbf y-X^TX\mathbf a^*=\mathbf 0\) gives the last line.

Sizes: \(X^T\) is \(2\times5\), so \(X^TX\) is \(2\times2\) and \(X^T\mathbf y\) is \(2\times1\). Five equations have become two.

Solving slide 15's example

Slide 15 writes the normal equations out in full, \(X^TX\,\mathbf a^*=X^T\mathbf y\):

\[ X^TX=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1&1&1\\1&3&4&6&7\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&3\\1&4\\1&6\\1&7\end{bmatrix}, \] \[ X^T\mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1&1&1\\1&3&4&6&7\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\\4\\5\end{bmatrix}. \]

Each entry is a row of \(X^T\) times a column (row-column rule):

  • \((1,1)\): \(1+1+1+1+1=5\) (the number of points); \((1,2)=(2,1)\): \(1+3+4+6+7=21\) (sum of \(x\)); \((2,2)\): \(1+9+16+36+49=111\) (sum of \(x^2\)).
  • \(X^T\mathbf y\): \(1+2+3+4+5=15\) (sum of \(y\)); sum of \(xy\): \[ \begin{aligned} &1(1)+3(2)+4(3)+6(4)+7(5)\\ &=1+6+12+24+35=78. \end{aligned} \]
\[ \Rightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}5&21\\21&111\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a_0^*\\a_1^*\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}15\\78\end{bmatrix}. \]

Solve with the \(2\times2\) inverse (L3): \(ad-bc=5(111)-21(21)=555-441=114\), so

Method: best-fit line \(y=a_0+a_1x\) through \(n\) points
  1. Do: Build \(X\) (first column all 1s, second column the \(x\) values) and \(\mathbf y\) (the \(y\) values). Row \(i\) of \(X\mathbf a=\mathbf y\) is the wish \(a_0+a_1x_i=y_i\); the column of 1s carries \(a_0\).
  2. Do: Compute \[ X^TX=\begin{bmatrix}n&\sum x\\ \sum x&\sum x^2\end{bmatrix}, \] \[ X^T\mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}\sum y\\ \sum xy\end{bmatrix}. \] These are the scalar products of the floor directions with each other and with the data; \(n\) equations shrink to two.
  3. Do: Solve \(X^TX\,\mathbf a^*=X^T\mathbf y\) (the \(2\times2\) inverse, or elimination). This finds the recipe for the point of the floor directly below \(\mathbf y\).
  4. Do: Write the line \(y=a_0^*+a_1^*x\). Optional check: the errors add to 0. The error is perpendicular to the column of 1s, and its scalar product with that column is just the sum of the errors.
Beyond the handouts: the closest point of any subspace

Nothing above used that the columns of \(X\) come from a line fit. For any matrix \(A\) and vector \(\mathbf y\): the point of the span of the columns of \(A\) closest to \(\mathbf y\) is \(A\mathbf c\), where \(\mathbf c\) solves \(A^TA\,\mathbf c=A^T\mathbf y\). This \(A\mathbf c\) is called the projection of \(\mathbf y\) onto the subspace, and \(\mathbf y-A\mathbf c\) is perpendicular to every column of \(A\).

Example. The point of \(V=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1,0),(0,1,1)\}\) closest to \(\mathbf y=(1,0,0)\). With these as the columns of \(A\):

\[ A^TA=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix},\quad A^T\mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf c=\frac13\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2/3\\-1/3\end{bmatrix}, \]

so the closest point is \(\frac23(1,1,0)-\frac13(0,1,1)=\left(\frac23,\frac13,-\frac13\right)\). Check: \(\mathbf y-A\mathbf c=\frac13(1,-1,1)\), and \((1,-1,1)\) is perpendicular to both spanning vectors (section 4) \(\checkmark\). Section 8 gives a second way, with an orthogonal basis.

If the columns of \(A\) are dependent, \(A^TA\) is not invertible and the normal equations have infinitely many solutions \(\mathbf c\); they all give the same closest point \(A\mathbf c\).

You can now: set up \(X\) and \(\mathbf y\) for a best-fit line; explain why the error must be perpendicular to the columns of \(X\) and derive \(X^TX\mathbf a^*=X^T\mathbf y\); solve for \(a_0^*,a_1^*\) exactly and check that the errors add to 0; find the closest point of any subspace with \(A^TA\mathbf c=A^T\mathbf y\).

8Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization ~8 min

What is this?

Gram–Schmidt builds perpendicular sticks one at a time. Keep the first stick. Take the second and remove its shadow on the first: what is left stands at right angles to the first. Take the third and remove its shadows on the first two: what is left is perpendicular to both. Finally cut every stick to length 1. This needs the starting vectors to be independent: a vector that lies in the span of the earlier ones is all shadow, and nothing is left of it.

Section 4 showed how convenient an orthonormal basis is, and slide 12 claimed one always exists. Gram–Schmidt is the recipe that builds it from any basis, using nothing but section 6's projection: take the vectors one at a time, and from each one subtract its projections along the vectors already built. What is left is perpendicular to all of them. Finally divide each by its length.

Method: Gram–Schmidt (the pattern of slide 16)

Given a basis \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\mathbf v_3,\dots\}\):

\[ \mathbf w_1=\mathbf v_1, \] \[ \mathbf w_2=\mathbf v_2-\frac{\langle\mathbf v_2,\mathbf w_1\rangle}{\langle\mathbf w_1,\mathbf w_1\rangle}\mathbf w_1, \] \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf w_3=\mathbf v_3&-\frac{\langle\mathbf v_3,\mathbf w_1\rangle}{\langle\mathbf w_1,\mathbf w_1\rangle}\mathbf w_1\\ &-\frac{\langle\mathbf v_3,\mathbf w_2\rangle}{\langle\mathbf w_2,\mathbf w_2\rangle}\mathbf w_2, \end{aligned} \]

and so on (each new \(\mathbf w_k\) subtracts the projections of \(\mathbf v_k\) along all earlier \(\mathbf w\)'s). Then \(\{\mathbf w_1,\mathbf w_2,\dots\}\) is an orthogonal basis, and

\[ \mathbf u_k=\frac{\mathbf w_k}{\|\mathbf w_k\|} \]

gives an orthonormal basis.

In words: keep the first vector. From the second, remove the part that points along the first. From the third, remove the parts that point along the first two. Then shrink every vector to length 1.

The recipe as steps:

  1. Do: Keep the first vector: \(\mathbf w_1=\mathbf v_1\). There is nothing earlier for it to be perpendicular to.
  2. Do: From the second, remove the part that points along the first: subtract the projection of \(\mathbf v_2\) along \(\mathbf w_1\). Removing the shadow on \(\mathbf w_1\) leaves only the part standing at right angles to it.
  3. Do: From the third, remove the parts that point along the first two: subtract the projections of \(\mathbf v_3\) along \(\mathbf w_1\) and along \(\mathbf w_2\) (the new vectors, not the old ones). \(\mathbf w_1\) and \(\mathbf w_2\) are already perpendicular, so removing one shadow does not bring back the other.
  4. Do: Check each new \(\mathbf w\) against the earlier ones (dot product 0). An arithmetic slip shows up at once as a leftover shadow.
  5. Do: Shrink every vector to length 1: \(\mathbf u_k=\mathbf w_k/\|\mathbf w_k\|\). Directions, and so the right angles, stay the same; only the lengths change, turning orthogonal into orthonormal.

Why \(\mathbf w_3\perp\mathbf w_1\): take the scalar product of the \(\mathbf w_3\) line with \(\mathbf w_1\). Since \(\langle\mathbf w_2,\mathbf w_1\rangle=0\) already, the last term contributes nothing, and the middle term contributes exactly \(-\langle\mathbf v_3,\mathbf w_1\rangle\), cancelling the first. The same argument with \(\mathbf w_2\) gives \(\mathbf w_3\perp\mathbf w_2\). The \(\mathbf w\)'s still span the same space as the \(\mathbf v\)'s, because each \(\mathbf w_k\) is \(\mathbf v_k\) plus a combination of earlier vectors.

Two practical points. (1) Multiplying a \(\mathbf w\) by a non-zero number does not change the projections along it (the number cancels top and bottom), so you may clear fractions, for example use \((4,1,-2)\) instead of \(\frac17(4,1,-2)\). Slide 16 does exactly this. (2) If some \(\mathbf w_k\) comes out as \(\mathbf 0\), then \(\mathbf v_k\) was a combination of the earlier vectors (the set was not independent): drop it and carry on.

Beyond the handouts: Gram–Schmidt for functions

The recipe uses only scalar products, so it works for the function scalar product \(\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1f(t)g(t)\,dt\) of section 1 too. Past papers ask this (May 2019 Q5).

Example. Orthonormalize \(\{1,\ t\}\) on \([0,1]\).

\(w_1=1\), with \(\langle1,1\rangle=\int_0^11\,dt=1\), so \(u_1=1\) already has length 1.

\(\langle t,1\rangle=\int_0^1t\,dt=\frac12\), so \(w_2=t-\frac{1/2}{1}\cdot1=t-\frac12\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \|w_2\|^2&=\int_0^1\left(t-\tfrac12\right)^2dt=\left[\tfrac13\left(t-\tfrac12\right)^3\right]_0^1\\ &=\tfrac1{24}+\tfrac1{24}=\tfrac1{12}, \end{aligned} \]

so

\[ \begin{aligned} u_2&=\dfrac{t-\frac12}{1/\sqrt{12}}=\sqrt{12}\left(t-\tfrac12\right)\\ &=\sqrt3\,(2t-1). \end{aligned} \]

Check: \(\int_0^1\sqrt3(2t-1)\,dt=\sqrt3\,[t^2-t]_0^1=0\ \checkmark\) (orthogonal to 1) and \(\int_0^13(2t-1)^2\,dt=3\cdot\frac13=1\ \checkmark\)

Adding \(t^2\) as a third function gives the third pair shown in section 1's figure; the exam taster finishes it.

Beyond the handouts: projection onto a subspace with an orthogonal basis

If \(\{\mathbf q_1,\dots,\mathbf q_k\}\) is an orthogonal basis of a subspace \(W\), the point of \(W\) closest to \(\mathbf y\) (its projection onto \(W\)) is the sum of the projections along each basis vector:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{proj}_W\mathbf y=&\ \frac{\langle\mathbf y,\mathbf q_1\rangle}{\langle\mathbf q_1,\mathbf q_1\rangle}\mathbf q_1+\cdots\\ &+\frac{\langle\mathbf y,\mathbf q_k\rangle}{\langle\mathbf q_k,\mathbf q_k\rangle}\mathbf q_k. \end{aligned} \]

In words: with a perpendicular basis, project along each basis vector separately and add. (With a basis that is not orthogonal this is wrong; use Gram–Schmidt first, or the normal equations of section 7.)

Example (section 7's): \(W=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1,0),(0,1,1)\}\), \(\mathbf y=(1,0,0)\). Gram–Schmidt: \(\mathbf q_1=(1,1,0)\), \(\mathbf q_2=(0,1,1)-\frac12(1,1,0)=\left(-\frac12,\frac12,1\right)\). Then \(\frac{\langle\mathbf y,\mathbf q_1\rangle}{\langle\mathbf q_1,\mathbf q_1\rangle}=\frac12\) and \(\frac{\langle\mathbf y,\mathbf q_2\rangle}{\langle\mathbf q_2,\mathbf q_2\rangle}=\frac{-1/2}{3/2}=-\frac13\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{proj}_W\mathbf y&=\tfrac12(1,1,0)-\tfrac13\left(-\tfrac12,\tfrac12,1\right)\\ &=\left(\tfrac23,\tfrac13,-\tfrac13\right), \end{aligned} \]

the same point as with the normal equations \(\checkmark\)

You can now: run Gram–Schmidt on two or three vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\), clearing fractions on the way, and normalize; check each new vector is perpendicular to the earlier ones; apply the same recipe to functions with \(\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1fg\,dt\); project onto a subspace by adding projections along an orthogonal basis.

9Summary

  • A scalar product \(\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle\) gives a number from two vectors and is commutative (SP1), distributive (SP2) and lets numbers out (SP3); also \(\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle\ge0\), with 0 only for \(\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\).
  • On \(\mathbb R^n\): \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\sum a_kb_k=\mathbf a^T\mathbf b\). On continuous functions on \([0,1]\): \(\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1f(t)g(t)\,dt\).
  • Length \(\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle}\); \(\mathbf v/\|\mathbf v\|\) is the unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf v\ne\mathbf 0\).
  • Cauchy–Schwarz \(|\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v|\le\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|\); angle: \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|}\). Triangle inequality \(\|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|\le\|\mathbf u\|+\|\mathbf v\|\).
  • \(\mathbf u\perp\mathbf v\) means \(\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle=0\). Orthogonal basis: basis vectors pairwise perpendicular; orthonormal: also of length 1.
  • In an orthonormal basis, coordinates are \(\alpha_i=\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf u_i\rangle\) and \(\|\mathbf v\|^2=\sum\alpha_i^2\); orthonormal vectors are independent.
  • Pythagoras: \(\mathbf u\perp\mathbf v\Rightarrow\|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|^2=\|\mathbf u\|^2+\|\mathbf v\|^2\). Parallelogram law: \(\|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|^2+\|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|^2=2\|\mathbf u\|^2+2\|\mathbf v\|^2\).
  • Projection of \(\mathbf v\) along \(\mathbf u\): \(\alpha\mathbf u\) with \(\alpha=\dfrac{\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle}{\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf u\rangle}\); \(\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\perp\mathbf u\) is the perpendicular component, and its length is the distance from \(\mathbf v\) to the line.
  • Least squares: the best \(\mathbf a^*\) for an unsolvable \(X\mathbf a=\mathbf y\) makes the error \(\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*\) perpendicular to the columns of \(X\): solve \(X^TX\mathbf a^*=X^T\mathbf y\). Same equations give the closest point of any subspace.
  • Gram–Schmidt: \(\mathbf w_k=\mathbf v_k-\) (projections of \(\mathbf v_k\) along \(\mathbf w_1,\dots,\mathbf w_{k-1}\)), then \(\mathbf u_k=\mathbf w_k/\|\mathbf w_k\|\).
Method: components along and perpendicular to u
  1. Compute \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v\) and \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u\) (the vector you project onto goes on the bottom).
  2. Along: \(\alpha\mathbf u\) with \(\alpha=\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}\).
  3. Perpendicular: \(\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\). Check its dot product with \(\mathbf u\) is 0.
  4. Its length: directly, or \(\|\mathbf v\|^2-\frac{(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)^2}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}\) under a square root.
Method: an orthonormal basis
  1. If the space is given by equations (\(A\mathbf x=\mathbf 0\), or "perpendicular to \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b\)"), first find a basis by row reduction (section 4).
  2. Gram–Schmidt: \(\mathbf w_1=\mathbf v_1\), then subtract projections along every earlier \(\mathbf w\). Scale a \(\mathbf w\) to clear fractions if you like.
  3. Check each new \(\mathbf w\) against the earlier ones (dot product 0).
  4. Divide each \(\mathbf w\) by its length.

10Common mistakes

✘Projection of \(\mathbf v\) on \(\mathbf u\) written as \(\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|}\mathbf u\) (dividing by the length, not its square), or as \(\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v}\mathbf v\) (wrong vector). ✔\(\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}\mathbf u\): the vector projected onto appears three times (twice on the bottom, once outside). Test: projecting \(\mathbf u\) on itself must give \(\mathbf u\).
✘Answering a "perpendicular component" question with the projection, or the other way round. ✔Along \(\mathbf u\): \(\alpha\mathbf u\). Perpendicular to \(\mathbf u\): \(\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u\). They add up to \(\mathbf v\).
✘In Gram–Schmidt, projecting along the original \(\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\) instead of the new \(\mathbf w_1,\mathbf w_2\), or forgetting one of the projections in \(\mathbf w_3\). ✔Every projection is along an already-orthogonal \(\mathbf w\). \(\mathbf w_3\) subtracts one projection for each earlier \(\mathbf w\). Check the dot products are 0 before normalizing.
✘Stopping at an orthogonal basis when the question says orthonormal (or normalizing when only "orthogonal" was asked, which is harmless but costs time). ✔Orthonormal = orthogonal and unit length: divide each \(\mathbf w\) by \(\|\mathbf w\|\) at the end.
✘In least squares, cancelling \(X^T\) from \(X^TX\mathbf a=X^T\mathbf y\) to get \(X\mathbf a=\mathbf y\), or computing \(XX^T\) (a \(5\times5\) matrix) instead of \(X^TX\). ✔\(X^T\) is not square and has no inverse; the whole point is that \(X\mathbf a=\mathbf y\) has no solution. \(X^TX\) is small: \(2\times2\) for a line.
✘Function scalar products: \(\langle f,f\rangle\) computed as \(\left(\int_0^1f\,dt\right)^2\), or \(\|f\|\) left as \(\int_0^1f^2\,dt\) without the square root. ✔\(\|f\|=\sqrt{\int_0^1f(t)^2\,dt}\): square first, then integrate, then take the root. For \(f=t\): \(\sqrt{1/3}\), not \(\frac12\).

3. For slide 15's data a student writes the normal equations from memory:

Answer

Orthogonal: \(1-1=0\). Not orthonormal: both have length \(\sqrt2\); \(\left\{\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,1),\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)\right\}\) is. Coefficients by projection: \(\frac{(3,1)\cdot(1,1)}{2}=2\) and \(\frac{(3,1)\cdot(1,-1)}{2}=1\), so \((3,1)=2(1,1)+1(1,-1)\).

5. With \(\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1f(t)g(t)\,dt\), find \(\langle1,t^2\rangle\) and \(\|t^2\|\).

Answer

\(\langle1,t^2\rangle=\int_0^1t^2\,dt=\frac13\). \(\|t^2\|=\sqrt{\int_0^1t^4\,dt}=\frac1{\sqrt5}\).

6. In a least-squares straight-line fit \(y=a_0+a_1x\), why do the errors \(y_i-(a_0^*+a_1^*x_i)\) always add up to 0?

Answer

The error vector is perpendicular to every column of \(X\) (the normal equations say \(X^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf a^*)=\mathbf 0\)). The first column is all 1s, and its dot product with the error vector is the sum of the errors.

7. True or false: \(\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle=u_1v_1-u_2v_2\) is a scalar product on \(\mathbb R^2\).

Answer

False. SP1 to SP3 hold, but \(\langle(0,1),(0,1)\rangle=-1\lt0\), so "length" \(\sqrt{\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle}\) would make no sense: the positivity rule (section 1, Beyond box) fails.

May 2019 Q410 marksolder formatGuide · L7.4 Proofs about orthonormal sets

Let \(\{\mathbf u_1,\mathbf u_2,\dots,\mathbf u_n\}\) be orthonormal vectors of a real vector space \(V\).

(a) [3 marks] Show that they are linearly independent.
(b) [3 marks] Show that the representation \(\mathbf v=\alpha_1\mathbf u_1+\cdots+\alpha_n\mathbf u_n\) is unique.
(c) [4 marks] Find the coordinates \(\alpha_i\) of \(\mathbf v\) with respect to \(\{\mathbf u_i\}\). Express \(\|\mathbf v\|\) in terms of the \(\alpha_i\).

Hint

One trick does all three parts: take the scalar product of the equation with \(\mathbf u_i\), and use \(\langle\mathbf u_j,\mathbf u_i\rangle=0\) for \(j\ne i\), \(=1\) for \(j=i\) (section 4, first Beyond box). For (b), suppose two representations and subtract them.

Answer

(a) Every weight in \(\sum c_j\mathbf u_j=\mathbf 0\) is \(c_i=0\). (b) The difference of two representations has all weights 0 by (a). (c)

\[ \alpha_i=\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf u_i\rangle, \] \[ \|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{\alpha_1^2+\cdots+\alpha_n^2}. \]
