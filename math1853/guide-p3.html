Exam guide: Common distributions

What the exam asks about chapter P3 and how to answer it: five question types in the current syllabus (plus one from older papers), a method card for each, and every past-paper question from Dec 2012 to May 2026 with a hint and a full solution. The distributions are taught in the P3 note; this page is for practice.

P3At a glance

This chapter is predictable. Almost every question names a distribution (or describes repeated trials, waiting times or a bell curve) and asks for one probability, a mean or a variance. Know the six distributions by heart (pmf or density, mean, variance), know how to read the normal table both ways, and the marks follow. The only real thinking is recognising the model from a story.

Exam format
  • No calculators since Dec 2022 (they were allowed Dec 2012 – Dec 2014, May 2015, Dec 2016 and Dec 2021). Questions then either say "you can simply give the final expression" or are built so that the table does the work. Leave \(\binom{9}{5}0.6^5\,0.4^4\) as it is; don't try to multiply it out.
  • Only the standard normal table \(\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z)\) is printed, for \(z=0.00\) to \(3.59\), four decimals. There is no table for negative \(z\): use \(\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)\). Beyond \(z=3.59\) take \(\Phi(z)\approx1\). Round each \(z\) to 2 decimals before you look it up, and write the \(z\) you used.
  • No formula sheet. The means and variances in the Ready answer of P3.1 and P3.3 must be memorised.
  • Multiple choice. Dec 2025 asked four P3 questions as MC (2 marks each, four options), and May 2026 Q7 asks ten answer-only short questions. No working is marked: one slip in a \(z\)-value costs the whole question, so check the sign of each \(z\).
  • Time. 3 hours for 100 marks is about 1.8 minutes per mark: a 5-mark table question should take about 9 minutes.

The cards

P3.1 Trials: binomial, geometric, negative binomial 22 parts · 15 papers · 2–5 marks each

Looks likeCoins, calls, draws, tests, "a batch of 10", "until the first / \(r\)-th success", "\(P(X\text{ even})\)", "\(P(X\le m\mid X\ge n)\)".

DoDecide what is fixed: the number of trials (binomial) or the number of successes (geometric, negative binomial). Write the pmf with its support, then sum or use the tail \(P(X\gt n)=q^n\).

WatchNegative binomial: the last trial is a success, so choose the other \(r-1\) successes among the first \(k-1\) trials: \(\binom{k-1}{r-1}\), not \(\binom{k}{r}\).

P3.2 Poisson 5 parts · 4 papers (older format only) · 3–4 marks

Looks like\(K\frac{\lambda^i}{i!}\), "arrivals", "approximate with a Poisson distribution", \(E[X^2]\).

DoUse \(\sum\frac{\lambda^i}{i!}=e^\lambda\); mean = variance = \(\lambda\); independent Poissons add; \(\text{Bin}(n,p)\approx\text{Poisson}(np)\).

Watch\(P(X\ge3)\) is \(1-P(0)-P(1)-P(2)\): three terms, not two.

P3.3 Exponential 13 parts · 11 papers · 3–10 marks

Looks like"Exponential with parameter \(\lambda\)", \(P(X\gt a\mid X\gt b)\), \(E[X+X^2]\), \(\min\) or \(\max\) of two lifetimes, a tail \(P(T\gt t)\).

DoEverything comes from the tail \(P(X\gt t)=e^{-\lambda t}\) and \(E(X)=\frac1\lambda\), \(E(X^2)=\frac2{\lambda^2}\).

WatchMemorylessness only applies to "\(X\gt\) something" conditions. \(P(X\gt1\mid X\lt2)\) is an ordinary conditional probability.

P3.4 Normal probabilities with the table 16 parts · 14 papers · 2–5 marks

Looks like\(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), "acceptable if between …", \(Y=aX+b\), a sum of independent normals, "find \(k\) such that …".

DoStandardise \(z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\) (with \(\sigma=\sqrt{\text{variance}}\)), look up \(\Phi\), use \(\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)\).

Watch\(N(50,16)\) means variance 16, so \(\sigma=4\). Dividing by 16 is the most common slip.

P3.5 Normal approximation and CLT 8 parts · 6 papers · 2–10 marks

Looks likeA binomial or Poisson with a large parameter and "use the table"; a sample mean or a sum of many i.i.d. variables.

DoReplace by a normal with the same mean and variance; for an integer-valued variable widen \([a,b]\) to \([a-0.5,\ b+0.5]\).

WatchThe correction always widens the interval for \(\le\) at both ends; for strict \(\lt\) first make it \(\le\) with integers.

Learn the distributions first in the chapter note: binomial, geometric, negative binomial, exponential, Poisson, standardising and the table, normal approximation; all formulas on one screen in the summary.

Conventions on this page (the handout's): Geometric(\(p\)) counts trials up to and including the first success, values \(1,2,3,\dots\); "exponential with parameter \(\lambda\)" means density \(\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\), mean \(\frac1\lambda\); \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) gives the variance second. Table values are the printed four-decimal \(\Phi(z)\) values.

P3.1Trials: binomial, geometric, negative binomial

22 parts · Dec 2025 (×2, MC), May 2024 (×2), May 2023, Dec 2022 (×2); older format Dec 2018, Dec 2017, May 2017, Dec 2016 (×2), May 2016, Dec 2015, Dec 2014, May 2014, Dec 2013, May 2013 (×3), Dec 2012 (×2) · Learn it: note §2, §3, §4, §5, §6

Method
  1. Find the trial and its success probability \(p\) (write \(q=1-p\)). If \(p\) itself needs a normal table or a total-probability step, do that first and write it as a number.
  2. Check the trials are independent with the same \(p\) ("with replacement", "independently", "each house").
  3. Choose the model by what is fixed:
    • number of trials \(n\) fixed, count successes → binomial;
    • wait for the 1st success, count trials → geometric;
    • wait for the \(r\)-th success, count trials → negative binomial.
  4. Write the event in terms of \(X\) (e.g. "more than 7 of 10" is \(X\ge8\); "more than two tests needed" is \(X\gt2\)) and use the pmf or the tail.
  5. For "show / find the distribution" questions, give the pmf with its support, and justify a mean or variance by writing \(X\) as a sum of simpler independent variables.
Ready answer · the trial distributions
Model\(P(X=k)\)\(E\), \(\operatorname{Var}\)
Bin\((n,p)\)\(\binom nk p^kq^{n-k}\), \(k=0,\dots,n\)\(np\), \(npq\)
Geometric\((p)\)\(q^{k-1}p\), \(k=1,2,\dots\)\(\frac1p\), \(\frac q{p^2}\)
NB\((r,p)\)\(\binom{k-1}{r-1}p^rq^{k-r}\), \(k\ge r\)\(\frac rp\), \(\frac{rq}{p^2}\)

Geometric tail: \(P(X\gt n)=q^n\) (the first \(n\) trials all fail), so \(P(X\ge n)=q^{n-1}\).

Useful sums: \(\sum_{k\ge0}q^k=\frac1{1-q}\), \(\sum_{k\ge1}kq^{k-1}=\frac1{(1-q)^2}\), \(\sum_{k\ge2}k(k-1)q^{k-2}=\frac2{(1-q)^3}\).

Dec 2025 Q182 marksmultiple choice

You flip a fair coin until you get heads for the third time. Let \(X\) be the number of flips needed. Which gives \(P(X=8)\)?

(A) \(\binom83\left(\frac12\right)^3\left(\frac12\right)^2\)   (B) \(\binom72\left(\frac12\right)^8\)   (C) \(\left(\frac12\right)^8\)   (D) \(\binom82\left(\frac12\right)^8\)

Hint

P3.1, negative binomial with \(r=3\). What must flip 8 be, and how many heads are in the first 7 flips?

Solution
  1. \(X=8\) means: flip 8 is a head, and flips 1–7 contain exactly 2 heads (and 5 tails).
  2. The 2 heads can be placed among the first 7 flips in \(\binom72\) ways; each sequence of 8 fair flips has probability \(\left(\frac12\right)^8\).
  3. So \[ \begin{aligned} &P(X=8)\\ &=\binom72\left(\frac12\right)^3\left(\frac12\right)^5\\ &=\binom72\left(\frac12\right)^8=\frac{21}{256}. \end{aligned} \]

Marker: (D) is the trap: \(\binom82\) also counts sequences whose last flip is a tail, where the third head came earlier. (A) has only 5 flips' worth of probability.

\[ \boxed{\text{(B)}} \]

Dec 2025 Q212 marksmultiple choice

Which of the following scenarios is best modeled by a geometric distribution?

(A) The number of customers arriving at a bank in one hour. (B) The number of heads when flipping a coin 10 times. (C) The number of cards drawn from a shuffled deck until you get a heart. (D) The lifetime of a machine before it fails.

Hint

P3.1, recognising the model. Geometric = number of trials up to the first success. Which option counts trials until something happens?

Solution
  1. (A) counts arrivals in a fixed time: Poisson. (B) counts successes in a fixed number of trials: binomial. (D) is a continuous waiting time: exponential.
  2. (C) counts draws until the first heart: the "trials until the first success" pattern of the geometric distribution.

Marker: (D) is tempting because it is a waiting time, but a lifetime is continuous; the geometric distribution counts discrete trials.

\[ \boxed{\text{(C)}} \]

Reading used: drawing from one deck without replacement is not exactly geometric (the chance of a heart changes after each draw); it is exactly geometric if each card is put back and the deck reshuffled. (C) is still the intended answer, being the only "until the first success" count.

May 2024 Q11(a), (b)5% + 5%

Part (c): P3.4 on this page.

(a) Let \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\) be independent and identically distributed random variables, each following a Bernoulli distribution with parameter \(p\). Determine the conditional probability that \(X_1=1\) given that \(\sum_{i=1}^nX_i=k\).

(b) Suppose that \(X\) has a geometric distribution with parameter \(p\). Determine the probability that the value of \(X\) is an even integer.

Hint

P3.1. (a) \(S=\sum X_i\) is Bin\((n,p)\); split \(S\) into \(X_1\) and the sum of the other \(n-1\). (b) Add \(P(X=2)+P(X=4)+\cdots\), a geometric series in \(q^2\).

Solution
  1. (a) Write \(S=X_1+T\) with \(T=X_2+\cdots+X_n\), which is \(\text{Bin}(n-1,p)\) and independent of \(X_1\). For \(1\le k\le n\): \[ \begin{aligned} &P(X_1=1\mid S=k)\\ &=\frac{P(X_1=1,\ T=k-1)}{P(S=k)}\\ &=\frac{p\binom{n-1}{k-1}p^{k-1}q^{n-k}}{\binom nkp^kq^{n-k}}\\ &=\frac{\binom{n-1}{k-1}}{\binom nk}=\frac kn. \end{aligned} \] (For \(k=0\) the probability is \(0\), which \(\frac kn\) also gives.)
  2. (b) With \(P(X=j)=q^{j-1}p\), \(j=1,2,\dots\): \[ \begin{aligned} &P(X\text{ even})\\ &=\sum_{m=1}^\infty q^{2m-1}p\\ &=pq\left(1+q^2+q^4+\cdots\right)\\ &=\frac{pq}{1-q^2}=\frac{pq}{(1-q)(1+q)}\\ &=\frac{q}{1+q}=\frac{1-p}{2-p}. \end{aligned} \]

Marker: in (a) state that \(X_1\) and \(X_2+\cdots+X_n\) are independent before multiplying their probabilities.

\[ \boxed{\text{(a) }\frac kn\qquad\text{(b) }\frac{1-p}{2-p}} \]

Check (a): by symmetry each of the \(n\) trials is equally likely to be one of the \(k\) successes. Check (b): \(p=1\) gives 0 (the first trial always succeeds).

Reading used: the handout's geometric distribution, \(X=1,2,3,\dots\). With the other convention (failures before the first success, \(X=0,1,2,\dots\)) the answer would be \(\frac1{2-p}\).

May 2023 Q10(a)5%

Parts (b), (c): P3.3 and P3.4 on this page.

A biased coin, when tossed, will come up heads with probability 0.6 and tails with probability 0.4. This coin is tossed 9 times and the results are observed. What is the probability that the number of heads observed is more than that of tails? (You can simply give the final expression without computing its numerical value.)

Hint

P3.1, binomial. With 9 tosses, "more heads than tails" is a condition on the number of heads alone.

Solution
  1. Let \(X\) be the number of heads: 9 independent tosses, \(p=0.6\), so \(X\sim\text{Bin}(9,0.6)\).
  2. Tails \(=9-X\). Heads more than tails: \(X\gt9-X\), i.e. \(X\gt4.5\), i.e. \(X\ge5\).
  3. \[ \begin{aligned} &P(X\ge5)\\ &=\sum_{k=5}^{9}\binom9k(0.6)^k(0.4)^{9-k}. \end{aligned} \]
\[ \boxed{\sum_{k=5}^{9}\binom9k(0.6)^k(0.4)^{9-k}} \]

Numerically \(\approx0.7334\) (not needed).

Dec 2022 Q7(a), (d)3% + 5%

Parts (b), (c): P3.3 and P3.4 on this page.

(a) Let \(X_1\) be a binomial random variable with parameters \(n_1\) and \(p\), and let \(X_2\) be an independent binomial random variable with parameters \(n_2\) and \(p\). What is the distribution of \(X_1+X_2\)? Justify your answer.

(d) Let \(X\) be a geometric random variable with parameter \(p\). Then, for any two positive integers \(m,n\) with \(m\gt n\), find \(P(X\le m\mid X\ge n)\).

Hint

P3.1. (a) A binomial is a count of successes in independent trials; put the two sets of trials together. (d) Use the tail \(P(X\ge n)=q^{n-1}\).

Solution
  1. (a) \(X_1\) is the number of successes in \(n_1\) independent Bernoulli(\(p\)) trials, and \(X_2\) the number in another \(n_2\) such trials, independent of the first.
  2. So \(X_1+X_2\) is the number of successes in \(n_1+n_2\) independent trials, each with success probability \(p\) (the same \(p\) is essential): \(X_1+X_2\sim\text{Bin}(n_1+n_2,p)\).
  3. (d) Write \(q=1-p\). For integers, \[ \begin{aligned} P(X\ge n)&=P(\text{first }n-1\text{ fail})\\ &=q^{n-1} \end{aligned} \] and \(P(X\le m)=1-q^m\).
  4. \(\{X\le m\}\cap\{X\ge n\}\) is the event \(n\le X\le m\), so \[ \begin{aligned} &P(X\le m\mid X\ge n)\\ &=\frac{P(X\ge n)-P(X\gt m)}{P(X\ge n)}\\ &=\frac{q^{n-1}-q^m}{q^{n-1}}\\ &=1-q^{m-n+1}. \end{aligned} \]

Marker: in (a) say why the trials can be pooled: independence of \(X_1,X_2\) and the same \(p\).

\[ \boxed{\begin{gathered}\text{(a) }X_1+X_2\sim\text{Bin}(n_1+n_2,\,p)\\ \text{(d) }1-(1-p)^{m-n+1}\end{gathered}} \]

(d) by lack of memory: given \(X\ge n\) (first \(n-1\) failed), \(X\le m\) needs a success within the next \(m-n+1\) trials. A formal proof of (a): \[ \begin{aligned} &P(X_1+X_2=k)\\ &=\sum_j\binom{n_1}j\binom{n_2}{k-j}p^kq^{n_1+n_2-k} \end{aligned} \] and Vandermonde's identity \[ \sum_j\tbinom{n_1}j\tbinom{n_2}{k-j}=\tbinom{n_1+n_2}k. \]

Dec 2018 Q7(a)3 marksolder format

Parts (b), (c), (d): P3.4, P3.3, P3.5 on this page.

At a "busy time," a telephone exchange is very close to its capacity, so callers have difficulty in successfully placing their calls. Suppose that for each attempt, the probability of a successful call is \(p=0.05\). What is the probability that the 5-th attempt is the first successful call? What is the probability that the 10-th attempt is the third successful call? (You can simply give the final expression without computing its numerical value.)

Hint

P3.1: geometric for the first question, negative binomial with \(r=3\) for the second.

Solution
  1. Attempts are independent trials with \(p=0.05\), \(q=0.95\).
  2. First success on attempt 5: four failures then a success, \(P=q^4p=(0.95)^4(0.05)\).
  3. Third success on attempt 10: attempt 10 succeeds and attempts 1–9 contain exactly 2 successes: \(P=\binom92(0.05)^3(0.95)^7\).
\[ \boxed{\begin{gathered}(0.95)^4(0.05)\\ \tbinom92(0.05)^3(0.95)^7\end{gathered}} \]

\(\binom92=36\); the values are about 0.0407 and 0.00314.

Dec 2017 Q7(c)10 points (whole question)older format

Parts (a), (b): conditional probability, in the P2 guide (P2.3). Part (a) is redone in step 1 below.

There are two boxes A and B. Box A contains 1 white ball and 5 red balls and Box B contains 3 white balls and 1 red ball. A player is asked to draw one ball at random from each box (one ball from Box A and one ball from Box B) with replacement. If two drawn balls have the same colour then the player will get a prize. The player is allowed to attempt this game for three times. (a) What is the probability that the player gets the prize in his/her "first attempt"? (b) Suppose that the player got the prize in his/her "first attempt", find the conditional probability that the white balls were drawn. (c) Find the probability that player can get the prize.

Hint

P3.1, geometric. Each attempt is a trial with success probability from (a). "Can get the prize" within three attempts is the complement of three failures.

Solution
  1. One attempt: both white or both red, \[ \begin{aligned} p&=\tfrac16\cdot\tfrac34+\tfrac56\cdot\tfrac14\\ &=\tfrac{3}{24}+\tfrac{5}{24}=\tfrac13. \end{aligned} \]
  2. Attempts are independent (balls replaced), each with success probability \(\frac13\).
  3. No prize in three attempts: \(\left(\frac23\right)^3=\frac8{27}\). So \(P(\text{prize})=1-\frac8{27}=\frac{19}{27}\).
\[ \boxed{\tfrac{19}{27}} \]

Same as \(P(X\le3)\) for \(X\sim\text{Geometric}(\frac13)\): \[ \begin{aligned} &\frac13+\frac23\cdot\frac13+\left(\frac23\right)^2\frac13\\ &=\frac{19}{27}. \end{aligned} \]

Reading used: "can get the prize" = at least one prize in the three allowed attempts (it does not matter whether the game stops after the first prize).

May 2017 Q6(a)10% (whole question)older format

Part (b): P3.4 on this page.

Let \(X\) be a Geometric(\(p\)) random variable, \(0\lt p\lt1\), and let \(Y\) be an independent Geometric(\(q\)) random variable, \(0\lt q\lt1\). Nathaniel conjectures that "\(X+Y\) is a Geometric(\(r\)) random variable". Is the conjecture true or false? If true, what is \(r\)? If not, give a counter-example.

Hint

P3.1. What is the smallest value \(X+Y\) can take? What is the smallest value a geometric variable can take?

Solution
  1. \(X\ge1\) and \(Y\ge1\), so \(X+Y\ge2\) and \(P(X+Y=1)=0\).
  2. Any Geometric(\(r\)) variable \(W\) (with \(0\lt r\le1\)) has \(P(W=1)=r\gt0\).
  3. So \(X+Y\) cannot be geometric, for any \(p,q\). Concrete counter-example: \(p=q=\frac12\); then \(P(X+Y=1)=0\) but a geometric variable has \(P(W=1)\gt0\).
\[ \boxed{\text{False: }P(X+Y=1)=0\ne r} \]

Even \(X+Y-1\) is not geometric in general: with \(p=q=\frac12\), \(P(X+Y=k)=(k-1)\left(\frac12\right)^k\), which is negative binomial NB(\(2,\frac12\)), not geometric.

Dec 2016 Q9(c)8 points (whole question)older format

Parts (a), (b): total probability and Bayes, in the P2 guide (P2.3). Part (a) is redone in step 1 below.

There are two boxes: A and B. Box A contains two red balls and one blue ball while Box B contains one red ball and one blue ball. Each time, a fair coin is tossed, if it is a head, then a ball will be randomly drawn from Box A (with replacement), otherwise a ball is randomly drawn from Box B (with replacement). (a) Find the probability that a red ball is drawn. (b) Suppose a red ball is drawn, what is the conditional probability that Box B was chosen? (c) The above experiment of drawing balls will continue until the first blue ball is observed. Find the probability that at least 3 draws are required for this experiment.

Hint

P3.1, geometric. Each draw is a trial with \(P(\text{blue})\) from total probability. At least 3 draws means the first two draws are red.

Solution
  1. One draw: \[ \begin{aligned} P(\text{red})&=\frac12\cdot\frac23+\frac12\cdot\frac12\\ &=\frac7{12}, \end{aligned} \] so \(P(\text{blue})=\frac5{12}\).
  2. A new coin toss and replacement each time make the draws independent with the same probabilities, so the number of draws \(N\sim\text{Geometric}\left(\frac5{12}\right)\).
  3. \[ \begin{aligned} P(N\ge3)&=P(\text{first two red})\\ &=\left(\frac7{12}\right)^2=\frac{49}{144}. \end{aligned} \]
\[ \boxed{\tfrac{49}{144}\approx0.340} \]

Dec 2016 Q10(a)(ii)15 points (whole question)older format

Part (a)(i): P3.4 on this page; part (b): confidence interval in the P4 guide (P4.1). Same numbers as Dec 2013 Q9(a).

A manufacturer produces certain tools of mean weight 10kg. Assume that the weight of the tools produced follows the Normal distribution \(N(10,0.01)\) with a standard deviation of 0.1kg. A produced tool is said to be acceptable if its weight is between 9.9kg and 10.1kg. (i) Find the probability that a tool produced is acceptable. (ii) A batch of 10 tools was produced independently. Find the probability that there are 8 or more acceptable tools.

Hint

P3.1 after P3.4: part (i) gives \(p\); the number of acceptable tools in the batch is Bin\((10,p)\).

Solution
  1. From (i): \[ \begin{aligned} p&=P(9.9\le W\le10.1)\\ &=2\Phi(1)-1=0.6826 \end{aligned} \] (\(z=\pm1.00\)); \(q=0.3174\).
  2. The tools are independent, so \(Y\) = number acceptable \(\sim\text{Bin}(10,\,0.6826)\).
  3. \[ \begin{aligned} &P(Y\ge8)\\ &=\tbinom{10}8p^8q^2+\tbinom{10}9p^9q+p^{10}\\ &=45p^8q^2+10p^9q+p^{10}\\ &\approx0.2134+0.1021+0.0221\\ &\approx0.338. \end{aligned} \]
\[ \boxed{P(Y\ge8)\approx0.338} \]

May 2016 Q6(c)15% (whole question)older format

Parts (a), (b): P3.3 and P3.5 on this page.

Two boy scouts Nathan and Daniel are asked to sell five candy bars to raise money for certain charity activity, and they are not supposed to return home until five candy bars have been sold. So the children go door to door, selling candy bars. At each house, there is a 0.4 probability of selling one candy bar and a 0.6 probability of selling nothing. Suppose that the last candy bar is sold at the \(X\)-th house. What is the distribution of \(X\)? Find \(E[X]\) and justify your answer.

Hint

P3.1, negative binomial: \(X\) is the trial on which the 5th success happens, \(p=0.4\).

Solution
  1. Each house is an independent trial; success = a bar sold, \(p=0.4\). \(X\) = number of houses until the 5th success: \(X\sim\text{NB}(5,\,0.4)\).
  2. House \(k\) sells the 5th bar, and the first \(k-1\) houses sell exactly 4: \[ \begin{aligned} P(X=k)&=\tbinom{k-1}{4}(0.4)^5(0.6)^{k-5},\\ k&=5,6,7,\dots \end{aligned} \]
  3. Write \(X=G_1+\cdots+G_5\), where \(G_i\) is the number of houses from the \((i-1)\)-th sale up to the \(i\)-th; the \(G_i\) are independent Geometric(0.4), each with mean \(\frac1{0.4}=2.5\).
  4. \(E[X]=5\times2.5=12.5\).
\[ \boxed{X\sim\text{NB}(5,0.4),\quad E[X]=12.5} \]

Dec 2015 Q7(a)(i)15 points (whole question)older format

Part (a)(ii): estimating \(p\), in the P4 guide (P4.3); part (b): confidence interval, P4.1.

Consider the experiment of tossing a coin for infinitely many times. Suppose that all tosses are independent, and upon each toss, the coin will come up heads with probability \(p\) and tails with probability \(1-p\), where \(0\lt p\lt1\). (i) What is the expected number of tosses needed to see the first head? Justify your answer.

Hint

P3.1: the number of tosses is Geometric(\(p\)). Justify by computing \(\sum kq^{k-1}p\).

Method
  1. Recognise it: a pmf of the form \(K\frac{\lambda^i}{i!}\), "arrivals" or "number of events" in a time or space, or "approximate with a Poisson distribution".

P3.3Exponential: memory, moments, sums, min and max

13 parts · Dec 2025, Dec 2024, May 2023, Dec 2022, Dec 2021; older format May 2019, Dec 2018, May 2016, May 2015, May 2014, May 2013 (×3) · Learn it: note §7, §8

Method
  1. Write the tail \(P(X\gt t)=e^{-\lambda t}\) (\(t\ge0\)). Every probability is a difference of tails: \(P(a\lt X\lt b)=e^{-\lambda a}-e^{-\lambda b}\).
  2. Conditional probability: if the condition is \(X\gt s\) and the event is about \(X\) beyond \(s\), use lack of memory, \(P(X\gt s+t\mid X\gt s)=e^{-\lambda t}\). Otherwise (e.g. condition \(X\lt2\)) use \(\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}\) with tails.
  3. Moments: \(E(X)=\frac1\lambda\), \(E(X^2)=\frac2{\lambda^2}\), then expand: \[ \begin{aligned} &E[(X+c)^2]\\ &=E(X^2)+2cE(X)+c^2. \end{aligned} \]
  4. Sums of independent exponentials: means add and variances add (the sum is not exponential). For \(E(S^2)\) use \(\operatorname{Var}(S)+E(S)^2\).
  5. Minimum: \(P(\min\gt t)=\prod P(X_i\gt t)\), so \(\min\sim\text{Expo}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\). Maximum: \(P(\max\le t)=\prod P(X_i\le t)\).
  6. A distribution given by its tail \(P(T\gt t)\): density \(f=-\frac{d}{dt}P(T\gt t)\), then integrate term by term.
Ready answer · Expo(\(\lambda\)) \[ \begin{aligned} f(x)&=\lambda e^{-\lambda x},\quad x\ge0\\ P(X\gt t)&=e^{-\lambda t},\quad t\ge0\\ E(X)&=\tfrac1\lambda,\quad E(X^2)=\tfrac2{\lambda^2}\\ \operatorname{Var}(X)&=\tfrac1{\lambda^2} \end{aligned} \]

"Parameter \(\lambda\)" is the rate; "mean \(\frac1\lambda\)" says the same thing. Memorylessness proof in one line: \(\frac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}}=e^{-\lambda t}\).

Method

Hint

P3.5. (a) The sum is \(P(41\le X\le45)\); \(np=50\), \(npq=25\); correct both ends by 0.5. (b) Pair the terms: \(W=\frac1{24}\sum(X_i-Y_i)\); find the mean and variance of one \(X_i-Y_i\).

Solution
  1. (a) \(\mu=np=50\), \(\sigma^2=npq=25\), \(\sigma=5\). The sum is \[ \begin{aligned} &P(41\le X\le45)\\ &\approx P(40.5\le Y\le45.5), \end{aligned} \] \(Y\sim N(50,25)\).
