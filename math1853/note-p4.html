Some elementary statistical inferences

0Where this chapter sits

P4 is the last chapter of MATH1853: Part I (L1 to L7) is linear algebra, and Part II is complex numbers (P1), probability (P2), distributions (P3) and this chapter. In P2 and P3 you were told the distribution (say \(X\sim N(600,20^2)\)) and worked out probabilities. Statistics runs the other way: you see some data and want to say something about the unknown parts of the distribution. This is statistical inference: point estimation (one best guess, such as "the mean is about 600", sections 1 to 4) and interval estimation (a range with a confidence level, such as "we are 95% confident that the mean lies between 593.8 and 606.2", sections 5 to 11).

1Parameters and the parameter space ~4 min

What is this?

Think of a machine with a dial. You know what kind of machine it is: say it counts the faulty bulbs in a box of 20, or it produces bell-shaped exam scores. What you cannot see is where the dial is set (the faulty rate, or the average score). That hidden setting is the parameter. The whole chapter is about reading the dial from the numbers the machine puts out.

The statistical problem

In a typical statistical problem, we have a random variable \(X\) of interest, but its density function \(f(u)\) or its distribution \(p(x):=P(X=x)\) is not known. Our ignorance about \(f(u)\) or \(p(x)\) can roughly be classified in one of two ways:

  1. \(f(u)\) or \(p(x)\) is completely unknown.
  2. The form of \(f(u)\) or \(p(x)\) is known down to a parameter \(\theta\), where \(\theta\) may be a vector, in which case we may use the bold character \(\boldsymbol\theta\).

For now (and for the whole chapter), we consider the second classification.

In words: we know what kind of distribution \(X\) has (binomial, normal, Poisson, ...), but one or more of the numbers in its formula are unknown. Those unknown numbers are the parameters.

Recall from P2 and P3: \(f\) is used for a continuous random variable (a density, probabilities are areas under \(f\)), and \(p(x)=P(X=x)\) for a discrete one. The letter \(u\) in \(f(u)\) is just the variable of the function.

Examples

The unknown parameters are the ones we want to find:

  1. \(X\sim\text{Binomial}(n,p)\), where \(n\) is known but \(p\) is unknown. So \(\theta=p\).
  2. \(X\) has a normal distribution \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), where both the mean \(\mu\) and the variance \(\sigma^2\) of \(X\) are unknown. So \(\boldsymbol\theta=(\mu,\sigma^2)\), a vector with two entries.

For instance in (a): a factory knows it packs \(n=20\) bulbs in a box, and \(X\) is the number of faulty bulbs in a box; the faulty rate \(p\) is what management wants to know. In (b): exam scores are roughly bell-shaped, but nobody knows the true average or spread for this year's cohort.

What is this? (parameter space)

The parameter space is every position the dial could possibly be in. A faulty rate can be anywhere from 0 to 1. A mean can be any real number, and a variance any positive number. Listing the possible settings comes before trying to read the actual one.

Notation: the parameter space

We often denote this problem by saying that the random variable \(X\) has a density or distribution function of the form

\[ f(u;\theta)\quad\text{or}\quad p(x;\theta),\qquad \theta\in\Theta, \]

for a specified set \(\Theta\) (capital theta), called the parameter space. For example, in (a) above, \(\Theta=[0,1]\).

We call \(\theta\) a parameter of the distribution. Because \(\theta\) is unknown, we want to estimate it.

In words: the semicolon in \(f(u;\theta)\) separates the variable \(u\) from the parameter \(\theta\): for each possible value of \(\theta\) there is one density. \(\Theta\) is the set of all values \(\theta\) could take.

In (a), \(\Theta=[0,1]\) because a probability lies between 0 and 1. In (b), \(\mu\) can be any real number and \(\sigma^2\) any positive number, so \(\Theta=\{(\mu,\sigma^2):\mu\in\mathbb R,\ \sigma^2\gt0\}\).

You can now: say what the unknown parameter is in a stated model (for example \(p\) in Binomial\((n,p)\) with \(n\) known); read the notation \(f(u;\theta)\); write down the parameter space \(\Theta\).

2Random samples and statistics ~5 min

What is this?

Picture rolling the same die again and again, a die whose weighting you do not know. Each roll is a fresh go of the same experiment, not affected by the others: together the rolls form a random sample. Before you roll, the results are random (capital letters); once rolled, they are numbers on paper (lower case).

Where the information comes from

Our information about the unknown distribution of \(X\), or the unknown parameters of the distribution of \(X\), comes from a sample on \(X\).

  • The sample observations have the same distribution as \(X\), and we denote them as the random variables \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\), where \(n\) denotes the sample size.
  • When the sample is actually drawn, we use lower case letters \(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n\) as the values or realisations of the sample.

Often we assume that the sample observations \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\) are also mutually independent, in which case we call the sample a random sample, which we now formally define.

Capital and lower case letters matter throughout this chapter. Before the survey, \(X_1\) is a random variable: "the height of the first person we will measure", which could be many things. After the survey, \(x_1=172\) is a number. The same rule gives \(\bar X\) (random) and \(\bar x\) (a number), \(S\) (random) and \(s\) (a number).

Definition 4.1: random sample

If the random variables \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\) are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), then these random variables constitute a random sample (or simply "sample") of size \(n\) from the common distribution.

If that common distribution is \(X\), then we say that \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\) is a sample on \(X\).

In words: each observation is a fresh, independent copy of \(X\). "Identically distributed" means all \(X_i\) have the distribution of \(X\), so the same mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\).

Often, functions of the sample are used to summarise the information in a sample. These are called statistics, defined as follows.

What is this? (statistic)

A statistic is any number you can work out from the rolls alone: their average, the largest roll, the first minus the second. If the recipe needs the hidden weighting of the die, it is not a statistic, because you could never compute it.

Definition 4.2: statistic

Let \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\) denote a sample on a random variable \(X\). Let \(T=g(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n)\) be a function of the sample. Then \(T\) is called a statistic.

In words: a statistic is any formula you can compute from the data alone. It may not contain unknown parameters. Because the \(X_i\) are random, a statistic is itself a random variable.

Realisation of a statistic
  • Once the sample is drawn, then \(t\) is called the realisation of \(T\), where \(t=g(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)\) and \(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n\) is the realisation of the sample.
  • The \(g\) in the definition is an ordinary function in \(n\) variables, so if there is no possibility of confusion, we will simply write \(T=T(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n)\) instead of \(T=g(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n)\).
Example: which are statistics?

Let \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) be a sample on \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) with \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) unknown.

  • \(\bar X=\frac1n(X_1+\cdots+X_n)\), \(\max(X_1,\dots,X_n)\) and \(X_1-X_2\) are statistics: you can compute them from the data.
  • \(\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\) is not a statistic: it contains the unknown \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\). (It is still a perfectly good random variable; section 6 uses it.)

With data \(x_1,x_2,x_3=2,5,8\), the statistic \(\bar X\) has realisation \(\bar x=5\).

You can now: state the two conditions for a random sample (independent and identically distributed); decide whether a formula is a statistic by checking it has no unknown parameters; tell random \(X_i\), \(\bar X\) from observed \(x_i\), \(\bar x\).

3Point estimators and unbiasedness ~7 min

What is this?

Picture a jittery kitchen scale. Each reading is the true weight plus a random wobble, sometimes up, sometimes down, and the wobbles are independent of each other. One reading can be well off. The average of ten readings wobbles less, and the average of a hundred hardly wobbles at all. The sample mean is that average: a noisy estimate of the true mean that gets steadier as you collect more data.

Estimator and estimate

Using the above terminology, it makes sense to consider a statistic \(T\) which is an estimator of \(\theta\). More formally, \(T\) is called a point estimator of \(\theta\). While we call \(T\) an estimator of \(\theta\), we call its realisation \(t\) an estimate of \(\theta\). Remember this:

wordwhat it isexample
estimatora random variable\(\bar X=\frac1n(X_1+\cdots+X_n)\)
estimatea realisation (real number)\(\bar x=600\)

In words: the estimator is the recipe (a formula in the random \(X_i\)); the estimate is the number the recipe gives for the data you actually got.

"Point" means a single value, as opposed to an interval (section 5). Point estimators can be judged by several properties (and built in several ways, for example maximum likelihood estimators and Bayes estimators), but this course only discusses the following one, which suits an introductory statistics course.

What is this? (unbiasedness)

Unbiased means the scale is not set wrong: its wobbles do not lean one way. If you repeated the whole weighing many times, the readings would average out to the true weight. A scale that always reads 5 g heavy is biased, however steady it is. Unbiased does not mean any one reading is right.

Definition 4.3: unbiasedness

Let \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\) denote a sample on a random variable \(X\) with density \(f(u;\theta)\) or distribution \(p(x;\theta)\), \(\theta\in\Theta\). Let \(T=T(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n)\) be a statistic. We say that \(T\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\theta\) if

\[ E(T)=\theta. \]

In words: on average over all possible samples, the estimator hits the true value. It does not systematically over- or under-estimate. (Any single estimate can still be off.)

Example

Let \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) with both parameters \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) unknown. If \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\) is a random sample on \(X\), then the statistics

  • \(\bar X_n:=\frac1n(X_1+\cdots+X_n)\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\mu\);
  • \(S^2:=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X_n)^2\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\).

The proof that \(E(\bar X_n)=\mu\) is Exercise 2.45 (chapter P2). The proof for \(S^2\) is in section 4.

The subscript \(n\) in \(\bar X_n\) only records the sample size; many books (and the exam) write \(\bar X\).

Exercise 2.45: the mean and variance of \(\bar X_n\)

This exercise from P2 is used all through the chapter, so here it is in full. Let \(X_1,X_2,\dots\) be independent with the same distribution, mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\).

Mean and variance of the sample mean (Exercise 2.45)

By linearity of expectation,

\[ \begin{aligned} E(\bar X_n)&=\tfrac1n\big(E(X_1)+\cdots+E(X_n)\big)\\ &=\tfrac1n(n\mu)=\mu. \end{aligned} \]

Because the \(X_i\) are independent, variances add, and \(\operatorname{Var}(cY)=c^2\operatorname{Var}(Y)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)&=\tfrac1{n^2}\big(\operatorname{Var}(X_1)+\cdots\\ &\qquad+\operatorname{Var}(X_n)\big)\\ &=\tfrac1{n^2}(n\sigma^2)=\frac{\sigma^2}n. \end{aligned} \]

In words: the sample mean is centred on the true mean, and its spread shrinks as the sample grows. Its standard deviation is \(\sigma/\sqrt n\), called the standard error. As \(n\to\infty\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)\to0\): the sample mean piles up at \(\mu\).

Reading the formula
\(E(\bar X_n)=\mu\)
over many repeat weighings, the averaged reading is centred on the true weight: no lean.
\(\sigma^2\)
how much one single reading wobbles (its variance).
\(\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\)
the averaged reading wobbles much less: independent wobbles partly cancel when added.
\(\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\)
the standard error: the typical size of the average's wobble. Four times the data halves it.

This answers a past-paper question (May 2013 Q8(a)): "when \(n\) is large, \(\bar X\) is an accurate estimator of \(\mu\); explain why." Answer: \(E(\bar X)=\mu\) (unbiased) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n\to0\), so \(\bar X\) is very likely to be close to \(\mu\). (Precisely, this is the weak law of large numbers, Theorem 2.47 in P2: for every fixed \(\varepsilon\gt0\), \(P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\lt\varepsilon)\to1\) as \(n\to\infty\).)

Estimating a proportion

If each \(X_i\) is 1 for a "success" and 0 otherwise (a Bernoulli trial with success probability \(p\)), then \(E(X_i)=p\), so \(\bar X_n\) is an unbiased estimator of \(p\). Here \(\bar X_n\) is the proportion of successes in the sample, written \(\hat p\) (section 9).

Example (Dec 2015 Q7(a)(ii))

A coin with \(P(\text{head})=p\) is tossed until the 1000th head, which comes at the 5000th toss. Estimate \(p\) and justify.

Answer. In 5000 tosses there were 1000 heads, so \(\hat p=\frac{1000}{5000}=0.2\). Justification: \(\hat p\) is the sample proportion \(\bar X\) of the 0/1 results, an unbiased estimator of \(p\), and with so many tosses its variance \(p(1-p)/n\) is tiny. (Another view: the waiting time for each head is geometric with mean \(1/p\); the 1000 waiting times add to 5000, average 5, so \(1/p\approx5\).)

The exam taster (section 16) has the most recent question of this kind.

You can now: tell an estimator (random) from an estimate (a number); show a statistic is unbiased by computing \(E(T)\); derive \(E(\bar X)=\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n\) and use them to explain why \(\bar X\) is accurate for large \(n\); estimate a proportion by \(\hat p\).

4The sample variance and why n − 1 ~5 min

What is this?

Back to the jittery scale. To measure how much it wobbles, you would like the distances of the readings from the true weight. You do not know the true weight, so you measure from the average of your readings instead. The readings always sit a little closer to their own average than to the true weight, because the average is pulled towards them. Dividing by one less than the number of readings makes up for this exactly, on average.

We now prove that \(S^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X_n)^2\) is unbiased for \(\sigma^2\). The proof has two steps: rewrite the sum without the subtraction inside the square, then take expectations.

Step 1: expand the sum

Expand each square, \((X_i-\bar X_n)^2=X_i^2-2\bar X_nX_i+\bar X_n^2\), and add over \(i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} S^2&=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X_n)^2\\ &=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n\big(X_i^2-2\bar X_nX_i+\bar X_n^2\big)\\ &=\frac1{n-1}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^nX_i^2-2\bar X_n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\\ &\qquad\qquad+n\bar X_n^2\Big). \end{aligned} \]

Now \(\sum X_i=n\bar X_n\) (the definition of the mean), so the middle term is \(-2\bar X_n\cdot n\bar X_n=-2n\bar X_n^2\), and

\[ S^2=\frac1{n-1}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^nX_i^2-n\bar X_n^2\Big). \]

Hence, taking expectations (linearity),

\[ \begin{aligned} E(S^2)&=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^nE(X_i^2)\\ &\quad-\frac n{n-1}E(\bar X_n^2). \end{aligned} \]
Step 2: the two expectations

Use \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)=E(Y^2)-E(Y)^2\), that is \(E(Y^2)=\operatorname{Var}(Y)+E(Y)^2\), twice: We know the means and variances of \(X_i\) and \(\bar X_n\), not their expected squares; this identity turns one into the other.

\[ E(X_i^2)=\operatorname{Var}(X_i)+E(X_i)^2=\sigma^2+\mu^2, \]

and, by Exercise 2.45 (\(E(\bar X_n)=\mu\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/n\)),

\[ E(\bar X_n^2)=\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)+E(\bar X_n)^2=\frac{\sigma^2}n+\mu^2. \]

So The \(\mu^2\) terms cancel. Of the \(n\sigma^2\), one \(\sigma^2\) is lost because \(\bar X_n\) itself wobbles (variance \(\sigma^2/n\), times \(n\)): that lost piece is exactly why we divide by \(n-1\).

\[ \begin{aligned} E(S^2)&=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(\sigma^2+\mu^2)\\ &\quad-\frac n{n-1}\Big(\frac{\sigma^2}n+\mu^2\Big)\\ &=\frac{n\sigma^2+n\mu^2-\sigma^2-n\mu^2}{n-1}\\ &=\frac{(n-1)\sigma^2}{n-1}=\sigma^2. \end{aligned} \]
Names

\(\bar X_n\) is called the sample mean, \(S^2\) the sample variance, and \(S=\sqrt{S^2}\) the sample standard deviation. These are statistics. Their realisations are written \(\bar x\), \(s^2\), \(s\).

In words: to estimate the spread, average the squared distances from the sample mean, but divide by \(n-1\), not \(n\). Dividing by \(n\) would on average give \(\frac{n-1}n\sigma^2\), slightly too small.

Reading the formula
\(X_i-\bar X_n\)
how far each reading is from the average of the readings (not from the unknown true value).
\((\cdots)^2\), summed
total squared scatter; squaring keeps ups and downs from cancelling.
\(\dfrac1{n-1}\)
divide by one less than the count, to make up for measuring from the sample's own centre.
\(S=\sqrt{S^2}\)
back in the original units: the typical size of one reading's wobble.

Why too small? The data are closer to their own mean \(\bar x\) than to the true mean \(\mu\) (the sample mean is, by construction, the number that makes \(\sum(x_i-c)^2\) smallest). So the squared distances from \(\bar x\) understate the spread; dividing by the smaller number \(n-1\) corrects this exactly.

Remark

The above calculations do not require \(X\) to be normal. In fact, \(X\) can follow any distribution with \(E(X)=\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sigma^2\), both finite.

In the case where \(\mu\) is known, \(\theta=\sigma\) is the only parameter. Then

\[ W^2:=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\mu)^2 \]

is an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\). (Exercise: show that \(E(W^2)=\sigma^2\).)

Exercise, solved

Each term has \(E\big((X_i-\mu)^2\big)=\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2\), by the definition of variance (\(\mu\) is the true mean of \(X_i\)). So

\[ \begin{aligned} E(W^2)&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nE\big((X_i-\mu)^2\big)\\ &=\frac1n\cdot n\sigma^2=\sigma^2. \end{aligned} \]

Here we divide by \(n\), not \(n-1\), because we measure distances from the true mean \(\mu\), not from the estimated \(\bar X_n\). \(W^2\) is a statistic only when \(\mu\) is known.

Example: computing \(\bar x\) and \(s^2\) by hand

Data \(2,5,8\): \(\bar x=5\); squared deviations \(9,0,9\), sum \(18\); \(s^2=\frac{18}{3-1}=9\), \(s=3\). (Dividing by \(n=3\) would give 6.)

Shortcut from step 1:

\[ \begin{aligned} s^2&=\frac1{n-1}\Big(\sum x_i^2-n\bar x^2\Big)\\ &=\tfrac12(4+25+64-75)=9. \end{aligned} \]

Without a calculator, it is usually easier to subtract a convenient number first: the deviations and \(s^2\) do not change if every \(x_i\) is shifted by the same amount.

Past paper May 2013 Q8(b) asks exactly this: "propose an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\) and explain why it is unbiased". Answer with \(S^2\) and the two steps above.

You can now: prove \(E(S^2)=\sigma^2\) in two steps; explain in words why we divide by \(n-1\), not \(n\); show \(W^2\) is unbiased when \(\mu\) is known; compute \(\bar x\), \(s^2\) and \(s\) by hand.

5What a confidence interval is ~8 min

What is this?

Picture a fish resting at one fixed spot in muddy water. You cannot see it, so you cast a net. Each sample gives one cast, centred wherever that sample happens to point. The fish never moves; the net lands somewhere different each time. A 95% confidence interval comes from a way of casting that catches the fish in 95% of casts in the long run. After one particular cast, the fish is either in the net or not: the 95% describes the casting method, not this one net.

The problem with a single estimate

Recall that the random variable of interest \(X\) has density \(f(u;\theta)\) or distribution \(p(x;\theta)\), \(\theta\in\Theta\), where the parameter \(\theta\) is unknown. We estimate \(\theta\) by a statistic

\[ \hat\theta=g(X_1,\dots,X_n), \]

where \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) is a sample from the distribution of \(X\). (Caution: \(\hat\theta\) is a random variable.)

When the sample is drawn, it is unlikely that the value (realisation) of \(\hat\theta\) is the true value of the parameter. In fact, if \(\hat\theta\) has a density, then \(P_\theta(\hat\theta=\theta)=0\), where the notation \(P_\theta\) denotes that the probability is computed when \(\theta\) is the true parameter.

What is needed is an estimate of the error of the estimation; that is, by how much did \(\hat\theta\) miss \(\theta\)? In this section, we embody this estimate of error in terms of a confidence interval, which we now formally define.

The hat in \(\hat\theta\) ("theta hat") means "an estimator of \(\theta\)". Why is \(P(\hat\theta=\theta)=0\)? For a continuous random variable, the probability of hitting any single exact value is an area of zero width under the density: zero. So a point estimate such as \(\bar x=600\) is almost surely a little wrong, and alone it does not say how wrong. An interval does.

Definition 4.4: confidence interval, CI

Let \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\) denote a sample on a random variable \(X\), where \(X\) has a density \(f(u;\theta)\) or a distribution \(p(x;\theta)\), \(\theta\in\Theta\). Let \(0\lt\alpha\lt1\) be specified. Let

\[ L=L(X_1,\dots,X_n) \]

and

\[ U=U(X_1,\dots,X_n) \]

be two statistics. We say that the interval \((L,U)\) is a \((1-\alpha)100\%\) confidence interval for \(\theta\) if

\[ P_\theta\big(\theta\in(L,U)\big)=(1-\alpha)100\%. \]

That is, the probability that the interval includes \(\theta\) is \((1-\alpha)100\%\), which is called the confidence level of the interval.

In words: the end points \(L\) and \(U\) are random (they depend on the sample); \(\theta\) is a fixed unknown number. Before sampling, the chance that the random interval will cover \(\theta\) is \(1-\alpha\). For a 95% interval, \(\alpha=0.05\).

Reading the formula
\(\theta\)
the fish: a fixed, unknown number that never moves.
\(L,\ U\)
the two edges of the net; they are computed from the sample, so they change from sample to sample.
\(\theta\in(L,U)\)
"this cast catches the fish".
\(P_\theta(\cdots)=1-\alpha\)
before casting, the chance that the cast will catch the fish; over many casts, the fraction that do.
\(\alpha\)
the fraction of casts that miss: 0.05 for a 95% interval.
Understanding the definition
  • Suppose a sample is drawn; we have the realisation \((X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n)=(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)\).
  • Then we obtain the realisations of \(L\) and \(U\), which are, respectively, \(\ell=L(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) and \(u=U(x_1,\dots,x_n)\).
  • So the CI, \((\ell,u)\), is an interval of real numbers. There are two possibilities: either \((\ell,u)\) traps \(\theta\) or it does not.
  • Consider this scenario: set \(\alpha=0.05\), and take 100 samples. This results in 100 realisations of \((L,U)\): \((\ell_1,u_1),(\ell_2,u_2),\dots,(\ell_{100},u_{100})\). Then, approximately 95 (\(=95\%\) of 100) of them will trap \(\theta\).

What "95% confidence" does and does not mean

Right: if we repeated the sampling many times, about 95% of the intervals computed this way would contain the true \(\mu\). The 95% is a property of the method (the random interval \((L,U)\)).

Wrong: "there is a 95% probability that \(\mu\) lies in \((593.8,606.2)\)". Once computed, \((593.8,606.2)\) is a fixed interval and \(\mu\) is a fixed number: it is either inside or not. (Section 8 returns to this point.)

Also wrong: "95% of the population lie in the interval" (the interval is about the mean, not individuals), and "the sample mean is in the interval with 95% certainty" (\(\bar x\) is always exactly at the centre).

6The CLT and the critical value z(α/2) ~8 min

What is this?

Roll a fair die once and every face is equally likely: a flat shape, nothing like a bell. Now average 30 rolls, and repeat that many times: the averages pile up in a bell shape around 3.5. That is the Central Limit Theorem: the average of many independent draws from the same distribution (with finite variance) is roughly normal, whatever the shape of one draw. So one table, the normal table, sets the width of every net in this chapter.

To build a confidence interval we need the distribution of the estimator. For a sample mean, the Central Limit Theorem gives it, approximately, whatever the distribution of \(X\).

Theorem 2.46′: Central Limit Theorem

Let \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\) be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with finite mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\gt0\). When \(n\) is big, then for \(k\gt0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} &P\Big(-k\lt\frac{\bar X_n-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\lt k\Big)\\ &\quad\approx\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-k}^{k}e^{-x^2/2}\,dx. \end{aligned} \]

(This is Theorem 2.46 of P2 with \(a=-k\), \(b=k\).)

In words: standardise the sample mean (subtract its mean \(\mu\), divide by its standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt n\), both from Exercise 2.45). For large \(n\) the result behaves like a standard normal \(Z\), even if \(X\) itself is not normal.

Reading the formula
\(\bar X_n-\mu\)
how far the average lands from the true mean: the miss.
\(\sigma/\sqrt n\)
the standard error: the typical size of that miss.
\(\dfrac{\bar X_n-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\)
the miss counted in standard errors; for large \(n\) it behaves like \(Z\).
\(\dfrac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\displaystyle\int_{-k}^{k}e^{-x^2/2}\,dx\)
the area under the standard bell between \(-k\) and \(k\): the chance the miss is under \(k\) standard errors.

If \(X\) is itself normal, \(\bar X_n\) is exactly \(N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\) for every \(n\) (a sum of independent normals is normal, P3), so then "\(\approx\)" is "\(=\)". That is why exam questions that say "\(X_1,\dots,X_n\) is a random sample from \(N(\mu,25)\)" need no condition on \(n\).

Replacing σ by S

In fact, the above approximation stays the same if we replace \(\sigma\) (a number) by \(S\) (a random variable), that is,

\[ \begin{aligned} &P\Big(-k\lt\frac{\bar X_n-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\lt k\Big)\\ &\quad\approx\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-k}^{k}e^{-x^2/2}\,dx, \end{aligned} \]

but we will not go into the details here. Having said that, it is worth noting that \(n\gt30\) is needed to make the approximation reasonably useful.

In words: when \(\sigma\) is unknown, use the sample standard deviation \(s\) in its place. For large samples \(S\) is close to \(\sigma\), so the normal approximation still works. Section 8 uses this.

What is this? (the critical value)

The critical value answers one question: how many standard errors must the net reach on each side so that it covers the middle part of the bell you asked for (90%, 95%, ...)? Whatever is left over is split equally between the two tails, because the bell is symmetric.

The right-hand side is a normal probability

Before running into several examples of CI, we examine the term on the right-hand side, which is actually

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-k}^{k}e^{-x^2/2}\,dx&=P(-k\lt Z\lt k)\\ &=P(|Z|\lt k), \end{aligned} \]

where \(Z\sim N(0,1)\), the standard normal distribution. Our aim is to use the CLT approximations to create CIs; we shall need to find \(k\) so that

\[ P(|Z|\lt k)=(1-\alpha)100\%. \]

This \(k\) is sometimes written as \(z_{\alpha/2}\). But why "\(\alpha/2\)"?

Why α/2

Say \(\alpha=0.05\); then \((1-\alpha)100\%=95\%\), so \(P(|Z|\lt z_{0.025})=0.95\). The remaining probability \(0.05\) is split equally between the two tails (the normal curve is symmetric), \(0.025\) in each. So this implies that

\[ P(Z\lt z_{0.025})=0.95+0.025=0.975. \]

From the table, \(\Phi(1.96)=0.9750\), so in this case \(z_{0.025}=1.96\).

When \(\alpha=0.01\), \((1-\alpha)100\%=99\%\), so

\[ \begin{gathered} P(|Z|\lt z_{0.005})=0.99\\ \Rightarrow\ P(Z\lt z_{0.005})=0.995, \end{gathered} \]

which appears to be in the middle of \(P(Z\lt2.57)=0.9949\) and \(P(Z\lt2.58)=0.9951\). So \(z_{0.005}\approx2.575\).

Exercise: show that \(z_{0.05}\approx1.645\). For 90%, \(\alpha=0.10\) and we need \(P(Z\lt z_{0.05})=0.95\). The table gives \(\Phi(1.64)=0.9495\) and \(\Phi(1.65)=0.9505\); 0.95 is exactly in the middle, so \(z_{0.05}\approx1.645\).

In words: \(z_{\alpha/2}\) is the point with area \(\alpha/2\) to its right. The name records the tail area, not the central area.

7CI for μ when σ is known ~8 min

What is this?

Now cast the net. Centre it on your sample mean and let it reach a fixed number of standard errors out on each side. The derivation rests on a plain fact about distance: if the sample mean is within some distance of the true mean, then the true mean is within that same distance of the sample mean. So "the average lands near the fish" turns into "the net around the average catches the fish", with the same probability.

Setting

Suppose \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\) is a random sample on a random variable \(X\) with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\); the distribution of \(X\) is not (necessarily) normal.

Case 1. \(\mu\) unknown, \(\sigma^2\) known. By the CLT (section 6), with \(k=z_{\alpha/2}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} &P_\mu\Big(-z_{\alpha/2}\lt\frac{\bar X_n-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\lt z_{\alpha/2}\Big)\\ &\quad\approx(1-\alpha)100\%. \end{aligned} \]

Now rearrange: turn the inequality inside the probability around, so that \(\mu\) stands alone in the middle. Here are the steps. Multiply all three parts by \(\sigma/\sqrt n\gt0\): A positive factor keeps the inequalities pointing the same way; the middle is now the miss \(\bar X_n-\mu\) in ordinary units.

\[ -z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\lt\bar X_n-\mu\lt z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

Subtract \(\bar X_n\) from all three parts:

\[ -\bar X_n-z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\lt-\mu\lt-\bar X_n+z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

Multiply by \(-1\), which reverses both inequalities, and write the result from left to right: Now \(\mu\) stands alone: "\(\mu\) is within \(z_{\alpha/2}\sigma/\sqrt n\) of \(\bar X_n\)", the same distance seen from the other end.

\[ \bar X_n-z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\lt\mu\lt\bar X_n+z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

The events are the same (each inequality was rearranged into an equivalent one), so they have the same probability: It is one event written two ways, so the chance the average lands near \(\mu\) equals the chance the net around the average catches \(\mu\).

\[ \begin{aligned} &P_\mu\Big(\bar X_n-z_{\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\lt\mu\\ &\qquad\lt\bar X_n+z_{\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\Big)\approx(1-\alpha)100\%. \end{aligned} \]
Remark

Here, \(\mu\) is an unknown constant, not a random variable, while

\[ \begin{aligned} L&=\bar X_n-z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n},\\ U&=\bar X_n+z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \end{aligned} \]

are random variables. So this matches Definition 4.4 exactly: \((L,U)\) is a random interval that traps \(\mu\) with probability about \(1-\alpha\).

CI for μ, σ known

Let \(\bar x\) denote the realised value of the statistic \(\bar X_n\) after the sample is drawn. An approximate \((1-\alpha)100\%\) CI for \(\mu\) is given by

\[ \Big(\bar x-z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n},\ \bar x+z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\Big). \]

Short form: \(\bar x\pm z_{\alpha/2}\,\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\).

In words: start at the sample mean and go out \(z_{\alpha/2}\) standard errors on each side. Higher confidence (bigger \(z_{\alpha/2}\)) gives a wider interval; a bigger sample (bigger \(\sqrt n\)) gives a narrower one.

Reading the formula
\(\bar x\)
the centre of the net: where this sample's average landed.
\(\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\)
the standard error: how far an average typically lands from \(\mu\).
\(z_{\alpha/2}\)
how many standard errors the net reaches each way; bigger for more confidence.
\(\pm\)
the net reaches equally far on both sides, because the bell is symmetric.

In particular, the 95% CI for \(\mu\) is \(\big(\bar x-1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n},\ \bar x+1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\big)\). This means that if you have 100 realisations \(\bar x_{(1)},\dots,\bar x_{(100)}\) of \(\bar X_n\), then among the CIs

\[ \begin{aligned} &\Big(\bar x_{(1)}-1.96\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n},\ \bar x_{(1)}+1.96\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\Big)\\ &\qquad\vdots\\ &\Big(\bar x_{(100)}-1.96\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n},\ \bar x_{(100)}+1.96\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\Big), \end{aligned} \]

about 95 of them trap \(\mu\). This is exactly what the figure in section 5 draws.

Example: demand for a new product

The average demand of a certain new product is \(\mu\) (unknown) across a network of department stores. A survey of 40 random samples yields a mean of 600 per day. Based on past experience with new products, the variance \(\sigma^2\) is known to be 400. Construct the 90% and 95% CI for the mean \(\mu\).

Answer. The realisation of \(\bar X_n\) is 600 (although the exact realisations of \(X_1,\dots,X_{40}\) are not known), \(n=40\), and \(\sigma^2=400\), so \(\sigma=\sqrt{400}=20\). The standard error is \(\frac{20}{\sqrt{40}}\approx3.162\).

The 90% CI uses \(z_{\alpha/2}=1.645\); the half-width is \(1.645\times3.162\approx5.20\), so the interval is

\[ \begin{aligned} &\Big(600-1.645\cdot\tfrac{20}{\sqrt{40}},\ 600+1.645\cdot\tfrac{20}{\sqrt{40}}\Big)\\ &=(594.8,\ 605.2). \end{aligned} \]

Similarly, the 95% CI uses 1.96; the half-width is \(1.96\times3.162\approx6.20\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\Big(600-1.96\cdot\tfrac{20}{\sqrt{40}},\ 600+1.96\cdot\tfrac{20}{\sqrt{40}}\Big)\\ &=(593.8,\ 606.2). \end{aligned} \]

The 95% interval is wider: to be more confident of trapping \(\mu\), we must allow a larger range.

8CI for μ when σ is unknown ~10 min

What is this?

Usually you do not know how much the scale wobbles either. So you estimate the wobble from the same readings, using the sample standard deviation, and build the net with it in place of the true one. With a large sample the estimate is close to the truth and the net is nearly as good. With a small sample the estimate itself wobbles, so the net catches the fish a little less often than the stated level.

In practice \(\sigma\) is rarely known. Section 6 noted that the CLT approximation still holds with \(\sigma\) replaced by the sample standard deviation \(S\). That gives the second case.

Case 2. μ unknown, σ² unknown

So to speak, we replace \(\sigma\) by \(S\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &P_\mu\Big(-z_{\alpha/2}\lt\frac{\bar X_n-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\lt z_{\alpha/2}\Big)\\ &\quad\approx(1-\alpha)100\%. \end{aligned} \]

Similar to the previous case, if \(\bar x\) and \(s\) denote the realised values of the statistics \(\bar X_n\) and \(S\), respectively, after the sample is drawn, an approximate \((1-\alpha)100\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu\) is given by

\[ \Big(\bar x-z_{\alpha/2}\frac{s}{\sqrt n},\ \bar x+z_{\alpha/2}\frac{s}{\sqrt n}\Big). \]

This is called a large sample confidence interval for \(\mu\).

In words: the same recipe as Case 1, with the sample standard deviation \(s\) (computed with \(n-1\), section 4) in place of \(\sigma\). The rearrangement is identical, with \(S\) for \(\sigma\).

Reading the formula
\(s\)
the wobble size estimated from the data (with \(n-1\)), standing in for the unknown \(\sigma\).
\(\dfrac{s}{\sqrt n}\)
the estimated standard error of the average.
"approximate", "large sample"
\(S\) is itself random, so the net is only close to the stated level when \(n\) is large (section 6: \(n\gt30\)).
Trap: s versus s²

Questions often give the sample variance (May 2016 Q7: "sample variance 4 years²"). Then \(s=\sqrt4=2\), not 4. If they give data, compute \(s^2\) with \(n-1\) and take the square root.

Example: HKU basketball

59, 62, 59, 74, 70, 61, 62, 66, 62, 75

Answer. The realisations of the sample mean and the sample variance are \(\bar x=65\) and \(s^2=35.778\). The working: the ten scores add to 650, so \(\bar x=\frac{650}{10}=65\). Deviations from 65:

\[ \begin{gathered} -6,\ -3,\ -6,\ 9,\ 5,\\ -4,\ -3,\ 1,\ -3,\ 10 \end{gathered} \]

For a 95% CI, \(z_{\alpha/2}=1.96\). The standard error is \(\frac{s}{\sqrt n}=\sqrt{\frac{s^2}{n}}=\sqrt{3.5778}\approx1.8915\). Hence the 95% CI for \(\mu\) is Putting \(s^2/n\) under one root saves taking two square roots; it gives the same number as \(s/\sqrt n\).

\[ \begin{aligned} &\big(65-1.96\sqrt{3.5778},\ 65+1.96\sqrt{3.5778}\big)\\ &=(61.293,\ 68.707). \end{aligned} \]

No: 95% confidence means that, in 95% of experiments, the random interval (realisations of \((L,U)\)) will contain the true \(\mu\). It is not the probability that \(\mu\) is in the given interval (one of the realisations); that depends on the prior distribution of \(\mu\), which we do not know.

The number \(3.5778\) under the root is \(s^2/n=35.778/10\).

The "prior distribution" mentioned above refers to Bayesian statistics, where \(\mu\) itself is treated as random; in this course \(\mu\) is a fixed unknown constant, so "the probability that \(\mu\) is in \((61.293, 68.707)\)" is either 0 or 1, we just do not know which.

9CI for a proportion p ~7 min

What is this?

A poll is the same picture with 0s and 1s. Ask each person "do you smoke?" and write 1 for yes, 0 for no. The proportion of yeses is just the average of these 0s and 1s, so it is a noisy estimate that steadies as more people are asked, and the same kind of net works. The only new part is the wobble size: for 0/1 answers it is set by the proportion itself.

Setting

Let \(X\) be a Bernoulli random variable with probability of success \(p\), where \(X\) is 1 or 0 if the outcome is success or failure, respectively. Suppose \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) is a random sample from the distribution of \(X\). Let \(\hat p=\bar X_n\) be the sample proportion of successes. Note that

\[ \hat p=\frac1n(X_1+\cdots+X_n) \]

(Caution: \(\hat p\) is a random variable)

is the sample average and that \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat p)=p(1-p)/n\). It follows immediately from the CLT that

\[ \begin{aligned} &P_p\Big(-z_{\alpha/2}\lt\frac{\hat p-p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}\lt z_{\alpha/2}\Big)\\ &\quad\approx(1-\alpha)100\%. \end{aligned} \]

Where the pieces come from: a Bernoulli variable has \(E(X)=p\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=p(1-p)\) (P3). The sum \(X_1+\cdots+X_n\) is the number of successes, which is Binomial\((n,p)\). By Exercise 2.45, \(E(\hat p)=p\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat p)=\frac{p(1-p)}n\). So this is the CLT of section 6 with \(\mu=p\) and \(\sigma=\sqrt{p(1-p)}\).

CI for p

As you can see, the inequality is hard to simplify into something like \(L\lt p\lt U\) (the unknown \(p\) appears both in the numerator and under the square root). Therefore, we replace \(p(1-p)\) with its estimate \(\hat p(1-\hat p)\). An approximate \((1-\alpha)100\%\) confidence interval for \(p\) is then given by

\[ \Big(\hat p-z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\tfrac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}n},\ \hat p+z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\tfrac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}n}\Big). \]

Short form: \(\hat p\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}n}\), where now \(\hat p\) means the observed proportion (a number).

In words: the same shape as the interval for a mean. The centre is the observed proportion; the standard error is \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\), because for 0/1 data the standard deviation is \(\sqrt{p(1-p)}\) and we plug in our estimate of \(p\).

Reading the formula
\(\hat p\)
the centre of the net: the observed proportion of yeses.
\(\hat p(1-\hat p)\)
the wobble (variance) of a single 0/1 answer, with our estimate in place of the unknown \(p\).
\(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\)
the standard error of the proportion: averaging \(n\) answers shrinks the wobble.
\(z_{\alpha/2}\)
how many standard errors the net reaches each way, as for a mean.
Example: smokers

To study the proportion of smokers in a population, a survey has been conducted and 1000 samples have been obtained. Among them 170 are smokers. Construct the 90% CI for the smoker proportion.

Answer. We have \(n=1000\), a realisation of \(\hat p=170/1000=0.17\), and \(z_{\alpha/2}\approx1.645\). Thus the 90% CI is

\[ \begin{aligned} &\Big(0.17-1.645\sqrt{\tfrac{0.17(0.83)}{1000}},\\ &\qquad 0.17+1.645\sqrt{\tfrac{0.17(0.83)}{1000}}\Big) \end{aligned} \]

or about \((0.1505,\ 0.1895)\). (Working: \(0.17\times0.83=0.1411\); \(\sqrt{0.0001411}\approx0.01188\); \(1.645\times0.01188\approx0.0195\).)

Exercise 4.9, solved

In a random sample of 500 families owning personal computers, it was found that 340 families do not own a Mac. Find a 95% C.I. for the actual proportion \(p\) of families that do not own a Mac.

Solution. \(\hat p=\frac{340}{500}=0.68\), \(1-\hat p=0.32\), \(z_{0.025}=1.96\). Standard error

\[ \sqrt{\frac{0.68\times0.32}{500}}=\sqrt{0.0004352}\approx0.02086. \]

Half-width \(1.96\times0.02086\approx0.0409\). The 95% CI is about \((0.639,\ 0.721)\).

Method: CI for a proportion
  1. Do: \(\hat p=\dfrac{\text{number of successes}}{n}\). "Success" is whatever the question asks the proportion of (smokers, infected, defective, heads). The proportion is the average of the 0/1 answers, so it is the centre of the net.
  2. Do: \(z_{\alpha/2}\) from the table (section 6). Exactly as for a mean: it sets how far the net reaches.
  3. Do: Write \(\hat p\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\). Use \(\hat p\), never \(p\), under the root. \(p\) is the unknown we are trying to catch; the net can only be built from numbers in the data.
  4. Do: Round numbers that are friendly without a calculator: \(\sqrt{0.16/10000}=0.004\), \(\sqrt{0.09/10000}=0.003\). Without a calculator, spotting a clean square root keeps the arithmetic short and safe.

Going further

The inequality that seemed hard to simplify in section 9 can in fact be solved: squaring \(|\hat p-p|\lt z\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\) gives a quadratic inequality in \(p\), whose solution is called the Wilson interval. For the large \(n\) of proportion questions in past papers (\(n\ge1000\)) its end points differ from the plug-in interval's by about 0.001 or less, and the course uses the simpler plug-in interval, so that is what you should write.

A common rule of thumb for the normal approximation to be good: at least about 10 successes and 10 failures (\(n\hat p\ge10\) and \(n(1-\hat p)\ge10\)). Every past-paper question satisfies this easily.

You can now: explain why \(\hat p\) is a sample mean of 0/1 data with variance \(p(1-p)/n\); write and evaluate \(\hat p\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\); say why \(\hat p\) replaces \(p\) under the root.

10Choosing the sample size ~6 min

What is this?

Before you cast, you can choose how big the net is by choosing how much data to collect. The reach of the net shrinks like one over the square root of the sample size, so precision is expensive: a net half as wide needs four times as many observations. These questions give the width you want and ask for the smallest sample that achieves it.

The next two exercises turn the CI around: instead of "here is the sample, find the interval", they ask "how big must the sample be for the interval to be this short?". Past papers ask this often (Dec 2023 Q9(c), May 2019 Q10, Dec 2017 Q8(b)).

Width and margin of error

The interval \(\bar x\pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\) has half-width (also called the margin of error) and width (length)

\[ E=z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}, \] \[ \text{width}=2E=2z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

To make the margin at most a target \(E_0\), solve \(z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le E_0\) for \(n\):

\[ \sqrt n\ge\frac{z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma}{E_0}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\ge\Big(\frac{z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma}{E_0}\Big)^2. \]

Then round up to the next whole number.

Reading the formula
\(E_0\)
the largest miss you are willing to allow: the half-width of the net.
\(z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma\)
the half-width the net would have with a single observation.
\(\Big(\dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}\sigma}{E_0}\Big)^2\)
how many times smaller the net must get, squared, because the width falls only like \(1/\sqrt n\).
round up
any smaller \(n\) gives a net slightly wider than allowed.

In words: the width shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\). To halve the width you need four times as many observations. Rounding down would give an interval slightly too wide.

Trap: "within ±500" versus "length 500"

"Estimate within \(\pm E_0\)" bounds the half-width: \(z\sigma/\sqrt n\le E_0\). "Interval of length at most \(\ell\)" bounds the full width: \(2z\sigma/\sqrt n\le\ell\). Read which one the question means.

Margin of error against sample size

The curve is \(E=z_{\alpha/2}\sigma/\sqrt n\); the dashed line is the target \(E_0\). The smallest whole \(n\) where the curve is at or below the line is the sample size needed. Halve the target: the required \(n\) roughly quadruples.

Exercise 4.10, solved

Let \(\bar x\) be the observed mean of a random sample of size \(n\) from a distribution having mean \(\mu\) and known variance \(\sigma^2\). Find \(n\) so that \(\bar x-\sigma/4\) to \(\bar x+\sigma/4\) is an approximate 95% confidence interval for \(\mu\).

Solution. The 95% CI is \(\bar x\pm1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\). Match the half-widths:

\[ 1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}=\frac{\sigma}{4}\ \Longrightarrow\ \sqrt n=4(1.96)=7.84. \]

So \(n=7.84^2\approx61.5\). Since \(n\) is a whole number, take \(n=62\) (then \(1.96\sigma/\sqrt{62}\approx0.249\sigma\le\sigma/4\), so \(\bar x\pm\sigma/4\) has confidence at least 95%); \(n\approx61\) is also an acceptable "approximate" answer. Note that \(\sigma\) cancels: the answer does not depend on the unknown size of \(\sigma\).

Exercise 4.11, solved

Assume a binomial model for a certain random variable. If we desire a 90% confidence interval for \(p\) that is at most 0.02 in length, find \(n\). [Hint: note that \(\sqrt{\frac yn\big(1-\frac yn\big)}\le\sqrt{\frac12\big(1-\frac12\big)}\).]

Solution. Here \(y\) is the number of successes, so \(\frac yn=\hat p\). The 90% CI for \(p\) has length

\[ 2(1.645)\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}n}. \]

We do not know \(\hat p\) before sampling, so use the hint: We must fix \(n\) before seeing data, so we plan for the worst case, \(\hat p=\tfrac12\), where the wobble of a 0/1 answer is largest. \(\hat p(1-\hat p)\le\frac14\) for every \(\hat p\), because \(\hat p(1-\hat p)=\frac14-\big(\hat p-\frac12\big)^2\). So the length is at most

\[ 2(1.645)\sqrt{\frac{1}{4n}}=\frac{1.645}{\sqrt n}. \]

We need \(\frac{1.645}{\sqrt n}\le0.02\), that is \(\sqrt n\ge82.25\), so \(n\ge82.25^2=6765.0625\). The smallest whole number is \(n=6766\).

Do not round \(6765.06\) to the nearest whole number: \(n=6765\) gives length \(1.645/\sqrt{6765}\approx0.0200001\), a hair over 0.02. In an exam, show \(n\ge82.25^2\) and round up.

11More exercises: Poisson mean and tacks ~3 min

What is this?

Some models tie the wobble to the centre. For Poisson counts the variance equals the mean, so once the sample mean estimates the mean, it also estimates the variance: one number from the data sets both the centre and the reach of the net. The tacks are one more 0/1 poll, point up or not.

Two more exercises close the chapter. The first shows a useful idea: when the variance of a distribution is a function of its mean, estimate the variance from the mean.

Exercise 4.12, solved

It is known that a random variable \(X\) has a Poisson distribution with parameter \(\mu\). A sample of 200 observations from this distribution has a mean equal to 3.4. Construct an approximate 90% confidence interval for \(\mu\).

Solution. For a Poisson(\(\mu\)) variable, \(E(X)=\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mu\) (P3). So \(\sigma=\sqrt\mu\) is unknown, but since \(n=200\) is large we may replace it by an estimate. The natural estimate of \(\sigma^2=\mu\) is \(\bar x=3.4\) (we are not given \(s\)). By the CLT,

\[ \bar x\pm z_{0.05}\sqrt{\frac{\bar x}{n}}=3.4\pm1.645\sqrt{\frac{3.4}{200}}. \]
Reading the formula
\(\bar x\)
the centre of the net, and also (Poisson) the estimate of the variance of one count.
\(\dfrac{\bar x}{n}\)
the estimated variance of the average of \(n\) counts.
\(z_{0.05}=1.645\)
the reach for a 90% net.

\(\sqrt{0.017}\approx0.1304\) and \(1.645\times0.1304\approx0.2145\), so the 90% CI is about \((3.19,\ 3.61)\).

In words: same recipe as section 8, but the "sample standard deviation" comes from the model: for Poisson data the variance equals the mean.

Exercise 4.13, solved

When 100 tacks were thrown on a table, 60 of them landed point up. Obtain a 95% confidence interval for the probability that a tack of this type lands point up. Assume independence.

Solution. Each tack is a Bernoulli trial ("point up" = success), so use section 9. \(\hat p=\frac{60}{100}=0.6\), \(\hat p(1-\hat p)=0.24\), \(z_{0.025}=1.96\):

\[ 0.6\pm1.96\sqrt{\frac{0.24}{100}}=0.6\pm1.96(0.04899). \]

The half-width is about \(0.0960\), so the 95% CI is about \((0.504,\ 0.696)\).

12Summary

  • A parameter \(\theta\) is an unknown number in a known family of distributions, \(f(u;\theta)\) or \(p(x;\theta)\), \(\theta\in\Theta\) (the parameter space).
  • A random sample \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) is i.i.d. copies of \(X\); capital letters are random, lower case \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) are the observed values.
  • A statistic is a function of the sample only (no unknown parameters). An estimator is a statistic used to guess \(\theta\) (a random variable); an estimate is its value (a number).
  • \(T\) is unbiased for \(\theta\) if \(E(T)=\theta\). \(\bar X\) is unbiased for \(\mu\), \(S^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2\) for \(\sigma^2\) (any distribution), \(\hat p\) for \(p\); with \(\mu\) known, \(W^2=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\mu)^2\) for \(\sigma^2\).
  • \(E(\bar X)=\mu\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n\): the sample mean gets more accurate as \(n\) grows.
  • A \((1-\alpha)100\%\) CI \((L,U)\) has random end points with \(P(\theta\in(L,U))=1-\alpha\). Meaning: about \((1-\alpha)100\%\) of intervals from repeated samples trap \(\theta\). It is not the probability that \(\theta\) is in one computed interval.
  • \(z_{\alpha/2}\) satisfies \(P(|Z|\lt z_{\alpha/2})=1-\alpha\), that is \(\Phi(z_{\alpha/2})=1-\frac\alpha2\). 90%: 1.645; 95%: 1.96; 99%: 2.575.
The three intervals
unknownwhen\((1-\alpha)100\%\) CI
\(\mu\)\(\sigma\) known\(\bar x\pm z_{\alpha/2}\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\)
\(\mu\)\(\sigma\) unknown, use \(s\)\(\bar x\pm z_{\alpha/2}\dfrac{s}{\sqrt n}\)
\(p\)proportion\(\hat p\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}n}\)

All three are "estimate \(\pm\ z_{\alpha/2}\times\) standard error". Sample size for margin \(\le E_0\): \(n\ge(z_{\alpha/2}\sigma/E_0)^2\), rounded up; for a proportion with no estimate yet, use \(\hat p(1-\hat p)\le\frac14\).

Method: any CI question in the exam
  1. Do: Decide what is estimated: a mean (data are measurements) or a proportion (data are counts of yes/no). This picks the standard error: \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) (or \(s/\sqrt n\)) for a mean, \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\) for a proportion.
  2. Do: For a mean: is \(\sigma\) given (population SD, "SD unchanged", "\(N(\mu,25)\)", or from the model)? Use it. Otherwise use \(s\) (take \(\sqrt{\ }\) of a sample variance). A known \(\sigma\) is the true wobble size; without it, the data's own estimate has to stand in.
  3. Do: \(z_{\alpha/2}\): solve \(\Phi(z)=1-\frac\alpha2\) in the table; state the value. This sets the net's reach for the level asked.
  4. Do: Write estimate \(\pm\ z_{\alpha/2}\times\) standard error as an interval \((\ell,u)\). An unevaluated expression is accepted when the paper says so. Every interval in the chapter is the same net: the estimate in the middle, \(z_{\alpha/2}\) standard errors each way.
  5. Do: If asked to interpret: "about \((1-\alpha)100\%\) of intervals constructed this way from repeated samples would contain the true value". The confidence belongs to the casting method, not to the one net you computed: that net either caught the true value or did not.

13Common mistakes

✘Dividing by \(n\) instead of \(\sqrt n\): \(\bar x\pm1.96\frac{\sigma}{n}\); or by \(\sqrt n\) twice in the proportion interval. ✔The standard error of a mean is \(\sigma/\sqrt n\); of a proportion \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\) (the \(n\) is inside the root, once).
✘Using \(\Phi(z)=1-\alpha\) (for 90%, \(z=1.28\)), or \(\Phi(z)=\) the level itself. ✔Two tails share \(\alpha\): solve \(\Phi(z)=1-\frac\alpha2\). For 90%, \(\Phi(z)=0.95\), \(z=1.645\).
✘Using the variance where the standard deviation belongs: from "\(N(\mu,25)\)" or "sample variance 4", writing \(\frac{25}{\sqrt n}\) or \(\frac{4}{\sqrt n}\). ✔\(N(\mu,25)\) means \(\sigma^2=25\), \(\sigma=5\). Sample variance 4 means \(s=2\).
✘"There is a 95% probability that \(\mu\) is in \((593.8,606.2)\)", or "95% of the data lie in the interval". ✔\(\mu\) is fixed; the 95% describes the procedure: about 95% of intervals from repeated samples trap \(\mu\). The interval is about the mean, not individual values.
✘Computing the sample variance with \(n\) in the denominator. ✔\(s^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\) (unbiased, sections 3 and 4).
✘In sample-size questions, rounding \(n\) down (\(n\ge43.3\Rightarrow n=43\)), or mixing up half-width and full length. ✔Always round up. "Within \(\pm E\)" is the half-width; "length at most \(\ell\)" is the full width \(2z\sigma/\sqrt n\).
✘Putting the unknown \(p\) under the square root in the final answer, or using \(p=0.5\) when \(\hat p\) is known. ✔Use the observed \(\hat p\). \(\frac14\) is only for planning a sample size before any data exist (Exercise 4.11).

14Find the mistake

1. A sample of 25 from a population with \(\sigma=10\) has \(\bar x=50\). A student's 95% CI:

\[ 50\pm1.96\cdot\frac{10}{25}=50\pm0.784, \] \[ \text{CI}=(49.216,\ 50.784). \]
Hint

What is the standard deviation of \(\bar X\)?

Answer

The student divided by \(n=25\) instead of \(\sqrt n=5\). Correct: \(50\pm1.96\cdot\frac{10}{5}=50\pm3.92\), so \((46.08,\ 53.92)\).

2. For a 90% CI a student writes: "\(\alpha=0.1\), so I need \(\Phi(z)=0.9\); the table gives \(z=1.28\)."

Hint

Draw the normal curve with the central 90% shaded. How much area is to the left of the right-hand cut?

Answer

The left of the right-hand cut holds the central 90% plus the left tail 5%: \(\Phi(z)=0.95\), so \(z_{0.05}=1.645\). With 1.28 the central area would be only 80%.

3. In a survey 120 out of 400 people say yes. A student's 95% CI for the proportion:

\[ \hat p=0.3,\quad 0.3\pm1.96\cdot\frac{\sqrt{0.3\times0.7}}{400}, \] \[ \text{CI}\approx(0.2978,\ 0.3022). \]
Hint

Where should the \(n\) be?

Answer

\(n\) belongs inside the square root: \(\sqrt{0.21/400}=\frac{\sqrt{0.21}}{20}\approx0.02291\). Correct CI: \(0.3\pm1.96(0.02291)=0.3\pm0.0449\), about \((0.255,\ 0.345)\). An interval only \(\pm0.002\) wide from 400 people should have looked suspicious.

4. Data 4, 6, 8, 10 from a population with unknown \(\sigma\). A student finds \(\bar x=7\) and

\[ s^2=\frac{9+1+1+9}{4}=5,\quad s=\sqrt5. \]
Hint

Which denominator makes \(S^2\) unbiased?

Answer

Divide by \(n-1=3\): \(s^2=\frac{20}{3}\approx6.67\), \(s\approx2.58\). Dividing by \(n\) underestimates \(\sigma^2\) on average by the factor \(\frac{n-1}n\).

15Concept checks

1. Let \(X_1,X_2,X_3\) be a random sample with mean \(\mu\). Which of \(T_1=\frac{X_1+X_2}2\), \(T_2=X_1+X_2-X_3\), \(T_3=2X_1\) are unbiased estimators of \(\mu\)?

Answer

\(E(T_1)=\frac{\mu+\mu}2=\mu\) and \(E(T_2)=\mu+\mu-\mu=\mu\): both unbiased. \(E(T_3)=2\mu\ne\mu\): biased. (Unbiased is not the same as good: \(T_2\) has variance \(3\sigma^2\), worse than a single observation.)

2. Is \(\dfrac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\) a statistic when \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are unknown? Is \(\bar X\pm1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt n\) a pair of statistics when \(\sigma\) is known?

Answer

No: it contains the unknown \(\mu\) (and \(\sigma\)). Yes: with \(\sigma\) known, \(L\) and \(U\) depend only on the sample, as Definition 4.4 requires.

3. Find \(z_{\alpha/2}\) for a 96% confidence interval from the table (\(\Phi(2.05)=0.9798\), \(\Phi(2.06)=0.9803\)).

Answer

\(\alpha=0.04\), so \(\Phi(z)=0.98\). 0.98 lies between the two entries, nearer to 0.9798: \(z_{0.02}\approx2.05\) (interpolation: \(2.05+0.01\times\frac25=2.054\)).

4. With everything else unchanged, what happens to the width of a CI for \(\mu\) if (a) the sample size is multiplied by 4, (b) the level goes from 95% to 99%?

Answer

(a) Width is proportional to \(1/\sqrt n\), so it halves. (b) It is multiplied by \(\frac{2.575}{1.96}\approx1.31\): higher confidence costs a wider interval.

5. A 95% CI for \(\mu\) (σ known) is \((10,\ 14)\). What are \(\bar x\) and the 90% CI from the same data?

Answer

The centre is \(\bar x=12\) and the half-width \(1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}=2\), so \(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}=\frac{2}{1.96}\). The 90% half-width is \(1.645\times\frac{2}{1.96}\approx1.679\): the 90% CI is about \((10.32,\ 13.68)\).

6. Two researchers each compute a 95% CI for the same \(\mu\) from independent samples. What is the probability (before sampling) that both intervals trap \(\mu\)?

Answer

Independent samples give independent events, each of probability 0.95: \(0.95^2=0.9025\). So even "95%" intervals miss quite often when many are computed, as the section 5 figure shows.

Dec 2025 Q252 marks · multiple choiceGuide · P4.1 Confidence interval for a mean

A 95% confidence interval for the mean height of students is (165 cm, 175 cm). Which is the correct interpretation?

(A) There is a 95% chance that the true mean is in the interval.   (B) 95% of students have heights in the interval.   (C) If sampling were repeated many times, 95% of the computed intervals would contain the true mean.   (D) The sample mean is 170 with 95% certainty.

Hint

Which quantity is random, the interval or the true mean? Re-read sections 5 and 8.

Answer

(C). (A) treats the fixed mean as random; (B) confuses the mean with individuals; (D): the sample mean is exactly 170, the centre, with no uncertainty.

Dec 2025 Q28(a)(ii)part of a 10-mark questionGuide · P4.1 Confidence interval for a mean

The daily energy output (MWh) of a wind turbine follows an exponential distribution with rate \(\lambda=\frac12\); \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are the outputs of different turbines. A sample of \(n=64\) turbines has observed mean \(\bar x=2.1\).

(a)(i) Approximate \(P(1.75\lt\bar X\lt2.25)\). (ii) Construct an 88% confidence interval for the true mean.

Only (a)(ii) belongs to this chapter; (a)(i) is a CLT probability (P3).

Hint

An exponential distribution with rate \(\lambda\) has mean \(\frac1\lambda\) and standard deviation \(\frac1\lambda\), so \(\sigma\) is known from the model (section 11). For 88%, solve \(\Phi(z)=0.94\) in the table (section 6).

Answer

\(\sigma=2\), \(z_{0.06}\approx1.555\) (between \(\Phi(1.55)=0.9394\) and \(\Phi(1.56)=0.9406\)):

\[ 2.1\pm1.555\cdot\frac{2}{\sqrt{64}}=2.1\pm0.389, \]

about \((1.711,\ 2.489)\). (If you treat \(\sigma\) as unknown, there is no \(s\) given, so the model value is the intended one.)

Dec 2023 Q910 marksGuide · P4.1 Confidence interval for a mean

(a) [4 marks] A random sample of 64 one-bedroom apartments in Central has a mean monthly rent of HKD 10000. The population standard deviation is HKD 2000. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the population mean.

(b) [3 marks] A friend says "the mean will fall within the calculated confidence interval 95% of the time." Do you agree? Explain.

(c) [3 marks] What sample size is needed to estimate the mean within \(\pm\)HKD 500 with 90% confidence? Answer as a rational number.

Hint

(a) \(\sigma\) is known: section 7. (b) What is fixed and what varies from sample to sample? (c) "Within \(\pm500\)" is the half-width: solve \(z_{0.05}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le500\) and round up (section 10).

Answer

(a) \(10000\pm1.96\cdot\frac{2000}{8}=10000\pm490\): \((9510,\ 10490)\).

(b) Disagree. The population mean is a fixed number; it does not "fall" anywhere. The 95% means that about 95% of intervals computed in this way from repeated samples would contain the mean.

(c) \(n\ge\big(\frac{1.645\times2000}{500}\big)^2=6.58^2=43.2964\), so \(n=44\).

May 2026 Q9(b)part of a 10-mark questionGuide · P4.2 Confidence interval for a proportion

(a) Find \(k\) such that \(P(|Z|\lt k)=0.97\), where \(Z\sim N(0,1)\).

(b) To estimate the proportion of infected persons in a population during a pandemic, a survey obtained 10,000 samples; 2,000 are infected. Use the standard normal table to construct a 97% confidence interval for the proportion of infected persons.

Part (a) is the critical value of section 6; part (b) belongs to this chapter's type P4.2.

Hint

The \(k\) of part (a) is \(z_{0.015}\): \(\Phi(k)=0.985\). Then use the proportion interval of section 9 with \(\hat p=\frac{2000}{10000}\). The square root comes out exactly.

Answer

\(k=2.17\), \(\hat p=0.2\), \(\sqrt{\frac{0.2\times0.8}{10000}}=\frac{0.4}{100}=0.004\):

\[ 0.2\pm2.17(0.004)=0.2\pm0.00868, \]

so the 97% CI is \((0.19132,\ 0.20868)\).

Dec 2021 Q810 marksGuide · P4.3 Point estimation

A biased coin, which shows heads with probability \(p\), is flipped independently 1000 times and shows 400 heads. What would your estimate of \(p\) be? Justify your answer.

Hint

Record each flip as \(X_i=1\) (head) or 0. Which statistic estimates \(p\), and what are its mean and variance (sections 3 and 9)?

Answer

\(\hat p=\frac{400}{1000}=0.4\). Justification: \(\hat p=\bar X\) with \(E(\hat p)=p\) (unbiased) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat p)=\frac{p(1-p)}{1000}\le\frac1{4000}\), so by the law of large numbers the sample proportion is close to \(p\).
