Exam guide: Orthogonality and Least Squares

L7At a glance

This chapter is predictable. Every recent question (May 2023 to May 2026) is a 5- or 6-mark computation from one formula: the projection \(\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}\mathbf u\), Gram–Schmidt (which is the projection used repeatedly), or the normal equations \(X^TX\mathbf a=X^T\mathbf y\). The short proofs of type L7.4 appear only in the older papers and all use one of two tricks. Learn the four cards below, then work through the questions on this page in order.

Exam format
  • No calculators in any sitting since Dec 2022. Keep fractions and surds exact (\(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{14}}\), not 0.267). Scale a vector to clear fractions before you use it again: its direction, and so every projection along it, does not change.
  • No formula sheet. You must know by heart the projection formula, the Gram–Schmidt step and the normal equations \(X^TX\mathbf a=X^T\mathbf y\).
  • Free response. Every L7 question so far has been written out in full, never multiple choice. The method is marked, so write the formula before you substitute.
  • Time. 3 hours for 100 marks is about 1.8 minutes per mark: a 5- or 6-mark projection or Gram–Schmidt question should take about 9–11 minutes, including the check that the dot products are 0.

The four cards

L7.1 Projections and components 8 parts · 6 papers · 5–6 marks (recent)

Looks like"the component of \(\mathbf v\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf u\)", "the projection onto \(\mathbf w\)", "the vector in \(V\) closest to \(\mathbf y\)", "write \(\mathbf b=\boldsymbol\alpha+\boldsymbol\beta\)".

DoAlong \(\mathbf u\): \(\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}\mathbf u\). Perpendicular: \(\mathbf v\) minus that. Onto a plane with basis \(\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\): solve the normal equations, or make the basis orthogonal and add two projections.

WatchRead the order of the words: "the projection onto \(\mathbf w\) of the component of \(\mathbf v\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf u\)" is two steps, inner one first. Check every perpendicular answer with a dot product.

L7.2 Gram–Schmidt, orthonormal bases 8 parts · 7 papers · 5–9 marks, up to 15 in old papers

Looks like"construct an orthonormal (orthogonal) basis", "a basis of the solution space", "a unit vector perpendicular to …", a scalar product \(\int_0^1 f g\,dx\).

DoGet a basis first (drop dependent vectors; solve \(A\mathbf x=\mathbf 0\) when the space is a solution set). Then \(\mathbf w_1=\mathbf v_1\), each next \(\mathbf w_k=\mathbf v_k-\) its projections on every earlier \(\mathbf w\); divide each by its length.

Watch"Orthogonal" basis: stop before dividing. "Orthonormal": divide. Vectors perpendicular to given vectors = the null space of the matrix with those vectors as rows.

L7.3 Least squares 3 parts · 2 papers · 6–15 marks

Looks like"best-fit line for these points", "the least-squares solution", "the solution of \(A^TA\mathbf x=A^T\mathbf b\)".

Do\(X\) = a column of 1s and the column of \(x\)-values; compute \(X^TX\) and \(X^T\mathbf y\) (sums); solve the \(2\times2\) system for the intercept and the slope.

WatchThe first unknown is the intercept, the second the slope: write the line \(y=a_0+a_1x\) at the end. If \(A^TA\) is singular there are infinitely many least-squares solutions; the shortest one is perpendicular to the free direction.

L7.4 Proofs about orthonormal sets 9 parts · 6 papers (older only) · 3–4 marks a part where printed

Looks like"show that orthonormal vectors are independent", "find the coordinates", "prove \(|U|=\pm1\)", "show \(\|P\mathbf u\|=\|\mathbf u\|\)", Bessel's inequality.

DoVectors: take the scalar product of the equation with \(\mathbf u_i\). Matrices: start from \(U^TU=I\) and use \(\det(AB)=\det A\det B\) or \((AB)^T=B^TA^T\).

Watch"Orthonormal matrix" in the old papers means an orthogonal matrix: a square matrix with orthonormal columns, \(U^TU=I\). State this definition as your first line.

Learn the ideas first in the chapter note: scalar product, orthogonality and orthonormal bases (with boxes on vectors perpendicular to given vectors and on orthogonal matrices), projection, least squares, Gram–Schmidt.

How to read the row operations on this page: the label above an arrow names the new row first. \(R_2-3R_1\) means "replace row 2 by row 2 minus 3 times row 1"; \(2R_2-3R_1\) means "replace row 2 by 2 times row 2 minus 3 times row 1" (allowed because the row being replaced is multiplied by a non-zero number).

L7.1Projections and orthogonal components

8 parts · May 2026, May 2025, May 2023, Dec 2021; older format May 2015, May 2013 (×3, of which (a) is earlier syllabus) · Learn it: note §6 Projection, §7 (closest point of a subspace), §1, §2

May 2013 Q2(a) (lengths, angle, component, cross product \(\vec a\times\vec b\)) is from an earlier syllabus: solved on the earlier-syllabus page.

Method
  1. Identify the two roles: the vector being split (\(\mathbf v\)) and the direction (\(\mathbf u\)). "Component of \(\mathbf v\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf u\)", "projection of \(\mathbf v\) onto \(\mathbf u\)", "\(\mathbf v\) along \(\mathbf u\)".
  2. Compute the two numbers \(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u\), and write the projection \(\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}\mathbf u\).
  3. Perpendicular component = \(\mathbf v\) minus the projection. Check: its dot product with \(\mathbf u\) is 0.
  4. Two-step questions ("the projection onto \(\mathbf w\) of the component of \(\mathbf v\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf u\)"): do the inner phrase first, scale the result to whole numbers, then use it in the outer step.
  5. Closest point of a plane \(V=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\}\) to \(\mathbf y\): put \(\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\) as the columns of \(A\), solve \(A^TA\mathbf c=A^T\mathbf y\), answer \(c_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\mathbf v_2\). Check \(\mathbf y-\mathbf v\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf v_1\) and \(\mathbf v_2\).
  6. A length is asked for (or given): \(\|\mathbf x\|=\sqrt{\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf x}\), and \(\sqrt{k^2}=|k|\).
Ready answer · the split of v along u \[ \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v=\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}\,\mathbf u \] \[ \mathbf v_\perp=\mathbf v-\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}\,\mathbf u,\qquad \mathbf v_\perp\cdot\mathbf u=0 \]

"The component of \(\mathbf v\) in the direction of \(\mathbf u\)" can mean the vector above or the number \(\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u}{\|\mathbf u\|}\) (its signed length); when unsure, give the vector and its length.

Scaling \(\mathbf u\) by any non-zero number does not change either answer, so replace \(\mathbf u\) by a whole-number multiple first.

May 2026 Q55 points

Given

\[ \mathbf u=\begin{bmatrix}k\\-k\\2k\end{bmatrix}\ \text{and}\ \mathbf w=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{bmatrix}, \]

if the length of the component of \(\mathbf u\) which is perpendicular to \(\mathbf w\) is 3 units, what are the values of \(k\)?

Hint

L7.1. Compute \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w\) and \(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf w\); every entry of \(\mathbf u\) carries a factor \(k\), so the perpendicular component is \(k\) times a fixed vector.

Solution
  1. \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w=k-2k-2k=-3k\) and \(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf w=1+4+1=6\), so the projection of \(\mathbf u\) on \(\mathbf w\) is \[ \frac{-3k}{6}\,\mathbf w=-\frac k2\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{bmatrix}. \]
  2. Perpendicular component: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf u_\perp&=\begin{bmatrix}k\\-k\\2k\end{bmatrix}+\frac k2\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{bmatrix}\\ &=\begin{bmatrix}3k/2\\0\\3k/2\end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned} \] Check: \(\mathbf u_\perp\cdot\mathbf w=\tfrac{3k}2-\tfrac{3k}2=0\ \checkmark\)
  3. Its length: \[ \|\mathbf u_\perp\|=\sqrt{\tfrac{9k^2}4+\tfrac{9k^2}4}=\frac{3\sqrt2}{2}|k|. \]
  4. Set it equal to 3: \(|k|=\frac{2}{\sqrt2}=\sqrt2\).

Marker: show the perpendicular component as a vector before taking its length, and give both signs of \(k\) (the length only fixes \(|k|\)).

\[ \boxed{k=\pm\sqrt2} \]

May 2025 Q56 points

Given \(\mathbf u=[1,2,3]^T\), \(\mathbf v=[-1,-2,2]^T\), and \(\mathbf w=[5,1,-3]^T\), find the component of \(\mathbf w\) which is perpendicular to a vector \(\mathbf z\) where \(\mathbf z\) is the component of \(\mathbf u\) which is perpendicular to \(\mathbf v\).

Hint

L7.1, two steps, inner one first: find \(\mathbf z=\mathbf u-\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v}\mathbf v\), scale it to whole numbers, then split \(\mathbf w\) along it.

Solution
  1. \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=-1-4+6=1\), \(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v=1+4+4=9\): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf z&=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix}-\frac19\begin{bmatrix}-1\\-2\\2\end{bmatrix}\\ &=\frac19\begin{bmatrix}10\\20\\25\end{bmatrix}=\frac59\begin{bmatrix}2\\4\\5\end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned} \] Check: \(\mathbf z\cdot\mathbf v\propto-2-8+10=0\ \checkmark\)
  2. Only the direction of \(\mathbf z\) matters, so use \(\mathbf z'=(2,4,5)\): \(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf z'=10+4-15=-1\), \(\mathbf z'\cdot\mathbf z'=4+16+25=45\).
  3. Perpendicular component of \(\mathbf w\): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf w-\frac{-1}{45}\mathbf z'&=\begin{bmatrix}5\\1\\-3\end{bmatrix}+\frac1{45}\begin{bmatrix}2\\4\\5\end{bmatrix}\\ &=\frac1{45}\begin{bmatrix}227\\49\\-130\end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]
  4. Check against \((2,4,5)\): \[ \begin{aligned} &2(227)+4(49)+5(-130)\\ &=454+196-650=0\ \checkmark \end{aligned} \]

Marker: show \(\mathbf z\) explicitly (it is half the work), then the second projection; say why replacing \(\mathbf z\) by \((2,4,5)\) is allowed.

\[ \boxed{\mathbf w_\perp=\frac1{45}\begin{bmatrix}227\\49\\-130\end{bmatrix}} \]

Dec 2021 Q4(b)5 marks

Parts (a), (c) of this question: a system of differential equations and a constrained quadratic form, in the L6 guide (L6.2) and (L6.1).

Let \(V\) be a subspace of \(\mathbb R^3\) with basis vectors \(\mathbf v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf v_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}\). Find the vector \(\mathbf v\in V\) which has the shortest Euclidean distance to the vector \(\mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}8\\0\\6\end{bmatrix}\).

Hint

L7.1, closest point of a plane. \(\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_2=1\ne0\), so you cannot simply add two projections. Put \(\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\) as columns of \(A\) and solve \(A^TA\mathbf c=A^T\mathbf y\).

Solution
  1. The closest point is the \(\mathbf v=c_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\mathbf v_2\) for which \(\mathbf y-\mathbf v\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf v_1\) and \(\mathbf v_2\). With \(A=[\mathbf v_1\ \mathbf v_2]\) this is \(A^TA\mathbf c=A^T\mathbf y\): \[ \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}8\\6\end{bmatrix} \] (entries: \(\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_1=2\), \(\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_2=1\), \(\mathbf v_2\cdot\mathbf v_2=2\), \(\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf y=8\), \(\mathbf v_2\cdot\mathbf y=6\)).
  2. Twice the first equation minus the second: \(3c_1=10\), \(c_1=\tfrac{10}3\); then \(c_2=8-\tfrac{20}3=\tfrac43\).
  3. \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf v&=\tfrac{10}3(1,1,0)+\tfrac43(0,1,1)\\ &=\big(\tfrac{10}3,\tfrac{14}3,\tfrac43\big). \end{aligned} \]
  4. Check: \[ \mathbf y-\mathbf v=\tfrac{14}3(1,-1,1), \] whose dot product with \((1,1,0)\) and with \((0,1,1)\) is 0 \(\checkmark\)

Marker: say why the answer is the closest point (\(\mathbf y-\mathbf v\perp V\)), not only the computation.

\[ \boxed{\mathbf v=\frac13\begin{bmatrix}10\\14\\4\end{bmatrix}} \]

Other route: the normal of the plane is \(\mathbf n=(1,-1,1)\) (perpendicular to \(\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\)); \(\mathbf v=\mathbf y-\frac{\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf n}{\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf n}\mathbf n=\mathbf y-\tfrac{14}3\mathbf n\). Shortest distance \(\tfrac{14}{\sqrt3}\).

May 2013 Q2(b), (e)8 marks for Q2older format

Part (a): earlier syllabus, earlier-syllabus page. Parts (c), (d): L7.4 on this page.

Part (e) is kept here because the work is all scalar products, but it quotes one earlier-syllabus fact: \(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf a\) and \(\mathbf b\). Cross products are not in the 2026 handouts; see the earlier-syllabus page if you want the background.

(b) Show that if \(\|\vec u\|=\|\vec v\|\) then \(\vec u+\vec v\) and \(\vec u-\vec v\) are perpendicular.

(e) Suppose that \(\mathbf u_1\), \(\mathbf u_2\) and \(\mathbf u_3\) are vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\) such that the scalar product \(\mathbf u_1\cdot(\mathbf u_2\times\mathbf u_3)\ne0\). Let

\[ \mathbf v_1=\frac{\mathbf u_2\times\mathbf u_3}{\mathbf u_1\cdot(\mathbf u_2\times\mathbf u_3)}. \]

Show that \(\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf u_1=1\) and \(\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf u_2=\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf u_3=0\).

Hint

(b) Perpendicular means the dot product is 0: expand \((\vec u+\vec v)\cdot(\vec u-\vec v)\) like \((a+b)(a-b)\). (e) Write \(d=\mathbf u_1\cdot(\mathbf u_2\times\mathbf u_3)\), a non-zero number, and use one property of the cross product (note §4, earlier-syllabus box).

Solution

(b)

  1. Expand, using \(\vec u\cdot\vec v=\vec v\cdot\vec u\): \[ \begin{aligned} &(\vec u+\vec v)\cdot(\vec u-\vec v)\\ &=\vec u\cdot\vec u-\vec u\cdot\vec v+\vec v\cdot\vec u-\vec v\cdot\vec v\\ &=\|\vec u\|^2-\|\vec v\|^2=0. \end{aligned} \]
  2. A zero dot product means the two vectors are perpendicular.

(e)

  1. Let \(d=\mathbf u_1\cdot(\mathbf u_2\times\mathbf u_3)\ne0\), a number, so \(\mathbf v_1=\frac1d(\mathbf u_2\times\mathbf u_3)\) and \(\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf x=\frac1d\,(\mathbf u_2\times\mathbf u_3)\cdot\mathbf x\) for every \(\mathbf x\).
  2. Since the dot product is symmetric, \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf u_1&=\tfrac1d\,\mathbf u_1\cdot(\mathbf u_2\times\mathbf u_3)\\ &=\tfrac dd=1. \end{aligned} \]
  3. The cross product \(\mathbf u_2\times\mathbf u_3\) is orthogonal to both \(\mathbf u_2\) and \(\mathbf u_3\), so \((\mathbf u_2\times\mathbf u_3)\cdot\mathbf u_2=0\) and \((\mathbf u_2\times\mathbf u_3)\cdot\mathbf u_3=0\). Dividing by \(d\): \(\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf u_2=\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf u_3=0\).

Marker: in (e) quote the property "the cross product is perpendicular to each factor" by name; it is the whole proof.

\[ \boxed{\begin{aligned} &(\vec u+\vec v)\cdot(\vec u-\vec v)=0;\\ &\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf u_1=1,\ \mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf u_2=\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf u_3=0 \end{aligned}} \]

(b) in words: the diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular.

L7.2Gram–Schmidt, orthonormal bases, orthogonal complements

8 parts · May 2024; older format May 2019, May 2017, May 2016, May 2015 (×2), Dec 2013, Dec 2012 · Learn it: note §8 Gram–Schmidt, §4 (orthonormal bases; vectors perpendicular to given vectors), §1 (scalar product of functions)

Method
  1. Get a basis of the space first.
    • Given vectors: check they are independent (rank); drop any that is a combination of the others.
    • "Solution space of \(A\mathbf x=\mathbf 0\)": row reduce, one basic solution per free variable.
    • "Vectors perpendicular to \(\mathbf a_1,\dots,\mathbf a_m\)": put the \(\mathbf a_i\) as the rows of a matrix and solve \(A\mathbf x=\mathbf 0\) the same way.
  2. Gram–Schmidt: \(\mathbf w_1=\mathbf v_1\); \(\mathbf w_2=\mathbf v_2-\frac{\mathbf v_2\cdot\mathbf w_1}{\mathbf w_1\cdot\mathbf w_1}\mathbf w_1\); \(\mathbf w_3=\mathbf v_3\) minus its projections on \(\mathbf w_1\) and \(\mathbf w_2\). Scale each \(\mathbf w_k\) to whole numbers before using it again.
  3. Check every pair: \(\mathbf w_i\cdot\mathbf w_j=0\).
  4. "Orthonormal": divide each \(\mathbf w_k\) by \(\|\mathbf w_k\|\). "Orthogonal": stop at step 3. "A unit vector": one vector, divided by its length (\(\pm\) both work).
  5. Functions: the same steps with \(\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1f(x)g(x)\,dx\) in place of the dot product.
Ready answer · how to write it

"The vectors \(\mathbf w_1,\mathbf w_2,\dots\) are non-zero and pairwise orthogonal (dot products 0), and they span the same space as \(\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots\); so they form an orthogonal basis. Dividing by the lengths:"

\[ \left\{\tfrac{\mathbf w_1}{\|\mathbf w_1\|},\ \tfrac{\mathbf w_2}{\|\mathbf w_2\|},\ \dots\right\} \]

The dimension of the space = the number of vectors in the basis (the number of free variables, for a solution space).

Construct an orthonormal basis for vectors \(\{(1,2,3),(2,3,4),(2,5,7)\}\).

Hint

L7.2 (word for word slide 16, note §8). The three vectors are independent, so Gram–Schmidt gives three vectors. \(\mathbf w_1=(1,2,3)\), \(\|\mathbf w_1\|^2=14\).

Solution

  2. \(\mathbf w_1=(1,2,3)\), \(\mathbf w_1\cdot\mathbf w_1=14\).

May 2019 Q59 marksolder format

The scalar product between two functions in a vector space \(V\) is given by

\[ \langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1f(x)g(x)\,dx. \]

Let \(V=\{f_1,f_2,f_3\}\) such that \(f_1(x)=1\), \(f_2(x)=x\), \(f_3(x)=x^2\). Find an orthonormal basis of \(V\).

Reading used: \(V\) is the space spanned by \(f_1,f_2,f_3\) (the polynomials of degree at most 2).

Hint

L7.2 with functions. The integrals you need: \(\int_0^1x^n\,dx=\frac1{n+1}\). Start with \(w_1=1\), \(\|w_1\|^2=1\); then \(w_2=x-\langle x,1\rangle\).

Solution
  1. \(w_1=1\): \(\langle 1,1\rangle=\int_0^1 1\,dx=1\).
  2. \(\langle x,1\rangle=\tfrac12\), so \(w_2=x-\tfrac12\). Then \[ \begin{aligned} \|w_2\|^2&=\int_0^1\big(x^2-x+\tfrac14\big)dx\\ &=\tfrac13-\tfrac12+\tfrac14=\tfrac1{12}. \end{aligned} \]
  3. \(\langle x^2,1\rangle=\tfrac13\) and \(\langle x^2,w_2\rangle=\tfrac14-\tfrac16=\tfrac1{12}\), so \[ \begin{aligned} w_3&=x^2-\tfrac13\cdot1-\tfrac{1/12}{1/12}\big(x-\tfrac12\big)\\ &=x^2-x+\tfrac16. \end{aligned} \]
  4. Its length: since \(w_3\perp1\) and \(w_3\perp x\), \(\|w_3\|^2=\langle w_3,x^2\rangle\): \[ \tfrac15-\tfrac14+\tfrac1{18}=\tfrac{36-45+10}{180}=\tfrac1{180}. \]
  5. Normalize: \[ \begin{aligned} &\sqrt{12}\,\big(x-\tfrac12\big)=\sqrt3\,(2x-1),\\ &\sqrt{180}\,\big(x^2-x+\tfrac16\big)\\ &\quad=\sqrt5\,(6x^2-6x+1). \end{aligned} \]

Marker: show the inner products you integrate, and check \(\langle w_2,w_3\rangle=0\) if time allows (\(\int_0^1(x-\frac12)(x^2-x+\frac16)\,dx=0\)).

\[ \boxed{\begin{aligned} &1,\quad \sqrt3\,(2x-1),\\ &\sqrt5\,(6x^2-6x+1) \end{aligned}} \]

L7.3Least squares

3 parts · May 2024; older format May 2018 (a, b) · Learn it: note §7 Least squares

Method
  1. Best-fit line \(y=a_0+a_1x\) through \(n\) points: \(X\) has a column of 1s and the column of \(x\)-values; \(\mathbf y\) is the column of \(y\)-values.
  2. Compute by sums (no need to write \(X^T\) out): \[ X^TX=\begin{bmatrix}n&\sum x\\ \sum x&\sum x^2\end{bmatrix} \] \[ X^T\mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}\sum y\\ \sum xy\end{bmatrix} \]
  3. Solve the normal equations \(X^TX\mathbf a=X^T\mathbf y\) (a \(2\times2\) system: eliminate, or Cramer).
  4. Write the line, and check it passes through the mean point \((\bar x,\bar y)\): \(a_0+a_1\bar x=\bar y\).
  5. General \(A\mathbf x=\mathbf b\): form \(A^TA\) (entry \(i,j\) = column \(i\) \(\cdot\) column \(j\)) and \(A^T\mathbf b\), then row reduce. If there is a free variable, the minimum-norm solution is the one perpendicular to the free direction.
Ready answer · the best-fit line \[ \begin{bmatrix}n&\sum x\\ \sum x&\sum x^2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a_0\\a_1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum y\\ \sum xy\end{bmatrix} \]

"The least-squares line is \(y=a_0+a_1x\)": it minimizes \(\sum\big(y_i-(a_0+a_1x_i)\big)^2\), the sum of the squared vertical errors.

Method · a decision procedure

It is known that one of the eigenvalues of \(B\) is 2. … c) Prove that for an orthonormal matrix \(\mathbf U\), we must have \(|\mathbf U||\mathbf U^T|=1\). Use this result to compute \(|\mathbf B|\).

Hint

L7.4, determinant: \(|\mathbf U||\mathbf U^T|=|\mathbf U\mathbf U^T|\). For \(|\mathbf B|\): \(\mathbf B\) is symmetric, so \(\mathbf B=\mathbf U\mathbf D\mathbf U^T\) with \(\mathbf U\) orthogonal (L6). \(\mathbf B-2I\) is the all-ones matrix: how many eigenvectors for 2?

Solution
  1. \(\mathbf U\) is square with orthonormal columns, so \(\mathbf U^T\mathbf U=I\), hence \(\mathbf U^{-1}=\mathbf U^T\) and also \(\mathbf U\mathbf U^T=I\). Then \[ |\mathbf U||\mathbf U^T|=|\mathbf U\mathbf U^T|=|I|=1. \]
  2. Eigenvalues of \(\mathbf B\): \(\mathbf B-2I\) has every entry 1, rank 1, so 2 has two independent eigenvectors (multiplicity 2). The trace is 9 = sum of the eigenvalues, so the third is \(9-2-2=5\) (indeed \(\mathbf B(1,1,1)^T=5(1,1,1)^T\)).
  3. \(\mathbf B\) is symmetric, so \(\mathbf B=\mathbf U\mathbf D\mathbf U^T\) with \(\mathbf U\) orthonormal and \(\mathbf D=\operatorname{diag}(2,2,5)\). Then \[ \begin{aligned} |\mathbf B|&=|\mathbf U||\mathbf D||\mathbf U^T|\\ &=|\mathbf D|\cdot1=2\cdot2\cdot5. \end{aligned} \]

Marker: the question says "use this result", so show \(\mathbf B=\mathbf U\mathbf D\mathbf U^T\) and the cancellation of \(|\mathbf U||\mathbf U^T|\), not only a direct cofactor expansion.

\[ \boxed{|\mathbf U||\mathbf U^T|=1,\quad |\mathbf B|=20} \]

Check by cofactors:

\[ \begin{aligned} &3(9-1)-1(3-1)+1(1-3)\\ &=24-2-2=20\ \checkmark \end{aligned} \]

(c) Show that if \(P\) is an orthonormal matrix, then \(\|P\vec u\|=\|\vec u\|\).
(d) Show that if \(Q\) is an orthonormal matrix, then \(P=QQ^T\) satisfies \(P^2=P\).

Reading used for (d): \(Q\) has orthonormal columns, \(Q^TQ=I\) (it may be \(n\times k\)). If \(Q\) is square, \(P=QQ^T=I\) and \(P^2=P\) is immediate; the proof below covers both.

Hint

L7.4, matrices. (c) Work with \(\|P\vec u\|^2=(P\vec u)^T(P\vec u)\). (d) Write \(P^2=QQ^TQQ^T\) and look at the middle.

Solution

(c)

  1. \(P\) has orthonormal columns, so \(P^TP=I\).
  2. Using \((AB)^T=B^TA^T\): \[ \begin{aligned} \|P\vec u\|^2&=(P\vec u)^T(P\vec u)\\ &=\vec u^TP^TP\vec u\\ &=\vec u^T\vec u=\|\vec u\|^2. \end{aligned} \]
  3. Lengths are \(\ge0\), so taking square roots, \(\|P\vec u\|=\|\vec u\|\).

(d)

  1. \(Q\) has orthonormal columns, so \(Q^TQ=I\).
  2. \[ \begin{aligned} P^2&=Q\,(Q^TQ)\,Q^T\\ &=Q\,I\,Q^T=QQ^T=P. \end{aligned} \]
\[ \boxed{\begin{aligned} &\text{(c) }\|P\vec u\|^2=\vec u^TP^TP\vec u=\|\vec u\|^2\\ &\text{(d) }P^2=Q(Q^TQ)Q^T=P \end{aligned}} \]

Meaning of (d): \(QQ^T\vec x\) is the projection of \(\vec x\) onto the column space of \(Q\); projecting twice changes nothing.

Let \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots,\mathbf v_n\}\) be an orthonormal basis of an \(n\) dimensional space \(V\). For any vector \(\mathbf y\in V\), prove that

\[ \|\mathbf y\|^2\ge\sum_{i=1}^rc_i^2 \]

with \(r\le n\) and \(c_i\) is the orthogonal projection coefficient of \(\mathbf y\) onto \(\mathbf v_i\).

Hint

L7.4, vectors. Write \(\mathbf y\) in the basis; the coefficients are exactly the \(c_i\) (May 2019 Q4(c)). Then \(\|\mathbf y\|^2\) is a sum of \(n\) squares; drop the last \(n-r\).

Solution
  1. The projection of \(\mathbf y\) onto \(\mathbf v_i\) is \(\frac{\langle\mathbf y,\mathbf v_i\rangle}{\langle\mathbf v_i,\mathbf v_i\rangle}\mathbf v_i=c_i\mathbf v_i\) with \(c_i=\langle\mathbf y,\mathbf v_i\rangle\), since \(\|\mathbf v_i\|=1\).
  2. \(\{\mathbf v_i\}\) is a basis, so \(\mathbf y=\sum_{j=1}^n\alpha_j\mathbf v_j\); taking the scalar product with \(\mathbf v_i\) gives \(\alpha_i=\langle\mathbf y,\mathbf v_i\rangle=c_i\).
  3. Expand, all cross terms 0 by orthonormality: \[ \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf y\|^2&=\Big\langle\sum_ic_i\mathbf v_i,\sum_jc_j\mathbf v_j\Big\rangle\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^nc_i^2. \end{aligned} \]
  4. For \(r\le n\) the terms \(c_{r+1}^2,\dots,c_n^2\) are \(\ge0\), so dropping them cannot increase the sum.
\[ \boxed{\|\mathbf y\|^2=\sum_{i=1}^nc_i^2\ \ge\ \sum_{i=1}^rc_i^2} \]
