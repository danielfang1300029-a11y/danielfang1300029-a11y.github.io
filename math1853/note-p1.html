Complex variables

0Where this chapter sits

1The imaginary unit and ℂ ~3 min

What is this?

Picture the real numbers as points on a long straight road, with 0 in the middle. Multiplying by \(-1\) swings any arrow from 0 round by a half turn: the arrow to 3 now points to \(-3\).

What number, used twice, gives \(-1\)? On the road there is none: the square of a real number is never negative. But leave the road, and a quarter turn done twice is a half turn. That quarter turn is \(i\). It points off the road, sideways, and doing it twice gives \(i^2=-1\). So complex numbers live on a whole flat map, not just on the road. Section 9 shows that multiplying by \(i\) really is a quarter turn.

The square of a real number is never negative, so the equation \(x^2+1=0\) has no real solution. Complex numbers are what you get if you simply declare that a solution exists and see what follows.

Definition: the imaginary number and ℂ

We consider an artificial number \(i\), called the imaginary number, which satisfies

\[ x^2+1=0,\quad\text{that is}\quad i^2=-1. \]

No real number satisfies this equation, so if a solution exists it must be "imaginary". The set of complex numbers is then

\[ \mathbb C=\{\,a+bi \mid a,b\in\mathbb R\,\}. \]

By demanding that \(a+0i=a\) for every real number \(a\), the real numbers sit inside the complex numbers:

\[ \mathbb R\subsetneq\mathbb C. \]

In words: a complex number is a pair of real numbers \(a\) and \(b\) glued together as \(a+bi\). Real numbers are the complex numbers with \(b=0\); the symbol \(\subsetneq\) means "is contained in, but is not the whole of" (for example \(i\) is in \(\mathbb C\) but not in \(\mathbb R\)).

Notation

\(\mathbb C=\{a+bi\mid a,b\in\mathbb R\}\) is read "the set of all elements of the form \(a+bi\), subject to the condition that \(a,b\in\mathbb R\)", that is, \(a\) and \(b\) are real numbers.

For real numbers we usually call unknowns \(x\) and \(y\). For complex numbers we use the letters \(z\) and \(w\) instead.

The set-builder notation \(\{\ \cdot \mid \text{condition}\}\) will be used all through the chapter: "the set of all things of this form such that the condition holds". The bar \(\mid\) is read "such that".

Going further: powers of i

From \(i^2=-1\): \(i^3=i^2\cdot i=-i\), \(i^4=(i^2)^2=1\), and then the pattern repeats with period 4:

\[ \begin{aligned} &i^0=1,\quad i^1=i,\quad i^2=-1,\\ &i^3=-i,\quad i^4=1,\ \dots \end{aligned} \]

So \(i^n\) depends only on the remainder of \(n\) on division by 4. For example \(i^{103}=i^{100}\cdot i^3=1\cdot(-i)=-i\), since \(100\) is a multiple of 4. Past papers use this often (for example May 2018 Q4(b), with \(w^{103}\) for a 4th root of unity \(w\)).

You can now: say what \(i\) and \(\mathbb C\) are; read set-builder notation \(\{\cdot\mid\text{condition}\}\); and reduce any power \(i^n\) using the remainder of \(n\) on division by 4.

2Arithmetic and the conjugate ~6 min

What is this?

Read \(a+bi\) as walking instructions on the map: go \(a\) along the road (east) and \(b\) sideways (north). Adding two complex numbers is doing one walk and then the other: the east parts add and the north parts add.

The conjugate is the mirror image of the point in the road, as if the road were the edge of a still lake: the same distance east, but north swapped for south.

Definition 1.1: real part, imaginary part, conjugate

For \(z=a+bi\) where \(a,b\in\mathbb R\), we say that

  • \(a=\operatorname{Re}(z)\), the real part of the complex number \(z\);
  • \(b=\operatorname{Im}(z)\), the imaginary part of \(z\);
  • the conjugate of \(z\) is \(\bar z:=a-bi\).

The imaginary part \(b\) in \(z\) changes to \(-b\) in \(\bar z\). (The symbol \(:=\) means "is defined to be".)

In words: \(\operatorname{Re}\) and \(\operatorname{Im}\) read off the two real numbers that make up \(z\). The imaginary part is the real number \(b\), not \(bi\). The conjugate keeps the real part and flips the sign of the imaginary part.

Example

If \(z=-1-\sqrt3 i\), then \(\operatorname{Re}(z)=-1\), \(\operatorname{Im}(z)=-\sqrt3\) and \(\bar z=-1+\sqrt3 i\).

The four operations

Complex arithmetic works like operations on polynomials in the letter \(i\), with one simplification rule: replace \(i^2\) by \(-1\) whenever it appears.

Rules

For \(z_1=a_1+b_1i\) and \(z_2=a_2+b_2i\), where all \(a_j,b_j\in\mathbb R\):

  • \(z_1=z_2\) if and only if \(a_1=a_2\) and \(b_1=b_2\);
  • \(z_1+z_2=(a_1+a_2)+(b_1+b_2)i\);
  • \(z_1-z_2=(a_1-a_2)+(b_1-b_2)i\);
  • \(z_1\cdot z_2=a_1a_2+a_1b_2i+a_2b_1i+b_1b_2i^2\), so
\[ z_1\cdot z_2=(a_1a_2-b_1b_2)+(a_1b_2+a_2b_1)i. \]

Since \(z=a+bi\) with \(a,b\in\mathbb R\), one immediately gets

\[ z\cdot\bar z=a^2+b^2. \]

In words: two complex numbers are equal only when their real parts agree and their imaginary parts agree; one complex equation is two real equations. Add and subtract part by part; multiply by expanding the brackets and using \(i^2=-1\). A number times its conjugate is always a real number, never negative.

Reading the formula
\(a_1a_2\)
real part times real part: an ordinary real number.
\(-b_1b_2\)
the two sideways parts multiplied: \(b_1i\cdot b_2i=b_1b_2\,i^2\). Two quarter turns make a half turn, so this lands back on the road with a minus sign.
\((a_1b_2+a_2b_1)i\)
the cross terms: one real part times the other sideways part, which stays sideways.
\(z\cdot\bar z=a^2+b^2\)
a number times its mirror image: the cross terms cancel, so the result is on the road and never below 0.

The "if and only if" rule is the key to every "find real \(x\) and \(y\)" question: compare real parts and imaginary parts separately.

Example 1.2

Let \(z_1=1+i\) and \(z_2=-1-i\). Then

\[ z_1+z_2=(1-1)+(1-1)i=0+0i=0, \] \[ z_1-z_2=(1+1)+(1+1)i=2+2i, \] \[ \begin{aligned} z_1\cdot z_2&=(1+i)(-1-i)\\ &=-1-i-i+i(-i)\\ &=-1-2i+1=-2i, \end{aligned} \]

using \(i(-i)=-i^2=1\). And

\[ \begin{aligned} z_1\cdot\bar z_1&=(1+i)(1-i)\\ &=1-i^2=1-(-1)=2. \end{aligned} \]
The "golden" formula

Above we have used

\[ (a-b)(a+b)=a^2-b^2. \]

In words: with \(b\) replaced by \(bi\) it gives

\[ \begin{aligned} (a-bi)(a+bi)&=a^2-(bi)^2\\ &=a^2-b^2i^2=a^2+b^2. \end{aligned} \]

This is why \(z\bar z\) is real, and it is the trick behind division (section 5).

Exercise 1.3

Show that (i) \(\overline{z+w}=\bar z+\bar w\); (ii) \(\overline{z\cdot w}=\bar z\cdot\bar w\).

To start, set generic forms \(z=a+bi\) and \(w=c+di\) (with \(a,b,c,d\) real), and compute both sides separately.

Solution

(i) \(z+w=(a+c)+(b+d)i\), so \(\overline{z+w}=(a+c)-(b+d)i\). On the other side,

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar z+\bar w&=(a-bi)+(c-di)\\ &=(a+c)-(b+d)i. \end{aligned} \]

They agree.

(ii) By the rules of section 2, \(zw=(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i\), so \(\overline{zw}=(ac-bd)-(ad+bc)i\). On the other side,

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar z\,\bar w&=(a-bi)(c-di)\\ &=ac-adi-bci+bdi^2\\ &=(ac-bd)-(ad+bc)i. \end{aligned} \]

They agree. \(\blacksquare\)

In words: conjugating a sum or a product is the same as conjugating each piece first. (The same then holds for differences, powers and quotients.)

Exercise 1.4

Express in the form \(x+yi\), where \(x,y\) are real: (a) \((-1+3i)^2\) (b) \((1+i)(1-i)\) (c) \((1+i)i(2-i)\) (d) \((i-1)(2-i)\) (e) \((7+\pi i)(\pi+i)\) (f) \((2i+1)\pi i\) (g) \((\sqrt2 i)(\pi+3i)\) (h) \((i+1)(i-2)(i+3)\).

Solution

(a)

\[ \begin{aligned} (-1+3i)^2&=1-6i+9i^2\\ &=1-6i-9=-8-6i. \end{aligned} \]

(b) \((1+i)(1-i)=1-i^2=2\) (the golden formula).

(c) First \((1+i)i=i+i^2=-1+i\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} (-1+i)(2-i)&=-2+i+2i-i^2\\ &=-2+3i+1\\ &=-1+3i. \end{aligned} \]

(d)

\[ \begin{aligned} (i-1)(2-i)&=2i-i^2-2+i\\ &=-1+3i. \end{aligned} \]

(Same as (c), because \((1+i)i=i-1\).)

(e)

\[ \begin{aligned} &(7+\pi i)(\pi+i)\\ &=7\pi+7i+\pi^2 i+\pi i^2\\ &=(7\pi-\pi)+(7+\pi^2)i\\ &=6\pi+(7+\pi^2)i. \end{aligned} \]

(f) \((2i+1)\pi i=2\pi i^2+\pi i=-2\pi+\pi i\).

(g)

\[ \begin{aligned} (\sqrt2 i)(\pi+3i)&=\sqrt2\pi i+3\sqrt2 i^2\\ &=-3\sqrt2+\sqrt2\pi i. \end{aligned} \]

(h)

\[ \begin{aligned} (i+1)(i-2)&=i^2-2i+i-2\\ &=-3-i. \end{aligned} \]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} (-3-i)(i+3)&=-3i-9-i^2-3i\\ &=-8-6i. \end{aligned} \]
Exercise 1.5

Show that (i) \(\bar{\bar z}=z\); (ii) \(\bar z=z\) if and only if \(z\) is real (of the form \(a+0i\) for some real \(a\)); (iii) \(\overline{-z}=-\bar z\).

Solution

Let \(z=a+bi\).

(i) \(\bar z=a-bi\), and conjugating again flips the sign back: \(\bar{\bar z}=a-(-b)i=a+bi=z\).

(ii) \(\bar z=z\) means \(a-bi=a+bi\). By the equality rule, the imaginary parts agree: \(-b=b\), so \(b=0\) and \(z=a+0i\) is real. Conversely, if \(b=0\) then \(\bar z=a-0i=a=z\).

(iii) \(-z=-a-bi\), so \(\overline{-z}=-a+bi=-(a-bi)=-\bar z\). \(\blacksquare\)

Going further: "find real x and y"

A common exam pattern (May 2016 Q4(a)): find real \(x,y\) with \(\frac{x}{1+i}+\frac{y}{2-i}=2+4i\). Method: clear the fractions or simplify each one to \(p+qi\) form (section 5), collect the real part and the imaginary part, then use the equality rule to get two real equations in \(x\) and \(y\) and solve them. Here \(\frac{x}{1+i}=\frac{x(1-i)}{2}\) and \(\frac{y}{2-i}=\frac{y(2+i)}{5}\), so

\[ \Big(\tfrac x2+\tfrac{2y}5\Big)+\Big(-\tfrac x2+\tfrac y5\Big)i=2+4i. \]

Adding the two real equations \(\frac x2+\frac{2y}5=2\) and \(-\frac x2+\frac y5=4\) gives \(\frac{3y}5=6\), so \(y=10\), and then \(x=2(2-4)=-4\).

You can now: add, subtract and multiply complex numbers and give the answer as \(x+yi\); take conjugates and use \(\overline{z+w}=\bar z+\bar w\) and \(\overline{zw}=\bar z\bar w\); and turn one complex equation into two real equations.

3The Argand diagram and the modulus ~4 min

What is this?

The Argand diagram is the map of section 2 drawn on paper: \(a+bi\) is the point you reach from home (0) by walking \(a\) east and \(b\) north. The modulus is how far that point is from home as the crow flies, found by Pythagoras. The distance between two points \(z\) and \(w\) is the length of the straight walk from one to the other.

The complex plane

Each element \(a+bi\) of \(\mathbb C\) is completely described by the two real numbers \(a,b\). So there is a one-to-one correspondence between \(\mathbb C\) and \(\mathbb R^2\) (the \(xy\)-plane):

\[ z=a+bi\ \longmapsto\ (a,b). \]

What happens in \(\mathbb C\) should therefore also happen in \(\mathbb R^2\), and vice versa: the \(x\)-\(y\) coordinate system translates into the complex plane. The first coordinate \(a\) is \(\operatorname{Re}(z)\), so the \(x\)-axis is called the Re-axis; the second coordinate \(b\) is \(\operatorname{Im}(z)\), so the \(y\)-axis is called the Im-axis.

In words: draw \(a+bi\) as the point \((a,b)\). Real numbers lie on the horizontal axis, numbers \(bi\) ("purely imaginary") on the vertical axis.

The same four points in ℂ and in ℝ²

The diagram on the left, representing \(\mathbb C\), is called the Argand diagram.

Geometric meaning of the conjugate and of z·z̄

Because of this translation, the conjugate \(\bar z=a-bi\) and the product \(z\cdot\bar z=a^2+b^2\) (for \(z=a+bi\), \(a,b\in\mathbb R\)) get geometric meanings:

  • \(z\) and \(\bar z\) are mirror images of each other across the Re-axis: \((a,b)\) and \((a,-b)\).
  • \(\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) is the distance between \((0,0)\) and \((a,b)\) in \(\mathbb R^2\) (Pythagoras).
Definition: modulus \[ |z|:=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\qquad(z=a+bi,\ a,b\in\mathbb R) \]

is called the modulus of \(z\). It is the distance between \(0\) and \(z\), and

\[ z\cdot\bar z=|z|^2. \]

In words: \(|z|\) is the length of the arrow from the origin to \(z\). It is always a real number \(\ge0\), and it is \(0\) only for \(z=0\). The word comes from the Latin modus, "measure".

Distance between two complex numbers

For \(z=a+bi\) and \(w=c+di\), \(z-w=(a-c)+(b-d)i\), so

\[ |z-w|=\sqrt{(a-c)^2+(b-d)^2}, \]

which is the distance between the points \((a,b)\) and \((c,d)\).

In words: \(|z-w|\) is the distance from \(w\) to \(z\). This one fact turns most "locus" questions (section 12) into geometry: \(|z-1|=4\) is "distance from 1 equals 4", a circle.

Reading the formula
\(a-c\)
the east gap between the two points.
\(b-d\)
the north gap.
\(\sqrt{(a-c)^2+(b-d)^2}\)
Pythagoras: the straight-line distance across the right-angled triangle with those two gaps as its sides.
Trap

\(|a+bi|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\), with no \(i\) inside the root: \(|3+4i|=\sqrt{9+16}=5\), not \(\sqrt{9-16}\). And \(|z|^2=z\bar z\), which is different from \(z^2\) in general: for \(z=i\), \(|z|^2=1\) but \(z^2=-1\).

You can now: plot \(a+bi\) on the Argand diagram; find \(|z|\) and \(|z-w|\) as distances; see \(\bar z\) as the reflection in the Re-axis; and use \(z\bar z=|z|^2\).

4Polar form and the argument ~6 min

What is this?

There are two ways to tell someone where a point is. With the map grid: so far east, so far north. Or the way a lighthouse keeper would: so far away, in this direction. Polar form is the second way: a length \(r\) (the modulus) and an angle \(\theta\) measured anticlockwise from the positive Re-axis (the argument).

Turning a full circle leaves you facing the same way, so angles that differ by whole turns describe the same point. At 0 itself there is no direction to give, so 0 has no argument.

A point of the plane can be given by its \(x\) and \(y\) coordinates, or by its distance from the origin and an angle. The second description is the one that makes multiplication easy (section 9).

Polar form and Arg(z)

From \(\mathbb R^2\) we know that any point \((a,b)\) has the polar form

\[ (a,b)=(r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta), \]

where \(r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\), which translates to \(|z|\) when \(z=a+bi\). The angle \(\theta\), measured from the positive Re-axis, is translated to \(\mathbb C\) as well and called the argument of \(z\), written \(\operatorname{Arg}(z)\).

In words: \(|z|\) says how far \(z\) is from 0; \(\operatorname{Arg}(z)\) says in which direction. Then \(a=|z|\cos\theta\) and \(b=|z|\sin\theta\).

Angles repeat every 2π

If \(\theta\) is allowed to be any real number, moving the angle by \(2k\pi\) (\(k\) an integer) makes no difference: we stay at the same position.

\[ \begin{aligned} (a,b)&=(r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)\\ &=\big(r\cos(\theta+2k\pi),\ r\sin(\theta+2k\pi)\big). \end{aligned} \]

So we usually restrict \(\theta\) to a range of length \(360^\circ\), that is \(2\pi\).

In this course, \(\operatorname{Arg}(z)\in[0,2\pi)\) or \((-\pi,\pi]\). Students can choose the interval they are more comfortable with. A negative argument means the angle is measured in the clockwise direction.

In words: \(\frac{5\pi}3\) and \(-\frac\pi3\) are the same direction; either is an acceptable value of \(\operatorname{Arg}\) for \(1-\sqrt3 i\), as long as you stick to one range.

Polar form

Any nonzero complex \(z\) can be written uniquely as

\[ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta), \]

where \(r=|z|\) and \(\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z)\in[0,2\pi)\).

In words: length times a unit arrow in direction \(\theta\). For \(z=0\) the length is 0 and the angle is meaningless, so 0 has no argument.

Reading the formula
\(r\)
how far from 0: the modulus \(|z|\).
\(\theta\)
which way: the angle anticlockwise from the positive Re-axis.
\(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\)
an arrow of length 1 pointing in direction \(\theta\).
\(r(\cdots)\)
that unit arrow stretched to length \(r\).
Method: finding the polar form (exam type P1.2)
  1. Do: \(r=|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\). This is the "how far" half of the description.
  2. Do: Mark \(z\) on a rough Argand diagram to see its quadrant. The direction depends on the signs of \(a\) and \(b\); the ratio \(\frac ba\) alone cannot tell quadrant I from III (see the trap below).
  3. Do: Find the reference angle \(\alpha\) in \([0,\frac\pi2]\) with \(\tan\alpha=\frac{|b|}{|a|}\) (no calculator: it will be \(0,\frac\pi6,\frac\pi4,\frac\pi3\) or \(\frac\pi2\)). In every quadrant the arrow makes the same right-angled triangle with the Re-axis, with sides \(|a|\) and \(|b|\); \(\alpha\) is its angle at 0.
  4. Do: Quadrant I: \(\theta=\alpha\). II: \(\theta=\pi-\alpha\). III: \(\theta=\pi+\alpha\) (or \(-\pi+\alpha\)). IV: \(\theta=2\pi-\alpha\) (or \(-\alpha\)). This turns "angle from the nearest part of the Re-axis" into "angle anticlockwise from the positive Re-axis".
  5. Do: Write \(z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\) and check: \(r\cos\theta\) should be \(a\), \(r\sin\theta\) should be \(b\). The check walks the description back to grid form; a wrong quadrant shows up as a wrong sign.
Trap: tan⁻¹(b/a) alone is not the argument

\(\tan^{-1}\frac ba\) always gives an angle between \(-\frac\pi2\) and \(\frac\pi2\), so it is wrong in quadrants II and III. For \(z=-1-\sqrt3 i\), \(\frac ba=\sqrt3\) and \(\tan^{-1}\sqrt3=\frac\pi3\), but \(z\) is in quadrant III: \(\operatorname{Arg}(z)=\frac{4\pi}3\) (or \(-\frac{2\pi}3\)).

Example 1

Let \(z=1+i\). Then \(|z|=\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt2\). By inspection (\(z\) is on the diagonal of quadrant I), \(\operatorname{Arg}(z)=45^\circ=\frac\pi4\). Hence

\[ z=1+i=\Big(\sqrt2\cos\frac\pi4\Big)+\Big(\sqrt2\sin\frac\pi4\Big)i. \]
Exercise 1.6

Find the polar form of \(z=1-\sqrt3 i\).

Solution

\(r=\sqrt{1+3}=2\). The point \((1,-\sqrt3)\) is in quadrant IV, with reference angle \(\alpha\) given by \(\tan\alpha=\frac{\sqrt3}{1}\), so \(\alpha=\frac\pi3\). Hence \(\operatorname{Arg}(z)=2\pi-\frac\pi3=\frac{5\pi}3\) (or \(-\frac\pi3\) in the range \((-\pi,\pi]\)):

\[ 1-\sqrt3 i=2\Big(\cos\frac{5\pi}3+i\sin\frac{5\pi}3\Big), \]

or, in the range \((-\pi,\pi]\), \(1-\sqrt3 i=2\big(\cos(-\frac\pi3)+i\sin(-\frac\pi3)\big)\).

Check: \(2\cos\frac{5\pi}3=2\cdot\frac12=1\) and \(2\sin\frac{5\pi}3=2\cdot(-\frac{\sqrt3}2)=-\sqrt3\ \checkmark\)

Going further: the nice angles
\(\theta\)\(0\)\(\frac\pi6\)\(\frac\pi4\)\(\frac\pi3\)\(\frac\pi2\)
\(\cos\theta\)\(1\)\(\frac{\sqrt3}2\)\(\frac{\sqrt2}2\)\(\frac12\)\(0\)
\(\sin\theta\)\(0\)\(\frac12\)\(\frac{\sqrt2}2\)\(\frac{\sqrt3}2\)\(1\)

The numbers that keep appearing in the exam: \(1\pm i=\sqrt2\,(\ldots\tfrac\pi4)\), \(1\pm\sqrt3 i=2\,(\ldots\tfrac\pi3)\), \(\sqrt3\pm i=2\,(\ldots\tfrac\pi6)\), with the angle moved to the right quadrant.

Move z: real and imaginary parts, conjugate, modulus and argument

The blue arrow is \(z\), its length is \(|z|\), and the arc shows \(\operatorname{Arg}(z)\) measured anticlockwise from the positive Re-axis. The red point is \(\bar z\), the mirror image across the Re-axis. The readout gives the argument in both allowed ranges.

You can now: convert \(a+bi\) to \(r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\) with the angle in the right quadrant; use one range, \([0,2\pi)\) or \((-\pi,\pi]\), consistently; and recall the polar forms of \(1\pm i\), \(1\pm\sqrt3i\) and \(\sqrt3\pm i\).

5Division ~3 min

What is this?

Dividing by \(z\) undoes multiplying by \(z\). Dividing by a real number is easy: divide each part. So the trick is to make the bottom of the fraction real. Multiplying \(z\) by its mirror image \(\bar z\) does exactly that, because \(z\bar z=|z|^2\) lies on the road. Multiply the top by \(\bar z\) as well, so the value of the fraction does not change. This needs \(z\ne0\): there is no undoing multiplication by 0.

Division in \(\mathbb C\) is not an operation that exists for points of \(\mathbb R^2\); it is the modulus, through \(z\bar z=|z|^2\), that makes it possible.

Division

Say \(z=a+bi\) and \(w=c+di\), and we want \(\dfrac wz\). Of course \(z\ne0\).

(a) Case \(b=0\) (dividing by a real number \(a\)): divide each part,

\[ \frac wz=\frac{c+di}{a}=\frac ca+\frac da\,i. \]

(b) Case \(b\ne0\): multiply top and bottom by \(\bar z\), which makes the bottom real:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac wz&=\frac{w\cdot\bar z}{z\cdot\bar z}=\frac{(c+di)(a-bi)}{(a+bi)(a-bi)}\\ &=\frac{ca+db}{a^2+b^2}+\frac{da-cb}{a^2+b^2}\,i. \end{aligned} \]

In words: to divide, multiply the top and the bottom by the conjugate of the bottom. The bottom becomes the real number \(|z|^2\), and then you divide each part by it.

Reading the formula
\(\dfrac{w\cdot\bar z}{z\cdot\bar z}\)
top and bottom multiplied by the same non-zero number \(\bar z\), so the value is unchanged.
\(z\cdot\bar z=a^2+b^2\)
the bottom is now the real number \(|z|^2\).
\(\dfrac{ca+db}{a^2+b^2}\)
the real part of \(w\bar z\), divided by \(|z|^2\).
\(\dfrac{da-cb}{a^2+b^2}\,i\)
the imaginary part of \(w\bar z\), divided by the same real number.
Caution

Do not attempt to memorise these formulas; focus on the underlying mechanism instead (multiply by the conjugate of the denominator).

Example 2 \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{1+2i}{1-i}&=\frac{(1+2i)(1+i)}{(1-i)(1+i)}\\ &=\frac{1+2i+i+2i^2}{1+1}\\ &=\frac{-1+3i}{2}=-\frac12+\frac32\,i. \end{aligned} \]

\(1+i\) is the conjugate of the bottom \(1-i\), so the bottom becomes \(1^2+1^2=2\), a real number.

Exercise 1.8

Show that (i) \(|z|^2=z\cdot\bar z\); (ii) \(|z\cdot w|=|z|\cdot|w|\); (iii) \(\overline{\left(\tfrac1z\right)}=\tfrac1{\bar z}\).

Solution

(i) With \(z=a+bi\): \(z\bar z=(a+bi)(a-bi)=a^2+b^2=|z|^2\) (golden formula).

(ii) Using (i) and Exercise 1.3(ii) (\(\overline{zw}=\bar z\bar w\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} |zw|^2&=(zw)\overline{(zw)}=z w\bar z\bar w\\ &=(z\bar z)(w\bar w)=|z|^2|w|^2. \end{aligned} \]

Both \(|zw|\) and \(|z||w|\) are \(\ge0\), so taking square roots gives \(|zw|=|z||w|\).

(iii) \(z\cdot\frac1z=1\). Conjugate both sides with Exercise 1.3(ii): \(\bar z\cdot\overline{\left(\frac1z\right)}=\bar 1=1\). So \(\overline{(1/z)}\) is the number that multiplies \(\bar z\) to give 1, namely \(\frac1{\bar z}\). \(\blacksquare\)

In words: the modulus of a product is the product of the moduli. Together with (iii) this gives \(\left|\frac wz\right|=\frac{|w|}{|z|}\): you can find the modulus of a fraction without dividing first (section 9).

with no multiplication of the brackets.

Similarly \(\left|\dfrac{\sqrt3+2i}{\sqrt5+\sqrt7 i}\right|^{10}=\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{3+4}}{\sqrt{5+7}}\right)^{10}=\left(\dfrac7{12}\right)^5\).

You can now: divide complex numbers by multiplying top and bottom by the conjugate of the bottom; and find the modulus of a product or quotient from the moduli of its pieces, without multiplying out.

6The triangle inequality ~4 min

What is this?

Walk from home along the arrow \(z\), then along the arrow \(w\): you end at \(z+w\). The straight road from home to there has length \(|z+w|\), and your detour had length \(|z|+|w|\). A detour is never shorter than the straight road. It is just as short only when the second leg carries on in the same direction as the first.

Theorem 1.9: triangle inequality

For any two complex numbers \(z\) and \(w\),

\[ |z+w|\le|z|+|w|. \]

In words: put the arrow for \(w\) on the end of the arrow for \(z\); the arrow for \(z+w\) closes the triangle. One side of a triangle is never longer than the other two together. Equality holds exactly when \(z\) and \(w\) point in the same direction (one is a non-negative multiple of the other).

Lengths: \(|z|\) (blue) \(+\ |w|\) (red) is at least \(|z+w|\) (green).

The proof of Theorem 1.9, step by step

Let \(z=a+bi\) and \(w=c+di\). Then \(z+w=(a+c)+(b+d)i\), and it is required to prove

\[ \begin{aligned} &\sqrt{(a+c)^2+(b+d)^2}\\ &\le\sqrt{a^2+b^2}+\sqrt{c^2+d^2}. \end{aligned} \]

Step 1: square both sides. Both sides are \(\ge0\), so the inequality is equivalent to the one between their squares:

\[ \begin{aligned} &(a+c)^2+(b+d)^2\\ &\le a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2+2\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\sqrt{c^2+d^2}. \end{aligned} \]

The left side is \(a^2+2ac+c^2+b^2+2bd+d^2\). Cancel \(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2\) from both sides and divide by 2:

\[ ac+bd\le\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\,\sqrt{c^2+d^2}. \]

Step 2: square again. If \(ac+bd\lt0\), this is true at once (the right side is \(\ge0\)). If \(ac+bd\ge0\), both sides are \(\ge0\), and it is equivalent to

\[ (ac+bd)^2\le(a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2). \]

Expand: the left is \(a^2c^2+2abcd+b^2d^2\); the right is \(a^2c^2+a^2d^2+b^2c^2+b^2d^2\). Cancel \(a^2c^2+b^2d^2\):

\[ 2abcd\le(ad)^2+(bc)^2. \]

Step 3. The final inequality is clearly valid, because

\[ (ad)^2+(bc)^2-2abcd=(ad-bc)^2\ge0. \]

Every step was reversible, so the original inequality holds. \(\blacksquare\) We worked from the inequality we want down to one that is obviously true; because each step can be undone, the chain also reads upwards, from the true one to the one we want.

Trap in this proof

Squaring an inequality is only safe when both sides are \(\ge0\). That is why Step 2 treats \(ac+bd\lt0\) separately; it is easy to skip this case. In a 5-mark "prove" question (May 2025 Q7(a)), say it.

Remark: n numbers

The result extends inductively to

\[ |z_1+\cdots+z_n|\le|z_1|+\cdots+|z_n|. \]

The proof needs the step

\[ |z_1+\cdots+z_n|\le|z_1+\cdots+z_{n-1}|+|z_n|, \]

which is Theorem 1.9 with \(z=z_1+\cdots+z_{n-1}\) and \(w=z_n\). If the statement holds for \(n-1\) numbers, then \(|z_1+\cdots+z_{n-1}|\le|z_1|+\cdots+|z_{n-1}|\), and adding \(|z_n|\) gives it for \(n\) numbers (mathematical induction, as in HKDSE M2).

In words: a path made of several arrows is never shorter than the straight line from its start to its end.

Going further: two useful companions
  • Reverse triangle inequality: \[ \big||z|-|w|\big|\le|z-w|. \] (Apply Theorem 1.9 to \(z=(z-w)+w\) to get \(|z|-|w|\le|z-w|\), and swap \(z,w\).)
  • Parts are shorter than the whole: \(|\operatorname{Re}z|\le|z|\) and \(|\operatorname{Im}z|\le|z|\), since \(\sqrt{a^2}\le\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) (Exercise 1.36, section 18).

Equality cases are often what a locus question is really about: Dec 2021 Q5(c) asks for all \(z\) with \(|z-i|=|z|+1\), which is the equality case of \(|z+(-i)|\le|z|+|-i|\) (solved in the concept checks, section 22).

You can now: state and draw the triangle inequality; prove it by squaring twice, treating \(ac+bd\lt0\) separately; and spot its equality case in a locus question.

7Taylor series and the exponential e^z ~3 min

What is this?

Taylor's theorem writes a smooth function as a recipe: so much of 1, so much of \(x\), so much of \(x^2\), and so on, with the amounts read off from the derivatives at one point. For \(e^x\), \(\cos x\) and \(\sin x\) the infinite sum always settles down to a finite number.

The recipe for \(e^x\) only asks you to multiply, divide by whole numbers and add. You can do all of that with complex numbers too, so the same recipe gives a meaning to \(e^z\) for complex \(z\).

What could \(e^{i\theta}\) mean? "\(e\) multiplied by itself \(i\theta\) times" makes no sense. The answer is to write \(e^x\) as an infinite sum of powers of \(x\); powers of a complex number do make sense, so the same sum can define \(e^z\).

Taylor's theorem

A result from Calculus, proved with the Mean Value Theorem (MATH1851): a "nice" function \(f(x)\) can be rewritten as its Taylor expansion at \(a\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x)&=f(a)+\frac{f'(a)}{1!}(x-a)\\ &\quad+\frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2+\frac{f^{(3)}(a)}{3!}(x-a)^3\\ &\quad+\cdots\\ &=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k. \end{aligned} \]

In words: near \(x=a\), a smooth function is an "infinitely long polynomial" whose coefficients come from its derivatives at \(a\). Here \(f^{(k)}\) is the \(k\)-th derivative and \(k!=1\cdot2\cdots k\) (with \(0!=1\)).

Reading the formula
\(f(a)\)
the value at the starting point.
\(\frac{f'(a)}{1!}(x-a)\)
the slope correction: with the first two terms you have the tangent line.
\(\frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2\)
the bending correction.
\(\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}\)
the amount of the ingredient \((x-a)^k\) in the recipe.
Three expansions at 0

Taking \(a=0\) for \(f(x)=e^x\), \(\cos x\) and \(\sin x\):

\[ e^x=1+\frac x{1!}+\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^3}{3!}+\cdots \] \[ \cos x=1-\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^4}{4!}-\frac{x^6}{6!}\pm\cdots \] \[ \sin x=\frac x{1!}-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\frac{x^5}{5!}\mp\cdots \]

It is a well-known fact that these infinite sums converge to a number (so they make sense) for every \(x\in\mathbb R\).

In words: \(e^x\) uses every power with coefficient \(\frac1{n!}\); \(\cos x\) keeps only the even powers and \(\sin x\) only the odd powers, with signs alternating \(+,-,+,-\).

Where the coefficients come from

For \(e^x\): every derivative is \(e^x\), and \(e^0=1\), so each coefficient is \(\frac1{k!}\).

For \(\cos x\): the derivatives cycle \(\cos,-\sin,-\cos,\sin,\cos,\dots\); at 0 they are \(1,0,-1,0,1,\dots\). So odd powers vanish and even powers alternate in sign.

For \(\sin x\): the derivatives cycle \(\sin,\cos,-\sin,-\cos,\dots\); at 0 they are \(0,1,0,-1,\dots\). So only odd powers appear, with alternating signs.

Definition 1.10: the complex exponential

From Complex Analysis (MATH3403) we know that the series \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{z^n}{n!}\) converges for all \(z\in\mathbb C\). Hence it is reasonable to define it as \(e^z\): for \(z\in\mathbb C\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \exp(z)=e^z&:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^n}{n!}\\ &=1+\frac z{1!}+\frac{z^2}{2!}+\frac{z^3}{3!}+\cdots \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(e^z\) for complex \(z\) is defined by the same series as the real \(e^x\). For real \(z\) it is the usual \(e^x\), so nothing old is broken.

You can now: write the Maclaurin series of \(e^x\), \(\cos x\) and \(\sin x\); and say how \(e^z\) is defined for complex \(z\).

8Euler's formula ~4 min

What is this?

Picture a runner on a circular track of radius 1 centred at 0. She starts at the point 1 and runs anticlockwise. After she has run a distance \(\theta\) along the track, she has turned through \(\theta\) radians, so she is at \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\). Euler's formula says this point is \(e^{i\theta}\). After a distance \(2\pi\), one lap, she is back at the start.

Proposition-Definition 1.11: Euler formula

Let \(\theta\) be a real number. The expression \(e^{i\theta}\) is

\[ e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta. \]

In words: \(e^{i\theta}\) is the point on the unit circle (\(|z|=1\)) at angle \(\theta\). As \(\theta\) grows, \(e^{i\theta}\) walks anticlockwise round the circle, once every \(2\pi\).

Reading the formula
\(\theta\)
how far the runner has gone round the unit circle, anticlockwise from 1 (in radians).
\(\cos\theta\)
her east position.
\(i\sin\theta\)
her north position.
\(e^{i\theta}\)
one short name for that point; it always has modulus 1.

Verification. Put \(z=i\theta\) into Definition 1.10. The powers of \(i\) cycle \(i,-1,-i,1\) (section 1), so

\[ \begin{aligned} e^{i\theta}&=1+\frac{i\theta}{1!}+\frac{(i\theta)^2}{2!}+\frac{(i\theta)^3}{3!}\\ &\quad+\frac{(i\theta)^4}{4!}+\frac{(i\theta)^5}{5!}+\cdots\\ &=1+i\theta-\frac{\theta^2}{2!}-i\frac{\theta^3}{3!}\\ &\quad+\frac{\theta^4}{4!}+i\frac{\theta^5}{5!}-\cdots \end{aligned} \]

Collect the terms without \(i\) and the terms with \(i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} e^{i\theta}&=\Big(1-\frac{\theta^2}{2!}+\frac{\theta^4}{4!}-\frac{\theta^6}{6!}\pm\cdots\Big)\\ &\quad+i\Big(\frac\theta{1!}-\frac{\theta^3}{3!}+\frac{\theta^5}{5!}\mp\cdots\Big)\\ &=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta, \end{aligned} \]

by the two series of section 7.

Adding up the series for eiθ term by term

Each arrow is one term \(\frac{(i\theta)^n}{n!}\); every term turns a right angle from the one before (multiplying by \(i\), section 9). The path of partial sums closes in on the green point \(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\) on the unit circle.

Theorem 1.12

Let \(\theta,\varphi\in\mathbb R\). Then

\[ e^{i\theta+i\varphi}=e^{i\theta}e^{i\varphi}, \]

and so, if \(z,w\) are complex numbers,

\[ e^{z+w}=e^ze^w. \]

In words: the usual index law \(e^{a+b}=e^ae^b\) still holds for complex exponents.

Proof (try it first)

Multiply out with Euler's formula and use the compound angle formulas of HKDSE M2:

\[ \begin{aligned} &e^{i\theta}e^{i\varphi}\\ &=(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)(\cos\varphi+i\sin\varphi)\\ &=(\cos\theta\cos\varphi-\sin\theta\sin\varphi)\\ &\quad+i(\sin\theta\cos\varphi+\cos\theta\sin\varphi)\\ &=\cos(\theta+\varphi)+i\sin(\theta+\varphi)=e^{i(\theta+\varphi)}. \end{aligned} \]

For general complex \(z,w\), one multiplies the two series \(\sum\frac{z^m}{m!}\) and \(\sum\frac{w^n}{n!}\) and groups the terms with \(m+n=N\); by the binomial theorem they add up to \(\frac{(z+w)^N}{N!}\). (Making this rigorous belongs to MATH3403; for this course just use the result.) In particular, for \(z=x+yi\):

\[ e^{x+yi}=e^x e^{iy}=e^x(\cos y+i\sin y), \]

so \(|e^{x+yi}|=e^x\) and the argument of \(e^{x+yi}\) is \(y\) (up to \(2\pi\)).

What is this? (cosine as an average)

Send a twin runner the other way round the track at the same speed: she is at \(e^{-i\theta}\). At every moment the two runners are mirror images in the Re-axis. Their north positions cancel and their east positions agree, so the point halfway between them is on the Re-axis at \(\cos\theta\). Half the gap between them, straight north, is \(\sin\theta\).

Corollary 1.13

For any \(\theta\in\mathbb R\),

\[ \cos\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}2, \] \[ \sin\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}. \]

In words: cosine is the average of the two points at angles \(\theta\) and \(-\theta\); sine comes from their difference.

Proof (try it first)

Euler at \(\theta\) and at \(-\theta\) (cosine is even, sine is odd): \(e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\) and \(e^{-i\theta}=\cos\theta-i\sin\theta\). Adding gives \(e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}=2\cos\theta\); subtracting gives \(e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}=2i\sin\theta\). Divide by \(2\) and by \(2i\). \(\blacksquare\)

Corollary 1.14: multiple angles

For all \(\theta\in\mathbb R\):

(a) \(\cos2\theta=\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta\); (b) \(\sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta\);

(c) \(\cos3\theta=4\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta\); (d) \(\sin3\theta=3\sin\theta-4\sin^3\theta\).

Proof of (a), (b). Use \(e^ze^w=e^{z+w}\) and Proposition 1.11:

\[ \begin{aligned} (e^{i\theta})^2&=e^{i\theta}e^{i\theta}=e^{i\theta+i\theta}\\ &=e^{i(2\theta)}=\cos2\theta+i\sin2\theta. \end{aligned} \]

On the other hand, expanding the square,

\[ \begin{aligned} (e^{i\theta})^2&=(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^2\\ &=(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta)+i(2\sin\theta\cos\theta). \end{aligned} \]

Comparing the real parts and the imaginary parts of the two equations gives (a) and (b).

In words: compute the same power two ways, once with "angles add" and once by expanding the bracket; match real and imaginary parts.

Proof of (c), (d) (try them first)

Write \(c=\cos\theta\), \(s=\sin\theta\). On one hand \((e^{i\theta})^3=e^{3i\theta}=\cos3\theta+i\sin3\theta\). On the other, by the binomial expansion with \(i^2=-1\), \(i^3=-i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} (c+is)^3&=c^3+3c^2(is)+3c(is)^2+(is)^3\\ &=(c^3-3cs^2)+i(3c^2s-s^3). \end{aligned} \]

Real parts:

\[ \begin{aligned} \cos3\theta&=c^3-3c s^2\\ &=c^3-3c(1-c^2)=4c^3-3c. \end{aligned} \]

Imaginary parts:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sin3\theta&=3c^2s-s^3\\ &=3(1-s^2)s-s^3=3s-4s^3.\ \blacksquare \end{aligned} \]

Past papers ask exactly this: Dec 2014 Q5(a) and Dec 2012 Q4(a).

Example: an exact value

These formulas give exact values of sines and cosines. With \(\theta=\frac\pi{12}\) in (a) and \(\sin^2=1-\cos^2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\sqrt3}2=\cos\frac\pi6&=\cos^2\frac\pi{12}-\sin^2\frac\pi{12}\\ &=2\cos^2\frac\pi{12}-1. \end{aligned} \]

Hence (taking the positive root, since \(\frac\pi{12}\) is an acute angle)

\[ \begin{aligned} \cos\Big(\frac\pi{12}\Big)&=\sqrt{\frac12\Big(1+\frac{\sqrt3}2\Big)}\\ &=\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}4. \end{aligned} \]

Euler's powerful formula leads to a few more important results, presented next.

You can now: derive Euler's formula from the series; write \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) through \(e^{\pm i\theta}\); and prove multiple-angle formulas such as \(\cos3\theta=4\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta\) by comparing real and imaginary parts.

9Products and quotients in polar form ~4 min

What is this?

Think of "multiply by \(w\)" as a machine that acts on the whole plane. It stretches every arrow from 0 by the factor \(|w|\) and turns it anticlockwise by the angle \(\operatorname{Arg}w\). Feed in \(z\) and out comes \(zw\): an arrow \(|w|\) times as long as \(z\), turned a further \(\operatorname{Arg}w\). So lengths multiply and angles add.

Dividing by \(w\) (which must not be 0) runs the machine backwards: shrink by \(|w|\) and turn back by \(\operatorname{Arg}w\).

Exponential polar form

Combining the polar form of section 4 with Euler's formula,

\[ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=re^{i\theta}, \]

where \(r=|z|\) and \(\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z)\in[0,2\pi)\); both \(r\) and \(\theta\) are unique when \(z\ne0\). Moreover, one should be aware that

\[ e^{i(\theta+2\pi)}=e^{i\theta}. \]

In words: \(re^{i\theta}\) is just a short way to write the polar form. Adding a full turn to the angle gives the same number, which is what makes \(n\)-th roots work (section 15).

Theorem 1.15

Suppose \(z\cdot w\ne0\). Then for some integer \(k\in\mathbb Z\):

(a) \(|z\cdot w|=|z||w|\) and \(\operatorname{Arg}(z\cdot w)=\operatorname{Arg}(z)+\operatorname{Arg}(w)+k(2\pi)\);

(b) \(\left|\frac zw\right|=\frac{|z|}{|w|}\) and \(\operatorname{Arg}\big(\frac zw\big)=\operatorname{Arg}(z)-\operatorname{Arg}(w)+k(2\pi)\).

In words: to multiply, multiply the lengths and add the angles; to divide, divide the lengths and subtract the angles. The \(k(2\pi)\) only brings the answer back into the chosen range.

Reading the formula
\(|z\cdot w|=|z||w|\)
stretches combine by multiplying: a stretch by 2 and then by 3 is a stretch by 6.
\(\operatorname{Arg}(z)+\operatorname{Arg}(w)\)
turns combine by adding.
\(k(2\pi)\)
whole turns thrown away, to bring the angle back into the chosen range.
\(\frac{|z|}{|w|}\) and \(-\operatorname{Arg}(w)\)
dividing undoes the stretch and the turn of \(w\).

Proof. Write \(z=|z|e^{i\theta}\) and \(w=|w|e^{i\varphi}\), where \(\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z)\) and \(\varphi=\operatorname{Arg}(w)\), both in \([0,2\pi)\). Then by Theorem 1.12,

\[ z\cdot w=|z||w|e^{i\theta}e^{i\varphi}=|z||w|e^{i(\theta+\varphi)}, \]

which means \(z\cdot w\) has modulus \(|z||w|\), that is \(|z\cdot w|=|z||w|\), and

\[ \operatorname{Arg}(z\cdot w)=\theta+\varphi+k(2\pi)\quad\text{for some }k. \]

The choice of \(k\) makes \(\theta+\varphi+k(2\pi)\in[0,2\pi)\). (Since \(\theta+\varphi\in[0,4\pi)\), \(k\) is \(0\) or \(-1\).) The results for \(z/w\) are argued in the same way, with the angles subtracting instead of adding: \(\frac zw=\frac{|z|}{|w|}e^{i(\theta-\varphi)}\).

Example

Find the modulus and argument of \(\dfrac{1+2i}{1-i}\). Some calculation gives \(-\frac12+\frac32 i\), but we can work directly on the fraction and skip those calculations.

\(1+2i=\sqrt5\,e^{i\tan^{-1}(2)}=\sqrt5\,e^{i(63.43^\circ)}\) and \(1-i=\sqrt2\,e^{i(-45^\circ)}\). Thus

\[ \left|\frac{1+2i}{1-i}\right|=\frac{\sqrt5}{\sqrt2}=\sqrt{\frac52} \] \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Arg}\Big(\frac{1+2i}{1-i}\Big)&=63.43^\circ-(-45^\circ)\\ &=108.43^\circ. \end{aligned} \]

Divide the lengths and subtract the angles: the fraction is never turned into \(a+bi\) form.

In fact, \(\left|-\frac12+\frac32 i\right|=\sqrt{\frac14+\frac94}=\sqrt{\frac52}\) and \(\operatorname{Arg}\big(-\frac12+\frac32 i\big)=108.43^\circ\), which agrees.

\(63.43^\circ\) is a calculator value of \(\tan^{-1}2\); exam angles are nicer.

Going further: multiplying by i, by e^{iα}, by a real number
  • \(i=e^{i\pi/2}\): multiplying by \(i\) rotates by \(90^\circ\) anticlockwise and keeps the length. Check: \(i(a+bi)=-b+ai\), and \((a,b)\mapsto(-b,a)\) is the quarter turn.
  • Multiplying by \(e^{i\alpha}\) rotates by \(\alpha\); multiplying by a real \(k\gt0\) scales by \(k\); by \(-1\) rotates by \(\pi\).
  • \(\bar z=re^{-i\theta}\) and \(\frac1z=\frac1re^{-i\theta}\): conjugating negates the angle; taking the reciprocal also inverts the length.

These give the images of sets under maps like \(f(z)=iz\), \(2iz\), \(iz+1\) (sections 13 and 14).

Multiply z by w: lengths multiply, angles add (Theorem 1.15)

Blue: \(z\); red: \(w\); green: \(zw\). Set \(|w|=1\), \(\operatorname{Arg}w=90^\circ\) to see multiplication by \(i\) as a quarter turn. When the angles add past \(360^\circ\), the readout subtracts \(2\pi\) (the \(k\) of Theorem 1.15).

You can now: multiply and divide in polar form by multiplying (dividing) lengths and adding (subtracting) angles; and describe multiplication by \(i\), by \(e^{i\alpha}\) or by a real \(k\) as a turn or a stretch.

10De Moivre's theorem and powers ~3 min

What is this?

Raising \(z\) to the power \(n\) runs the stretch-and-turn machine of section 9 \(n\) times, with the same setting each time. Each run multiplies the length by \(r\) and turns by \(\theta\), so after \(n\) runs the length is \(r^n\) and the total turn is \(n\theta\). That is De Moivre's theorem.

Plotted one after another, the powers climb a spiral staircase: outwards if \(r\gt1\), inwards if \(r\lt1\), round a circle if \(r=1\).

Theorem 1.16: De Moivre

Let \(n\in\mathbb N\) and \(r\gt0\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} &\big(r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\big)^n\\ &=r^n(\cos n\theta+i\sin n\theta) \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} &\big(r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\big)^{-n}\\ &=r^{-n}(\cos n\theta-i\sin n\theta). \end{aligned} \]

In words: to raise to the power \(n\), raise the length to the power \(n\) and multiply the angle by \(n\). A negative power inverts the length and turns the angle the other way.

Reading the formula
\(r^n\)
the stretch, applied \(n\) times.
\(n\theta\)
the turn, applied \(n\) times.
\(r^{-n}\), \(\cos n\theta-i\sin n\theta\)
a negative power runs the machine backwards \(n\) times: shrink, and turn clockwise.

Proof. By Euler's formula and Theorem 1.12 used \(n\) times,

\[ \begin{aligned} \big(r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\big)^n&=r^n(e^{i\theta})^n=r^ne^{i(n\theta)}\\ &=r^n(\cos n\theta+i\sin n\theta). \end{aligned} \]

For the second identity, a simple calculation (golden formula and \(\cos^2+\sin^2=1\)) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} &r^n(\cos n\theta+i\sin n\theta)\cdot r^{-n}(\cos n\theta-i\sin n\theta)\\ &=\cos^2n\theta+\sin^2n\theta=1. \end{aligned} \]

Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} r^{-n}(\cos n\theta-i\sin n\theta)&=\frac1{\big(r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\big)^n}\\ &=:\big(r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\big)^{-n}. \end{aligned} \]
Method: a big power of a + bi (exam type P1.2)
  1. Do: Write the base in polar form \(re^{i\theta}\) (section 4). The machine's setting (stretch \(r\), turn \(\theta\)) can only be read off in polar form.
  2. Do: De Moivre: \((re^{i\theta})^n=r^ne^{in\theta}\). \(n\) runs of the machine: length \(r^n\), total turn \(n\theta\).
  3. Do: Reduce \(n\theta\) by a multiple of \(2\pi\) to a nice angle in \([0,2\pi)\). Whole turns leave you facing the same way, so they can be thrown away.
  4. Do: Convert back: \(r^n(\cos\phi+i\sin\phi)\), and evaluate \(\cos\phi\), \(\sin\phi\). The question usually wants \(a+bi\), and the reduced angle is a nice one whose sine and cosine you know.
Example (Dec 2021 Q5(b))

Find \((1+\sqrt3 i)^{100}\).

\(1+\sqrt3i=2e^{i\pi/3}\). So \((1+\sqrt3i)^{100}=2^{100}e^{i\,100\pi/3}\). Now \(\frac{100\pi}3=32\pi+\frac{4\pi}3\), and \(32\pi\) is 16 full turns, so \(2^{100}\) is the stretch, and \(\frac{100\pi}3\) is 100 turns of \(\frac\pi3\): 16 full laps, which change nothing, plus \(\frac{4\pi}3\).

\[ \begin{aligned} (1+\sqrt3 i)^{100}&=2^{100}\Big(\cos\frac{4\pi}3+i\sin\frac{4\pi}3\Big)\\ &=2^{100}\Big(-\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}2 i\Big)\\ &=-2^{99}-2^{99}\sqrt3\,i. \end{aligned} \]
Example (May 2015 Q4(b))

\(z=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\). Find \(z^n-\frac1{z^n}\).

By De Moivre, \(z^n=\cos n\theta+i\sin n\theta\) and \(z^{-n}=\cos n\theta-i\sin n\theta\). Subtract: \(z^n-\frac1{z^n}=2i\sin n\theta\). (Similarly \(z^n+\frac1{z^n}=2\cos n\theta\).)

Trap: sin θ + i cos θ is not in polar form

De Moivre needs exactly \(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\): cosine first, plus sign. Rewrite other shapes first, using \(\sin\theta=\cos(\frac\pi2-\theta)\) and \(\cos\theta=\sin(\frac\pi2-\theta)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\sin\theta+i\cos\theta\\ &=\cos\Big(\frac\pi2-\theta\Big)+i\sin\Big(\frac\pi2-\theta\Big)\\ &=e^{i(\pi/2-\theta)}. \end{aligned} \]

So \((\sin\theta+i\cos\theta)^n=e^{i(n\pi/2-n\theta)}\), not \(\sin n\theta+i\cos n\theta\). This shape appears in Dec 2022 Q5(c), Dec 2023 Q5(c), May 2014 Q4(b) and May 2026 Q7(c) (section 23).

Powers z, z², z³, … (De Moivre)

Each power turns a further \(\operatorname{Arg}z\) and multiplies the length by \(|z|\): a spiral outwards if \(|z|\gt1\), inwards if \(|z|\lt1\), a walk round the unit circle if \(|z|=1\).

You can now: compute big powers such as \((1+\sqrt3i)^{100}\) with De Moivre; reduce angles by multiples of \(2\pi\); find \(z^n\pm\frac1{z^n}\) on the unit circle; and rewrite shapes like \(\sin\theta+i\cos\theta\) in polar form first.

11Hyperbolic functions, complex sine and cosine ~3 min

What is this?

Section 8 showed that \(\cos\theta\) is the average of two twin runners going opposite ways round the unit circle, \(e^{i\theta}\) and \(e^{-i\theta}\). The hyperbolic functions use the same average-and-difference recipe on \(e^{x}\) and \(e^{-x}\), one growing and one shrinking.

Once \(e^z\) makes sense for every complex \(z\), the same recipes define \(\sin z\), \(\cos z\), \(\sinh z\) and \(\cosh z\) for complex inputs. They agree with the old functions on real inputs, but they can do new things: \(\cosh\) can be 0, and \(\cos\) can be bigger than 1.

Hyperbolic functions

In engineering the following hyperbolic functions are used (MATH1851): for \(x\in\mathbb R\),

\[ \sinh x:=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}2, \] \[ \cosh x:=\frac{e^x+e^{-x}}2. \]

Since \(e^z\) is well defined for all \(z\in\mathbb C\), these expressions also work for complex numbers:

\[ \sinh z:=\frac{e^z-e^{-z}}2, \] \[ \cosh z:=\frac{e^z+e^{-z}}2. \]

\(\sinh\) is the hyperbolic sine ("shine") and \(\cosh\) the hyperbolic cosine ("cosh").

In words: \(\cosh\) is the average of \(e^x\) and \(e^{-x}\); \(\sinh\) is half their difference. Compare Corollary 1.13: the same shapes, without the \(i\)'s.

cosh can be zero in ℂ

Unlike the real case, where \(\cosh x\) is never zero (it is at least 1), this is not true in the complex case:

\[ \begin{aligned} \cosh\Big(i\frac\pi2\Big)&=\frac{e^{i\pi/2}+e^{-i\pi/2}}2\\ &=\frac{i+(-i)}2=0. \end{aligned} \]
Complex sine and cosine

We further observe that, when \(\theta\in\mathbb R\),

\[ e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta, \] \[ e^{-i\theta}=\cos\theta-i\sin\theta, \]

hence also

\[ \sin\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}, \] \[ \cos\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}2. \]

We use these to define complex versions of sine and cosine.

Definition 1.17

For \(z\in\mathbb C\), we define

\[ \sin z=\frac{e^{iz}-e^{-iz}}{2i}, \] \[ \cos z=\frac{e^{iz}+e^{-iz}}2. \]

In words: the formulas that are theorems for real \(\theta\) (Corollary 1.13) become the definitions for complex \(z\). For real \(z\) they give the usual sine and cosine.

Reading the formula
\(e^{iz}\), \(e^{-iz}\)
the two twins, now with a complex input.
\(\dfrac{\cdots+\cdots}2\)
their average: \(\cos z\).
\(\dfrac{\cdots-\cdots}{2i}\)
half their difference, divided by \(i\): \(\sin z\).
real \(z\)
the formulas are Corollary 1.13, so nothing old changes.
Exercise 1.18

Show that for \(x\in\mathbb R\), \(\sin(ix)=i\sinh x\) and \(\cos(ix)=\cosh x\).

Solution

Put \(z=ix\) into Definition 1.17. Then \(iz=i^2x=-x\) and \(-iz=x\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sin(ix)&=\frac{e^{-x}-e^{x}}{2i}\\ &=-\frac1i\cdot\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}2=i\sinh x, \end{aligned} \]

because \(\frac1i=\frac{i}{i^2}=-i\), so \(-\frac1i=i\). And

\[ \cos(ix)=\frac{e^{-x}+e^{x}}2=\cosh x.\quad\blacksquare \]

In words: along the imaginary axis, cosine becomes cosh and sine becomes \(i\) times sinh. So \(|\cos(ix)|=\cosh x\) grows without bound: complex cosine is not stuck between \(-1\) and \(1\).

Exam use (May 2017 Q4(d))

Find \(\cosh^2x-\sinh^2x\), \(x\) real.

\[ \cosh^2x=\frac{e^{2x}+2+e^{-2x}}4, \] \[ \sinh^2x=\frac{e^{2x}-2+e^{-2x}}4, \]

since \(e^xe^{-x}=1\). Subtracting, \(\cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=\frac44=1\). (Compare \(\cos^2+\sin^2=1\).)

Exercise 1.19

For arbitrary complex \(z\), define \(\sin z\) and \(\cos z\) by Definition 1.17. Show that the only values of \(z\) with \(\cos z=0\) and \(\sin z=0\) are the usual real values from trigonometry.

Solution

Read it as two statements: the zeros of \(\cos z\) are \(z=\frac\pi2+k\pi\), and the zeros of \(\sin z\) are \(z=k\pi\) (\(k\in\mathbb Z\)), with no non-real zeros.

cos.

\[ \begin{aligned} \cos z=0&\iff e^{iz}=-e^{-iz}\\ &\iff e^{2iz}=-1 \end{aligned} \]

(multiply by \(e^{iz}\), which is never 0 since \(e^{iz}e^{-iz}=e^0=1\)). Write \(z=x+yi\). Then \(2iz=-2y+2xi\), and by Theorem 1.12

\[ e^{2iz}=e^{-2y}\big(\cos2x+i\sin2x\big). \]

Its modulus is \(e^{-2y}\), and \(|-1|=1\), so \(e^{-2y}=1\), that is \(y=0\): \(z\) is real. Then \(\cos2x+i\sin2x=-1\) gives \(2x=\pi+2k\pi\), so \(x=\frac\pi2+k\pi\).

sin.

\[ \begin{aligned} \sin z=0&\iff e^{iz}=e^{-iz}\\ &\iff e^{2iz}=1. \end{aligned} \]

As before \(e^{-2y}=1\), so \(y=0\), and \(\cos2x+i\sin2x=1\) gives \(2x=2k\pi\), \(x=k\pi\). \(\blacksquare\)

You can now: define \(\sinh\), \(\cosh\), \(\sin z\) and \(\cos z\) through exponentials; show \(\cos(ix)=\cosh x\), \(\sin(ix)=i\sinh x\) and \(\cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=1\); and find all zeros of \(\sin z\) and \(\cos z\).

12Loci: sets of points in the plane ~6 min

What is this?

A locus is the set of points that pass a test. Picture a goat tied to a post at the point \(a\) with a rope of length \(r\). With the rope tight, the goat stands somewhere on the circle \(|z-a|=r\); with the rope allowed to go slack, it can graze anywhere in the disc \(|z-a|\le r\).

The points equally far from two posts form a straight fence between them: the perpendicular bisector. Most locus questions are one of these pictures in disguise.

We begin with two simple examples: a condition on \(z\) picks out a set of points of the complex plane, called a locus (plural loci), and that set is usually a familiar curve or region.

Example: |z| = 1

For \(|x|=1\) with \(x\in\mathbb R\) there are only two possibilities, \(x=1\) or \(x=-1\). The complex case is more "complex"! Writing \(z=x+yi\) with \(x,y\in\mathbb R\),

\[ \begin{aligned} |z|=1&\iff\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=1\\ &\iff x^2+y^2=1^2. \end{aligned} \]

Hence \(|z|=1\) represents the unit circle centred at \(0=0+0i\) on the complex plane: infinitely many solutions.

Example: Re(z²) = 0

Set \(z=x+yi\), \(x,y\in\mathbb R\). Then \(z^2=(x^2-y^2)+i(2xy)\), and so

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Re}(z^2)=x^2-y^2=0&\iff y^2=x^2\\ &\iff y=\pm x, \end{aligned} \]

which gives a pair of straight lines crossing at 0, with slopes \(1\) and \(-1\).

Exercise 1.20

What are the complex numbers \(z\) that satisfy \(|z-1|=4\)? Sketch the graph on the complex plane.

Solution

\(|z-1|\) is the distance from \(z\) to the point 1 (section 3). So the set is the circle with centre 1 and radius 4. Algebraically, with \(z=x+yi\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &|(x-1)+yi|=4\\ &\iff(x-1)^2+y^2=16. \end{aligned} \]

It crosses the Re-axis at \(-3\) and \(5\) and passes through \(1\pm4i\). (Pick it in the figure below.)

Method: describing a locus (exam type P1.4)
  1. Do: Try geometry first. \(|z-a|=r\): circle, centre \(a\), radius \(r\). \(|z-a|\le r\): the closed disc. \(|z-a|=|z-b|\): points equally far from \(a\) and \(b\), the perpendicular bisector of the segment from \(a\) to \(b\). \(\operatorname{Re}z\gt0\), \(\operatorname{Im}z\ge0\): half-planes. \(|z-a|\) is a distance (section 3), so many conditions are already a rope or a fence.
  2. Do: Otherwise substitute \(z=x+yi\). Write each modulus as \(\sqrt{(\text{real part})^2+(\text{imaginary part})^2}\). Rewrite \(\bar z=x-yi\) first. When no picture is obvious, \(x\) and \(y\) turn the condition into an ordinary equation in two real unknowns.
  3. Do: Square to remove roots (both sides must be \(\ge0\)), expand, collect. Squaring removes the square roots in the moduli, and keeps the equation equivalent because both sides are distances.
  4. Do: Recognise the equation: complete the square to get \((x-p)^2+(y-q)^2=R^2\) (circle) or a line \(Ax+By=C\). State the answer as a set in words and as an equation. Completing the square shows where the post is and how long the rope is: the centre and the radius.
  5. Do: Watch for excluded points (a denominator that cannot be 0) and for conditions lost by squaring. A point where a denominator is 0 fails the test even if it satisfies the squared equation.

Example: a ratio that is not 1 (Dec 2013 Q6(a))

Find all \(z\) with \(\dfrac{|\bar z+i|}{|z+i|}=\sqrt2\).

\[ x^2+(1-y)^2=2x^2+2(y+1)^2. \]

Expand: \(x^2+1-2y+y^2=2x^2+2y^2+4y+2\), so \(0=x^2+y^2+6y+1\), and completing the square

\[ x^2+(y+3)^2=8. \]

The first condition is the pair of lines \(y=\pm x\); the second is the circle \((x-1)^2+y^2=1\). Intersect: with \(y^2=x^2\),

\[ \begin{aligned} (x-1)^2+x^2=1&\iff2x^2-2x=0\\ &\iff x=0\ \text{or}\ x=1. \end{aligned} \]

So \(z=0\), \(z=1+i\), \(z=1-i\): three points.

13Images of sets: f(z) = kz + ℓ ~6 min

What is this?

Draw a shape on a transparent sheet laid over the complex plane. A function \(f\) moves every point of the sheet somewhere, and the image set \(f(\mathcal D)\) is where your drawing ends up.

For \(f(z)=kz+\ell\) with real \(k\ne0\) and real \(\ell\), the move is simple: enlarge the sheet from 0 by the factor \(|k|\) (a negative \(k\) also gives it a half turn about 0), then slide it along the Re-axis by \(\ell\). None of these moves bends a line or changes its direction.

Domain and image set

We study the geometry of complex functions \(f:\mathcal D\to\mathbb C\) through the image set \(f(\mathcal D)\). Here \(\mathcal D\subset\mathbb C\) is called the domain of the function, and the image set (also called the range) is

\[ f(\mathcal D):=\{\,f(z)\mid z\in\mathcal D\,\}. \]

In words: apply \(f\) to every point of \(\mathcal D\) and collect the results. \(f(\mathcal D)\) is where the set \(\mathcal D\) "lands".

For a real function \(f:\mathcal D\to\mathbb R\) with \(\mathcal D\subset\mathbb R\), we understand its geometry through its graph, the points \((x,f(x))\) for \(x\in\mathcal D\). If we try the same for complex functions, plotting \((z,f(z))\) for \(z\in\mathcal D\), we need a 4-dimensional graph (two real numbers for \(z\), two for \(f(z)\)) on 2-dimensional paper, which is impossible. So the reasonable way to study a complex function is through image sets: draw \(\mathcal D\) in one plane and \(f(\mathcal D)\) in another. Two examples that are elementary for real functions follow.

Example 1.21 (i): a straight line under kz + ℓ

Let \(f:\mathcal D\to\mathbb C\), \(f(z)=kz+\ell\), where \(k,\ell\) are fixed real numbers and \(k\ne0\). We study \(f(\mathcal D)\) for different domains \(\mathcal D\).

Let \(\mathcal D\) be a non-vertical straight line: for some fixed real \(m\) and \(c\), \(\mathcal D=\{x+yi\mid y=mx+c,\ x,y\in\mathbb R\}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathcal D)&=\{f(z)\mid z\in\mathcal D\}\\ &=\{k(x+yi)+\ell\mid y=mx+c,\\ &\qquad\quad x,y\in\mathbb R\}\\ &=\{(kx+\ell)+(ky)i\mid y=mx+c,\\ &\qquad\quad x,y\in\mathbb R\}. \end{aligned} \]

If we let \(u=kx+\ell\) and \(v=ky\) (the real and imaginary parts of the output), then

\[ f(\mathcal D)=\{u+vi\mid\text{rules describing }u,v\}. \]

We find these rules from the conditions on \(x,y\): \(y=mx+c\) and \(x,y\in\mathbb R\). Since \(kx=u-\ell\),

\[ \begin{aligned} v=ky&=k(mx+c)=m(kx)+kc\\ &=mu+kc-m\ell. \end{aligned} \]

Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathcal D)=\{u+vi\mid\ &v=mu+kc-m\ell,\\ &u,v\in\mathbb R\}. \end{aligned} \]

(Every real \(u\) occurs, because \(x=\frac{u-\ell}k\) can be anything.)

Reading the formula
\(m\)
the slope survives: enlarging and sliding do not turn lines.
\(kc\)
the intercept is enlarged by \(k\), like everything else.
\(-m\ell\)
sliding a line of slope \(m\) to the right by \(\ell\) makes it \(m\ell\) lower above any fixed point of the Re-axis.
Conclusion

For \(\mathcal D\) a straight line with slope \(m\) and intercept \(c\), \(f(\mathcal D)\) is also a straight line with slope \(m\), but with intercept \(kc-m\ell\). Hence, as a set, the effect of \(f\) is to move a (non-vertical) straight line parallel to itself, from intercept \(c\) to intercept \(kc-m\ell\).

In words: multiplying by a real \(k\) stretches the plane from 0 and adding a real \(\ell\) shifts it sideways; neither changes directions, so lines stay parallel to where they were. (Individual points do move along the line: \(f\) scales as well as shifts.)

Example 1.21 (ii), exercise: a vertical line

Solution

\(f(d+yi)=(kd+\ell)+(ky)i\). So \(u=kd+\ell\) is fixed and \(v=ky\) takes every real value as \(y\) does (\(k\ne0\)). Hence

\[ f(\mathcal D)=\{u+vi\mid u=kd+\ell,\ v\in\mathbb R\}, \]

the vertical line \(\operatorname{Re}w=kd+\ell\).

Going further: complex k, as in the past papers

The exam uses \(f(z)=az+b\) with complex \(a\) too: \(iz\), \(2iz\), \(iz+1\), \(z+1+i\). Write \(a=|a|e^{i\alpha}\). Then \(z\mapsto az+b\) is: rotate by \(\alpha\), scale by \(|a|\), then shift by \(b\). Lines go to lines, circles to circles (centre \(p\mapsto ap+b\), radius \(R\mapsto|a|R\)), discs to discs.

14Images of sets: f(z) = 1/z and the polar approach ~10 min

What is this?

\(f(z)=\frac1z\) turns the plane inside out. A point at distance \(r\) from 0 goes to distance \(\frac1r\), and its angle is reflected: \(\theta\) becomes \(-\theta\). Points near 0 are thrown far away, far points are pulled in close to 0, and the unit circle is carried onto itself.

A straight line not through 0 runs off to infinity in both directions, so its image comes back towards 0 from both ends and closes up into a circle through 0. But 0 itself is never reached, because no point of the line is infinitely far away.

Example 1.22 (i): a line not through 0, under 1/z

Let \(f:\mathcal D\to\mathbb C\), \(f(z)=\dfrac1z\). Note: \(0\notin\mathcal D\) is required. Let \(\mathcal D=\{x+yi\mid y=mx+c,\ x,y\in\mathbb R\}\) with fixed \(m,c\in\mathbb R\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathcal D)&=\{f(z)\mid z\in\mathcal D\}\\ &=\left\{\frac1{x+yi}\ \middle|\ y=mx+c,\ x,y\in\mathbb R\right\} \end{aligned} \]

Step 1: real and imaginary parts. Using the division technique of section 5, \(\frac1{x+yi}=\frac{x-yi}{x^2+y^2}\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathcal D)=\Big\{&\Big(\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\Big)+\Big(\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\Big)i\ \Big|\\ &y=mx+c,\ x,y\in\mathbb R\Big\}. \end{aligned} \]

Next set \(u=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\) and \(v=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\), and look for a recognisable rule linking \(u\) and \(v\): rewrite \(f(\mathcal D)=\{u+vi\mid\text{rules describing }u,v\}\). If we understand \(f(\mathcal D)\), we know how \(f\) acts on \(\mathcal D\).

Step 2: express \(x,y\) through \(u,v\). The ingredients are \(u=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\), \(v=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\) and \(y=mx+c\). Notice that

\[ \begin{aligned} u^2+v^2&=\frac{x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}+\frac{y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\\ &=\frac1{x^2+y^2}. \end{aligned} \]

So \(x=u(x^2+y^2)=\dfrac{u}{u^2+v^2}\) and \(y=\dfrac{-v}{u^2+v^2}\). Putting these into \(y=mx+c\) gives

\[ \frac{-v}{u^2+v^2}=\frac{mu}{u^2+v^2}+c. \]

Writing \(x,y\) through \(u,v\) runs the map backwards, so the line's test \(y=mx+c\) can be applied to an output point \(u+vi\).

Step 3: rearrange. Multiply by \(u^2+v^2\):

\[ v=-mu-c(u^2+v^2). \]

Since \(0\notin\mathcal D\), \(c\ne0\) (the line \(y=mx+c\) passes through 0 exactly when \(c=0\)). Dividing by \(c\), the equation becomes

\[ u^2+v^2+\frac mc\,u+\frac1c\,v=0, \]

and by completing the squares

\[ \Big(u+\frac m{2c}\Big)^2+\Big(v+\frac1{2c}\Big)^2=\frac{m^2}{4c^2}+\frac1{4c^2}. \]

Conclusion.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathcal D)=\Big\{u+vi\ \Big|\ &\big(u+\tfrac m{2c}\big)^2+\big(v+\tfrac1{2c}\big)^2\\ &=\tfrac{m^2+1}{4c^2},\ u,v\in\mathbb R\Big\}. \end{aligned} \]

So \(f\) transforms the straight line \(y=mx+c\) into a circle with centre \(-\frac m{2c}-\frac1{2c}i\) and radius \(\sqrt{\frac{m^2+1}{4c^2}}\).

Reading the formula
\(-\frac m{2c}-\frac1{2c}i\)
the centre of the image circle.
\(\frac{m^2+1}{4c^2}\)
the radius squared. It equals the squared distance from the centre to 0, so the circle passes through 0 (but 0 itself is missing, see the note below).
\(c\ne0\)
the line misses 0. A line through 0 would go to a line through 0 instead.

Note. The circle found passes through \(0\): put \(u=v=0\) into the equation and both sides equal \(\frac{m^2+1}{4c^2}\). But \(0\) is never a value of \(\frac1z\) (\(\frac1z=0\) has no solution). So the image is the circle with the point 0 removed. (Step 2 divided by \(u^2+v^2\), which silently assumed \((u,v)\ne(0,0)\).) Every other point of the circle is reached, because \(z=\frac1w\) gives back a point of the line. So \(f(\mathcal D)=\) the circle minus \(\{0\}\). The far ends of the line, where \(|z|\to\infty\), are what approach \(0\).

Example 1.22 (ii), exercise: a vertical line

Redo with \(\mathcal D\) a vertical line, \(\mathcal D=\{d+yi\mid y\in\mathbb R\}\).

Solution

If \(d=0\), \(\mathcal D\) contains 0, which is not allowed; on the imaginary axis without 0, \(\frac1{yi}=-\frac1yi\), so the image is again the imaginary axis without 0.

If \(d\ne0\): \(u=\frac{d}{d^2+y^2}\), \(v=\frac{-y}{d^2+y^2}\). As in Step 2, \(x=d=\frac u{u^2+v^2}\), so \(u^2+v^2=\frac ud\), that is

\[ \Big(u-\frac1{2d}\Big)^2+v^2=\frac1{4d^2}. \]

The image is the circle with centre \(\frac1{2d}\) and radius \(\frac1{2|d|}\), again without the point 0. For example, the line \(\operatorname{Re}z=1\) goes to the circle \(|w-\frac12|=\frac12\) minus \(0\).

Example 1.22 (iii): a circle centred at 0

What if \(\mathcal D\) is the circle centred at \(0+0i\) with radius \(r\gt0\), \(\mathcal D=\{x+yi\mid x^2+y^2=r^2,\ x,y\in\mathbb R\}\)? We could use the "\(u,v\) approach", but it does not look pleasant. Another approach: rewrite \(\mathcal D=\{re^{i\theta}\mid\theta\in\mathbb R\}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathcal D)&=\left\{\frac1{re^{i\theta}}\ \middle|\ \theta\in\mathbb R\right\}\\ &=\left\{\frac1re^{i(-\theta)}\ \middle|\ \theta\in\mathbb R\right\}\\ &=\left\{\frac1re^{i\varphi}\ \middle|\ \varphi\in\mathbb R\right\}, \end{aligned} \]

where we set \(\varphi=-\theta\) (as \(\theta\) runs over all reals, so does \(\varphi\)). Clearly \(f(\mathcal D)\) is the circle with centre \(0+0i\) and radius \(\frac1r\). So \(f\) either expands or shrinks \(\mathcal D\).

In words: \(\frac1z\) inverts the length and reflects the angle. A circle \(|z|=r\) goes to the circle \(|w|=\frac1r\), traced the opposite way round.

Example 1.22 (iv), exercise: the hyperbola xy = 1 (asked as May 2025 Q7(b))

What is \(f(\mathcal D)\) if \(\mathcal D=\{x+yi\mid xy=1,\ x,y\in\mathbb R\}\)?

Solution

Note \(0\notin\mathcal D\) (since \(0\cdot0\ne1\)). As in Step 1, \(u=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\), \(v=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\), and as in Step 2, \(x=\frac u{u^2+v^2}\), \(y=\frac{-v}{u^2+v^2}\). Put these into \(xy=1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{-uv}{(u^2+v^2)^2}=1\\ &\iff(u^2+v^2)^2=-uv, \end{aligned} \]

with \((u,v)\ne(0,0)\). So

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathcal D)=\{u+vi\mid\ &(u^2+v^2)^2=-uv,\\ &u+vi\ne0\}. \end{aligned} \]

What the graph looks like. In polar form \(w=\rho e^{i\varphi}\): \(u^2+v^2=\rho^2\) and \(uv=\rho^2\cos\varphi\sin\varphi=\frac{\rho^2}2\sin2\varphi\), so the equation is \(\rho^2=-\frac12\sin2\varphi\). This is a figure-of-eight curve (a lemniscate) through 0, with its two loops in the 2nd and 4th quadrants (where \(\sin2\varphi\le0\)), the farthest points at distance \(\frac1{\sqrt2}\) in the directions \(\varphi=\frac{3\pi}4\) and \(\frac{7\pi}4\), and with the point 0 itself missing. It is symmetric about the lines \(v=-u\) and \(v=u\), just as the hyperbola is. Each branch of the hyperbola gives one loop: \(x,y\gt0\) gives \(u\gt0\), \(v\lt0\).

Two approaches to f(𝒟)
  • Cartesian. Let \(z=x+yi\), \(x,y\) real. Then \[ \begin{aligned} f(z)&=f(x+yi)\\ &=u(x,y)+iv(x,y)=u+vi, \end{aligned} \] and we relate \(u,v\) through the given conditions on \(x,y\) in \(\mathcal D\). Best for lines and for circles not centred at 0.
  • Polar. Write \(z=re^{i\theta}\), \(r,\theta\) real. Then \(f(z)=f(re^{i\theta})=t(r,\theta)e^{i\varphi(r,\theta)}=te^{i\varphi}\), and we relate \(t,\varphi\) through the given conditions on \(r,\theta\) in \(\mathcal D\). Best for circles centred at 0, discs, sectors, and maps like \(z^n\), \(\frac1z\), \(\frac1{z^2}\).

15Roots of unity and n-th roots ~8 min

What is this?

Picture a wheel with \(n\) evenly spaced spokes, one of them pointing at 1. Going from one spoke to the next is a turn of \(\frac{2\pi}n\). Do that turn \(n\) times and you have gone exactly once round, back to 1. The spoke \(k\) steps along has angle \(\frac{2k\pi}n\); turning by it \(n\) times gives \(k\) full laps, again back to 1. So every spoke solves \(z^n=1\), and there are \(n\) different spokes.

For \(z^n=w\) the wheel is the same shape, but its radius is \(|w|^{1/n}\) and it is turned so that the first spoke has angle \(\frac{\operatorname{Arg}w}n\).

z³ = 1

In real numbers, \(x^3=1\) has only one solution, \(x=1\). This is seen by factoring:

\[ \begin{aligned} &x^3-1=0\\ &\iff(x-1)(x^2+x+1)=0. \end{aligned} \]

A product is zero when one factor is zero: \(x-1=0\) or \(x^2+x+1=0\). The latter has no real roots, as its discriminant is \(b^2-4ac=1-4\lt0\). However, the quadratic formula gives

\[ z=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1-4}}2=-\frac12\pm\frac{\sqrt3}2\,i \]

as the complex roots of \(z^2+z+1=0\), reading \(\sqrt{-3}\) as \(\sqrt3\,i\). So the equation \(z^3=1\) has in fact 3 roots: one real and two non-real complex roots. (Compare Exercise 1.30: the two non-real roots are conjugates.)

z⁴ = 1

\(z^4=1\) is easy to handle:

\[ z^4-1=0\iff(z^2-1)(z^2+1)=0, \]

with the four solutions \(z=\pm1,\pm i\). Questions to consider:

  1. Does \(z^n=1\) have \(n\) roots?
  2. Are they all distinct, or could they be repeated?
  3. Does every polynomial equation of degree \(n\) have \(n\) roots?
The n-th roots of unity

Question 3 has a known and affirmative answer, the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: a polynomial equation of degree \(n\) has exactly \(n\) complex roots, counted with repetition. For Questions 1 and 2 there are constructive answers:

1. The \(n\) complex numbers

\[ z_k=e^{i\left(\frac{2k\pi}n\right)},\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots,n-1, \]

satisfy \(z^n=1\). Reason: \(\big(e^{i(2k\pi/n)}\big)^n=e^{i(2k\pi)}=1\).

2. These \(n\) roots are distinct. Reason: they lie at different positions on the unit circle (their angles \(0,\frac{2\pi}n,\dots,\frac{2(n-1)\pi}n\) are different and all in \([0,2\pi)\)).

Note. The \(n\) distinct roots of \(z^n=1\) are called the \(n\)-th roots of unity.

In words: the \(n\)-th roots of 1 are \(n\) equally spaced points on the unit circle, starting at 1, a turn of \(\frac{2\pi}n\) apart: the corners of a regular \(n\)-gon.

The six 6th roots of unity \(z_k=e^{ik\pi/3}\).

Method: all n-th roots of any w ≠ 0 (exam type P1.3; Exercise 1.32)

The same idea solves \(z^n=w\) for any nonzero \(w\). Write \(w=Re^{i\phi}\) and look for \(z=re^{i\theta}\). By De Moivre, \(z^n=r^ne^{in\theta}\), so we need \(r^n=R\) and \(n\theta=\phi+2k\pi\) (angles equal up to full turns, section 4):

\[ z_k=R^{1/n}\,e^{i\left(\frac{\phi+2k\pi}n\right)}, \] \[ k=0,1,\dots,n-1. \]
  1. Do: Polar form of the right side: \(w=Re^{i\phi}\). The length and angle of \(w\) fix the size of the wheel and where its first spoke points.
  2. Do: Modulus of every root: the real positive root \(R^{1/n}\). All the spokes have the same length, and its \(n\)-th power must be \(R\).
  3. Do: First angle \(\frac\phi n\); then add \(\frac{2\pi}n\) each time, \(n\) times in all. \(n\) steps of \(\frac{2\pi}n\) make one full turn, which disappears when you raise to the power \(n\).
  4. Do: Convert each root to \(a+bi\) if asked; there must be exactly \(n\) different answers. There are exactly \(n\) spokes: fewer answers means one is missing, more means a repeat.

In words: one root is found by taking the \(n\)-th root of the length and dividing the angle by \(n\); the others are that root turned by multiples of \(\frac{2\pi}n\). They form a regular \(n\)-gon centred at 0.

Reading the formula
\(R^{1/n}\)
every root has the same length, the positive real \(n\)-th root of \(|w|\): the radius of the wheel.
\(\frac\phi n\)
the first spoke: the angle that, taken \(n\) times, gives \(\operatorname{Arg}w\).
\(+\frac{2k\pi}n\)
the other spokes, \(k\) equal steps further on. Taken \(n\) times this is \(k\) full turns, which do not change \(z^n\).
\(k=0,\dots,n-1\)
\(k=n\) would land back on the first spoke, so these are all the roots.
Trap: forgetting the 2kπ

Dividing only \(\phi\) by \(n\) gives one root, not \(n\) of them. The \(2k\pi\) must be added before dividing by \(n\): the roots of \(z^4=-16\) are \(2e^{i(\pi+2k\pi)/4}\), with angles \(\frac\pi4,\frac{3\pi}4,\frac{5\pi}4,\frac{7\pi}4\), not \(\frac\pi4+2k\pi\) (which is the same point every time).

Example: z⁴ = −16

\(-16=16e^{i\pi}\). Modulus of each root: \(16^{1/4}=2\). Angles \(\frac{\pi+2k\pi}4\): \(\frac\pi4,\frac{3\pi}4,\frac{5\pi}4,\frac{7\pi}4\). So \(-16\) points along the negative Re-axis (angle \(\pi\)); the first spoke has a quarter of that angle, and the others follow a quarter turn apart.

\[ \begin{aligned} z&=2e^{i\pi/4}=\sqrt2+\sqrt2 i,\quad -\sqrt2+\sqrt2 i,\\ &\quad -\sqrt2-\sqrt2 i,\quad \sqrt2-\sqrt2 i. \end{aligned} \]

Check one: \((\sqrt2+\sqrt2i)^2=2+4i-2=4i\), and \((4i)^2=-16\ \checkmark\)

Going further: square roots in a + bi form, and quadratics with complex coefficients

For square roots, the algebraic way avoids trigonometry. To solve \((p+qi)^2=a+bi\) with \(p,q\) real: compare parts, \(p^2-q^2=a\) and \(2pq=b\); taking moduli also gives \(p^2+q^2=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\). Add and subtract to get \(p^2\) and \(q^2\), then choose signs so that \(2pq=b\). Example: \(\sqrt{3+4i}\): \(p^2-q^2=3\), \(p^2+q^2=5\), so \(p^2=4\), \(q^2=1\), and \(pq=2\gt0\): the square roots are \(\pm(2+i)\).

The quadratic formula \(z=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\) still works when \(a,b,c\) are complex: "\(\pm\sqrt{\ \ }\)" means "the two square roots" of the complex discriminant. Example: \(z^2-3z+(3-i)=0\). The discriminant is \(9-4(3-i)=-3+4i\). Its square roots: \(p^2-q^2=-3\), \(p^2+q^2=5\), so \(p^2=1\), \(q^2=4\), \(pq=2\gt0\): \(\pm(1+2i)\). Hence

\[ z=\frac{3\pm(1+2i)}2=2+i\ \text{ or }\ 1-i. \]

Check: sum of roots \(3\), product \((2+i)(1-i)=3-i\ \checkmark\) (they should be \(-\frac ba\) and \(\frac ca\)). Note that the roots are not conjugates here: the conjugate pairs of Exercise 1.30 need real coefficients.

The n-th roots of w on the complex plane

The roots \(z_0,\dots,z_{n-1}\) form a regular \(n\)-gon on the circle of radius \(|w|^{1/n}\); \(z_0\) (blue) has angle \(\frac{\operatorname{Arg}w}n\). The grey point is \(w\) itself (shown when it fits).

16Primitive roots of unity ~5 min

What is this?

Back to the wheel with \(n\) spokes. Start on the spoke at 1 and keep hopping the same number of spokes, \(k\), each time: that is multiplying by \(w=z_k\) again and again. Sometimes you land on every spoke before you are back at the start; sometimes you go round a smaller pattern and miss some.

With 4 spokes and hops of 1 you visit all four; with hops of 2 you only bounce between two. A primitive root is a hop that visits every spoke.

Within roots of unity there is the notion of a primitive root of unity: a root of unity that can generate all the other roots of unity, in the following sense.

Definition 1.23

Let \(w\in\mathbb C\), and define

  • \(\langle w\rangle:=\{w^0,w^1,w^2,\dots\}\), the set generated by \(w\);
  • for a non-empty subset \(S\subset\mathbb C\), \(w\in S\) is a generator of \(S\) if \(\langle w\rangle=S\);
  • if \(S\) is the set of all \(n\)-th roots of unity, \(S=\{z\in\mathbb C\mid z^n=1\}\), then \(w\) is a primitive root of unity (a primitive \(n\)-th root of unity) if \(\langle w\rangle=S\).

In words: keep multiplying \(w\) by itself (\(1,w,w^2,w^3,\dots\)) and collect every number you meet. If you meet all \(n\) of the \(n\)-th roots of unity, \(w\) is primitive. As a set, repeated elements count once, so \(\{1,1,1,\dots\}=\{1\}\).

Example 1.24

The roots of \(z^4=1\) are \(\pm1,\pm i\), so \(S=\{z\mid z^4=1\}=\{1,i,-1,-i\}\). Calculate \(\langle w\rangle\) for each \(w\in S\):

  • \(\langle1\rangle=\{1,1,1,1,\dots\}=\{1\}\ne S\);
  • \(\langle i\rangle=\{1,i,-1,-i,\dots\}=S\);
  • \[ \begin{aligned} \langle-1\rangle&=\{1,-1,1,-1,\dots\}\\ &=\{1,-1\}\ne S; \end{aligned} \]
  • \(\langle-i\rangle=\{1,-i,-1,i,\dots\}=S\).

Hence \(i\) and \(-i\) are the primitive 4th roots of unity.

Going further: which roots are primitive?

Write the \(n\)-th roots of unity as \(z_k=e^{2k\pi i/n}\) (\(k=0,\dots,n-1\)). Then \(z_k^j=e^{2jk\pi i/n}=z_{jk\bmod n}\): the powers of \(z_k\) step round the \(n\)-gon \(k\) corners at a time. They visit every corner exactly when \(k\) and \(n\) have no common factor other than 1 (\(\gcd(k,n)=1\)). So

\[ \begin{aligned} &z_k=e^{2k\pi i/n}\ \text{is primitive}\\ &\iff\gcd(k,n)=1. \end{aligned} \]

Example (Dec 2025 Q17, MC): which is a primitive 6th root of unity, \(e^{i2\pi/3}\), \(e^{i\pi}\), \(e^{i4\pi/3}\) or \(e^{i5\pi/3}\)? Write each as \(e^{2k\pi i/6}\): \(k=2,3,4,5\). Only \(k=5\) has \(\gcd(5,6)=1\), so the answer is \(e^{i5\pi/3}\). (Check: \(e^{i2\pi/3}\) only generates the three cube roots of unity.) The number of primitive \(n\)-th roots is the number of \(k\) in \(1,\dots,n\) with \(\gcd(k,n)=1\) (Exercise 1.33(b), section 18).

Going further: simplifying expressions in a root of unity w

If \(w^n=1\), many past-paper expressions collapse with four facts:

  1. Do: Reduce powers: \(w^m=w^{r}\), where \(r\) is the remainder of \(m\) on division by \(n\) (since \(w^n=1\)). E.g. for \(w^3=1\), \(w^{71}=w^{2}\) because \(71=3\cdot23+2\). \(n\) hops of \(w\) bring you back to the start, so whole blocks of \(n\) hops can be dropped.
  2. Do: Reciprocal = conjugate: \(|w|^n=|w^n|=1\), so \(|w|=1\), and then \(w\bar w=|w|^2=1\): \(\frac1w=\bar w=w^{n-1}\). \(w\) is a spoke of length 1, so undoing its turn is the same as turning by the mirror angle.
  3. Do: The sum vanishes: if \(w\ne1\), then \(1+w+w^2+\cdots+w^{n-1}=0\) (Exercise 1.33(c)): from \[ \begin{aligned} &(w-1)(1+w+\cdots+w^{n-1})\\ &=w^n-1=0 \end{aligned} \] and \(w-1\ne0\). Multiplying the sum by \(w\) moves each term on to the next one (and \(w^n\) back to 1), so the sum is unchanged by a turn that is not a whole turn (\(w\ne1\)); the only point such a turn about 0 leaves in place is 0 itself.
  4. Do: Case split: "\(w\) is a root of \(z^n=1\)" includes \(w=1\). Unless the question says \(w\ne1\), give the answer for \(w=1\) and for \(w\ne1\) separately. \(w=1\) is a spoke too, and there the sum is \(n\), not 0.

May 2017 Q4(b): \(w\) a root of \(z^3=1\); find \(\frac1w+\frac1{w^2}\). If \(w=1\): \(1+1=2\). If \(w\ne1\): \(\frac1w=w^2\) and \(\frac1{w^2}=w\), so the sum is \(w^2+w=-1\) (fact 3 with \(n=3\)).

Does w = z_k generate all the n-th roots? (Definition 1.23)

The path \(1\to w\to w^2\to\cdots\) jumps \(k\) corners at a time. Filled points are in \(\langle w\rangle\); hollow points are never reached. Try \(n=4\) with \(k=1,2,3\) (Example 1.24), and \(n=6\) with \(k=5\).

You can now: decide whether \(w\) generates all the \(n\)-th roots by listing its powers; pick out the primitive roots with \(\gcd(k,n)=1\); and simplify expressions in a root \(w\), treating \(w=1\) separately.

Exercise 1.26

Solve: (i) \(x^2+ix+1=0\); (ii) \(x^4+x^2+1=0\); (iii) \(x^2+2ix-1=0\); (iv) the system \(ix-(1+i)y=3\), \((2+i)x+iy=4\); (v) \(x^3-x^2-x-2=0\).

Solution

(i) Quadratic formula: discriminant \(i^2-4=-5\), whose square roots are \(\pm\sqrt5\,i\). So \(x=\frac{-i\pm\sqrt5 i}2=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt5}2\,i\): two purely imaginary roots.

(ii) A quadratic in \(x^2\): \(x^2=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt3i}2\). These are \(e^{\pm i2\pi/3}\) (the non-real cube roots of unity, section 15). Their square roots (section 15) are \(\pm e^{i\pi/3}\) and \(\pm e^{-i\pi/3}\), so the four solutions are

\[ x=\pm\Big(\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}2i\Big),\quad\pm\Big(\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}2i\Big). \]

(Check: \(x^4+x^2+1=\frac{x^6-1}{x^2-1}\), so the solutions are the 6th roots of unity other than \(\pm1\).)

(iii)

\[ \begin{aligned} x^2+2ix-1&=x^2+2ix+i^2\\ &=(x+i)^2. \end{aligned} \]

So \(x=-i\) is a repeated root. (The discriminant \((2i)^2+4=0\).)

(iv) From the second equation, \(iy=4-(2+i)x\); multiply by \(-i\) (since \(\frac1i=-i\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} y&=-4i+(2+i)ix\\ &=-4i+(-1+2i)x. \end{aligned} \]

Substitute into the first:

\[ ix-(1+i)\big(-4i+(-1+2i)x\big)=3. \]

Now \((1+i)(-4i)=4-4i\) and \((1+i)(-1+2i)=-3+i\), so \(ix-4+4i+3x-ix=3\), that is \(3x=7-4i\):

\[ x=\frac73-\frac43i, \] \[ \begin{aligned} y&=-4i+(-1+2i)\frac{7-4i}3\\ &=\frac13+2i. \end{aligned} \]

(v) Try small integers: \(x=2\) gives \(8-4-2-2=0\). Divide: \(x^3-x^2-x-2=(x-2)(x^2+x+1)\). So \(x=2\) or \(x=-\frac12\pm\frac{\sqrt3}2i\).

Exercise 1.27

Describe the set of all complex \(z\) such that (i) \(\bar z=-z\); (ii) \(\bar z=z^{-1}\); (iii) \(|z-a|=|z-b|\); (iv) \(|z-a|+|z-b|=c\); (v) \(|z|\lt1-\operatorname{Re}(z)\).

Solution

(i) \(x-yi=-x-yi\iff x=0\): the imaginary axis (the purely imaginary numbers, and 0).

(ii) Needs \(z\ne0\); then \(\bar z=\frac1z\iff z\bar z=1\iff|z|=1\): the unit circle.

(iii) (\(a\ne b\)) The points equally far from \(a\) and \(b\): the perpendicular bisector of the segment from \(a\) to \(b\). (If \(a=b\), every \(z\).)

(iv) (real \(c\)) The points whose distances to \(a\) and \(b\) add up to \(c\). By the triangle inequality \(|a-b|\le|z-a|+|z-b|\), so: if \(c\gt|a-b|\), an ellipse with foci \(a\) and \(b\); if \(c=|a-b|\), the segment from \(a\) to \(b\); if \(c\lt|a-b|\), no points at all.

(v) With \(z=x+yi\): \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\lt1-x\). The right side must be positive. Squaring, \(x^2+y^2\lt1-2x+x^2\), that is \(y^2\lt1-2x\), or \(x\lt\frac{1-y^2}2\) (which already forces \(x\lt\frac12\lt1\)). The set is the inside of the parabola \(x=\frac{1-y^2}2\) (opening to the left, vertex \(\frac12\)), boundary excluded. (Geometrically: points closer to 0 than to the vertical line \(\operatorname{Re}z=1\).)

Exercise 1.28

Prove that \(|z|=|\bar z|\), and that \(\operatorname{Re}(z)=\frac{z+\bar z}2\) and \(\operatorname{Im}(z)=\frac{z-\bar z}{2i}\).

Solution

With \(z=a+bi\): \(|\bar z|=\sqrt{a^2+(-b)^2}=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}=|z|\). Next \(z+\bar z=2a\), so \(\frac{z+\bar z}2=a=\operatorname{Re}z\); and \(z-\bar z=2bi\), so \(\frac{z-\bar z}{2i}=b=\operatorname{Im}z\). \(\blacksquare\)

Exercise 1.29

Prove that \(|z+w|^2+|z-w|^2=2(|z|^2+|w|^2)\), and interpret this statement geometrically.

Solution

Use \(|u|^2=u\bar u\) and Exercise 1.3:

\[ \begin{aligned} |z+w|^2&=(z+w)(\bar z+\bar w)\\ &=|z|^2+z\bar w+\bar zw+|w|^2, \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} |z-w|^2&=(z-w)(\bar z-\bar w)\\ &=|z|^2-z\bar w-\bar zw+|w|^2. \end{aligned} \]

Adding, the middle terms cancel: the sum is \(2|z|^2+2|w|^2\). \(\blacksquare\)

Geometry: the parallelogram with sides \(z\) and \(w\) has diagonals \(z+w\) and \(z-w\). The statement is the parallelogram law: the squares of the two diagonals add up to the squares of all four sides.

Exercise 1.30

(a) Prove that if \(a_0,\dots,a_{n-1}\) are real and \(a+bi\) (\(a,b\) real) satisfies \(z^n+a_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0=0\), then \(a-bi\) also satisfies it. (So the non-real roots of such an equation come in pairs, and the number of non-real roots is even.)

(b) Conclude that \(z^n+a_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0\) is divisible by \(z^2-2az+(a^2+b^2)\), whose coefficients are real.

Solution

(a) Let \(\alpha=a+bi\) and \(p(z)=z^n+a_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0\), with \(p(\alpha)=0\). Conjugate both sides. By Exercise 1.3 (conjugate of sums and products) and \(\bar a_k=a_k\) (real, Exercise 1.5(ii)):

\[ \begin{aligned} 0=\overline{p(\alpha)}&=\bar\alpha^n+a_{n-1}\bar\alpha^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0\\ &=p(\bar\alpha). \end{aligned} \]

So \(\bar\alpha=a-bi\) is a root too. Non-real roots pair off as \(\alpha,\bar\alpha\) (different when \(b\ne0\)), so there is an even number of them.

(b) Suppose \(b\ne0\). By the factor theorem, \(p(z)=(z-\alpha)q(z)\). Then \(0=p(\bar\alpha)=(\bar\alpha-\alpha)q(\bar\alpha)\) with \(\bar\alpha-\alpha=-2bi\ne0\), so \(q(\bar\alpha)=0\) and \(q(z)=(z-\bar\alpha)r(z)\). Hence \(p(z)\) is divisible by

\[ \begin{aligned} &(z-\alpha)(z-\bar\alpha)\\ &=z^2-(\alpha+\bar\alpha)z+\alpha\bar\alpha\\ &=z^2-2az+(a^2+b^2). \end{aligned} \]

Example: \(z^3-1\) has the root \(-\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}2i\), and \(z^3-1=(z-1)(z^2+z+1)\), where \(z^2+z+1=z^2-2(-\frac12)z+(\frac14+\frac34)\).

(a) Let \(c\) be an integer which is not the square of another integer. For integers \(a,b\), define the conjugate of \(a+b\sqrt c\) as \(\overline{a+b\sqrt c}:=a-b\sqrt c\). Show that this is well defined, by showing that a number can be written as \(a+b\sqrt c\), with \(a,b\) integers, in only one way.

(b) Show that for all \(\alpha,\beta\) of the form \(a+b\sqrt c\): \(\bar{\bar\alpha}=\alpha\); \(\bar\alpha=\alpha\) if and only if \(\alpha\) is an integer; \(\overline{\alpha+\beta}=\bar\alpha+\bar\beta\); \(\overline{(-\alpha)}=-\bar\alpha\); \(\overline{\alpha\cdot\beta}=\bar\alpha\cdot\bar\beta\); and \(\overline{\alpha^{-1}}=(\bar\alpha)^{-1}\) if \(\alpha\ne0\).

(b) These are the same calculations as Exercises 1.3 and 1.5, with \(\sqrt c\) playing the role of \(i\) and \((\sqrt c)^2=c\) the role of \(i^2=-1\). For the product:

\[ \begin{aligned} &(a+b\sqrt c)(a'+b'\sqrt c)\\ &=(aa'+bb'c)+(ab'+a'b)\sqrt c; \end{aligned} \]

replacing \(\sqrt c\) by \(-\sqrt c\) on both sides gives \(\overline{\alpha\beta}=\bar\alpha\bar\beta\). \(\bar\alpha=\alpha\iff b=-b\iff b=0\), an integer. Exercise 1.32

Exercise 1.34

(a) Prove that if \(z_1,\dots,z_k\) lie on one side of some straight line through 0, then \(z_1+\cdots+z_k\ne0\). [Hint: obvious from the geometric meaning of addition, but an analytic proof is also easy: the assertion is clear if the line is the real axis, and a trick reduces the general case to this one.]

(b) Show further that \(z_1^{-1},\dots,z_k^{-1}\) lie on one side of a straight line through 0, so that \(z_1^{-1}+\cdots+z_k^{-1}\ne0\).

Solution

(a) Real axis case: if all \(z_j\) are strictly above the real axis, \(\operatorname{Im}z_j\gt0\), so \(\operatorname{Im}(z_1+\cdots+z_k)\gt0\) and the sum is not 0 (same for "all below"). (b) After the rotation, \(\operatorname{Im}(e^{-i\alpha}z_j)\gt0\) for all \(j\) (say). Now \(\frac1{z_j}=\frac{\bar z_j}{|z_j|^2}\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Im}\big(e^{i\alpha}\bar z_j\big)&=\operatorname{Im}\big(\overline{e^{-i\alpha}z_j}\big)\\ &=-\operatorname{Im}(e^{-i\alpha}z_j)\lt0. \end{aligned} \]

Dividing by the positive \(|z_j|^2\) keeps the sign: \(\operatorname{Im}\big(e^{i\alpha}z_j^{-1}\big)\lt0\) for all \(j\). So the \(z_j^{-1}\) lie on one side of the line through 0 at angle \(-\alpha\), and (a) gives \(\sum z_j^{-1}\ne0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Exercise 1.35

Why we may take \(z_1\) real: multiplying all three by \(e^{-i\operatorname{Arg}z_1}\) rotates the picture about 0 (section 9); it keeps the moduli, keeps the sum 0, and does not change the shape of the triangle. Exercise 1.39

Let \(f(z)=1/z\). Describe what \(f\) does to the inside and outside of the unit circle, and to points on the unit circle. This map is called inversion through the unit circle.

Solution

With \(z=re^{i\theta}\) (\(z\ne0\)): \(f(z)=\frac1re^{-i\theta}\). Inside (\(0\lt r\lt1\)) goes to outside (\(\frac1r\gt1\)), and outside goes to inside (minus 0); the angle is negated each time. Exercise 1.40

Let \(f(z)=1/\bar z\). Describe what \(f\) does to the inside and outside of the unit circle, and to points on it. This map is called reflection through the unit circle.

\(\frac1{\bar z}=\frac z{|z|^2}=\frac1re^{i\theta}\): the same direction as \(z\), at the inverted distance \(\frac1r\).   • \(f(\mathcal D)=\{f(z)\mid z\in\mathcal D\}\). \(kz+\ell\) (real \(k\ne0\)): lines to parallel lines. \(\frac1z\): lines not through 0 \(\to\) circles through 0 (minus 0); circles \(|z|=r\to|w|=\frac1r\); circles through 0 \(\to\) lines.
  • \(z^n=1\) has \(n\) distinct roots \(e^{2k\pi i/n}\), \(k=0,\dots,n-1\); \(z^n=Re^{i\phi}\) has roots \(R^{1/n}e^{i(\phi+2k\pi)/n}\).
  • \(w\) is a primitive \(n\)-th root of unity if its powers give all \(n\)-th roots; \(e^{2k\pi i/n}\) is primitive iff \(\gcd(k,n)=1\). Primitive 4th roots: \(\pm i\).

20Common mistakes

✘\(\operatorname{Im}(3-4i)=-4i\). ✔\(\operatorname{Im}(3-4i)=-4\), a real number (Definition 1.1).
✘\(|3+4i|=\sqrt{3^2+(4i)^2}=\sqrt{-7}\), or \(|z|^2=z^2\). ✔\(|a+bi|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) with real \(a,b\): \(|3+4i|=5\). And \(|z|^2=z\bar z\), not \(z^2\).
✘\(\operatorname{Arg}(-1-\sqrt3 i)=\tan^{-1}\frac{-\sqrt3}{-1}=\frac\pi3\). ✔Plot the point first: it is in quadrant III, so \(\operatorname{Arg}=\frac{4\pi}3\) (or \(-\frac{2\pi}3\)).
✘\((\sin\theta+i\cos\theta)^n=\sin n\theta+i\cos n\theta\). ✔De Moivre needs \(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\). Rewrite: \(\sin\theta+i\cos\theta=e^{i(\pi/2-\theta)}\), so the power is \(e^{in(\pi/2-\theta)}\).
✘Giving one root of \(z^n=w\), or \(n\) "roots" with angles \(\frac\phi n+2k\pi\) (all the same point). ✔Angles \(\frac{\phi+2k\pi}n\), \(k=0,\dots,n-1\): \(n\) different points, \(\frac{2\pi}n\) apart.
✘"\(w\) is a root of \(z^3=1\), so \(1+w+w^2=0\)." ✔Only if \(w\ne1\). If the question allows \(w=1\), treat that case separately (the sum is then 3).
✘"The image of the line \(y=mx+c\) under \(\frac1z\) is the whole circle", or forgetting to remove 0 from a domain where \(\frac1z\) is undefined. ✔\(\frac1z\) is never 0: the image circle is missing the point 0 (section 14). A domain through 0 must lose 0 first (Dec 2025 Q26(c)).
✘Solving a locus by squaring \(A=B\) without checking that both sides are \(\ge0\), e.g. accepting all \(y=\pm|z|\) from \(-y=|z|\). ✔Keep the sign condition from before squaring: \(-y=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) needs \(y\le0\).
✘Expecting conjugate roots for \(z^2+2z+(1+2i)=0\). ✔Roots come in conjugate pairs only when all coefficients are real (Exercise 1.30).

21Find the mistake

1. A student writes \(-\sqrt3+i\) in polar form:

\[ r=\sqrt{3+1}=2, \] \[ \theta=\tan^{-1}\frac{1}{-\sqrt3}=-\frac\pi6, \] \[ -\sqrt3+i=2e^{-i\pi/6}. \]
Hint

Work out \(2e^{-i\pi/6}\) in \(a+bi\) form. Which quadrant is it in, and which quadrant is \(-\sqrt3+i\) in?

Answer

\(2e^{-i\pi/6}=\sqrt3-i\), in quadrant IV, the opposite point. \(-\sqrt3+i\) is in quadrant II: reference angle \(\frac\pi6\), so \(\theta=\pi-\frac\pi6=\frac{5\pi}6\) and \(-\sqrt3+i=2e^{i5\pi/6}\). \(\tan^{-1}\) cannot tell \((-\sqrt3,1)\) from \((\sqrt3,-1)\).

2. A student finds the cube roots of \(8\):

"\(8=8e^{i0}\), so \(z=8^{1/3}e^{i(0+2k\pi)}=2e^{2k\pi i}=2\) for every \(k\). The only cube root is 2."

Hint

When \(n\theta=\phi+2k\pi\), what do you divide by \(n\)?

Answer

The \(2k\pi\) must be divided by 3 as well: \(z_k=2e^{i2k\pi/3}\), \(k=0,1,2\), giving \(2\), \(-1+\sqrt3 i\), \(-1-\sqrt3 i\): three roots (2 times the cube roots of unity, section 15).

3. A student finds the image of the circle \(|z|=2\) under \(f(z)=iz+1\):

"Multiplying by \(i\) rotates the circle, adding 1 moves it. Rotating a circle about 0 does nothing, so

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathcal D)&=\{w\mid|w|=2\}+1\\ &=\{w\mid|w|=3\}.\text{"} \end{aligned} \]
Hint

Adding 1 shifts every point one unit to the right. Does it change the distance of every point from 0 by 1?

Answer

The rotation step is right: \(\{iz\mid|z|=2\}\) is the circle \(|w|=2\). But adding 1 moves the centre from 0 to 1; it does not enlarge the circle. \(f(\mathcal D)=\{w\mid|w-1|=2\}\). Check by inverse substitution: \(z=\frac{w-1}i\), and \(|z|=2\iff|w-1|=2|i|=2\).

22Concept checks

1. Without expanding, find \(|(2-i)^3(1+3i)|\) and \(\left|\dfrac{3+4i}{1-i}\right|\).

Answer
\[ \begin{aligned} |2-i|^3|1+3i|&=(\sqrt5)^3\sqrt{10}\\ &=5\sqrt5\cdot\sqrt{10}=25\sqrt2. \end{aligned} \]

And \(\frac{|3+4i|}{|1-i|}=\frac5{\sqrt2}=\frac{5\sqrt2}2\). (Exercise 1.8, Theorem 1.15.)

2. What is \(i^{2026}\)? And \((1+i)^{8}\)?

Answer

\(2026=4\cdot506+2\), so \(i^{2026}=i^2=-1\). \((1+i)^8=(\sqrt2)^8e^{i8\pi/4}=16e^{2\pi i}=16\). (Or \((1+i)^2=2i\), \((2i)^4=16\).)

3. True or false: if \(z\ne0\) then \(\operatorname{Arg}(\bar z)=-\operatorname{Arg}(z)\), whichever range you use.

Answer

Only up to \(2\pi\). With \([0,2\pi)\): \(\operatorname{Arg}(\bar i)=\operatorname{Arg}(-i)=\frac{3\pi}2\), not \(-\frac\pi2\). With \((-\pi,\pi]\) it fails for negative reals: \(\operatorname{Arg}(-1)=\pi\), but \(-\pi\) is outside the range. So "false as stated"; true as \(\operatorname{Arg}\bar z\equiv-\operatorname{Arg}z\pmod{2\pi}\).

4. How many primitive 6th roots of unity are there, and what are they in \(a+bi\) form?

Answer

Two: \(k=1,5\) (\(\gcd(k,6)=1\)): \(e^{i\pi/3}=\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}2i\) and \(e^{i5\pi/3}=\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}2i\). The others generate only \(\{1\}\), \(\{1,-1\}\) or the cube roots of unity.

5. Find all \(z\) with \(|z-i|=|z|+1\) (Dec 2021 Q5(c)).

Answer

Put \(z=x+yi\) and square: \(x^2+(y-1)^2=x^2+y^2+2|z|+1\), so \(-2y=2|z|\), that is \(-y=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). This needs \(y\le0\), and squaring again gives \(x^2=0\). So \(\{yi\mid y\le0\}\): the imaginary axis from 0 downwards. Geometrically it is the equality case of the triangle inequality \(|z+(-i)|\le|z|+|-i|\): \(z\) and \(-i\) point the same way.

6. Under \(f(z)=\frac1z\), what is the image of the vertical line \(\operatorname{Re}z=2\)? Does the image contain \(\frac12\)? Does it contain 0?

Answer

By Example 1.22(ii) with \(d=2\): the circle with centre \(\frac14\), radius \(\frac14\), minus 0. It contains \(\frac12=f(2)\); it does not contain 0 (no \(z\) has \(\frac1z=0\)).

For \(z,w\in\mathbb C\), prove that \(|z+w|\le|z|+|w|\).
