1Quadratic forms and symmetric matrices ~5 min

What is this?

A quadratic form is a recipe that turns a vector into one number, using only squares and products of its entries: no single \(x\) on its own, no constant. A familiar example is the energy stored in springs: it depends on the squares of the stretches, and with two springs joined together it also has a cross term. The symmetric matrix \(A\) is just a tidy way to store the recipe: the squares go on the diagonal and each cross term is shared between two mirror-image places.

The object to be maximized is \(q(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x=\sum_{i,j=1}^n a_{ij}x_ix_j\). This section unpacks that notation and collects the facts from earlier chapters that section 2 uses without comment.

Definition: quadratic form

Let \(A=[a_{ij}]\) be an \(n\times n\) symmetric matrix (\(A^T=A\), so \(a_{ij}=a_{ji}\)) and \(\mathbf x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) a column vector. The quadratic form of \(A\) is the number

\[ q(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}x_ix_j . \]

For \(n=2\), with \(A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x&=\begin{bmatrix}x&y\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}ax+by\\bx+dy\end{bmatrix}\\&=ax^2+2bxy+dy^2 .\end{aligned} \]

In words: a quadratic form is a sum of squares and cross products of the variables, every term of degree exactly 2 (no \(x\) on its own, no constant). The diagonal entries of \(A\) are the coefficients of the squares; each cross term \(x_ix_j\) gets its coefficient from two entries, \(a_{ij}\) and \(a_{ji}\).

The sum \(\sum_{i,j=1}^n\) is shorthand for the double sum above: every pair \((i,j)\), so with \(n=2\) the four terms \(a_{11}x_1^2+a_{12}x_1x_2+a_{21}x_2x_1+a_{22}x_2^2\). Since \(a_{12}=a_{21}\), the middle two combine to \(2a_{12}x_1x_2\).

Method: from a polynomial to its symmetric matrix
  1. Do: Put the coefficient of \(x_i^2\) in the diagonal position \((i,i)\). The diagonal entry \(a_{ii}\) meets \(x_i\) twice when you multiply out, so it produces exactly the \(x_i^2\) term.
  2. Do: Split the coefficient of the cross term \(x_ix_j\) (\(i\ne j\)) in half: put one half in position \((i,j)\) and the other in \((j,i)\). Both mirror-image places produce an \(x_ix_j\) term, so each must carry half of it, and the matrix comes out symmetric.
  3. Do: A cross term that does not appear has coefficient 0. An empty place in the matrix would otherwise be easy to fill by mistake; a 0 says "this pair never meets".
  4. Do: Check by multiplying out \(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x\). It catches the commonest slip, a whole cross coefficient in both places, which doubles the cross term.
Example (section 3, May 2026 Q6)

\(3x^2+4xy+5y^2\): squares give the diagonal 3 and 5; the cross term \(4xy\) gives \(2\) and \(2\) off the diagonal:

\[ 3x^2+4xy+5y^2=\begin{bmatrix}x&y\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\2&5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}. \]

Three variables: \(g=2x^2+3y^2+2z^2-2xy-2xz-2yz\) has diagonal \(2,3,2\) and every cross coefficient \(-2\) split into \(-1,-1\):

\[ g=\mathbf x^T\begin{bmatrix}2&-1&-1\\-1&3&-1\\-1&-1&2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x,\qquad \mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{bmatrix}. \]
Trap

Putting the whole cross coefficient off the diagonal, for example \(\begin{bmatrix}3&4\\4&5\end{bmatrix}\) for \(3x^2+4xy+5y^2\). Multiplying out gives \(3x^2+8xy+5y^2\): the cross term is counted twice. A non-symmetric choice such as \(\begin{bmatrix}3&4\\0&5\end{bmatrix}\) does give the right polynomial, but the theorem of section 2 needs the symmetric matrix.

Length and the sphere

What is this? (length)

The length of a vector is Pythagoras in any number of dimensions: square the entries, add, take the square root. "All vectors of length \(c\)" is a circle of radius \(c\) in the plane, or the surface of a ball of radius \(c\) in space. Fixing the length means we only compare directions, like comparing arrows that all have the same length.

The constraint in section 2 is \(\|\mathbf x\|=c\), where the length (or norm) of \(\mathbf x\) is

\[ \|\mathbf x\|=\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2+\cdots+x_n^2}, \] \[ \|\mathbf x\|^2=\mathbf x^T\mathbf x=\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf x . \]

In \(\mathbb R^2\) the vectors with \(\|\mathbf x\|=c\) form the circle \(x^2+y^2=c^2\) of radius \(c\); in \(\mathbb R^3\) the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=c^2\). A unit vector has length 1. Exam questions write the constraint in several ways that all mean the same: \(\|\mathbf x\|=3\), \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}=3\), \(x^2+y^2+z^2=9\). Note that \(x^2+y^2=10\) means \(c^2=10\), not \(c=10\).

What L5 tells us about symmetric matrices

What is this? (eigenbasis)

Think of the matrix as a machine that stretches space. A symmetric matrix is a special machine: it has its own favourite axes, at right angles to each other, and along each one it only stretches (by the eigenvalue), with no turning. Rotate your graph paper to line up with those axes and the machine becomes as simple as possible. Because the new axes are still perpendicular, lengths are measured exactly as before.

Facts from L5 used in section 2

If \(A\) is an \(n\times n\) real symmetric matrix, then

  1. all its eigenvalues are real, so they can be listed in order \(\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_n\) (repeats allowed);
  2. there is an orthonormal eigenbasis \(\{\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_n\}\): \(A\mathbf v_i=\lambda_i\mathbf v_i\), each \(\|\mathbf v_i\|=1\), and \(\mathbf v_i\cdot\mathbf v_j=0\) for \(i\ne j\);
  3. eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are automatically perpendicular, so you only need to normalize them (divide by their length). For a repeated eigenvalue you choose perpendicular ones.

The consequence that drives section 2: if \(\mathbf x=\alpha_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+\alpha_n\mathbf v_n\) in an orthonormal basis, then

\[ \begin{aligned}\|\mathbf x\|^2&=\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf x=\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j\,\mathbf v_i\cdot\mathbf v_j\\&=\alpha_1^2+\alpha_2^2+\cdots+\alpha_n^2 ,\end{aligned} \]

because every term with \(i\ne j\) has \(\mathbf v_i\cdot\mathbf v_j=0\) and every term with \(i=j\) has \(\mathbf v_i\cdot\mathbf v_i=1\).

In words: the coordinates \(\alpha_i\) along perpendicular unit directions obey Pythagoras, exactly like the ordinary \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) coordinates. The eigenbasis is just a rotated set of axes.

You can now: write a quadratic form in two or three variables as \(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x\) with \(A\) symmetric; read \(c\) or \(c^2\) off a length condition; and say why, in an orthonormal eigenbasis, \(\|\mathbf x\|^2\) is the sum of the squared coordinates.

2Largest and smallest value on a sphere ~5 min

What is this?

Feed the stretching machine an arrow of fixed length. In the machine's own axes the quadratic form is a mix of the stretch factors \(\lambda_i\), with weights \(\alpha_i^2\) that always add up to the same total \(c^2\). To get the biggest mix you put all the weight on the biggest stretch, which means pointing the arrow along that axis. To get the smallest, point it along the axis with the smallest stretch.

The problem: among all vectors of a fixed length \(c\gt0\), find the largest (and smallest) value of the quadratic form.

\[ \begin{aligned}&\text{maximize } q(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x=\sum_{i,j=1}^n a_{ij}x_ix_j\\&\text{subject to } \|\mathbf x\|=c .\end{aligned} \]

Without the constraint the question usually makes no sense: doubling \(\mathbf x\) multiplies \(q\) by 4, so \(q\) can be made as large as we like (unless every eigenvalue is \(\le0\)). Fixing the length makes it a fair contest between directions.

Theorem

Let \(A\) be symmetric with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_n\) and orthonormal eigenvectors \(\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_n\). Subject to \(\|\mathbf x\|=c\):

\[ \max q(\mathbf x)=\lambda_1c^2,\ \text{reached at } \mathbf x=c\mathbf v_1; \] \[ \min q(\mathbf x)=\lambda_nc^2,\ \text{reached at } \mathbf x=c\mathbf v_n . \]
Reading the formula
\(\lambda_1\)
the biggest stretch factor of the machine.
\(c^2\)
the total weight to share out: the arrow's length squared. Doubling the length multiplies \(q\) by 4.
\(c\mathbf v_1\)
an arrow of the allowed length pointing straight along the most-stretched axis (\(\mathbf v_1\) is a unit eigenvector).
\(\lambda_nc^2\) at \(c\mathbf v_n\)
the same story with the least-stretched axis.

In words: on a sphere of radius \(c\), the quadratic form is largest in the direction of the eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue and smallest in the direction of the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue; the values are those eigenvalues times \(c^2\). Every other point of the sphere gives a value in between.

Why it is true, line by line

  1. Orthonormal eigenbasis. "As \(A\) is a symmetric matrix, there exists an orthonormal eigenbasis \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots,\mathbf v_n\}\)." This is the L5 fact of section 1; the eigenvalues are arranged in descending order \(\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_n\).
  2. Write \(\mathbf x\) in that basis. Any \(\mathbf x\) is a combination \(\mathbf x=\alpha_1\mathbf v_1+\alpha_2\mathbf v_2+\cdots+\alpha_n\mathbf v_n\). Multiplying by \(A\) and using \(A\mathbf v_i=\lambda_i\mathbf v_i\): \[ A\mathbf x=\alpha_1\lambda_1\mathbf v_1+\alpha_2\lambda_2\mathbf v_2+\cdots+\alpha_n\lambda_n\mathbf v_n . \] In this basis \(A\) just stretches the \(i\)-th coordinate by \(\lambda_i\).
  3. Compute \(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x\). \(\mathbf x^T\mathbf y\) is the dot product \(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf y\), so \[ \begin{aligned}\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x&=(\alpha_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+\alpha_n\mathbf v_n)\\&\quad\cdot(\alpha_1\lambda_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+\alpha_n\lambda_n\mathbf v_n)\\&=\alpha_1^2\lambda_1+\alpha_2^2\lambda_2+\cdots+\alpha_n^2\lambda_n .\end{aligned} \] Multiplying out the dot product, the cross terms \(\mathbf v_i\cdot\mathbf v_j\) (\(i\ne j\)) are 0 and \(\mathbf v_i\cdot\mathbf v_i=1\), leaving one term per \(i\).
  4. Bound it. Every \(\lambda_i\le\lambda_1\) and every \(\alpha_i^2\ge0\), so replacing each \(\lambda_i\) by \(\lambda_1\) can only increase the sum: \[ \begin{aligned}\alpha_1^2\lambda_1+\cdots+\alpha_n^2\lambda_n&\le\lambda_1(\alpha_1^2+\cdots+\alpha_n^2)\\&=\lambda_1c^2 ,\end{aligned} \] since \(\alpha_1^2+\cdots+\alpha_n^2=\|\mathbf x\|^2=c^2\) (section 1).
  5. The bound is reached. Take \(\mathbf x=c\mathbf v_1\) (so \(\alpha_1=c\), all other \(\alpha_i=0\)). Then \(\|\mathbf x\|=c\) and \((c\mathbf v_1)^TA(c\mathbf v_1)=c^2\,\mathbf v_1^T(\lambda_1\mathbf v_1)=\lambda_1c^2\). So \(\lambda_1c^2\) is the maximum, not just an upper bound.
  6. Similarly for the minimum. Each \(\lambda_i\ge\lambda_n\), so \(q(\mathbf x)\ge\lambda_n(\alpha_1^2+\cdots+\alpha_n^2)=\lambda_nc^2\), with equality at \(\mathbf x=c\mathbf v_n\): "the minimum of \(q(\mathbf x)\) subject to \(\|\mathbf x\|=c\) is given by \(\lambda_nc^2\), which is equal to \(q(c\mathbf v_n)=(c\mathbf v_n)^TA(c\mathbf v_n)\)."

In words: in eigen-coordinates, \(q\) is a weighted average-like sum: \(q=\sum\alpha_i^2\lambda_i\) where the weights \(\alpha_i^2\) always add up to \(c^2\). To make it as big as possible, put all the weight on the biggest \(\lambda\); to make it small, on the smallest.

Going further: the Rayleigh quotient

Dividing by \(\|\mathbf x\|^2\) removes the length altogether. For any non-zero \(\mathbf x\), the Rayleigh quotient \(\rho(\mathbf x)=\dfrac{\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x}{\mathbf x^T\mathbf x}=\dfrac{\alpha_1^2\lambda_1+\cdots+\alpha_n^2\lambda_n}{\alpha_1^2+\cdots+\alpha_n^2}\) satisfies

\[ \lambda_n\le\rho(\mathbf x)\le\lambda_1 . \]

This is the same theorem with \(c=1\) (and May 2016 Q3 asks you to prove it). It also shows: \(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x\gt0\) for every \(\mathbf x\ne\mathbf 0\) exactly when \(\lambda_n\gt0\) (all eigenvalues positive; \(A\) is then called positive definite), and \(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x\lt0\) for every \(\mathbf x\ne\mathbf 0\) exactly when \(\lambda_1\lt0\) (negative definite). With eigenvalues of both signs, \(A\) is indefinite. Older papers use these words.

You can now: state the max/min theorem with its conditions (symmetric \(A\), fixed length \(c\)); explain the proof by writing \(\mathbf x\) in the eigenbasis and bounding \(\sum\alpha_i^2\lambda_i\); and use the Rayleigh quotient to decide whether a form is positive definite, negative definite or indefinite.

3Example: 3x² + 4xy + 5y² on a circle ~5 min

What is this?

This is the theorem of section 2 on one concrete machine, \(A=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\2&5\end{bmatrix}\), and arrows of length 2. We find the two stretch factors, take the bigger one, and multiply by the length squared.

Exercise

Maximize \(q(\mathbf x)=3x^2+4xy+5y^2\) subject to the condition \(\|\mathbf x\|=2\).

Solution

Step 1: the matrix. By section 1's method (split the \(4xy\) into \(2\) and \(2\)):

\[ q(\mathbf x)=3x^2+4xy+5y^2=\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x, \] \[ \mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix},\quad A=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\2&5\end{bmatrix}. \]

Step 2: the eigenvalues. Solve the characteristic equation \(\det(\lambda I-A)=0\):

\[ \begin{aligned}&\det\begin{bmatrix}\lambda-3&-2\\-2&\lambda-5\end{bmatrix}\\&\quad=(\lambda-3)(\lambda-5)-2(2)=0\end{aligned} \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \lambda^2-8\lambda+11=0 \] \[ \begin{aligned}\Rightarrow\ \lambda&=\frac{8\pm\sqrt{64-4(11)}}{2}\\&=\frac{8\pm\sqrt{20}}{2}=4\pm\sqrt5 .\end{aligned} \]

So \(\lambda_1=4+\sqrt5\approx6.24\) and \(\lambda_2=4-\sqrt5\approx1.76\).

Step 3: the maximum. Here \(c=2\), so \(c^2=4\): \(q\) is built from squares, so an arrow twice as long gives four times the value: the eigenvalue is the value for a unit arrow.

\[ \begin{aligned}\max q&=\lambda_1c^2=4(4+\sqrt5)\\&=16+4\sqrt5\approx24.94 .\end{aligned} \]

That answers the question as asked. Two natural follow-ups, which exam questions usually ask as well:

Going further: where the maximum is reached, and the minimum

Where. The maximum is at \(\mathbf x=\pm2\mathbf v_1\) with \(\mathbf v_1\) a unit eigenvector for \(\lambda_1=4+\sqrt5\). Solve \((A-\lambda_1I)\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}-1-\sqrt5&2\\2&1-\sqrt5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}. \]

The rows are multiples of each other (the matrix is singular), so one row is enough: \((-1-\sqrt5)x+2y=0\), i.e. \(y=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}x\). Take \(\mathbf v=(2,\ 1+\sqrt5)\); its length squared is \(4+(1+\sqrt5)^2=10+2\sqrt5\). So

\[ \mathbf x=\pm\frac{2}{\sqrt{10+2\sqrt5}}\begin{bmatrix}2\\1+\sqrt5\end{bmatrix}\approx\pm\begin{bmatrix}1.05\\1.70\end{bmatrix}. \]

Minimum.

\[ \begin{aligned} \min q&=\lambda_2c^2=4(4-\sqrt5)\\ &=16-4\sqrt5\approx7.06, \end{aligned} \]

at

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf x&=\pm\frac{2}{\sqrt{10-2\sqrt5}}(2,\ 1-\sqrt5)\\ &\approx\pm(1.70,\,-1.05), \end{aligned} \]

perpendicular to the maximizer.

Sanity check. Two easy points on the circle: \(q(2,0)=12\) and \(q(0,2)=20\). Both lie between \(7.06\) and \(24.94\), as they must.

May 2019 Q6 asks for the maximum and minimum of exactly this \(q\) on \(x^2+y^2=10\): there \(c^2=10\), so the answers are \(10(4+\sqrt5)\) and \(10(4-\sqrt5)\).

What is this? (level curves)

Think of contour lines on a map: each curve joins the points where \(q\) has the same value. Walk round the circle and read off the contour you are on. The highest contour you ever touch is the maximum; the lowest is the minimum. At those two places the contour only just grazes the circle.

Fix a value \(L\). The points where \(q(\mathbf x)=L\) form a level curve; for this \(A\) (both eigenvalues positive) it is an ellipse whose axes point along the eigenvectors. Because \(q(r\mathbf u)=r^2q(\mathbf u)\), a bigger \(L\) gives a bigger ellipse. Too big, and the ellipse lies entirely outside the circle \(\|\mathbf x\|=c\); too small, entirely inside. The largest \(L\) whose level curve still meets the circle is the maximum: that ellipse lies outside the circle and touches it at the two ends of its short axis, which point along \(\mathbf v_1\). The smallest such \(L\) is the minimum: that ellipse lies inside and touches the circle at the ends of its long axis, along \(\mathbf v_2\). Move the point round the circle and watch \(q\).

You can now: find the maximum and minimum of \(3x^2+4xy+5y^2\) (or a similar form) on a circle, with the points where they are reached; and sanity-check the answer with easy points such as \((c,0)\) and \((0,c)\).

4Using the result in exam questions ~5 min

What is this?

The exam version of the same picture: build the machine (the symmetric matrix), measure the arrows (the radius), find the stretch factors (the eigenvalues), and point along the right axis. The five notes after the recipe are the small twists examiners add, each of which follows from the same picture.

Sections 2 and 3 give the theorem and one example. Exam questions on it (type Guide · L6.1, the most frequent type of this chapter: 28 question parts) add a few twists. This section collects them in one recipe and five short notes.

Method: max / min of \(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x\) on \(\|\mathbf x\|=c\)
  1. Do: Matrix. Write the form as \(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x\) with \(A\) symmetric (halve the cross coefficients). Check by multiplying out. The theorem is about the symmetric machine; only that one is guaranteed perpendicular favourite axes.
  2. Do: Radius. Read off \(c^2\): \(\|\mathbf x\|=3\) gives \(c^2=9\); \(x^2+y^2=10\) gives \(c^2=10\); "unit vector" gives \(c^2=1\). \(c^2\) is the total weight shared among the stretch factors, so it multiplies every answer.
  3. Do: Eigenvalues. Solve \(\det(\lambda I-A)=0\). Often the question gives one eigenvector or eigenvalue first; use it to factor the cubic. Check: the eigenvalues add up to the trace (sum of the diagonal). These are the stretch factors along the favourite axes; the largest and smallest are all you need.
  4. Do: Values. \(\max=\lambda_{\max}c^2\), \(\min=\lambda_{\min}c^2\). All the weight goes on the biggest (or smallest) stretch.
  5. Do: Where. Find an eigenvector \(\mathbf v\) for \(\lambda_{\max}\) (or \(\lambda_{\min}\)), divide by its length, multiply by \(c\): \(\mathbf x=\pm c\,\mathbf v/\|\mathbf v\|\). Check \(q(\mathbf x)\) gives the value of step 4. The best arrow points along that axis and must have the allowed length \(c\); either end of the axis works.
Going further: five things exam questions add

1. The answer is never a single vector. If \(\mathbf x\) is a maximizer then so is \(-\mathbf x\) (\(q(-\mathbf x)=q(\mathbf x)\)), so always give \(\pm\). If the largest eigenvalue is repeated, every unit vector of its eigenspace works. Example: \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0\\1&1&0\\0&0&2\end{bmatrix}\) has eigenvalue \(2\) with eigenvectors \((1,1,0)\) and \((0,0,1)\), and eigenvalue \(0\) with \((1,-1,0)\). On the unit sphere the maximum 2 is reached at every \(\mathbf x=a\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)+b(0,0,1)\) with \(a^2+b^2=1\): a whole circle of maximizers. The minimum 0 is reached only at \(\pm\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1,0)\) ("unique up to sign").

2. Shifted matrices. If \(A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v\) then \((A+kI)\mathbf v=(\lambda+k)\mathbf v\): adding \(kI\) adds \(k\) to every eigenvalue and keeps the eigenvectors. So the maximum of \(\mathbf y^T(A+kI)\mathbf y\) on the unit sphere is \(\lambda_{\max}(A)+k\), at the same \(\mathbf y\). Similarly \(-A\) has eigenvalues \(-\lambda\): its maximum is minus the minimum of \(A\).

3. A value in between. "Find a unit \(\mathbf x\) with \(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x=L\)." If \(L\) is \(\lambda_{\max}\) or \(\lambda_{\min}\), the answer is the eigenvector. If \(L\) is strictly between, mix two eigenvectors: \(\mathbf x=\cos\theta\,\mathbf v_1+\sin\theta\,\mathbf v_n\) is a unit vector (Pythagoras) with \(q(\mathbf x)=\lambda_1\cos^2\theta+\lambda_n\sin^2\theta\); choose \(\theta\) to make this \(L\). In example 1 with \(L=1\): \(2\cos^2\theta+0=1\) gives \(\cos^2\theta=\frac12\), and \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf x&=\frac1{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)\\ &\quad+\frac1{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1,0)\\ &=(1,0,0); \end{aligned} \] check: \(q(1,0,0)=a_{11}=1\). In particular the smallest absolute value is 0 whenever \(\lambda_{\min}\le0\le\lambda_{\max}\).

4. No constraint at all. Without \(\|\mathbf x\|=c\), \(q(t\mathbf x)=t^2q(\mathbf x)\). If every eigenvalue is \(\ge0\), the global minimum is \(0\) (at \(\mathbf x=\mathbf 0\), and along any eigenvector of eigenvalue 0) and there is no maximum; if some eigenvalue is negative, \(q\) has no minimum (it goes to \(-\infty\) along that eigenvector). A question that asks for a max and min with no constraint printed almost certainly means unit vectors; say both.

5. Conics. The level curve \(ax^2+2bxy+dy^2=k\) becomes \(\lambda_1y_1^2+\lambda_2y_2^2=k\) in eigen-coordinates \(y_1,y_2\) (the coordinates along \(\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\)). Both eigenvalues of the same sign as \(k\): an ellipse; opposite signs: a hyperbola. Its principal axes (axes of symmetry) point along the eigenvectors, as in the figure of section 3.

You can now: run the five-step recipe on a 2 × 2 or 3 × 3 form; give every maximizer (with \(\pm\), and whole circles for a repeated eigenvalue); handle \(A+kI\) and \(-A\); find a unit vector with a given value in between; deal with a question that prints no constraint; and name a conic as an ellipse or hyperbola from the eigenvalue signs.

5Systems of linear ODEs: the idea ~6 min

What is this?

Picture two savings accounts whose interest depends on both balances: each account's growth is pulled by the other. That tangle is the coupling in \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x\). If you look along the machine's favourite directions (its eigenvectors) instead of along \(x\) and \(y\), the tangle disappears: you see separate accounts, each growing (or shrinking) at its own fixed rate \(\lambda\), ignoring the others. Solve each one, then turn back to \(x\) and \(y\).

Diagonalization also solves differential equations. This section explains what that means and why it works; section 6 does a full example.

Definition: differential equation, system

An ordinary differential equation (ODE) is an equation for an unknown function \(x(t)\) that involves its derivatives \(x'=\frac{dx}{dt}\), \(x''\), … . Solving it means finding every function that makes it true for all \(t\).

A linear system of ODEs with constant coefficients has several unknown functions whose rates of change are linear combinations of the functions themselves, for example

\[ x'=9x-2y,\qquad y'=-2x+6y . \]

In matrix form, with \(\mathbf x(t)=\begin{bmatrix}x(t)\\y(t)\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf x'=\begin{bmatrix}x'\\y'\end{bmatrix}\) (differentiate each entry):

\[ \mathbf x'=A\mathbf x,\qquad A=\begin{bmatrix}9&-2\\-2&6\end{bmatrix}. \]

In words: each quantity grows or shrinks at a rate that depends on all the quantities. Here \(x\) grows at 9 times itself but is pulled down by \(2y\); \(y\) grows at 6 times itself but is pulled down by \(2x\). The quantities are coupled: you cannot solve for \(x\) without knowing \(y\).

One equation: \(u'=\lambda u\)

What is this? (one equation)

One account with continuously compounded interest at rate \(\lambda\): the balance grows at a rate proportional to the balance itself, so it grows exponentially. A negative \(\lambda\) is a steady loss, like value draining away, and the balance decays towards 0. The starting balance \(a\) just scales the whole curve.

A single equation is easy. Solve \(u'=5u\) by separating the variables:

\[ \begin{aligned}u'=5u\ &\Rightarrow\ \frac{du}{u}=5\,dt\\&\Rightarrow\ \ln u=5t+c\\&\Rightarrow\ u=ae^{5t} ,\end{aligned} \]

with \(a=e^c\). Similarly \(v'=10v\) gives \(v=be^{10t}\).

Fact: the solutions of \(u'=\lambda u\)

Every solution of \(u'=\lambda u\) is \(u(t)=ae^{\lambda t}\) for a constant \(a\), and \(a=u(0)\).

In words: a quantity whose rate of change is proportional to itself grows exponentially (\(\lambda\gt0\)), decays exponentially (\(\lambda\lt0\)) or stays constant (\(\lambda=0\)).

Check by differentiating: \(\frac{d}{dt}(ae^{\lambda t})=\lambda ae^{\lambda t}=\lambda u\ \checkmark\). The route through \(\ln u\) assumes \(u\gt0\), but the formula works for every constant \(a\), including \(a=0\) (the zero solution) and \(a\lt0\).

Many equations: uncouple them with eigenvectors

What is this? (uncoupling)

Diagonalizing is switching to the machine's own favourite axes, where it only stretches. Here those axes need not be perpendicular, but there must be \(n\) independent ones so that every vector can be built from them. In those axes the matrix is the diagonal \(D\): each new coordinate changes at a rate that depends only on itself.

Suppose \(A\) is diagonalizable (L5): \(A=QDQ^{-1}\), where the columns of \(Q\) are eigenvectors \(\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_n\) and \(D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\). Then:

  1. Do: \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x=QDQ^{-1}\mathbf x\). Read right to left: \(Q^{-1}\) switches to eigenvector coordinates, \(D\) stretches each one, \(Q\) switches back.
  2. Do: Multiply on the left by \(Q^{-1}\): \(Q^{-1}\mathbf x'=DQ^{-1}\mathbf x\). Now both sides are written in eigenvector coordinates.
  3. Do: New unknowns. Let \(\mathbf u=Q^{-1}\mathbf x\) (equivalently \(\mathbf x=Q\mathbf u\)). Since \(Q^{-1}\) is a constant matrix, \(\mathbf u'=Q^{-1}\mathbf x'\). So step 2 reads \[ \mathbf u'=D\mathbf u . \] \(\mathbf u\) is the same point described along the eigenvector axes; a constant change of axes does not disturb rates of change.
  4. Do: \(D\) is diagonal, so this is \(n\) separate equations \(u_i'=\lambda_iu_i\), with solutions \(u_i=c_ie^{\lambda_it}\). These are the separate accounts: each grows at its own rate, untangled from the rest.
  5. Do: Go back: \(\mathbf x=Q\mathbf u\). The question asks about \(x\) and \(y\), so rebuild them: each eigen-coordinate times its eigenvector, added up.
Theorem: solution of \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x\)

If \(A\) has \(n\) linearly independent eigenvectors \(\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_n\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n\), the general solution of \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x\) is

\[ \begin{aligned}\mathbf x(t)&=Q\begin{bmatrix}c_1e^{\lambda_1t}\\ \vdots\\ c_ne^{\lambda_nt}\end{bmatrix}\\&=c_1e^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf v_1+\cdots+c_ne^{\lambda_nt}\mathbf v_n .\end{aligned} \]

The constants come from the starting value: at \(t=0\), \(\mathbf x(0)=c_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf v_n\), i.e. solve \(Q\mathbf c=\mathbf x(0)\).

Reading the formula
\(\mathbf v_i\)
one of the machine's favourite directions; the piece never leaves this line.
\(e^{\lambda_it}\)
that piece's own growth (or decay) curve, like one account compounding at rate \(\lambda_i\).
\(c_i\)
how much of the start lies along \(\mathbf v_i\): the opening balance of that account.
\(+\)
the pieces simply add, because the system is linear.

In words: split the starting vector into eigenvector pieces. Each piece stays on its own eigen-direction and is simply multiplied by \(e^{\lambda t}\): it grows or decays at its own rate, ignoring the others.

Why each piece is a solution on its own: \(\frac{d}{dt}(e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v)=\lambda e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v=e^{\lambda t}A\mathbf v=A(e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v)\). Sums of solutions are solutions, because the system is linear.

Trap

\(Q\) does not need to be orthogonal here, and the eigenvectors need not be unit vectors: any non-zero multiples work, the constants \(c_i\) absorb the scaling. (For the quadratic forms of sections 2 to 4 you do need unit vectors.) But the order must match: the \(i\)-th column of \(Q\) goes with \(\lambda_i\) in \(D\).

Going further: long-run behaviour

As \(t\) grows, the term with the largest eigenvalue (among those with \(c_i\ne0\)) dominates, since \(e^{\lambda_1t}/e^{\lambda_2t}=e^{(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)t}\to\infty\) when \(\lambda_1\gt\lambda_2\). So \(\mathbf x(t)\) turns towards the direction of that eigenvector (Dec 2023 Q3 asks this). If all eigenvalues are negative, \(\mathbf x(t)\to\mathbf 0\). If \(A\) is not diagonalizable, or has complex eigenvalues, this method needs extra tools that the course does not use, except in the second-order form of section 8.

You can now: write a system of linear ODEs as \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x\); solve \(u'=\lambda u\); explain how \(\mathbf u=Q^{-1}\mathbf x\) uncouples the system; write the general solution \(\sum c_ie^{\lambda_it}\mathbf v_i\); and say which direction \(\mathbf x(t)\) turns towards as \(t\) grows.

6Example: a 2 × 2 system ~3 min

What is this?

Two tangled accounts, \(x\) and \(y\), untangled by hand: find the favourite directions and their rates (5 and 10), let each piece grow on its own, and add the pieces back up.

Example

Solve \(x'=\dfrac{dx}{dt}=9x-2y\) and \(y'=\dfrac{dy}{dt}=-2x+6y\).

Step 1: matrix form. As in section 5,

\[ \begin{bmatrix}x'\\y'\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}9&-2\\-2&6\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \begin{bmatrix}x'\\y'\end{bmatrix}=A\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=QDQ^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \Rightarrow\ Q^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}x'\\y'\end{bmatrix}=DQ^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}u'\\v'\end{bmatrix}=D\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&10\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\end{bmatrix}, \]

where \(Q^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\end{bmatrix}\). (\(D\) is written first here; the next step shows where 5 and 10 come from.)

Step 2: eigenvalues.

\[ \det(\lambda I-A)=\det\begin{bmatrix}\lambda-9&2\\2&\lambda-6\end{bmatrix}=0 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ (\lambda-9)(\lambda-6)-2(2)=0 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \lambda^2-15\lambda+50=0 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ (\lambda-5)(\lambda-10)=0\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda_1=5,\ \lambda_2=10 . \]

Check: \(5+10=15=9+6\) (the trace) and \(5\times10=50=\det A=54-4\).

Step 3: eigenvectors. Solve \((\lambda I-A)\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\) for each eigenvalue.

\[ \lambda_1=5:\ \begin{bmatrix}-4&2\\2&-1\end{bmatrix}\mathbf v_1=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix} \]

(the second row \(2v-w=0\) gives \(w=2v\)).

\[ \lambda_2=10:\ \begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}\mathbf v_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf v_2=\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix} \]

(the first row \(v+2w=0\) gives \(v=-2w\)). So \(Q=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&-1\end{bmatrix}\), \(D=\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&10\end{bmatrix}\). Since \(A\) is symmetric, \(\mathbf v_1\perp\mathbf v_2\) (\(1\cdot2+2\cdot(-1)=0\)), though they are not unit vectors; that does not matter here.

Step 4: solve the uncoupled equations.

\[ \begin{aligned}u'=5u\ &\Rightarrow\ \frac{du}{u}=5\,dt\\&\Rightarrow\ \ln u=5t+c\\&\Rightarrow\ u=ae^{5t} ;\end{aligned} \]

similarly, \(v=be^{10t}\).

Step 5: back to \(x\) and \(y\). \(u\) and \(v\) are amounts along \(\mathbf v_1\) and \(\mathbf v_2\), so \(Q\mathbf u=u\mathbf v_1+v\mathbf v_2\) puts the two pieces back together.

\[ \begin{gathered} Q^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\end{bmatrix}\\ \Rightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=Q\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\end{bmatrix}: \end{gathered} \] \[ \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&-1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}ae^{5t}\\be^{10t}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}ae^{5t}+2be^{10t}\\2ae^{5t}-be^{10t}\end{bmatrix}. \]

Equivalently \(\mathbf x(t)=ae^{5t}\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}+be^{10t}\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix}\), with \(a\), \(b\) any constants.

In words: notice that \(Q^{-1}\) was never computed. We only need \(Q\) (to go back) and the eigenvalues (for the exponents).

Check (always worth 30 seconds): \(x'=5ae^{5t}+20be^{10t}\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} 9x-2y&=9ae^{5t}+18be^{10t}\\ &\quad-4ae^{5t}+2be^{10t}\\ &=5ae^{5t}+20be^{10t}\ \checkmark \end{aligned} \]
Going further: a starting value

Exam questions usually give \(\mathbf x(0)\). Say \(x(0)=3\), \(y(0)=1\). Put \(t=0\) (so every \(e^{0}=1\)):

\[ a+2b=3,\quad 2a-b=1 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ a=1,\ b=1 , \]

so \(x(t)=e^{5t}+2e^{10t}\), \(y(t)=2e^{5t}-e^{10t}\). For large \(t\) the \(e^{10t}\) term dominates, and \(\mathbf x(t)\) points more and more along \(\mathbf v_2=(2,-1)\).

7A third-order equation as a system ~3 min

What is this?

A car's dashboard can show position, speed and acceleration on three separate dials. Each dial's rate of change is the next dial along, and the equation tells you how the last one changes. Giving the derivatives their own names does exactly this: one equation with \(x'''\) becomes three first-order equations, which is the form section 5 can solve.

The same method also handles a single equation with higher derivatives. The trick is to give the derivatives their own names, which turns one third-order equation into a system of three first-order ones.

Example

Solve \(x'''-x''-4x'+4x=0\).

Step 1: new unknowns. Let The method of section 5 only handles first derivatives, so each higher derivative gets its own dial.

\[ x_1=x,\qquad x_2=x',\qquad x_3=x'' . \]

Then \(x_1'=x'=x_2\) and \(x_2'=x''=x_3\), and the equation itself gives the third derivative: \(x_3'=x'''=x''+4x'-4x=-4x_1+4x_2+x_3\). In matrix form:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}x_1'\\x_2'\\x_3'\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\\-4&4&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{bmatrix}=A\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{bmatrix}. \]

In words: the first two rows only say "the derivative of each name is the next name"; the last row is the original equation solved for \(x'''\).

As in section 6, write \(Q^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}u_1\\u_2\\u_3\end{bmatrix}\), so that \(\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x\\x'\\x''\end{bmatrix}=Q\begin{bmatrix}u_1\\u_2\\u_3\end{bmatrix}\).

Step 2: eigenvalues. Expand along the first column:

\[ \det(\lambda I-A)=\det\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&-1&0\\0&\lambda&-1\\4&-4&\lambda-1\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \begin{aligned}&=\lambda\big[\lambda(\lambda-1)-4\big]+4\big[(-1)(-1)-0\cdot\lambda\big]\\&=\lambda^2(\lambda-1)+4-4\lambda .\end{aligned} \]

Group the last two terms: \(4-4\lambda=-4(\lambda-1)\), so

\[ \lambda^2(\lambda-1)-4(\lambda-1)=(\lambda-1)(\lambda^2-4)=0 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ (\lambda-1)(\lambda-2)(\lambda+2)=0 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \lambda_1=1,\ \lambda_2=2,\ \lambda_3=-2 . \]

Step 3: eigenvectors. Solve \((\lambda I-A)\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\), writing \(\mathbf v=(a,b,c)\).

\[ \lambda_1=1:\ \begin{bmatrix}1&-1&0\\0&1&-1\\4&-4&0\end{bmatrix}\mathbf v_1=\mathbf 0 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix} \]

(row 1: \(a=b\); row 2: \(b=c\); row 3 is \(4\times\)row 1.)

\[ \lambda_2=2:\ \begin{bmatrix}2&-1&0\\0&2&-1\\4&-4&1\end{bmatrix}\mathbf v_2=\mathbf 0 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf v_2=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\4\end{bmatrix} \]

(row 1: \(b=2a\); row 2: \(c=2b=4a\); row 3: \(4-8+4=0\ \checkmark\).)

\[ \lambda_3=-2:\ \begin{bmatrix}-2&-1&0\\0&-2&-1\\4&-4&-3\end{bmatrix}\mathbf v_3=\mathbf 0 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf v_3=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\\4\end{bmatrix} \]

(row 1: \(b=-2a\); row 2: \(c=-2b=4a\); row 3: \(4+8-12=0\ \checkmark\).) Every eigenvector has the pattern \((1,\lambda,\lambda^2)\), because the rows of \(A\) say "next entry = derivative".

So \(Q=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\1&2&-2\\1&4&4\end{bmatrix}\) and \(D=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&-2\end{bmatrix}\).

Step 4: solve and go back.

\[ \begin{bmatrix}u_1'\\u_2'\\u_3'\end{bmatrix}=D\begin{bmatrix}u_1\\u_2\\u_3\end{bmatrix}\ \Rightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}u_1\\u_2\\u_3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}c_1e^{t}\\c_2e^{2t}\\c_3e^{-2t}\end{bmatrix}, \] \[ \begin{bmatrix}x\\x'\\x''\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\1&2&-2\\1&4&4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_1e^{t}\\c_2e^{2t}\\c_3e^{-2t}\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}x\\x'\\x''\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}c_1e^{t}+c_2e^{2t}+c_3e^{-2t}\\c_1e^{t}+2c_2e^{2t}-2c_3e^{-2t}\\c_1e^{t}+4c_2e^{2t}+4c_3e^{-2t}\end{bmatrix}. \]
Answer

The general solution of \(x'''-x''-4x'+4x=0\) is

\[ x(t)=c_1e^{t}+c_2e^{2t}+c_3e^{-2t} , \]

with \(c_1,c_2,c_3\) any constants (fixed by three starting values \(x(0)\), \(x'(0)\), \(x''(0)\)).

In words: the first row is the answer; the second and third rows are its first and second derivatives, which you can check by differentiating the first row.

Going further: a shortcut, and a check

Try \(x=e^{\lambda t}\) directly: \(x'=\lambda e^{\lambda t}\), \(x''=\lambda^2e^{\lambda t}\), \(x'''=\lambda^3e^{\lambda t}\), so the equation becomes \((\lambda^3-\lambda^2-4\lambda+4)e^{\lambda t}=0\). The bracket is exactly \(\det(\lambda I-A)\) from step 2. So for a single equation you can read the polynomial straight off the coefficients (\(x'''\to\lambda^3\), \(x''\to\lambda^2\), …), and the matrix route above explains why the answer is a combination of these exponentials. Check one term: \(x=e^{2t}\) gives \(8-4-8+4=0\ \checkmark\).

You can now: turn a third-order (or second-order) linear equation into a system \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x\); find its eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the form \((1,\lambda,\lambda^2)\); write the general solution; and use the shortcut \(x=e^{\lambda t}\) to check it.

8Simple harmonic motion (optional) ~6 min

What is this?

Two masses joined by springs pull on each other, so their motions are tangled. There are two special ways to start them, the eigenvectors: both swinging the same way (slow), or swinging against each other (fast). Started in one of these shapes, the system keeps that shape and simply oscillates. Any other motion is a mix of the two.

This section is optional, and no past paper asks about it. It shows the same diagonalization idea on a physical system with second derivatives, where the uncoupled equations give oscillations (sines and cosines) instead of exponentials. Read it for understanding; skip it if time is short.

The set-up

Two masses are connected by three identical springs between two walls. The displacements of the two masses from their equilibrium (rest) positions are \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\) (positive = to the right).

Each spring pushes or pulls with force \(k\times\)(its stretch) (Hooke's law), and Newton's second law says mass \(\times\) acceleration \(=\) total force:

\[ m_1x''(t)=-kx(t)+k\big(y(t)-x(t)\big), \] \[ m_2y''(t)=-ky(t)-k\big(y(t)-x(t)\big). \]

For \(m_1\): the left spring is stretched by \(x\) and pulls it back (\(-kx\)); the middle spring is stretched by \(y-x\) and pulls it towards \(m_2\) (\(+k(y-x)\)). For \(m_2\): the right spring is squeezed by \(y\) and pushes it back (\(-ky\)); the middle spring pulls it back towards \(m_1\) (\(-k(y-x)\)).

Take \(m_2=2m_1=m\), so \(m_1=\frac m2\). Dividing by the masses:

\[ x''(t)=\frac km\big(2y(t)-4x(t)\big), \] \[ y''(t)=\frac km\big(x(t)-2y(t)\big). \]

In matrix form, with \(\gamma=\frac km\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}x''\\y''\end{bmatrix}=\frac km\begin{bmatrix}-4&2\\1&-2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\gamma A\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}. \]

Eigenpairs of \(A\).

\[ \begin{aligned} &\det(\lambda I-A)\\ &=(\lambda+4)(\lambda+2)-2\\ &=\lambda^2+6\lambda+6=0 \end{aligned} \]

gives \(\lambda=-3\pm\sqrt3\), with eigenpairs

\[ (\lambda_1,\mathbf v_1)=\left(\sqrt3-3,\ \begin{bmatrix}\sqrt3-1\\1\end{bmatrix}\right), \] \[ (\lambda_2,\mathbf v_2)=\left(-\sqrt3-3,\ \begin{bmatrix}\sqrt3+1\\-1\end{bmatrix}\right). \]

Check \(\mathbf v_1\) with the second row of \(A\): \((\sqrt3-1)-2=\sqrt3-3=\lambda_1\times1\ \checkmark\). Both eigenvalues are negative (\(\lambda_1\approx-1.27\), \(\lambda_2\approx-4.73\)).

Uncouple. With \(Q=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt3-1&\sqrt3+1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\end{bmatrix}=Q^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}\), exactly as in section 6:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}x''\\y''\end{bmatrix}=\gamma QDQ^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \Rightarrow\ Q^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}x''\\y''\end{bmatrix}=\gamma DQ^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}u''\\v''\end{bmatrix}=\gamma D\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\end{bmatrix}. \]

Solving \(u''=-\omega^2u\)

To solve for \(u\): \(\dfrac{d^2u}{dt^2}=(\sqrt3-3)\gamma u\), a negative number times \(u\). Let \(u=a\sin\alpha t+b\cos\alpha t\) be a solution. Then

\[ u'=a\alpha\cos\alpha t-b\alpha\sin\alpha t, \] \[ u''=-\alpha^2(a\sin\alpha t+b\cos\alpha t)=-\alpha^2u , \]

so we need \(-\alpha^2=(\sqrt3-3)\gamma\), i.e. \(\alpha=\sqrt{(3-\sqrt3)\gamma}\). Similarly

\[ \frac{d^2v}{dt^2}=-(3+\sqrt3)\gamma v \] \[ \Rightarrow\ v=c\sin\beta t+d\cos\beta t, \] \[ \beta=\sqrt{(3+\sqrt3)\gamma}. \]

In words: a quantity whose acceleration is a negative multiple of itself, \(u''=-\omega^2u\), oscillates like a sine wave with angular frequency \(\omega\). Each eigenvector gives one such oscillation (a normal mode).

Back to \(x\) and \(y\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=Q\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(\sqrt3-1)u+(\sqrt3+1)v\\u-v\end{bmatrix}. \]

Starting values

These conditions are starting (initial) values (sometimes loosely called boundary conditions): both masses start at rest position, \(x(0)=y(0)=0\), and the first is given a kick, \(x'(0)=u_0\), \(y'(0)=0\). At \(t=0\), \(\sin0=0\) and \(\cos0=1\), so \(u(0)=b\), \(v(0)=d\):

\[ x(0)=(\sqrt3-1)b+(\sqrt3+1)d=0, \] \[ y(0)=b-d=0\ \Rightarrow\ d=b \] \[ \Rightarrow\ 2\sqrt3\,b=0\ \Rightarrow\ b=d=0 . \]

Both masses start at their rest positions, so the cosine parts (which start at full size) must be absent; only sines, which start at 0, remain.

So \(u=a\sin\alpha t\), \(v=c\sin\beta t\), and \(u'(0)=a\alpha\), \(v'(0)=c\beta\):

\[ y'(0)=u'(0)-v'(0)=a\alpha-c\beta=0 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ c\beta=a\alpha , \] \[ \begin{aligned}x'(0)&=(\sqrt3-1)u'(0)+(\sqrt3+1)v'(0)\\&=(\sqrt3-1)a\alpha+(\sqrt3+1)a\alpha\\&=2\sqrt3\,a\alpha=u_0 .\end{aligned} \]
Result \[ a=\frac{u_0}{2\sqrt3\,\alpha},\quad c=\frac{u_0}{2\sqrt3\,\beta}, \] \[ u(t)=\frac{u_0\sin\alpha t}{2\sqrt3\,\alpha},\quad v(t)=\frac{u_0\sin\beta t}{2\sqrt3\,\beta}, \]

and \(x=(\sqrt3-1)u+(\sqrt3+1)v\), \(y=u-v\).

In words: the motion is a mix of a slow mode (\(\alpha\), shape \(\mathbf v_1\approx(0.73,1)\): both masses swing the same way) and a fast mode (\(\beta\), shape \(\mathbf v_2\approx(2.73,-1)\): the masses swing in opposite directions). Since \(\beta/\alpha=(3+\sqrt3)/\sqrt6\approx1.93\) is not a whole-number ratio, the mix never repeats exactly, which is why the graph below looks irregular.

9Earlier syllabus: difference equations ~4 min

What is this?

Run the machine once a year. Working out \(A^k\mathbf x_0\) by \(k\) multiplications is slow, but along the machine's favourite directions it only stretches, so \(k\) runs simply stretch \(k\) times: by \(\lambda^k\). Split the start into eigenvector pieces, raise each eigenvalue to the power \(k\), and add the pieces back. Powers become easy.

The 2026 course stops at differential equations, but four past papers (Dec 2025 Q15(i), Dec 2024 Q12, Dec 2017 Q3(c), Dec 2012 Q3(b)) ask the "step by step" version: a vector that changes in jumps, \(\mathbf x_{k+1}=A\mathbf x_k\). The method is the same eigenvector splitting as section 5, with powers \(\lambda^k\) in place of \(e^{\lambda t}\).

Earlier syllabus — not taught in 2026

Taught here because older past papers ask it. All such questions are solved on the earlier-syllabus page.

The equation. A (linear) difference equation is \(\mathbf x_{k+1}=A\mathbf x_k\) for \(k=0,1,2,\dots\), with a given start \(\mathbf x_0\). You met one in L3, section 14: populations of a city and its suburbs, year by year. Repeating the step gives \(\mathbf x_k=A^k\mathbf x_0\); the task is a closed form (a formula in \(k\)) instead of \(k\) multiplications.

The key fact. If \(A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v\), then \(A^2\mathbf v=A(\lambda\mathbf v)=\lambda^2\mathbf v\), and in general \(A^k\mathbf v=\lambda^k\mathbf v\). So split the start into eigenvectors, \(\mathbf x_0=c_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf v_n\) (solve \(Q\mathbf c=\mathbf x_0\)), and then

\[ \mathbf x_k=c_1\lambda_1^k\mathbf v_1+c_2\lambda_2^k\mathbf v_2+\cdots+c_n\lambda_n^k\mathbf v_n . \]
Reading the formula
\(\mathbf v_i\)
a favourite direction of the machine: one run only stretches it.
\(\lambda_i^k\)
\(k\) runs stretch that piece \(k\) times over.
\(c_i\)
how much of the start lies along \(\mathbf v_i\).
a term with \(\lambda_i=1\)
kept the same every step: this is where a population settles.

In words: each eigenvector piece is multiplied by its eigenvalue at every step. Equivalently \(A^k=QD^kQ^{-1}\) with \(D^k=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k,\dots,\lambda_n^k)\).

What is this? (long-run behaviour)

Picture people moving between a city and its suburbs every year, with nobody born, dying or leaving (each column of the matrix adds up to 1). The piece along the eigenvalue-1 direction is kept exactly each year. Every other piece here has \(|\lambda|\lt1\), so it shrinks year after year. In the long run only the eigenvalue-1 piece is left: the population settles into that direction, a steady state where the moves in each direction balance. This needs every other \(|\lambda|\lt1\), as in the example below.

Long-run behaviour (\(k\to\infty\)). A term with \(|\lambda|\lt1\) dies away; \(\lambda=1\) stays constant; \(|\lambda|\gt1\) blows up; a negative \(\lambda\) flips sign every step. So \(\mathbf x_k\) approaches the part along eigenvalue 1 if every other \(|\lambda|\lt1\), and if some \(|\lambda|\gt1\) it grows in the direction of the eigenvector with the largest \(|\lambda|\) (whose \(c_i\ne0\)). An eigenvalue \(0\) wipes its piece out after one step.

Example (the population model of L3). \(M=\begin{bmatrix}0.95&0.03\\0.05&0.97\end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf x_0=(600{,}000,\ 400{,}000)\).

\[ \begin{aligned}&\det(\lambda I-M)\\&=(\lambda-0.95)(\lambda-0.97)-0.0015\\&=\lambda^2-1.92\lambda+0.92\\&=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-0.92).\end{aligned} \]

\(\lambda=1\): \(-0.05a+0.03b=0\) gives \(\mathbf v_1=(3,5)\). \(\lambda=0.92\): \(0.03a+0.03b=0\) gives \(\mathbf v_2=(1,-1)\). Split the start: \(3c_1+c_2=600{,}000\) and \(5c_1-c_2=400{,}000\), so \(c_1=125{,}000\), \(c_2=225{,}000\). Hence

\[ \mathbf x_k=125{,}000\begin{bmatrix}3\\5\end{bmatrix}+225{,}000\,(0.92)^k\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Check: \(k=1\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} &(375{,}000+207{,}000,\\ &\quad\ 625{,}000-207{,}000)\\ &=(582{,}000,\ 418{,}000), \end{aligned} \]

as in L3. As \(k\to\infty\), \((0.92)^k\to0\), so \(\mathbf x_k\to(375{,}000,\ 625{,}000)\): the steady state that L3's figure showed. The \((1,-1)\) piece only moves people between city and suburbs and shrinks by 8% a year; the eigenvalue-1 piece holds all 1,000,000 people and never changes.

A single equation with two earlier terms. \(x_k=\alpha x_{k-1}+\beta x_{k-2}\) becomes a system by the trick of section 7: put \(\mathbf y_k=\begin{bmatrix}x_k\\x_{k-1}\end{bmatrix}\). Then

\[ \mathbf y_k=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\mathbf y_{k-1}, \]

whose characteristic polynomial is \(\det\begin{bmatrix}\lambda-\alpha&-\beta\\-1&\lambda\end{bmatrix}=\lambda^2-\alpha\lambda-\beta\). If its roots (the eigenvalues) are \(r_1\) and \(r_2\), then \(\lambda^2-\alpha\lambda-\beta=(\lambda-r_1)(\lambda-r_2)\), so comparing coefficients \(\alpha=r_1+r_2\) and \(\beta=-r_1r_2\); and \(x_k=a\,r_1^k+b\,r_2^k\) when \(r_1\ne r_2\).

10Summary

  • A quadratic form is \(q(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x=\sum a_{ij}x_ix_j\) with \(A\) symmetric: squares on the diagonal, each cross coefficient split in half off the diagonal.
  • A symmetric \(A\) has real eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_n\) and an orthonormal eigenbasis; in that basis \(q=\alpha_1^2\lambda_1+\cdots+\alpha_n^2\lambda_n\) and \(\|\mathbf x\|^2=\alpha_1^2+\cdots+\alpha_n^2\).
  • On \(\|\mathbf x\|=c\): \(\max q=\lambda_1c^2\) at \(\pm c\mathbf v_1\), \(\min q=\lambda_nc^2\) at \(\pm c\mathbf v_n\) (\(\mathbf v_i\) unit eigenvectors). Every value in between is also reached.
  • Section 3: \(3x^2+4xy+5y^2\) on \(\|\mathbf x\|=2\) has maximum \(4(4+\sqrt5)\) (and minimum \(4(4-\sqrt5)\)).
  • Adding \(kI\) to \(A\) adds \(k\) to every eigenvalue, same eigenvectors.
  • \(u'=\lambda u\) has solutions \(u=ae^{\lambda t}\), \(a=u(0)\).
  • \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x\) with \(A=QDQ^{-1}\): put \(\mathbf u=Q^{-1}\mathbf x\), then \(\mathbf u'=D\mathbf u\), so \(\mathbf x(t)=c_1e^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf v_1+\cdots+c_ne^{\lambda_nt}\mathbf v_n\); the \(c_i\) solve \(Q\mathbf c=\mathbf x(0)\).
  • A higher-order equation becomes a system by naming the derivatives \(x_1=x\), \(x_2=x'\), …; section 7: \(x'''-x''-4x'+4x=0\) gives \(x=c_1e^t+c_2e^{2t}+c_3e^{-2t}\).
  • Second-order systems \(\mathbf x''=\gamma A\mathbf x\) with negative eigenvalues uncouple into oscillations \(u''=-\omega^2u\), \(u=a\sin\omega t+b\cos\omega t\) (section 8, optional).
  • Earlier syllabus: \(\mathbf x_{k+1}=A\mathbf x_k\) gives \(\mathbf x_k=\sum c_i\lambda_i^k\mathbf v_i\).
Method: solve \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x\), \(\mathbf x(0)\) given
  1. Do: Write the system as \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x\) (check each row against the question). This builds the machine; one wrong entry changes every favourite direction.
  2. Do: Find the eigenvalues from \(\det(\lambda I-A)=0\); check with the trace. If the question gives one eigenvector, use \(A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v\) to get its eigenvalue and factor the polynomial. They are the growth rates of the separate, untangled pieces.
  3. Do: Find one eigenvector for each eigenvalue (any non-zero multiple). These are the directions along which each piece moves; the scaling is absorbed by the constants.
  4. Do: Write \(\mathbf x(t)=c_1e^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf v_1+\cdots+c_ne^{\lambda_nt}\mathbf v_n\). Each piece grows on its own along its direction, and the pieces add.
  5. Do: Put \(t=0\) and solve \(c_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf v_n=\mathbf x(0)\) for the constants. At \(t=0\) every \(e^{\lambda t}=1\), so this splits the start into its eigenvector pieces.
  6. Do: Check: \(\mathbf x(0)\) comes out right, and one row of \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x\) holds. Two quick tests catch a wrong eigenvector or a slip in the constants.

11Common mistakes

✘Putting the whole cross coefficient in each off-diagonal place: \(5x^2+6xy+5y^2\to\begin{bmatrix}5&6\\6&5\end{bmatrix}\). ✔Halve it: \(\begin{bmatrix}5&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix}\). Multiply out \(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x\) to check.
✘Answering \(\lambda_{\max}\) when the constraint is \(\|\mathbf x\|=c\) with \(c\ne1\), or multiplying by \(c\) instead of \(c^2\); reading \(x^2+y^2=10\) as \(c=10\). ✔The maximum is \(\lambda_{\max}c^2\). \(\|\mathbf x\|=2\) means \(c^2=4\); \(x^2+y^2=10\) means \(c^2=10\); \(\|\mathbf x\|=10\) means \(c^2=100\).
✘Giving the raw eigenvector, such as \((1,1)\), as the maximizer. ✔The maximizer must satisfy the constraint: divide by the length, multiply by \(c\), and give both signs: \(\pm c\,\mathbf v/\|\mathbf v\|\).
✘Using the eigenvector of the wrong eigenvalue (for example the first one you computed), or for a negative eigenvalue taking the one with the largest absolute value as the maximum. ✔List the eigenvalues in order with their eigenvectors before answering. \(-7\lt3\): the maximum uses the largest signed eigenvalue.
✘In \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x\): pairing \(e^{\lambda_1t}\) with the eigenvector of \(\lambda_2\); or writing \(\mathbf x=Q^{-1}\mathbf u\) instead of \(\mathbf x=Q\mathbf u\). ✔Each eigenvector carries its own eigenvalue in the exponent: \(c_ie^{\lambda_it}\mathbf v_i\). Since \(\mathbf u=Q^{-1}\mathbf x\), going back is \(\mathbf x=Q\mathbf u\): you never need \(Q^{-1}\).
✘Dropping the constants or the \(t\): writing \(e^{5}\) or \(5^t\), or one constant for all terms. ✔One free constant per eigenvalue, each term \(c_ie^{\lambda_it}\). With a starting value, find all the constants.
✘In section 7's conversion, copying the coefficients of \(x'''-x''-4x'+4x=0\) straight into the last row as \((4,-4,-1)\). ✔Solve for the top derivative first: \(x'''=-4x+4x'+x''\), so the last row is \((-4,\ 4,\ 1)\).

12Find the mistake

1. Find the maximum of \(5x^2+6xy+5y^2\) for unit vectors \((x,y)\).

\[ A=\begin{bmatrix}5&6\\6&5\end{bmatrix}, \] \[ \det(\lambda I-A)=(\lambda-5)^2-36 \] \[ \Rightarrow\ \lambda=11\ \text{or}\ -1 . \]

"Maximum \(=11\)."

Hint

Multiply out \(\begin{bmatrix}x&y\end{bmatrix}A\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}\) with the student's \(A\). Do you get \(6xy\)?

Answer

The student's matrix gives \(5x^2+12xy+5y^2\). The symmetric matrix is \(\begin{bmatrix}5&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix}\), with \((\lambda-5)^2=9\), so \(\lambda=8\) or \(2\). The maximum is \(8\), at \(\pm\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)\). (Sanity check: \(q\big(\frac1{\sqrt2},\frac1{\sqrt2}\big)=\frac{5+6+5}{2}=8\); the student's 11 is never reached.)

2. Same form, now with \(\|\mathbf x\|=2\):

"The largest eigenvalue 8 has eigenvector \((1,1)\). So the maximum is at \(\mathbf x=(1,1)\), and it equals \(q(1,1)=5+6+5=16\)."

Hint

What is the length of \((1,1)\)? Is that point allowed?

Answer

\(\|(1,1)\|=\sqrt2\ne2\): the point is not on the constraint circle. Scale the unit eigenvector by \(c=2\): \(\mathbf x=\pm\frac{2}{\sqrt2}(1,1)=\pm(\sqrt2,\sqrt2)\), and the maximum is \(\lambda_1c^2=8\times4=32\). Check: \(q(\sqrt2,\sqrt2)=10+12+10=32\ \checkmark\).

3. Solve \(x'=x+4y\), \(y'=2x+3y\) with \(x(0)=3\), \(y(0)=0\).

"\(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&4\\2&3\end{bmatrix}\): eigenvalue \(5\) with \(\mathbf v_1=(1,1)\), eigenvalue \(-1\) with \(\mathbf v_2=(2,-1)\). So"

\[ \mathbf x(t)=c_1e^{-t}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}+c_2e^{5t}\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix}, \]

"and \(\mathbf x(0)=(3,0)\) gives \(c_1=c_2=1\)."

Hint

Differentiate the student's first term and compare with \(A\) times it.

Answer

The exponents are swapped: \(e^{-t}(1,1)\) has derivative \(-e^{-t}(1,1)\), but \(A(1,1)=(5,5)\). Each eigenvector must carry its own eigenvalue: \(\mathbf x(t)=c_1e^{5t}(1,1)+c_2e^{-t}(2,-1)\). The constants are still \(c_1=c_2=1\) (\(c_1+2c_2=3\), \(c_1-c_2=0\)), so \(x=e^{5t}+2e^{-t}\), \(y=e^{5t}-e^{-t}\).

13Concept checks

1. Without any calculation: the maximum and minimum of \(4x^2+y^2\) on the unit circle, and where?

Answer

The matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) is already diagonal: eigenvalues 4 and 1 with eigenvectors \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\). Maximum 4 at \((\pm1,0)\), minimum 1 at \((0,\pm1)\). Directly: \(4x^2+y^2=1+3x^2\), largest when \(x^2=1\).

2. A symmetric \(3\times3\) matrix \(A\) has eigenvalues \(5,2,-1\). What are the maximum and minimum of \(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x\) on \(\|\mathbf x\|=3\)? And of \(\mathbf x^T(A+2I)\mathbf x\) on unit vectors?

Answer

On \(\|\mathbf x\|=3\): \(5\times9=45\) and \(-1\times9=-9\). \(A+2I\) has eigenvalues \(7,4,1\), so on unit vectors: maximum 7, minimum 1.

3. Can \(x^2+4xy+y^2\) equal 10 for some unit vector \((x,y)\)?

Answer

No. Its matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}\) has eigenvalues 3 and \(-1\), so on the unit circle the form only takes values in \([-1,3]\).

4. \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x\) where \(A\) has eigenvalues \(-1\) and \(-3\). What happens to \(\mathbf x(t)\) as \(t\to\infty\)? What if the eigenvalues are \(2\) and \(-1\)?

Answer

Both \(e^{-t}\) and \(e^{-3t}\) go to 0, so \(\mathbf x(t)\to\mathbf 0\) from every start. With \(2\) and \(-1\): \(\mathbf x(t)=c_1e^{2t}\mathbf v_1+c_2e^{-t}\mathbf v_2\) grows without bound along \(\pm\mathbf v_1\), unless \(c_1=0\) (a start on the \(\mathbf v_2\) line), when it goes to \(\mathbf 0\).

5. Write \(x''+3x'+2x=0\) as a system \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x\) and find its general solution.

Answer

With \(x_1=x\), \(x_2=x'\): \(x_1'=x_2\), \(x_2'=-2x_1-3x_2\), so \(A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-2&-3\end{bmatrix}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \det(\lambda I-A)&=\lambda(\lambda+3)+2\\ &=(\lambda+1)(\lambda+2), \end{aligned} \]

eigenvalues \(-1,-2\), so \(x=c_1e^{-t}+c_2e^{-2t}\).

6. For the maximum of a quadratic form you must normalize the eigenvector; for \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x\) you need not. Why the difference?

Answer

In the maximum problem the eigenvector itself is the answer, and it must have length \(c\). In the differential equation each eigenvector is multiplied by a free constant \(c_i\) that is fixed later by \(\mathbf x(0)\), so any scaling is absorbed into \(c_i\).
