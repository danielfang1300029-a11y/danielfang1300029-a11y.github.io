Vectors

0Where this chapter sits

A vector is a list of walking instructions: \([3,2]^T\) means "go 3 along the first axis, then 2 along the second", drawn as an arrow. This chapter asks where you can get to by mixing a few given arrows, and whether any of them is wasted. It is L1's linear systems read column by column ("can the right-hand side be built out of the columns?"), and the working is still row reduction, as in L1 section 7.

Chapter on one page
  1. §1 A vector is an arrow: an ordered list of steps, one per axis. \(\mathbb R^n\) is all lists of \(n\) numbers.
  2. §2 Adding is walking one arrow, then the other; scaling is walking further, shorter or backwards.
  3. §3 A linear combination is a recipe: so much of each arrow, all added up.
  4. §4 Can some recipe reach \(\mathbf b\)? Put the arrows in as columns, \(\mathbf b\) last, and row reduce.
  5. §5 The span is everywhere the recipes can reach: a line or a plane through the origin, or the whole space.
  6. §6 Example 2: \(\mathbf b\) lies off the plane, so no recipe works.
  7. §7 Independent means no arrow is redundant: the only recipe that brings you back to the start is "none of each". Test: a pivot in every column.
  8. §8 Dependent means at least one arrow can be built from the others.
  9. §9 A basis reaches everything with nothing spare. Its size is the dimension (beyond the handouts).
  10. §10 The diet: each food is a nutrient vector, and the amounts are the recipe.
  11. §11 to §15 Summary, common mistakes, practice and the exam taster.

1Vectors in ℝ², ℝ³ and ℝⁿ ~7 min

What is this?

A vector is a set of walking instructions from a fixed starting point, the origin. The column with entries 3 and 2 says "walk 3 along the first axis, then 2 along the second". Drawn as an arrow from the start to where you end up, its length is how far away you are and its direction is which way you went. The order matters: the first number is always the step along the first axis, so swapping the numbers gives a different walk. In space there is a third instruction (up), and with \(n\) entries there are \(n\) instructions, one per axis, even when you can no longer picture the walk.

Slide 3 says why the chapter exists: important properties of linear systems can be described with the concept and notation of vectors. It sets three kinds of object side by side.

Scalar, vector, matrix (slide 3) \[ \underbrace{1}_{\text{scalar}}\qquad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{column vector}}\quad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&2\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{row vector}}\qquad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&3\\2&4\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{matrix}} \]
  • A scalar is a single number.
  • A vector is an ordered list of numbers, written in a column (a column vector) or in a row (a row vector). The numbers are its entries (or elements, or components).
  • A matrix is a 2-D array of numbers (rows and columns), as in L1 section 7.

In words: a scalar is one number, a vector is a list of numbers in which the order matters, a matrix is a table of numbers.

"Ordered" matters: \(\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}\) are different vectors. In this course vectors are column vectors unless said otherwise; they are printed in bold (\(\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf b\)), and in handwriting you underline them (\(\underline u\)) or put an arrow on top. To save space, a column vector is often written on one line as a transposed row: \([3,2]^T\) means \(\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix}\) (the \(T\), "transpose", turns a row into a column; it comes back in L3). The past papers use this a lot.

Points of the plane as vectors (slides 4 and 5)

A real number is a point on the real line \(\mathbb R\). To describe a point on a plane, called \(\mathbb R^2\) ("R two"), we use two numbers, for example \((3,2)\): go 3 along the \(x_1\)-axis and 2 up the \(x_2\)-axis. This is the rectangular or Cartesian coordinate system of HKDSE, with the axes called \(x_1,x_2\) instead of \(x,y\).

Slide 4's picture is the meeting point of two lines \(\ell_1,\ell_2\) at \((3,2)\), the solution \(x_1=3,\ x_2=2\) of a system of two equations. (It is the system \(x_1-2x_2=-1,\ -x_1+3x_2=3\) of L1 section 3.) The slide writes the solution in two ways:

\[ \text{column vector } \begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \text{row vector } [x_1,x_2]=[3,2]. \]

Slide 5: we can represent the coordinates of the point as \(\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}\) or \(\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix}\): we have a vector! And a vector has a magnitude and a direction: drawn as an arrow from the origin \((0,0)\) to the point, its length is the magnitude and the way it points is the direction.

Beyond the handouts: magnitude

The magnitude (length) of \(\mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}\) is \(\|\mathbf x\|=\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2}\) by Pythagoras, as in M2's \(|\overrightarrow{OP}|\). For \(\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix}\) it is \(\sqrt{13}\). Lengths are used properly in chapter L7 (orthogonality); in this chapter only the arithmetic of vectors matters.

Example (slide 6): vectors drawn in the plane \[ \mathbf u=\begin{bmatrix}2\\2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf v=\begin{bmatrix}3\\-1\end{bmatrix}, \] \[ \mathbf w=\begin{bmatrix}-2\\-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf 0=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}. \]

Each vector is an arrow from the origin to its point: \(\mathbf u\) to \((2,2)\), up and to the right; \(\mathbf v\) to \((3,-1)\), right and slightly down; \(\mathbf w\) to \((-2,-1)\), left and down. The zero vector \(\mathbf 0\) is the origin itself: an arrow of length 0 (with no direction).

The two coordinates of the point are the two entries of the vector; the arrow from the origin shows its direction and its length.

Slide 8: similarly, a point in an \(n\)-dimensional space \(\mathbb R^n\) is described by a vector with \(n\) entries, though you can no longer "see" it:

\[ \mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\\vdots\\x_n\end{bmatrix}. \]
What is this? (ℝⁿ)

\(\mathbb R^n\) is the collection of every possible walk with \(n\) instructions. \(\mathbb R^2\) is every walk on a flat floor, \(\mathbb R^3\) every walk in a room. Two vectors are equal when they are the same walk: the same instruction along every axis.

Definition: ℝⁿ (slides 4 to 8)

\(\mathbb R^n\) is the set of all vectors with \(n\) real entries. \(\mathbb R^2\) is the plane, \(\mathbb R^3\) is space. Two vectors are equal exactly when they have the same number of entries and their corresponding entries are equal (slide 19).

In words: \(\mathbb R^n\) is "all lists of \(n\) numbers"; for \(n=2,3\) you can draw them, for larger \(n\) you just compute with them.

Why bother with \(n\gt3\)? A system with 5 unknowns has solutions \((x_1,\dots,x_5)\), which are vectors in \(\mathbb R^5\); the four flows of a traffic network, the prices of three sectors (L1 section 11) are vectors too. The algebra is the same in every \(\mathbb R^n\); only the pictures stop at 3.

2Operations on vectors ~7 min

What is this?

Adding two vectors is doing one walk and then the other: walk \(\mathbf u\), then walk \(\mathbf v\) from where you stopped, and you end at \(\mathbf u+\mathbf v\). Multiplying by a number is the same walk stretched: 2 times means twice as far the same way, \(-1\) times means the same distance backwards, 0 times means staying put. Subtracting \(\mathbf v\) is walking \(\mathbf v\) backwards.

Two operations are all we need: adding two vectors, and multiplying a vector by a number.

Addition (slides 9 to 11)

Definition: vector addition (slide 9)

As the entries of a vector are coordinates, adding two vectors means adding corresponding entries:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}2\\2\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}-6\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2-6\\2+1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-4\\3\end{bmatrix}. \]

In words: first entry plus first entry, second plus second, and so on.

Sizes must match (slide 9)

If \(\mathbf a=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf b=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix}\), then \(\mathbf a+\mathbf b\) cannot be operated: it is not defined. \(\mathbf a\) is in \(\mathbb R^2\), \(\mathbf b\) in \(\mathbb R^3\), and the third entry of \(\mathbf b\) has no partner. Never "pad" \(\mathbf a\) with a 0.

Slide 10 plots \(\mathbf u=\begin{bmatrix}2\\2\end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf v=\begin{bmatrix}-6\\1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf u+\mathbf v=\begin{bmatrix}-4\\3\end{bmatrix}\): the four points \(\mathbf 0,\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf u+\mathbf v\) form a parallelogram. Slide 11 states the rule (it is the "parallelogram law" of M2 vectors):

Parallelogram rule for addition (slide 11)

If \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\) in \(\mathbb R^2\) are represented as points in the plane, then \(\mathbf u+\mathbf v\) corresponds to the fourth vertex of the parallelogram whose other vertices are \(\mathbf u\), \(\mathbf 0\) and \(\mathbf v\).

In words: walk along \(\mathbf u\), then along \(\mathbf v\) (or the other way round); you end at \(\mathbf u+\mathbf v\), the far corner of the parallelogram.

Scalar multiplication (slide 12)

Definition: multiplying by a scalar (slide 12)

If a vector is multiplied by a scalar \(c\), all its entries are scaled by the same factor. For example, if \(\mathbf u=\begin{bmatrix}3\\-1\end{bmatrix}\) then

\[ 5\mathbf u=5\begin{bmatrix}3\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5\times3\\5\times(-1)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}15\\-5\end{bmatrix}. \]

In words: \(c\mathbf u\) multiplies every entry of \(\mathbf u\) by \(c\).

Graphically, scaling changes the magnitude along the direction of the original vector. Slide 12's picture shows, for the same \(\mathbf u=\begin{bmatrix}3\\-1\end{bmatrix}\): \(2\mathbf u=\begin{bmatrix}6\\-2\end{bmatrix}\) (twice as long, same direction), \(0\mathbf u=\mathbf 0\) (the origin), and \(-\tfrac23\mathbf u=\begin{bmatrix}-2\\ \tfrac23\end{bmatrix}\) (shorter, and pointing the opposite way). All multiples of \(\mathbf u\) lie on one line through the origin; this line is important in section 5.

Subtraction (slide 13)

For subtraction, \(\mathbf u-\mathbf v\) is interpreted as \(\mathbf u+(-1)\mathbf v\): add the vector \(-\mathbf v\), which has the same length as \(\mathbf v\) but points the opposite way. In entries, subtract corresponding entries: with slide 10's vectors, \(\mathbf u-\mathbf v=\begin{bmatrix}2-(-6)\\2-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}8\\1\end{bmatrix}\).

Subtraction picture (slide 13)

For vectors in \(\mathbb R^2\), the points \(\mathbf 0\), \(\mathbf u\), \(-\mathbf v\) and \(\mathbf u-\mathbf v\) are also the vertices of a parallelogram.

In words: \(\mathbf u-\mathbf v\) is the parallelogram rule applied to \(\mathbf u\) and \(-\mathbf v\). Equivalently, \(\mathbf u-\mathbf v\) is the arrow that goes from the tip of \(\mathbf v\) to the tip of \(\mathbf u\), moved to start at the origin.

The rules of vector algebra (slide 14)

What is this? (the rules)

Every rule on this slide is a fact about walks. East then north ends at the same place as north then east. Standing still adds nothing. Walking a trip there and back brings you home. Doing a whole trip twice as far is the same as doing each leg twice as far.

Algebraic properties of ℝⁿ (slide 14)

For all \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\) in \(\mathbb R^n\) and all scalars \(c\) and \(d\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{(i)}\ \ \mathbf u+\mathbf v=\mathbf v+\mathbf u\\ &\text{(ii)}\ \ (\mathbf u+\mathbf v)+\mathbf w=\mathbf u+(\mathbf v+\mathbf w)\\ &\text{(iii)}\ \ \mathbf u+\mathbf 0=\mathbf 0+\mathbf u=\mathbf u\\ &\text{(iv)}\ \ \mathbf u+(-\mathbf u)=-\mathbf u+\mathbf u=\mathbf 0 \end{aligned} \]

where \(-\mathbf u\) denotes \((-1)\mathbf u\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{(v)}\ \ c(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)=c\mathbf u+c\mathbf v\\ &\text{(vi)}\ \ (c+d)\mathbf u=c\mathbf u+d\mathbf u\\ &\text{(vii)}\ \ c(d\mathbf u)=(cd)\mathbf u\\ &\text{(viii)}\ \ 1\mathbf u=\mathbf u \end{aligned} \]

In words: vectors obey the same rules as ordinary numbers for adding, and for multiplying by numbers: the order and grouping of a sum do not matter, \(\mathbf 0\) changes nothing, every vector has a negative, and brackets expand as usual.

Reading the formula
(i), (ii)
the order and grouping of the legs of a walk do not change where you end up.
(iii)
\(\mathbf 0\) is "stay where you are": adding it changes nothing.
(iv)
walk \(\mathbf u\), then walk it backwards: you are back at the start.
(v)
stretching a two-leg trip by \(c\) is the same as stretching each leg by \(c\).
(vi), (vii)
\(c\) times \(\mathbf u\) and then \(d\) more times is \(c+d\) times; \(d\) times, then all of that \(c\) times, is \(cd\) times.
(viii)
doing the walk once is just the walk.

Each rule is true because it is true entry by entry for ordinary numbers. For instance (v) in \(\mathbb R^2\): \(c\begin{bmatrix}u_1+v_1\\u_2+v_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}cu_1+cv_1\\cu_2+cv_2\end{bmatrix}=c\mathbf u+c\mathbf v\). In practice they mean you can rearrange vector expressions exactly as you would rearrange \(3a+2b\) in algebra; for example \(2(\mathbf u-3\mathbf v)+5\mathbf v=2\mathbf u-\mathbf v\).

You can now: add, subtract and scale vectors entry by entry; say when a sum is not defined (different sizes); draw \(\mathbf u+\mathbf v\), \(c\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf u-\mathbf v\) with the parallelogram rule; rearrange vector expressions with the slide 14 rules.

3Linear combinations ~3 min

What is this?

A linear combination is a recipe. The vectors are the ingredients, here arrows you are allowed to walk, and the weights say how much of each to use. "Three of \(\mathbf v_1\) and two of \(\mathbf v_2\)" means: walk \(\mathbf v_1\) three times, then \(\mathbf v_2\) twice, and see where you end up. A weight can be a fraction, negative (walk that arrow backwards) or zero (leave that ingredient out).

Combine the two operations of section 2: scale some vectors, then add the results.

Definition: linear combination (slide 15)

Given vectors \(\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots,\mathbf v_p\) in \(\mathbb R^n\) and scalars \(c_1,c_2,\dots,c_p\), the vector

\[ \mathbf y=c_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\mathbf v_2+\cdots+c_p\mathbf v_p \]

is called a linear combination of \(\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_p\) with weights \(c_1,\dots,c_p\). The weights can be any real numbers, including zero.

Reading the formula
\(\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_p\)
the ingredients: the arrows you are allowed to walk.
\(c_1,\dots,c_p\)
the amounts: how many times to walk each arrow (negative means backwards, 0 means not at all).
\(c_k\mathbf v_k\)
one leg of the trip.
\(+\)
do the legs one after the other; the order does not matter.
\(\mathbf y\)
where the whole recipe takes you.

In words: a linear combination is "some number times each vector, all added up". The numbers are the weights.

Slide 15's examples of linear combinations of \(\mathbf v_1\) and \(\mathbf v_2\):

\[ \sqrt3\,\mathbf v_1+\mathbf v_2,\qquad \tfrac12\mathbf v_1\ \left(=\tfrac12\mathbf v_1+0\mathbf v_2\right), \] \[ \mathbf 0\ \left(=0\mathbf v_1+0\mathbf v_2\right). \]

The weights need not be whole numbers (\(\sqrt3\)), a weight of 0 simply leaves a vector out (\(\frac12\mathbf v_1\)), and with all weights 0 you always get the zero vector. Remember that last one: it is the reason \(\mathbf 0\) is in every span (section 5) and the starting point of independence (section 7).

Try it (written for these notes)

With \(\mathbf v_1=\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf v_2=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\2\end{bmatrix}\), compute \(\mathbf y=\mathbf v_1+2\mathbf v_2\).

Answer
\[ \mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}+2\begin{bmatrix}-1\\2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3-2\\1+4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\5\end{bmatrix}. \]

The reverse question is the important one: given a vector \(\mathbf b\), can we find weights that produce it? Play with the figure: with two vectors that point in different directions you can reach any target in the plane; with two vectors on the same line you cannot.

Build a target from c₁v₁ + c₂v₂

\(c_1\mathbf v_1\)\(c_2\mathbf v_2\)\(c_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\mathbf v_2\)

The target is \(\mathbf b=[1,5]^T\). With the first pair it is reached at \(c_1=1,\ c_2=2\). With the second pair, \(\mathbf v_2=2\mathbf v_1\), so every combination lies on the dashed line through the origin and \(\mathbf b\) can never be reached.

You can now: compute a linear combination from given weights; say what the weights mean; explain why weights of zero always give \(\mathbf 0\); see from a picture whether two arrows in the plane can reach a given target.

4Example 1: is b a linear combination? ~6 min

What is this?

Section 3 followed a recipe to see where it leads. Example 1 runs it backwards: you are given a destination \(\mathbf b\) and two arrows, and you ask whether some amount of the first arrow plus some amount of the second lands exactly on \(\mathbf b\). Landing exactly means matching \(\mathbf b\) along every axis at once, so each axis gives one equation, and the two amounts are the unknowns.

Example 1 (slide 16)

Let

\[ \mathbf a_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\\-5\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf a_2=\begin{bmatrix}2\\5\\6\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf b=\begin{bmatrix}7\\4\\-3\end{bmatrix}. \]

Determine whether \(\mathbf b\) can be written as a linear combination of \(\mathbf a_1\) and \(\mathbf a_2\).

Solution idea (slide 17). Let \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) be the weights such that \(c_1\mathbf a_1+c_2\mathbf a_2=\mathbf b\). If there is a solution \(c_1,c_2\) of this equation, then \(\mathbf b\) can be written as a linear combination of \(\mathbf a_1\) and \(\mathbf a_2\); if there is none, it cannot.

Turn the vector equation into a linear system (slides 18 and 19). We solve

\[ c_1\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\\-5\end{bmatrix}+c_2\begin{bmatrix}2\\5\\6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}7\\4\\-3\end{bmatrix}. \]

Scaling and adding entry by entry, the left side is one vector:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}c_1+2c_2\\-2c_1+5c_2\\-5c_1+6c_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}7\\4\\-3\end{bmatrix}. \]

The two vectors on both sides are equal iff (if and only if) their corresponding entries are equal. So the vector equation is the same thing as three ordinary equations: Landing on \(\mathbf b\) means getting the first-axis position right, the second-axis position right and the third-axis position right: one equation per axis.

\[ \begin{cases} c_1+2c_2=7 & (1)\\ -2c_1+5c_2=4 & (2)\\ -5c_1+6c_2=-3 & (3) \end{cases} \]

"iff" is short for "if and only if": each statement implies the other. You will see it all through this chapter.

Gaussian elimination (slides 20 and 21)

Clear \(c_1\) from (2) and (3) using (1):

\(2\times(1)+(2)\): Adding a multiple of one true equation to another gives another true equation; the multiple 2 is chosen so that \(c_1\) drops out.

\[ \begin{array}{rrr|r} &2c_1&+4c_2&14\\ +&-2c_1&+5c_2&4\\ \hline &0&+9c_2&18 \end{array} \]

so \(c_2=\frac{18}9=2\). And \(5\times(1)+(3)\):

\[ \begin{array}{rrr|r} &5c_1&+10c_2&35\\ +&-5c_1&+6c_2&-3\\ \hline &0c_1&+16c_2&32 \end{array} \]

so \(c_2=\frac{32}{16}=2\).

The system is now \(c_1+2c_2=7\), \(c_2=2\), \(c_2=2\). The last two agree, so there is no contradiction. From (1): \(c_1+2\times2=7\), so \(c_1=7-4=3\).

Checking (slide 21)

Put \(c_1=3,\ c_2=2\) into (1), (2), (3):

\[ \begin{aligned} (1)&:\ 3+2\times2=7\ \checkmark\\ (2)&:\ -6+10=4\ \checkmark\\ (3)&:\ -15+12=-3\ \checkmark \end{aligned} \]

So \(\mathbf b\) is a linear combination of \(\mathbf a_1\) and \(\mathbf a_2\):

\[ \mathbf b=3\mathbf a_1+2\mathbf a_2. \]

In words: three steps along \(\mathbf a_1\) plus two steps along \(\mathbf a_2\) land exactly on \(\mathbf b\).

The same working with an augmented matrix (L1 section 7), one matrix per line; the columns are \(\mathbf a_1\), \(\mathbf a_2\) and, after the bar, \(\mathbf b\):

\[ \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1&2&7\\ -2&5&4\\ -5&6&-3 \end{array}\right] \] \[ \xrightarrow[R_3+5R_1]{R_2+2R_1} \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1&2&7\\ 0&9&18\\ 0&16&32 \end{array}\right] \] \[ \xrightarrow[R_3\div16]{R_2\div9} \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1&2&7\\ 0&1&2\\ 0&1&2 \end{array}\right] \] \[ \xrightarrow{R_3-R_2} \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1&2&7\\ 0&1&2\\ 0&0&0 \end{array}\right] \]

No row \([\,0\ 0\mid\text{non-zero}\,]\), so the system is consistent; back substitution gives \(c_2=2,\ c_1=3\).

A minor change (slides 22 to 24)

Now change the middle entry of \(\mathbf b\) from 4 to 5: \(\mathbf b=\begin{bmatrix}7\\5\\-3\end{bmatrix}\). The system becomes

\[ \begin{cases} c_1+2c_2=7 & (1)\\ -2c_1+5c_2=5 & (2)\\ -5c_1+6c_2=-3 & (3) \end{cases} \]

The same two steps give

\[ 2\times(1)+(2):\ 9c_2=19\ \Rightarrow\ c_2=\tfrac{19}9=2+\tfrac19 \] \[ 5\times(1)+(3):\ 16c_2=32\ \Rightarrow\ c_2=2. \]

Originally the system reduced to \(c_1+2c_2=7,\ c_2=2,\ c_2=2\) and \(\mathbf b=3\mathbf a_1+2\mathbf a_2\). Now it reduces to

\[ \begin{cases} c_1+2c_2=7 & (1)\\ c_2=2 & \\ c_2=2+\tfrac19 & \end{cases} \]

and subtracting the two \(c_2\) equations gives \(0=\frac19\), which is impossible. Two of the axes demand different amounts of \(\mathbf a_2\). One recipe cannot use two different amounts, so no recipe satisfies both. No solution \(c_1,c_2\) can be found: \(\mathbf b\) is not a linear combination of \(\mathbf a_1\) and \(\mathbf a_2\). In matrix form the last row is \(\left[\,0\ \ 0\mid \tfrac19\,\right]\) (or, without dividing, \(9R_3-16R_2\) gives \([\,0\ \ 0\mid -16\,]\)).

From slide 23 it is the other way round: (2) gave \(c_2=2+\frac19\) and (3) gave \(c_2=2\). The general rule (slide 25)

Vector equation = linear system (slide 25)

A vector equation

\[ c_1\mathbf a_1+c_2\mathbf a_2+\cdots+c_p\mathbf a_p=\mathbf b \]

has the same solution set as the linear system whose augmented matrix is

\[ \left[\,\mathbf a_1\ \ \mathbf a_2\ \ \cdots\ \ \mathbf a_p\mid\mathbf b\,\right]. \]

In particular, \(\mathbf b\) can be expressed as a linear combination of \(\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2,\dots,\mathbf a_p\) iff the linear system with this augmented matrix has a solution (is consistent).

In words: put the vectors in as columns, \(\mathbf b\) as the last column, and row reduce. Consistent means \(\mathbf b\) can be built, and the solution gives the weights; a row \([\,0\ \cdots\ 0\mid\text{non-zero}\,]\) means it cannot.

The method, step by step:

  1. Do: Put the vectors in as columns, \(\mathbf b\) as the last column. Each column is one arrow, and each row collects one axis: "the amounts of the arrows must add up to \(\mathbf b\)'s entry on this axis".
  2. Do: Row reduce. Row operations combine the axis conditions into simpler ones without changing which recipes satisfy them.
  3. Do: Look for a row \([\,0\ \cdots\ 0\mid\text{non-zero}\,]\): if there is one, \(\mathbf b\) cannot be built. That row says \(0=\) something non-zero: the axis conditions contradict each other, so no recipe lands on \(\mathbf b\).
  4. Do: Otherwise the system is consistent; back substitute. The solution is the recipe itself: the amount of each arrow.

This is the "column view" of a linear system promised in section 0: every system in L1 can be read as "which combination of the coefficient columns gives the right-hand side?".

You can now: turn "is \(\mathbf b\) a linear combination of \(\mathbf a_1,\dots,\mathbf a_p\)?" into the augmented matrix \([\,\mathbf a_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf a_p\mid\mathbf b\,]\); row reduce it and read off yes or no; find the weights when the answer is yes; tell a harmless zero row from a contradiction row.

5Span ~6 min

What is this?

The span is everywhere the recipes can reach: every place you can get to using the given arrows, in any amounts, forwards or backwards. With one arrow (not the zero arrow) you can only move back and forth along its line. With two arrows that point in different directions you can reach every point of the flat sheet they lie in, but nothing off that sheet. The start is always included, because the recipe "none of each" keeps you there.

Section 4 asked whether one vector can be built from \(\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2\). Now collect everything that can be built.

Definition: span (slide 26)

If \(\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots,\mathbf v_p\) are in \(\mathbb R^n\), then the set of all linear combinations of \(\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_p\) is denoted by \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\) and is called the subset of \(\mathbb R^n\) spanned (or generated) by \(\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_p\). That is, \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\) is the collection of all vectors that can be written in the form

\[ c_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\mathbf v_2+\cdots+c_p\mathbf v_p \]

with any scalars \(c_1,\dots,c_p\).

In words: the span is the set of every vector you can reach by scaling the given vectors and adding them, using all possible weights.

Slide 26 adds: span is also known as image or range.

Beyond the handouts: image, range, subspace

Image / range. When \(\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_p\) are the columns of a matrix \(A\) (chapter L3), their span is called the column space, range or image of \(A\): it is the set of all results \(A\mathbf x\) can produce. So "span", "range" and "image" are three names for the same set when the vectors are the columns of a matrix.

Subspace. The slides (29, 46) call a span a subspace. A subspace of \(\mathbb R^n\) is a set of vectors that contains \(\mathbf 0\) and is "closed": adding two of its vectors, or scaling one, never takes you outside it. Every span is a subspace (a combination of combinations is again a combination). In \(\mathbb R^3\) the subspaces are: just \(\{\mathbf 0\}\), lines through the origin, planes through the origin, and all of \(\mathbb R^3\). The past papers use "subspace", "vector space generated by" and "span" for the same idea.

The origin is in every span (slide 27)

The zero vector (the origin) must be in \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\). Why? Take all the weights zero, \(c_1=c_2=\cdots=c_p=0\):

\[ 0\mathbf v_1+0\mathbf v_2+\cdots+0\mathbf v_p=\mathbf 0. \]

In words: using none of each vector builds the zero vector, so every span passes through the origin.

A quick consequence: a line or plane that does not pass through the origin (such as the plane \(x_1+x_2+x_3=1\)) is never a span.

What a span looks like (slides 28 and 29)

Let \(\mathbf v\) and \(\mathbf u\) be non-zero vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\).

  • One vector (slide 28, left, Lay's Figure 10): \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v\}\) is all multiples \(c\mathbf v\): the line through the origin in the direction of \(\mathbf v\) (compare the scaling picture of slide 12).
  • Two vectors (slide 28, right, Figure 11): \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v,\mathbf u\}\) is all \(c_1\mathbf v+c_2\mathbf u\): the plane through the origin containing \(\mathbf v\) and \(\mathbf u\). The figure shades the parallelogram of \(\mathbf v\), \(\mathbf u\), \(\mathbf u+\mathbf v\) inside that plane. (This needs \(\mathbf u\) not to be a multiple of \(\mathbf v\); if it is, the span is still only a line. Section 8 returns to this.)

Span membership (slide 29)
  • The subspace spanned by \(\{\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\) may not reach the whole space (for example when \(p\lt n\): two vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\) give at most a plane).
  • Therefore, asking whether \(\mathbf b\) is in \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\) is the same as asking whether \(\mathbf b\) lies in that subspace, which is the same as asking whether the vector equation \[ c_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\mathbf v_2+\cdots+c_p\mathbf v_p=\mathbf b \] has a solution.

In words: "is \(\mathbf b\) in the span?" = "can \(\mathbf b\) be built?" = "is \([\,\mathbf v_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf v_p\mid\mathbf b\,]\) consistent?".

7Linear independence ~6 min

What is this?

A set of arrows is independent when none of them is redundant: no arrow is a trip you could already make with the others. Test it with round trips. The recipe "none of each" always leaves you at the start. If that is the only recipe that brings you back to the start, nothing is redundant. If some other recipe also brings you back, then an arrow it uses can be replaced by the rest of that round trip walked backwards, so that arrow is redundant.

By slide 27, \(\mathbf 0\) can always be built with all weights zero. The question that defines independence is: is that the only way?

Definition: linear independence (slide 33)

An indexed set of vectors \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\) in \(\mathbb R^n\) is said to be linearly independent if the vector equation

\[ x_1\mathbf v_1+x_2\mathbf v_2+\cdots+x_p\mathbf v_p=\mathbf 0 \]

has only the trivial solution \(x_1=x_2=\cdots=x_p=0\).

Intuitive understanding: none of the vectors \(\mathbf v_k\) (\(k=1,\dots,p\)) can be written as a linear combination of the others.

In words: independent means the only way to combine the vectors into \(\mathbf 0\) is to use none of each. "Trivial solution" means the all-zero solution, which always works; the question is whether anything else does.

Note on slide 33. The intuitive line reads "none of the vectors \(x_n\) (for n = 1, …, p)". The \(x\)'s are the weights, not the vectors, and \(n\) is already the size of the vectors; it should read "none of the vectors \(\mathbf v_k\) (\(k=1,\dots,p\))", as written above.

What is this? (dependence)

Dependent means there is a real round trip: a walk using the arrows, with at least one amount not zero, that ends exactly where it started. In such a trip the arrows cancel each other out, so any arrow used in it is not needed.

Definition: linear dependence (slide 34)

Conversely, the set \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\) is said to be linearly dependent if there exist \(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_p\) not all zero such that

\[ x_1\mathbf v_1+x_2\mathbf v_2+\cdots+x_p\mathbf v_p=\mathbf 0. \]

In words: dependent means there is a non-trivial recipe for \(\mathbf 0\): some weights, at least one of them non-zero, that make the vectors cancel out. Such an equation is called a linear dependence relation.

Every set is one or the other, never both. "Not all zero" means at least one weight is non-zero; the others may be zero.

Method: test for independence (from slides 35 to 38)
  1. Do: Write the matrix whose columns are \(\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_p\) (the augmented column of zeros may be left out: row operations keep it zero). Each column is one arrow; each row is the condition "back to the start along this axis".
  2. Do: Row reduce to echelon form. Row operations simplify the conditions without changing which recipes bring you back to the start.
  3. Do: Read the columns. A pivot in every column: no free variable, only the trivial solution: independent. Every amount is forced, and the forced amounts are all zero, so there is no real round trip.
  4. Do: Some column without a pivot: a free variable, which can be chosen non-zero: dependent. Set the free variable to 1 and back substitute to get a dependence relation. A free amount means you may walk that arrow and let the other arrows cancel it: a real round trip, so that arrow is redundant.

In words: independent iff every column of \([\,\mathbf v_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf v_p\,]\) has a pivot.

Example 3 (slide 35)

Determine if the following set is linearly independent:

\[ \left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix}4\\5\\6\end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}\right\}. \]

We need to solve the following equations and see whether the solutions \(x_1,x_2,x_3\) are all zero:

\[ x_1\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix}+x_2\begin{bmatrix}4\\5\\6\end{bmatrix}+x_3\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \begin{matrix}(1)\\(2)\\(3)\end{matrix} \]

Augmented matrix (slide 36):

\[ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&4&2&0\\ 2&5&1&0\\ 3&6&0&0 \end{array}\right]\quad\begin{matrix}(1)\\(2)\\(3)\end{matrix} \]

Clear column 1 (slide 36). \(2\times(1)-(2)\): \([\,2\ 8\ 4\mid0\,]-[\,2\ 5\ 1\mid0\,]=[\,0\ 3\ 3\mid0\,]\), call it (4). \(3\times(1)-(3)\): \([\,3\ 12\ 6\mid0\,]-[\,3\ 6\ 0\mid0\,]=[\,0\ 6\ 6\mid0\,]\), call it (5). (Replacing row 2 by \(2R_1-R_2\) is legal: it is "scale \(R_2\) by \(-1\), then add \(2R_1\)", and it can be undone.)

Clear column 2 (slide 37). \(2\times(4)-(5)\): \([\,0\ 6\ 6\mid0\,]-[\,0\ 6\ 6\mid0\,]=[\,0\ 0\ 0\mid0\,]\), call it (6).

\[ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&4&2&0\\ 2&5&1&0\\ 3&6&0&0 \end{array}\right] \] \[ \xrightarrow[3R_1-R_3]{2R_1-R_2} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&4&2&0\\ 0&3&3&0\\ 0&6&6&0 \end{array}\right] \] \[ \xrightarrow{2R_2-R_3} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&4&2&0\\ 0&3&3&0\\ 0&0&0&0 \end{array}\right] \]

The last row says \(0x_3=0\), true for every \(x_3\): column 3 has no pivot, so \(x_3\) is a free variable (slide 37).

Back substitution (slide 38). The echelon form is the system

\[ \begin{cases} x_1+4x_2+2x_3=0 & (1)\\ 3x_2+3x_3=0 & (4) \end{cases} \]

From (4): \(x_2=-x_3\). Into (1): \(x_1-4x_3+2x_3=0\), so \(x_1=2x_3\). The solution set is

\[ x_1=2x_3,\quad x_2=-x_3,\quad x_3\ \text{free}. \]

\(x_3\) is a free variable and we can choose it non-zero. With \(x_3=1\): \(x_1=2,\ x_2=-1,\ x_3=1\). Check: Setting \(x_3=1\) picks one particular round trip: two of \(\mathbf v_1\), one of \(\mathbf v_2\) backwards, one of \(\mathbf v_3\). Any other non-zero choice of \(x_3\) gives a multiple of it.

\[ 2\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}4\\5\\6\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2-4+2\\4-5+1\\6-6+0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}\ \checkmark \]
Conclusion (slide 38)

There is a non-trivial solution, so the set is linearly dependent. The dependence relation is \(2\mathbf v_1-\mathbf v_2+\mathbf v_3=\mathbf 0\), so for example \(\mathbf v_3=\mathbf v_2-2\mathbf v_1\): the third vector is built from the other two.

In words: one of the three vectors is redundant; all three lie in one plane through the origin.

Beyond the handouts: quick facts used in the exam
  • Too many vectors. If \(p\gt n\) (more vectors than entries), the set is always dependent: the matrix has only \(n\) rows, so at most \(n\) pivots, and some column has none. Four vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\) are always dependent.
  • Square case. \(n\) vectors in \(\mathbb R^n\) are independent iff the square matrix with these columns has a non-zero determinant (chapter L4). For Example 3, \(\det\begin{bmatrix}1&4&2\\2&5&1\\3&6&0\end{bmatrix}=0\).
  • Order does not matter. Independence is a property of the set; listing the vectors in another order changes nothing.

You can now: state the definitions of linear independence and dependence; test a set of vectors by row reducing the matrix of columns and counting pivots; write down a dependence relation from a free variable; spot at once that more than \(n\) vectors in \(\mathbb R^n\) are dependent.

8What independence and dependence mean ~6 min

What is this?

Independence is about new places to go. Each arrow of an independent set lets you reach somewhere the others could not. On a flat floor, two arrows in different directions already reach everywhere, so any third arrow is redundant. In a room, a third arrow adds something only if it sticks out of the sheet that the first two can reach.

Meaning of linear independence (slides 39 and 40)

What is the physical meaning of linear independence? Each vector in the set represents a unique, non-redundant direction or piece of information that cannot be expressed as a linear combination of the other vectors.

For a linearly independent set, no vector can be expressed as a linear combination of the other vectors.

In words: every vector of an independent set adds a new direction; removing any one of them makes the span smaller.

Slide 40's two pictures:

  • In \(\mathbb R^2\): the arrows to \((3,2)\) and \((6,2)\) point in different directions. Neither is a multiple of the other: linearly independent.
  • In \(\mathbb R^3\): \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\) span a (shaded) plane, and \(\mathbf w\) sticks out of it: "linearly independent, \(\mathbf w\) not in \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}\)". If \(\mathbf w\) were pushed down into the plane it would become a combination of \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\), and the set would be dependent.

Slide 40 in 3D: push w into the plane of u and v

\(\mathbf u=[2,0,0]^T\)\(\mathbf v=[0,2,0]^T\)\(\mathbf w=[1,1,h]^T\)

Drag to turn. Here \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}\) is the floor \(x_3=0\). Only at \(h=0\) is \(\mathbf w\) in it, and then \(\mathbf w=\tfrac12\mathbf u+\tfrac12\mathbf v\).

Two vectors (slides 41 and 42)

Two vectors \(\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\) are linearly dependent iff

\[ c_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\mathbf v_2=\mathbf 0 \]

for some \(c_1,c_2\) not both zero.

Suppose \(c_1\ne0\): divide by \(c_1\) and rearrange to get \(\mathbf v_1=-\dfrac{c_2}{c_1}\mathbf v_2\). Or suppose \(c_2\ne0\): then \(\mathbf v_2=-\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\mathbf v_1\). Either way one vector is a multiple of the other.

Two vectors (slide 42)

Two vectors are linearly dependent iff they are multiples of each other. Conversely, two vectors are linearly independent iff they are not multiples of each other.

In words: two arrows on the same line through the origin are dependent; two arrows in different directions are independent.

Slide 42's pictures: \(\mathbf v_1=(3,1)\) and \(\mathbf v_2=(6,2)\) lie on one line, \(\mathbf v_2=2\mathbf v_1\): linearly dependent (\(2\mathbf v_1-\mathbf v_2=\mathbf 0\)). \(\mathbf v_1=(3,2)\) and \(\mathbf v_2=(6,2)\): \(6=2\times3\) but \(2\ne2\times2\), not multiples: linearly independent. (The "dependent" pair is the second choice in the figure of section 3.)

To test "multiple" quickly, divide entry by entry: \([6,2]\div[3,1]\) gives \(2,2\) (all the same ratio: multiples); \([6,2]\div[3,2]\) gives \(2,1\) (different ratios: not multiples). Careful with zeros: \([0,1]\) and \([0,5]\) are multiples (\(5\) times), and \(\mathbf 0\) is a multiple (\(0\) times) of anything.

Any number of vectors (slide 43)

In general, \(\{\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\) is linearly dependent iff

\[ c_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\mathbf v_2+\cdots+c_p\mathbf v_p=\mathbf 0 \]

for some \(c_k\ (k=1,\dots,p)\) not all zero.

Suppose \(c_i\ne0\). Move every other term to the right and divide by \(c_i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf v_i=&-\frac{c_1}{c_i}\mathbf v_1-\cdots-\frac{c_{i-1}}{c_i}\mathbf v_{i-1}\\ &-\frac{c_{i+1}}{c_i}\mathbf v_{i+1}-\cdots-\frac{c_p}{c_i}\mathbf v_p. \end{aligned} \]
Reading the formula
\(c_i\ne0\)
arrow \(i\) is really used in the round trip, so you can solve for it.
\(-\dfrac{c_k}{c_i}\mathbf v_k\)
the rest of the round trip walked backwards, rescaled so that it replaces exactly one copy of \(\mathbf v_i\).
\(\mathbf v_i=\cdots\)
arrow \(i\) is a recipe of the others, so it is redundant.
Theorem (slide 43)

A set of vectors \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\) is linearly dependent iff at least one of the vectors can be expressed as a linear combination of the other vectors.

In words: dependent = some vector is redundant. (The other direction: if \(\mathbf v_i=\sum_{k\ne i}d_k\mathbf v_k\), then \(\sum_{k\ne i}d_k\mathbf v_k-1\cdot\mathbf v_i=\mathbf 0\) is a relation with the weight \(-1\ne0\).)

"At least one", not "every one"

In a dependent set, not every vector need be a combination of the others. The set \[ \left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}2\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\} \] is dependent (the first two are multiples), but \(\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}\) is not a combination of the other two: they only reach the \(x_1\)-axis. The vector you can solve for is one whose weight \(c_i\) in the relation is non-zero. Dec 2018 Q2(a)(i) asks exactly this as a true/false question.

Sets containing the zero vector (slide 44)

What is this? (the zero vector)

The zero vector is the instruction "stay where you are". Walking it any number of times changes nothing, so "one copy of the zero arrow and none of the others" is already a round trip that ends at the start.

Remark (slide 44)

If the set \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\) contains a zero vector, the set is linearly dependent. Why? Say \(\mathbf v_1=\mathbf 0\). Then

\[ c_1\mathbf 0+c_2\mathbf v_2+\cdots+c_p\mathbf v_p=\mathbf 0 \] \[ \text{with } c_1\ne0,\ c_2=c_3=\cdots=c_p=0, \]

for example \(c_1=1\). That is a solution with not all weights zero.

In words: the zero vector adds no direction at all, so it is always redundant.

You can now: explain independence as "every vector adds a new direction"; decide at a glance whether two vectors are dependent (multiples); turn a dependence relation into "this vector is a combination of the others", choosing one with a non-zero weight; say why any set containing \(\mathbf 0\) is dependent.

9Basis ~9 min

What is this?

A basis is a smallest complete toolkit of arrows: with it you can reach every place in the space (it spans), and no arrow is spare (it is independent). Because nothing is spare, every place has exactly one recipe, so the amounts work as coordinates. The standard basis gives the familiar "so many along the first axis, so many along the second". A space has many different toolkits.

A span may contain redundant vectors (section 8). A basis is a spanning set with no redundancy.

Definition: basis (slide 45)

A set of vectors \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\) forms a basis of a vector space \(V\) iff

  1. \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\) are linearly independent, and
  2. \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\) span \(V\) (every vector of \(V\) is a linear combination of them).

In words: a basis is a set of building blocks that can build everything in \(V\) (spanning) with nothing wasted (independent).

Here a vector space \(V\) means \(\mathbb R^n\) itself or a subspace of it (a span, section 5): a set of vectors in which you can add and scale freely without leaving it.

Beyond the handouts: why a basis is useful

If \(\{\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\) is a basis of \(V\), every \(\mathbf x\) in \(V\) is a combination \(\mathbf x=a_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+a_p\mathbf v_p\) in exactly one way. (If also \(\mathbf x=b_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+b_p\mathbf v_p\), subtract: \((a_1-b_1)\mathbf v_1+\cdots+(a_p-b_p)\mathbf v_p=\mathbf 0\), and independence forces every \(a_k-b_k=0\).) The weights \(a_1,\dots,a_p\) act as coordinates of \(\mathbf x\) in that basis.

Example 4: a basis of a plane (slide 46)

Example 4 (slide 46)

Let \(\mathbf v_1=\begin{bmatrix}3\\6\\2\end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf v_2=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}\). It can be shown that they are linearly independent: \(c_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\mathbf v_2=\mathbf 0\) iff \(c_1=c_2=0\). So they are a basis for the subspace \(H=\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\}\).

Showing it. Two vectors: independent iff not multiples (slide 42). The second entries are \(6\) and \(0\): a multiple \(\mathbf v_2=k\mathbf v_1\) would need \(0=6k\), so \(k=0\), but \(\mathbf v_2\ne\mathbf 0\). In full: \(c_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\mathbf v_2=\mathbf 0\) reads \(3c_1-c_2=0,\ 6c_1=0,\ 2c_1+c_2=0\); the second gives \(c_1=0\), then the first gives \(c_2=0\).

They span \(H\) by definition of \(H\), so \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\}\) is a basis of \(H\), a plane through the origin in \(\mathbb R^3\).

The slide's picture (from Lay) shows \(H\) as a plane ruled by a grid: lines through \(\mathbf v_1,2\mathbf v_1,\dots\) parallel to \(\mathbf v_2\), and through \(\mathbf v_2,2\mathbf v_2,3\mathbf v_2\) parallel to \(\mathbf v_1\). Every point \(\mathbf x=a_1\mathbf v_1+a_2\mathbf v_2\) of \(H\) sits on this grid; the marked point is \(\mathbf x=2\mathbf v_1+3\mathbf v_2=\begin{bmatrix}6-3\\12+0\\4+3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\12\\7\end{bmatrix}\). The basis vectors act like the axes of a (slanted) coordinate system on the plane.

Example 5: a basis of ℝ³ (slides 47 and 48)

Example 5 (slide 47)

In \(\mathbb R^3\), are the following three vectors a basis?

\[ \left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix}2\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\} \]

Yes, as they are linearly independent and span \(\mathbb R^3\). Why? Two things to check.

Independent (slide 47). \(x_1\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}+x_2\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}+x_3\begin{bmatrix}2\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}\) iff \(x_1=x_2=x_3=0\). The slide states it; here is the working:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&2\\ 1&1&0\\ 1&1&1 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ \xrightarrow[R_3-R_1]{R_2-R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&2\\ 0&1&-2\\ 0&1&-1 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ \xrightarrow{R_3-R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&2\\ 0&1&-2\\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} \]

A pivot in every column, no free variable: only the trivial solution. So they are linearly independent.

Span \(\mathbb R^3\) (slide 48). Any vector \(\begin{bmatrix}a\\b\\c\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^3\) can be represented by these three vectors. Tip: use Gaussian elimination to solve the system with augmented matrix \(\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&0&2&a\\1&1&0&b\\1&1&1&c\end{array}\right]\); you will find it always has a solution. In full:

\[ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&0&2&a\\ 1&1&0&b\\ 1&1&1&c \end{array}\right] \] \[ \xrightarrow[R_3-R_1]{R_2-R_1} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&0&2&a\\ 0&1&-2&b-a\\ 0&1&-1&c-a \end{array}\right] \] \[ \xrightarrow{R_3-R_2} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&0&2&a\\ 0&1&-2&b-a\\ 0&0&1&c-b \end{array}\right] \]

No row of the form \([\,0\ 0\ 0\mid\text{non-zero}\,]\) can appear, whatever \(a,b,c\) are. Keeping \(a,b,c\) as letters tests every destination of \(\mathbb R^3\) at once; a pivot in every row means no axis condition can collapse to \(0=\) something. Back substitution:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_3&=c-b,\\ x_2&=(b-a)+2x_3=-a-b+2c,\\ x_1&=a-2x_3=a+2b-2c. \end{aligned} \]

Check with \((a,b,c)=(1,0,0)\): \(x=(1,-1,0)\), and \(1\cdot[1,1,1]^T-1\cdot[0,1,1]^T+0=[1,0,0]^T\) ✓. So every vector of \(\mathbb R^3\) is a combination: they span \(\mathbb R^3\), and with independence they form a basis.

Method: does a set span ℝⁿ? (from slide 48)

Row reduce the matrix with the vectors as columns. The vectors span \(\mathbb R^n\) iff there is a pivot in every row (then no row \([\,0\ \cdots\ 0\mid\text{something}\,]\) can appear, for any right-hand side). Each row is one axis. A row without a pivot could turn into \(0=\) something non-zero for a badly chosen destination, which would then be out of reach.

In words: independence = pivot in every column; spanning \(\mathbb R^n\) = pivot in every row; a basis of \(\mathbb R^n\) needs both, so it has exactly \(n\) vectors.

The standard bases (slides 50 and 51)

What is this? (standard basis)

The standard basis is the set of arrows "one step along axis 1", "one step along axis 2", and so on. The recipe for any vector in this toolkit is just its own list of entries.

Standard basis (slides 50 and 51)

In \(\mathbb R^n\), \(\mathbf e_k\) is the vector with 1 in entry \(k\) and 0 everywhere else. \(\{\mathbf e_1,\dots,\mathbf e_n\}\) is the standard basis of \(\mathbb R^n\). Placed side by side as columns they form the identity matrix \(I_n=[\,\mathbf e_1\ \mathbf e_2\ \cdots\ \mathbf e_n\,]\).

\[ \mathbb R^2:\ \mathbf e_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\ \mathbf e_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \mathbb R^3:\ \mathbf e_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix},\ \mathbf e_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{bmatrix},\ \mathbf e_3=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix} \]

In words: the weights in the standard basis are just the entries of the vector.

Slide 50's examples in \(\mathbb R^2\) (the vectors of slide 6):

\[ \mathbf u=\begin{bmatrix}2\\2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\2\end{bmatrix}=2\mathbf e_1+2\mathbf e_2 \] \[ \mathbf w=\begin{bmatrix}-2\\-1\end{bmatrix}=-2\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}=-2\mathbf e_1-\mathbf e_2 \] \[ \mathbf v=\begin{bmatrix}3\\-1\end{bmatrix}=3\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}=3\mathbf e_1-\mathbf e_2 \]

In \(\mathbb R^3\) (the vectors of Example 1):

\[ \mathbf a_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\\-5\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf e_1-2\mathbf e_2-5\mathbf e_3, \] \[ \mathbf a_2=\begin{bmatrix}2\\5\\6\end{bmatrix}=2\mathbf e_1+5\mathbf e_2+6\mathbf e_3. \]

Slide 51, in \(\mathbb R^4\) with \(\mathbf e_1=[1,0,0,0]^T,\dots,\mathbf e_4=[0,0,0,1]^T\):

\[ \mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}5\\-1\\3\\-9\end{bmatrix}=5\mathbf e_1-\mathbf e_2+3\mathbf e_3-9\mathbf e_4, \]

and so on (\(\dots\)) up to \(I_n=[\,\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_3,\dots,\mathbf e_q,\dots,\mathbf e_n\,]\) in \(\mathbb R^n\).

Beyond the handouts: dimension (the exam asks for it)

Every basis of the same space has the same number of vectors (\(\mathbb R^3\): always 3, as in Examples 5 and 6 and the standard basis). That number is the dimension, written \(\dim V\). So \(\dim\mathbb R^n=n\), a plane through the origin has dimension 2 (Example 4's \(H\)), a line through the origin dimension 1, and \(\{\mathbf 0\}\) dimension 0.

Method: dimension of \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_p\}\). Row reduce \([\,\mathbf v_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf v_p\,]\) to echelon form. The dimension is the number of pivots, and the original vectors in the pivot columns form a basis of the span (the others are redundant). Each pivot column brings a new direction; a column without a pivot is a recipe of the pivot columns before it, so it adds no new places to reach.

In words: dimension = how many independent directions the vectors really contain = number of pivots.

Example 3: two pivots, so \(\operatorname{Span}\{[1,2,3]^T,[4,5,6]^T,[2,1,0]^T\}\) is a plane, dimension 2, with basis \(\{[1,2,3]^T,[4,5,6]^T\}\). Past papers ask "find the dimension of the span" (L2.2 in the exam guide), sometimes with \(2\times2\) matrices in place of vectors: a \(2\times2\) matrix is treated as a vector with 4 entries, and independence and span work the same way.

You can now: check both conditions for a basis; show that a set spans \(\mathbb R^n\) (a pivot in every row) and is independent (a pivot in every column); write a vector in the standard basis; find the dimension of a span and a basis for it from the pivot columns.

What is this?

The diet is a recipe in the literal sense. Each food is a vector of nutrients: one 100 g portion of nonfat milk moves you 36 g along the protein axis, 52 g along the carbohydrate axis and 0 g along the fat axis. The day\'s target is the destination. Finding the amounts of each food is the question of Example 1: is the target a linear combination of the food vectors, and with which weights? A negative weight is fine on paper but means un-eating food, so it has to be checked.

11Summary ~3 min

  • A vector in \(\mathbb R^n\) is an ordered list of \(n\) numbers, usually a column. In \(\mathbb R^2\) and \(\mathbb R^3\) it is a point, or an arrow from the origin with a magnitude and a direction.
  • Add vectors of the same size entry by entry (parallelogram rule); scale by multiplying every entry (stretches, shrinks or flips along the same line); \(\mathbf u-\mathbf v=\mathbf u+(-1)\mathbf v\). The usual algebra rules hold (slide 14).
  • A linear combination is \(c_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+c_p\mathbf v_p\); the \(c\)'s are the weights. The span is the set of all linear combinations: always contains \(\mathbf 0\); one non-zero vector spans a line, two non-parallel vectors a plane, through the origin.
  • \(\mathbf b\) is a combination of \(\mathbf a_1,\dots,\mathbf a_p\) (\(\mathbf b\) is in their span) iff \([\,\mathbf a_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf a_p\mid\mathbf b\,]\) is consistent; the solution gives the weights.
  • Independent: \(x_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+x_p\mathbf v_p=\mathbf 0\) only for all \(x_k=0\). Dependent: some non-trivial combination is \(\mathbf 0\), equivalently at least one vector is a combination of the others. Two vectors: dependent iff multiples. A set containing \(\mathbf 0\) is dependent.
  • A basis of \(V\): independent and spanning \(V\). \(\mathbb R^n\) has infinitely many bases; the standard one is \(\mathbf e_1,\dots,\mathbf e_n\), the columns of \(I_n\). (Beyond: every basis has the same number of vectors, the dimension.)
Method: everything is one row reduction

Put the vectors in as the columns of a matrix and row reduce to echelon form. Then:

  1. Is \(\mathbf b\) in the span? Add \(\mathbf b\) as an augmented column. A row \([\,0\ \cdots\ 0\mid\text{non-zero}\,]\): no. Otherwise yes; back substitute for the weights. The contradiction row means the axis conditions on the recipe disagree.
  2. Independent? Pivot in every column: yes. A column without a pivot: no; set its free variable to 1 to get a dependence relation. A free amount gives a real round trip back to the start.
  3. Span all of \(\mathbb R^n\)? Pivot in every row: yes. No axis can turn into a contradiction, whatever the destination.
  4. Basis of \(\mathbb R^n\)? Pivot in every row and every column (so exactly \(n\) vectors). Every destination reachable, with no arrow spare.
  5. Dimension of the span (beyond): number of pivots; the pivot columns of the original vectors are a basis. Each pivot is one genuinely new direction.

In applications (the diet problem) each column is the "contribution vector" of one ingredient and the right-hand side is the target; solve as usual, then check the answer makes physical sense (no negative amounts).

12Common mistakes ~2 min

✘Writing the vectors as the rows of the matrix when testing whether \(\mathbf b\) is a combination of them. ✔The vectors are the columns and \(\mathbf b\) is the last column: \([\,\mathbf a_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf a_p\mid\mathbf b\,]\). Each row is one entry-position (slide 25).
✘"No vector is a multiple of another, so the set is independent", for three or more vectors. ✔The multiple test works only for two vectors. \([1,0,0]^T,[0,1,0]^T,[1,1,0]^T\) contain no multiples, yet the third is the sum of the first two. Row reduce.
✘Seeing a zero row in the echelon form of \([\,\mathbf a_1\ \mathbf a_2\mid\mathbf b\,]\) and concluding "\(\mathbf b\) is not in the span". ✔\([\,0\ 0\mid0\,]\) is \(0=0\), harmless (Example 1). Only \([\,0\ 0\mid\text{non-zero}\,]\) means "not in the span".
✘"Dependent, so every vector is a combination of the others", or "the last vector must be the redundant one". ✔At least one is (slide 43): one whose weight in the dependence relation is non-zero.
✘"Three vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\), so they form a basis" (or "so they span \(\mathbb R^3\)"). ✔Example 3 is three vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\) that span only a plane. Three is necessary, not enough; check for three pivots.
✘Answering "independent" because "\(x_1=x_2=x_3=0\) is a solution". ✔The zero solution always works. Independent means it is the only one: no free variable.
✘Adding vectors of different sizes by padding with zeros, or confusing \([1,2]\) with \([2,1]\). ✔Sums need the same size (slide 9); a vector is an ordered list.

"No two of the vectors are multiples of each other: the first and second have a 0 in different places, and the third is not a multiple of either (ratios \(2,\,?,\,\frac32\) and \(?,\,-1,\,3\)). So the set is linearly independent."

Is the third vector a combination of the first two? Try \(2\mathbf v_1-\mathbf v_2\).

\[ \begin{bmatrix}1&0&2\\0&1&-1\\2&1&3\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \xrightarrow{R_3-2R_1} \begin{bmatrix}1&0&2\\0&1&-1\\0&1&-1\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \xrightarrow{R_3-R_2} \begin{bmatrix}1&0&2\\0&1&-1\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix} \]

No pivot in column 3: \(x_3\) free. With \(x_3=1\): \(x_2=1\), \(x_1=-2\), i.e. \(-2\mathbf v_1+\mathbf v_2+\mathbf v_3=\mathbf 0\), the same relation.

\[ \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1&2&7\\ -2&5&4\\ -5&6&-3 \end{array}\right]\to\left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1&2&7\\ 0&1&2\\ 0&0&0 \end{array}\right] \]

"The last row is all zeros, so the three equations are dependent and the system has no solution. \(\mathbf b\) is not a linear combination."

3. A student is asked whether \(\{[1,2,3]^T,[4,5,6]^T,[2,1,0]^T\}\) is a basis of \(\mathbb R^3\):

The last row is \(0=1\): no solution.

14Concept checks ~1 min

Written for these notes.

1. Can two vectors span \(\mathbb R^3\)?

Answer

No. The matrix \([\,\mathbf v_1\ \mathbf v_2\,]\) has 2 columns, so at most 2 pivots, but spanning \(\mathbb R^3\) needs a pivot in each of its 3 rows. Geometrically, two vectors span at most a plane through the origin (slide 28).

2. Is a set containing just one vector, \(\{\mathbf v\}\), linearly independent?

Answer

Yes if \(\mathbf v\ne\mathbf 0\): \(x\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\) forces \(x=0\) (some entry of \(\mathbf v\) is non-zero). No if \(\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\): \(1\cdot\mathbf 0=\mathbf 0\) (slide 44).

3. \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\mathbf v_3\}\) is linearly independent. Must \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\}\) be independent? And if \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\}\) is dependent, what about \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\mathbf v_3\}\)?

Answer

Yes: a relation \(x_1\mathbf v_1+x_2\mathbf v_2=\mathbf 0\) with \(x_1,x_2\) not both 0 would give \(x_1\mathbf v_1+x_2\mathbf v_2+0\mathbf v_3=\mathbf 0\), a non-trivial relation for the three. By the same argument, if \(\{\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\}\) is dependent, then adding any vector keeps it dependent. In short: subsets of independent sets are independent; supersets of dependent sets are dependent.

4. Is the set

\[ \left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\} \]

a basis of \(\mathbb R^2\)?

Answer

No. It spans \(\mathbb R^2\) (it contains \(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2\)), but it is dependent: \(\mathbf e_1+\mathbf e_2-[1,1]^T=\mathbf 0\). Three vectors in \(\mathbb R^2\) are always dependent. Removing any one of them leaves a basis.

5. Is \([2,4,6]^T\) in \(\operatorname{Span}\{[1,2,3]^T\}\)? Is \([1,2,4]^T\)?

Answer

The span of one non-zero vector is its multiples (a line). \([2,4,6]^T=2[1,2,3]^T\): yes. \([1,2,4]^T=c[1,2,3]^T\) would need \(c=1\) from the first entry and \(c=\frac43\) from the third: no.

6. Is \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2\}\) the same set as \(\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2,3\mathbf a_1-\mathbf a_2\}\)?

Answer

Yes. Anything built from \(\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2\) is also built from the three (weight 0 on the third). And anything built from the three, \[ \begin{aligned} &c_1\mathbf a_1+c_2\mathbf a_2+c_3(3\mathbf a_1-\mathbf a_2)\\ &\quad=(c_1+3c_3)\mathbf a_1+(c_2-c_3)\mathbf a_2, \end{aligned} \] is built from \(\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2\). Adding a redundant (dependent) vector never enlarges a span.
