Exam guide: Probability Theory

P2At a glance

This chapter is half predictable. Density questions (find the constant, then a moment, a variance or a cdf) and joint-pmf tables are pure routine. Conditional probability, independence and counting questions come as short word problems with a new story each time, so there is no single method: you need to recognise which rule the story is asking for. The cards below give a decision procedure for each type, and the questions on this page show every story the exam has used.

Exam format
  • No calculators since Dec 2022. Keep fractions exact (\(\tfrac{81}{209}\), not 0.3876); decimals given in the question (0.535, 0.02) are fine to multiply by hand. Older papers (Dec 2012 – Dec 2014, May 2015, Dec 2016) allowed calculators, but every answer on this page is done by hand.
  • No formula sheet. Learn by heart: the four axioms (asked in Dec 2023, Dec 2024 and May 2025), \(P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)\), total probability and Bayes, \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-E(X)^2\), \(\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\).
  • Multiple choice in Part II since Dec 2025 (2 points each, four options), and in May 2026 Question 7 is ten answer-only short parts (marks per part not printed, about 2 each). No working is read, so check each answer once (probabilities add to 1, a probability lies in \([0,1]\)).
  • Long questions ("provide detailed arguments"): write the rule you use before the numbers ("by the law of total probability…", "since \(X\), \(Y\) are independent…"). A bare number gets little credit.

The seven cards

P2.1 Sample spaces, axioms, event algebra 7 parts · 5 papers · 2–5 marks

Looks like"State the Axioms of Probability"; "find the sample space"; three of \(P(A)\), \(P(B)\), \(P(A\cup B)\), \(P(A\cap B)\) given, one asked.

DoWrite the four axioms word for word. For numbers: inclusion–exclusion, and De Morgan \(A'\cap B'=(A\cup B)'\).

WatchAxiom (iv) needs the events pairwise disjoint. A sample space is a set: write it with braces and say what each element is.

P2.2 Counting and classical probability 5 parts · 4 papers · 2–5 marks

Looks like"In how many ways…", "choose at random without replacement", keys, coins tossed \(n\) times.

DoDecide ordered or unordered, with or without replacement; count the favourable cases the same way as the total; "or" means inclusion–exclusion.

WatchMixing ordered and unordered counts in one fraction. Look for a symmetry before counting (7 tosses: "more heads" and "more tails" are equally likely).

P2.3 Conditional probability and Bayes 21 parts · 15 papers · 2–5 marks a part

Looks likeTwo urns or boxes and a coin; factories with defect rates; "given that…, what is the probability that…"; true-or-false claims about \(P(A\mid B)\).

DoName the causes \(H_i\) (urn, box, plant) and the evidence \(E\). Tree: \(P(E)=\sum P(E\mid H_i)P(H_i)\); then Bayes \(P(H_j\mid E)=P(E\mid H_j)P(H_j)/P(E)\).

Watch\(P(A\mid B)\ne P(B\mid A)\). "Given" goes in the denominator. A repeated draw from the same urn is not independent of the first draw unless you condition on the urn.

P2.4 Independence 11 parts · 10 papers · 3–5 marks

Looks like"Are \(X\), \(Y\) independent? Justify." with a \(2\times2\) table; "Is the following statement true?"; cards from a deck.

DoCompute marginals. Independent only if \(p(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y)\) in every cell; one failing cell proves dependence.

WatchIndependent \(\ne\) disjoint. For a "true?" claim: a proof if true, one concrete counterexample if false.

P2.5 Discrete random variables 19 parts · 13 papers · 2–5 marks

Looks likeA pmf or joint table (sometimes with a missing cell); \(E\), \(\operatorname{Var}\), \(P(\cdot\mid\cdot)\), a function such as \(X+Y\), \(\min(X,Y)\), \(X^2\).

DoFill the missing cell (total 1). Make the pmf of the new variable by listing its value in each cell, then \(E\) and \(E(\cdot^2)\).

Watch\(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}X+\operatorname{Var}Y\) only if independent. "Can it be determined?" questions: say yes or no first, then why.

P2.6 Continuous random variables 25 parts · 15 papers · 2–10 marks

Looks like\(f(x)=Kx^a\), \(K\sqrt x\), \(Ke^{-x}\), \(K|x|\) on an interval; "find \(K\), the cdf, \(E(X^n)\) for all \(n\), \(\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)\)".

Do\(\int f=1\) gives \(K\); \(E(X^n)=\int x^nf(x)\,dx\) as a formula in \(n\); \(\operatorname{Var}=E(X^2)-E(X)^2\); \(\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}X\).

WatchThe cdf is defined on all of \(\mathbb R\): give it below and above the support too. Split \(|\cdot|\) at the sign change.

P2.7 Distribution of \(Y=g(X)\) 8 parts · 4 papers · 2–5 marks

Looks like"Find the density of \(Y=X^3\), \(2X+1\), \(|X|\), \(X^2\)", often "by computing \(\frac{d}{dy}P(Y\le y)\)".

DoCdf method: \(F_Y(y)=P(g(X)\le y)\), rewrite as an event about \(X\), use \(F_X\), differentiate.

Watch\(X^2\le y\) and \(|X|\le y\) mean \(-\sqrt y\le X\le\sqrt y\) (two sides). Say what happens for \(y\) outside the range.

Learn the ideas first in the chapter note: counting, axioms, conditional probability, Bayes, independence, pmfs, densities and cdfs, transformed variables, joint distributions, variance.

Notation on this page: \(A'\) is the complement of \(A\); \(p(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y)\); "pmf" is a probability mass function, "pdf" or density is \(f\); \(F(x)=P(X\le x)\) is the cdf. Section numbers (§) refer to the P2 note.

P2.1Sample spaces, axioms, event algebra

7 parts · May 2026, May 2025, Dec 2024, Dec 2023 (×3), Dec 2021 · Learn it: note §6 Sample spaces, events and the axioms, §7 Classical probability and first rules, §8 Conditional probability

Decision procedure
  1. "State the Axioms of Probability": write the Ready answer below, all four, with the disjointness condition. Nothing else is asked.
  2. "What is the sample space?": write \(S=\{\dots\}\) and say what one outcome is (an ordered pair, a sequence). If it is small, list it; if not, describe it by a rule. Then an event is a subset of \(S\): list its elements.
  3. Two of \(P(A)\), \(P(B)\), \(P(A\cup B)\), \(P(A\cap B)\) plus one more given: inclusion–exclusion \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\) gives the fourth.
  4. Complements (\(A'\cap B'\), \(A'\cup B'\)): turn them into a complement with De Morgan, \(A'\cap B'=(A\cup B)'\), \(A'\cup B'=(A\cap B)'\), then \(P(E')=1-P(E)\).
  5. A conditional probability appears: \(P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)\), with step 3 or 4 for the top.
Ready answer · the Axioms of Probability

Let \(S\) be the sample space and \(\mathcal F\) the set of events (subsets of \(S\)). A probability \(P\) assigns a number \(P(A)\) to each event \(A\) so that:

  1. (a) \(S\in\mathcal F\); (b) if \(A\in\mathcal F\) then \(A'\in\mathcal F\); (c) if \(A_1,A_2,\dots\in\mathcal F\) then \(A_1\cup A_2\cup\cdots\in\mathcal F\).
  2. \(0\le P(A)\le1\) for every event \(A\).
  3. \(P(S)=1\).
  4. If \(A_1,A_2,A_3,\dots\) are events with \(A_i\cap A_j=\emptyset\) for all \(i\ne j\), then \[ \begin{aligned} &P(A_1\cup A_2\cup\cdots)\\ &\quad=P(A_1)+P(A_2)+\cdots \end{aligned} \]

This is the handout's list (note §6). Many textbooks give only (ii)–(iv), with (ii) as \(P(A)\ge0\); writing all four as above is safe.

The experiment is about flipping a coin infinite times. What is the sample space?

Reading used: marks per part are not printed (20 points for ten parts, so about 2 each).

Hint

P2.1. One outcome of the experiment is a whole infinite record of flips. How do you write one such record?

Solution
  1. One outcome is an infinite sequence \((\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3,\dots)\), where \(\omega_k\in\{H,T\}\) is the result of the \(k\)-th flip.
  2. The sample space is the set of all such sequences.
\[ \boxed{\begin{gathered} S=\{(\omega_1,\omega_2,\dots):\\ \omega_k\in\{H,T\}\ \text{for all } k\} \end{gathered}} \]

Shorter: \(S=\{H,T\}^{\mathbb N}\), the set of all infinite H/T sequences. It is an infinite (in fact uncountable) set.

Dec 2023 Q6(b), (c)2 + 3 points

Part (a) is the axioms (above); part (d): P2.3.

(b) An experiment consists of tossing two dice. i. Find the sample space \(S\). ii. Find the event \(A\) that the sum of the dots on the dice is greater than 10.

(c) Given that \(P(A)=0.4\), \(P(B)=0.2\), and \(P(A\cup B)=0.535\). Find i. \(P(A\cap B)\) ii. \(P(A'\cap B')\) iii. \(P(A'\mid B')\)

Hint

P2.1. (b) An outcome is an ordered pair (first die, second die). (c) Inclusion–exclusion; De Morgan for \(A'\cap B'\); then the definition of conditional probability with \(P(B')=1-P(B)\).

Solution

(b)

  1. \(S=\{(i,j): i,j\in\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\}\), where \(i\) and \(j\) are the numbers on the first and second die; 36 equally likely outcomes.
  2. Sum greater than 10 means sum 11 or 12.
\[ \boxed{A=\{(5,6),(6,5),(6,6)\}} \]

(c)

  1. Inclusion–exclusion: \[ \begin{aligned} P(A\cap B)&=P(A)+P(B)\\ &\quad-P(A\cup B)\\ &=0.4+0.2-0.535\\ &=0.065 \end{aligned} \]
  2. De Morgan: \(A'\cap B'=(A\cup B)'\), so \[ P(A'\cap B')=1-0.535=0.465 \]
  3. \(P(B')=1-0.2=0.8\), so \[ \begin{aligned} P(A'\mid B')&=\frac{P(A'\cap B')}{P(B')}\\ &=\frac{0.465}{0.8} \end{aligned} \]
\[ \boxed{\begin{aligned} &\text{i. } 0.065\quad \text{ii. } 0.465\\ &\text{iii. } 0.58125=\tfrac{93}{160} \end{aligned}} \]

By hand: \(0.465/0.8=4.65/8=0.58125\).

P2.2Counting and classical probability

5 parts · May 2026 (×2), Dec 2025 (MC), Dec 2024; older format Dec 2014 · Learn it: note §1 Counting: the fundamental rule, §2 Sampling and combinations, §7 Classical probability

Decision procedure
  1. Is it a count or a probability? "In how many ways" wants a whole number. "What is the probability" with equally likely outcomes wants \(\dfrac{\#\text{favourable}}{\#\text{total}}\), both counted the same way.
  2. Fill positions one at a time (multiplication rule) when the objects go into slots with conditions: "alternating", "begins with an odd digit". Count the choices for the most restricted slot first.
  3. Unordered choice without replacement ("two numbers are selected", "a hand"): use \(\binom nk\) for both top and bottom.
  4. "A or B": \(|A\cup B|=|A|+|B|-|A\cap B|\). "At least one": count the complement.
  5. Look for a symmetry before counting: if swapping heads and tails (or the roles of two keys) turns the event into its "mirror", the two have equal probability.
  6. If the counting gets messy, think of the random experiment as a random ordering of all the objects and ask where the special ones land (Dec 2024 Q15(b)).
Ready answer · the four sampling counts

Choosing \(k\) from \(n\) distinct objects:

order mattersorder ignored
without replacement\(\dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}\)\(\dbinom nk\)
with replacement\(n^k\)\(\dbinom{n+k-1}{k}\)

(b) In how many ways can five boys and five girls stand in a row in alternating gender?

(d) How many positive 4-digit numbers begin with an odd digit or are divisible by 5?

P2.2. (b) First choose the pattern (who stands first), then arrange the boys in their 5 places and the girls in theirs. (d) Count the two sets and their overlap; a 4-digit number is 1000 to 9999, and "divisible by 5" means the last digit is 0 or 5.

  3. \(A\cap B\): \(5\cdot10\cdot10\cdot2=1000\).
  4. \(|A\cup B|=5000+1800-1000\).

Dec 2025 Q192 pointsmultiple choice

Two numbers are selected randomly from \(\{1,2,\dots,10\}\) without replacement. What is the probability their sum is even?

(A) \(2/9\)   (B) \(4/9\)   (C) \(5/9\)   (D) \(7/9\)

Hint

P2.2, unordered pairs. A sum is even exactly when both numbers are odd or both are even.

Solution
  1. Total: \(\binom{10}2=45\) equally likely pairs.
  2. Both odd: \(\binom52=10\); both even: \(\binom52=10\).
  3. \[ P(\text{even sum})=\frac{10+10}{45}=\frac49 \]

Marker: (C) \(5/9\) is the trap: it is the probability of an odd sum (\(5\cdot5=25\) mixed pairs out of 45).

\[ \boxed{\text{B}} \]

Quick way: whatever the first number, 4 of the remaining 9 have the same parity, so \(4/9\).

Dec 2024 Q15(b)5 points

Parts (a), (c): P2.1 (axioms), P2.3.

There are two locks on the door and the keys are among the six different ones you carry in your pocket. In a hurry you dropped one somewhere. What is the probability that you can still open the door? What is the probability that the first two keys you try will open the door?

Reading used: opening the door needs both locks, so both lock keys; "the first two keys you try will open the door" = the first two keys tried are exactly the two lock keys (in either order), from the five keys left.

Hint

P2.2. The dropped key is equally likely to be any of the six. For the second question, picture the six keys in a random order: the first is the one dropped, the next two are the ones you try.

Solution
  1. Of the six keys, two are lock keys. You can still open the door exactly when the dropped key is one of the other four: \[ P(\text{can open})=\frac46=\frac23 \]
  2. Second question: dropping one key and then trying two at random is the same as choosing 3 keys in order. The two you try form an unordered pair chosen at random from the six keys (the dropped one is just one of the other four). Only 1 of the \(\binom62=15\) pairs is the pair of lock keys: \[ P=\frac{1}{\binom62}=\frac1{15} \]
  3. Check by conditioning: the lock keys must both survive (\(\tfrac23\)), then the two tried must be them, 1 pair out of \(\binom52=10\): \(\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac1{10}=\tfrac1{15}\).
\[ \boxed{\tfrac23\ \text{and}\ \tfrac1{15}} \]

P2.3Conditional probability, total probability and Bayes

21 parts · Dec 2025 (MC, and Q27(b)), May 2025, Dec 2024, May 2024, Dec 2023, Dec 2021; older format May 2019, Dec 2018 (×2), May 2018, Dec 2017 (×2), Dec 2016 (×2), May 2015, Dec 2014, May 2014 (×2), Dec 2013 (×2) · Learn it: note §8 Conditional probability, §9 Total probability and Bayes's theorem

Decision procedure
  1. Find the two stages. Most stories have a hidden first stage (which urn, box, plant, sensor, folder) and an observed second stage (the colour, the defect, the corrupted photo). Call the first-stage outcomes \(H_1,H_2,\dots\) (they must split the sample space) and the observation \(E\).
  2. Write what is given as \(P(H_i)\) and \(P(E\mid H_i)\). "Equally likely" or "a fair coin decides" means \(P(H_i)=\frac1k\). A rate "of the products made by plant B" is a conditional probability \(P(\text{defect}\mid B)\).
  3. Asked for the probability of the observation? Law of total probability (a tree: multiply along each branch, add the branches).
  4. Asked "given the observation, which first stage?" Bayes: one branch over the total from step 3.
  5. Two observations from the same hidden stage (draw again from the same urn, three photos from the same sensor): they are independent given \(H_i\), so multiply inside each branch first (\(P(E_1E_2\mid H_i)=P(E_1\mid H_i)P(E_2\mid H_i)\)), then add the branches. Never multiply the two unconditional probabilities.
  6. No hidden stage, just two events (dice sums, \(A\) and \(A\cup B\)): use the definition \(P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)\) and count or compute the intersection directly. If \(A\subset B\), the top is just \(P(A)\).
  7. Sequential games (roll again if…, throw the crown): draw the tree of what happens first and condition on it.
  8. "Is the statement true?" about conditional probabilities: rewrite each side with the definition; a proof if true, a concrete counterexample (die, two disjoint events) if false.
Ready answer · total probability and Bayes

If \(H_1,\dots,H_k\) are disjoint, cover \(S\), and \(P(H_i)\gt0\):

\[ P(E)=\sum_{i=1}^kP(E\mid H_i)\,P(H_i) \] \[ P(H_j\mid E)=\frac{P(E\mid H_j)\,P(H_j)}{P(E)} \]

Lay it out as a table: one row per \(H_i\), columns \(P(H_i)\), \(P(E\mid H_i)\), their product. The column of products adds up to \(P(E)\); the row you want, divided by that sum, is the Bayes answer.

Part (a)(i)–(iii): P2.5; (a)(iv): P2.4.

A Mars rover's imaging system has two equally likely sensor types: Type A sensors: corruption probability \(p_A=\frac1{10}\); Type B sensors: corruption probability \(p_B=\frac15\).

(b) The rover randomly selects a sensor and uses it to take 3 independent photos under identical conditions. Given that exactly 1 of the 3 photos is corrupted, what is the probability the sensor is Type A?

Hint

P2.3, step 5. Hidden stage: the sensor type. Given the type, the number of corrupted photos out of 3 is binomial.

Solution

Given the type, photos are independent, so \[ \begin{aligned} P(E\mid A)&=3\cdot\tfrac1{10}\cdot\left(\tfrac9{10}\right)^2\\ &=\tfrac{243}{1000} \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} P(E\mid B)&=3\cdot\tfrac15\cdot\left(\tfrac45\right)^2\\ &=\tfrac{48}{125}=\tfrac{384}{1000} \end{aligned} \]

  3. Bayes (the \(\frac12\) cancels): \[ P(A\mid E)=\frac{243}{243+384}=\frac{243}{627} \]
  4. Both are divisible by 3: \(\frac{81}{209}\) (\(209=11\cdot19\)).
\[ \boxed{P(\text{Type A}\mid E)=\tfrac{81}{209}\approx0.388} \]

Decision procedure
  1. A joint table: "are \(X\), \(Y\) independent?" Fill any missing cell (the table adds to 1). Write the row and column sums (marginals). Compare \(p(x,y)\) with \(p_X(x)p_Y(y)\):
    • one cell where they differ ⇒ not independent; quote that cell;
    • all cells equal ⇒ independent; for a \(2\times2\) table show at least one cell and say the others follow (or show all four, it is quick).
  2. A zero in the table with non-zero row and column sums: not independent at once (\(0\ne\) product).
  3. Events from a story (cards, dice): compute \(P(A)\), \(P(B)\), \(P(A\cap B)\) by counting and test \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\).
  4. Independence given, something asked: use \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\), \(p(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y)\), and that complements of independent events are independent (\(P(A\cap B')=P(A)P(B')\)).
  5. "Must they be independent?" / "Is the statement true?": if it fails, give one small explicit counterexample (often \(Y=X\) or \(Y=1-X\)); if it holds, prove it from the definition.
  6. An optimisation with independent trials (May 2025 chess): write the winning event as a union of intersections, multiply inside, subtract the overlap, then compare the candidates algebraically.
Ready answer · how to write "independent?"

Not independent: "\(P(X=0,Y=0)=0.1\), but \(P(X=0)P(Y=0)\) is \(0.35\times0.3=0.105\ne0.1\). Hence \(X\) and \(Y\) are not independent."

Independent: "\(p(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y)\) for all four pairs \((x,y)\): [list the four checks]. Hence \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent."

Independent is not disjoint: two disjoint events with positive probabilities are never independent, since \(P(A\cap B)=0\ne P(A)P(B)\).

Decision procedure
  1. Complete the table. A missing cell or constant \(k\): all probabilities add up to 1. Write the row sums (pmf of \(X\)) and column sums (pmf of \(Y\)) at the margins.
  2. A probability of an interval such as \(P(0.5\le X\le3.5)\): translate into the list of values of \(X\) inside it (\(X\in\{1,2,3\}\)). With a condition, divide by the probability of the condition.
  3. A conditional pmf \(P(Y=y\mid X=x)\): the row of \(x\) divided by its row sum.
  4. \(E\) and \(\operatorname{Var}\) of \(X\): \(E(X)=\sum x\,p(x)\), \(E(X^2)=\sum x^2p(x)\), \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-E(X)^2\).
  5. A function \(g(X,Y)\) (\(X+Y\), \(\min(X,Y)\), \(X^2\)): write the value of \(g\) in every cell, collect equal values into a new pmf, then step 4. This works whether or not \(X\), \(Y\) are independent.
  6. Only means or moments given (\(E(X)\), \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\)): use linearity, \(E(aX+b)=aE(X)+b\), \(\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\), and \(E(X^2)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+E(X)^2\). If \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=0\), \(X\) is a constant.

  8. A sum of i.i.d. 0/1 variables: recognise a binomial count, \(E=np\), \(\operatorname{Var}=np(1-p)\).

List \(S=X+Y\) cell by cell, merge into the pmf of \(S\), then

\[ \operatorname{Var}(S)=E(S^2)-E(S)^2 \]

  2. (ii) \(x^n|x|\) is odd for odd \(n\), so \(E(X^n)=0\). For even \(n\): \[ E(X^n)=2\int_0^1x^{n+1}\,dx=\frac2{n+2} \]
  3. (iii) \(E(2X+1)=2\cdot0+1=1\). \(E(X^2)=\frac24=\frac12\), \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac12\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(2X+1)=4\cdot\frac12=2\).
\[ \boxed{\begin{gathered} A=1;\\ E(X^n)=\begin{cases}0,&n\text{ odd}\\ \tfrac2{n+2},&n\text{ even}\end{cases}\\ E(2X+1)=1,\\ \operatorname{Var}(2X+1)=2 \end{gathered}} \]

P2.7Distribution of a transformed variable \(Y=g(X)\)

8 parts · May 2026 (×2), Dec 2023 (×4); older format May 2019, May 2018 · Learn it: note §15 The density of a transformed variable, §14 Distribution function

Method
  1. Range of \(Y\). From the range of \(X\), find where \(Y\) can lie (\(X\in[0,3]\Rightarrow X^3\in[0,27]\); \(|X|\ge0\); \(X^2\ge0\)). Outside it the cdf is 0 (below) or 1 (above) and the density is 0.
  2. Write the cdf. \(F_Y(y)=P(g(X)\le y)\) for \(y\) inside the range.
  3. Solve the inequality for \(X\). Increasing \(g\) (\(x^3\), \(2x+1\)): \(g(X)\le y\iff X\le g^{-1}(y)\). For \(X^2\) or \(|X|\): \(-\sqrt y\le X\le\sqrt y\) (or \(-y\le X\le y\)), an interval with two ends.
  4. Use \(X\)'s distribution: integrate \(f_X\) over that interval, or write it with \(F_X\) (for a normal \(X\), with \(\Phi\)).
  5. Differentiate in \(y\) (chain rule for \(\sqrt y\), \(y^{1/3}\)) to get \(f_Y\). State it piecewise, with 0 outside the range.
  6. Moments of \(Y\): either from \(f_Y\), or directly \(E(g(X))=\int g(x)f_X(x)\,dx\); the two must agree (a free check).
Ready answer · the cdf method layout

For \(y\) in the range of \(Y\):

\[ \begin{aligned} F_Y(y)&=P(g(X)\le y)\\ &=P(X\in\{x: g(x)\le y\})\\ &=\int_{\{g(x)\le y\}}f_X(x)\,dx \end{aligned} \] \[ f_Y(y)=\frac{d}{dy}F_Y(y) \]

Linear case, \(Y=aX+b\) with \(a\ne0\): \(f_Y(y)=\frac1{|a|}f_X\!\left(\frac{y-b}a\right)\).

Part (a): P2.6.

Let \(\lambda\gt0\) and define \(f\) as follows:

\[ f(u)=\begin{cases} \frac12\lambda e^{-\lambda u},&\text{if } u\ge0,\\[2pt] \frac12\lambda e^{\lambda u},&\text{if } u\lt0. \end{cases} \]

(b) Find \(P(|X|\le t)\). You should consider the cases where \(t\ge0\) and \(t\lt0\). (c) Find the density function of \(|X|\) by computing \(\frac{d}{dt}P(|X|\le t)\).

Reading used: \(X\) is not defined in the question; it is read as a random variable with density \(f\).

Hint

P2.7, steps 1–5. \(|X|\le t\) with \(t\ge0\) means \(-t\le X\le t\); \(f\) is symmetric, so the integral is twice the right half.

Solution
  1. (b) \(t\lt0\): \(|X|\ge0\gt t\) always, so \(P(|X|\le t)=0\).
  2. \(t\ge0\): by symmetry of \(f\), \[ \begin{aligned} P(|X|\le t)&=P(-t\le X\le t)\\ &=2\int_0^t\tfrac12\lambda e^{-\lambda u}\,du\\ &=1-e^{-\lambda t} \end{aligned} \]
  3. (c) Differentiate in \(t\): \(\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\) for \(t\gt0\), and 0 for \(t\lt0\).
\[ \boxed{\begin{gathered} P(|X|\le t)=\begin{cases}0,&t\lt0\\ 1-e^{-\lambda t},&t\ge0\end{cases}\\ f_{|X|}(t)=\begin{cases}\lambda e^{-\lambda t},&t\ge0\\ 0,&t\lt0\end{cases} \end{gathered}} \]

So \(|X|\) is exponential with parameter \(\lambda\) (P3); the value of a density at the single point \(t=0\) does not matter.

Suppose \(X\) is a continuous random variable having range \([0,3]\) with the cdf (cumulative distribution function) given as

where \(f(t)=kt^2\) for \(t\in[0,3]\), and 0 otherwise. (a) Find the value of \(k\). (b) Write down an explicit formula for the cdf of \(Y=X^3\), \(P(Y\le y)\). Reading used: the paper prints the lower limit as \(\infty\); it is read as \(-\infty\).

Hint

P2.7, steps 1–6 with the increasing function \(x^3\): \(X^3\le y\iff X\le y^{1/3}\). After (c) you should recognise a uniform density.

Solution
  1. (b) \(k=\frac19\). \(Y\in[0,27]\). For \(0\le y\le27\): \[ \begin{aligned} P(Y\le y)&=P(X\le y^{1/3})\\ &=\int_0^{y^{1/3}}\tfrac{t^2}9\,dt\\ &=\frac{(y^{1/3})^3}{27}=\frac y{27} \end{aligned} \] and \(P(Y\le y)=0\) for \(y\lt0\), \(=1\) for \(y\gt27\).
  2. (c) \(f_Y(y)=\frac1{27}\) for \(0\le y\le27\), 0 otherwise: \(Y\) is uniform on \([0,27]\).
  3. (d) \(E(Y)=\int_0^{27}\frac y{27}\,dy=\frac{27}2\).

Part (a) (two Poisson variables): P3 guide, P3.2.

Let \(X\) be a standard normal random variable and let \(Y=X^2\). Find the probability density function of \(Y\).

Hint

P2.7, step 3 with two ends: for \(y\gt0\), \(X^2\le y\iff-\sqrt y\le X\le\sqrt y\). Write it with \(\Phi\), then differentiate with the chain rule; \(\Phi'=\varphi\).

Solution
  1. \(Y\ge0\), so \(F_Y(y)=0\) and \(f_Y(y)=0\) for \(y\lt0\).
  2. For \(y\gt0\), using \(\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)\): \[ \begin{aligned} F_Y(y)&=P(-\sqrt y\le X\le\sqrt y)\\ &=\Phi(\sqrt y)-\Phi(-\sqrt y)\\ &=2\Phi(\sqrt y)-1 \end{aligned} \]
  3. Chain rule, with \(\varphi(z)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}\): \[ \begin{aligned} f_Y(y)&=2\varphi(\sqrt y)\cdot\frac1{2\sqrt y}\\ &=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi y}}\,e^{-y/2} \end{aligned} \]

Marker: show the two-sided event \(-\sqrt y\le X\le\sqrt y\); writing \(P(X\le\sqrt y)\) loses the negative half and gives half the right density.

\[ \boxed{f_Y(y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{e^{-y/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi y}},&y\gt0\\ 0,&y\le0\end{cases}} \]
