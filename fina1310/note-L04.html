FINA1310 · Corporate Finance · Lecture 4

Bond valuation

A bond is an annuity of coupons plus one lump sum. Its price, its yield, why the price moves when rates move, inflation, and why yields differ by maturity.

0Where this sits

L3's interest-only loan ($10,000 at 5% for 10 years: $500 a year, $10,500 in year 10) is a bond. Pricing it needs only two L3 tools.

Recap from L2–L3 · the two formulas \[C\times AF(r,t)=C\times\frac{1-\frac{1}{(1+r)^t}}{r}\] \[PV=\frac{FV_t}{(1+r)^t}\]

In words: PV of \(t\) equal payments; PV of one payment at \(t\). A bond's price is the PV of its promised cash flows at today's market rate.

Sign convention

Received +, paid −. Buying a bond: the price is −, coupons and face value are +. Excel/calculator: PV has the opposite sign to PMT and FV. \(t=0\) is today.

1Bonds and their cash flows

A bond is a loan cut into pieces: the contract fixes the payments, the market sets the price.

  • Bond: the issuer borrows from many investors at once and promises fixed payments. A firm needing $5 million sells 5,000 bonds of $1,000 each.
  • Issuers (borrowers): governments, corporations. Investors (lenders, creditors): hold a fixed claim, are paid before shareholders, have no votes.

A coupon \(C\) every period; the face value \(F\) comes with the last coupon at maturity.

Vocabulary
Fixed by the contractSet by the market (changes daily)
Face (par) value \(F\): principal repaid at maturity, usually $1,000Price: what the bond trades for today
Coupon \(C\): fixed interest payment each periodYield to maturity (YTM, “yield”): the return investors require = the discount rate
Coupon rate = annual coupon ÷ face value
Maturity: date \(F\) is paid; time to maturity counts from today

Bonds also differ in other features: registered or bearer, secured or unsecured, callable or not.

Watch · two rates that look alike

Coupon rate: written in the contract, fixed for life; it tells you the cash flows. Yield: required by investors, different every day; it tells you what they are worth. Both are % a year and both appear in the price formula. Never discount at the coupon rate.

Market size (end-2025, US$ trillion): world bonds 161 vs stocks 158 (bonds 50%); US 61 vs 69 (47%); mainland China 28 vs 16 (64%); Hong Kong 1.2 vs 6.1 (16%). Globally the bond market is as large as the stock market; Hong Kong is the exception.

2Bond price

Price = PV of the coupons (an annuity) + PV of the face value (a lump sum), both at the yield.

coupons: an annuity, \(C\times AF(r,t)\)

face value: a lump sum, \(F/(1+r)^t\)

Result · bond price \[\text{Price}=C\times\frac{1-\frac{1}{(1+r)^t}}{r}+\frac{F}{(1+r)^t}\]

\(C\) = coupon per period, \(F\) = face value, \(r\) = yield per period, \(t\) = periods to maturity.

In words: price = PV of the coupons + PV of the face value. For now payments are certain (no default).

Reading the formula
\(C\times\dfrac{1-\frac{1}{(1+r)^t}}{r}\)
the coupons: equal payments every period, so an ordinary annuity (L3)
\(\dfrac{F}{(1+r)^t}\)
the face value: one lump sum at maturity, discounted like any single cash flow
\(r\)
the yield, the return the market requires per period; it sets the price, the coupon rate does not
\(t\)
periods left to maturity, counted from today

How to price a bond

  1. Do: Read the cash flows off the contract: coupon \(C\) = coupon rate × face value (per period), plus \(F\) at maturity. The contract fixes what you will receive; the only question is what that is worth today.
  2. Do: Match the periods: for semi-annual coupons, halve the coupon and the yield and double the number of periods. The rate must be per period, the same “exchange rate” rule as in L2.
  3. Do: Value the coupons as an annuity at the yield, \(C\times AF(r,t)\). Equal payments at equal intervals are exactly an ordinary annuity.
  4. Do: Value the face value as a lump sum, \(F/(1+r)^t\), at the same yield, and add the two. The face value is one payment at maturity; both parts are paid by the same issuer, so they get the same rate.
  5. Do: Sense-check: yield above the coupon rate → price below \(F\); yield below → price above \(F\). If the market wants more than the coupon pays, the only way to give buyers that return is a lower price.
Tools

Excel: PV(rate, nper, pmt, fv) with pmt = coupon, fv = face value. Calculator: N, I/Y, PMT, FV, solve for PV.

Example · 8% annual-coupon bond, 10 years, at issue; market requires 8%

Coupon \(=1{,}000\times8\%=\$80\).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Price}&=80\,AF(8\%,10)+\tfrac{1{,}000}{1.08^{10}}\\&=536.81+463.19=\$1{,}000\end{aligned}\]

It sells at par: when yield = coupon rate, the coupons are exactly the interest the market wants on $1,000.

Shortcut · the discount or premium is the PV of the coupon gap

At 10% the market wants $100 a year on $1,000; this bond pays $80, $20 short for 9 years: \(20\,AF(10\%,9)=\$115.18\), so price \(=1{,}000-115.18=\$884.82\).

At 6% the market wants $60; this bond pays $20 extra: \(20\,AF(6\%,9)=\$136.03\), so price \(=\$1{,}136.03\).

\[\text{Price}=F+(C-rF)\times AF(r,t)\]

In words: par, plus the PV of each period's coupon surplus (or minus the shortfall) against what the market now wants.

Price against yield · the 8% bond with 9 years left

Higher yield, lower price. The curve is convex: steeper on the left, so a fall in yield raises the price more than an equal rise lowers it (+$136 vs −$115).

Pull to par · the same 8% bond over its 10-year life if the yield stays at 6%, 8% or 10%

6%8%10%

At 6% the price falls from $1,147 to $1,000; at 10% it rises from $877 to $1,000. At maturity it is worth exactly \(F\), so premiums and discounts shrink as time passes.

Semi-annual coupons

Most bonds pay twice a year, and both the coupon rate and the yield are quoted as APRs (L3 section 5). So halve both and double the periods.

Example · 8% coupon paid semi-annually, 9 years left, yield 10%

\(C=\$40\), \(r=5\%\) per half-year, \(t=18\).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Price}&=40\,AF(5\%,18)+\tfrac{1{,}000}{1.05^{18}}\\&=467.58+415.52=\$883.10\end{aligned}\]

(vs $884.82 with annual coupons). Quoted YTM \(=5\%\times2=10\%\); effective annual yield \(=1.05^2-1=10.25\%\).

You can now: price a bond with annual or semi-annual coupons; say whether it trades at par, a discount or a premium; use the coupon-gap shortcut.

3Yields and returns

What is this?

The yield to maturity is the price translated into a rate. Think of a bond as a fixed bundle of promised payments. The contract never changes them, so the only way the market can make the deal better or worse is through the price: pay less for the same payments and you earn more. The YTM is the one discount rate that makes those fixed payments worth exactly today's price. That is why price and yield always move in opposite directions: they are two ways of saying the same thing.

In the market you see the price; the yield is the rate that makes the PV of the promised cash flows equal to it.

Definition · yield to maturity

The \(r\) that solves Price \(=C\,AF(r,t)+F/(1+r)^t\) for the observed price. No closed form: trial and error, as for an annuity's rate in L3, or Excel RATE() / calculator I/Y. Quoted as an APR (semi-annual bond: twice the six-month rate).

It is also the return you earn if you buy at this price, hold to maturity and collect every payment.

Example · trial and error

8% annual coupon, $1,000 face, 6 years left, price $955.14. Below par, so the yield is above 8%.A lower price for the same payments needs a higher discount rate to match it, so the first guess goes above the coupon rate.

  • Try 10%: \(348.42+564.47=\$912.89\), below the price → guess too high.
  • Try 9%: \(358.87+596.27=\$955.14\) = the price → YTM = 9%.

Rule: value below the price → guess too high; above → too low. Excel: RATE(6, 80, −955.14, 1000) = 9.00%.

Holding-period return: selling before maturity

Buy at \(P_0\), sell at date \(s\lt t\): you get \(s\) coupons plus the resale price \(P_s\) (the PV of the next owner's cash flows).

\[\begin{aligned}P_0&=\frac{C}{1+r}+\dots+\frac{C+P_s}{(1+r)^s}\\&=C\,AF(r,s)+\frac{P_s}{(1+r)^s}\end{aligned}\]

In words: the \(r\) that solves this is the holding-period return. \(P_s\) depends on the yield on the day you sell, unknown today. At \(s=t\), \(P_s=F\) and \(r\) is the YTM: holding to maturity is the one case with no resale price to guess.

One year: current yield + capital-gain yield \[\text{return}=\underbrace{\frac{C}{P_0}}_{\text{current yield}}+\underbrace{\frac{P_1-P_0}{P_0}}_{\text{capital-gain yield}}\]

In words: income part plus price-change part.

8% bond at 10% with 9 years left: \(P_0=\$884.82\), current yield \(80/884.82=9.04\%\). A year later, yield still 10%, 8 years left: \(P_1=\$893.30\), capital gain \(0.96\%\). Total \(10.00\%\): when the YTM does not change, current yield + capital-gain yield = YTM.

Example · hold three years, then sell

Buy the 8% bond with 7 years left at a 9% yield for $949.67; sell after 3 years (4 left). Cash flows: −949.67, +80, +80, +80 + \(P_3\). The return depends on the yield in year 3:

Rising rates hurt anyone who must sell before maturity: that is section 4.

You can now: find a YTM by trial and error or with RATE(); split a one-year return into current yield and capital-gain yield; work out a holding-period return when you sell early.

4Interest-rate risk

What is this?

You own a bond paying 8%, and next week new bonds of the same kind pay 10%. Nobody will pay full price for your 8% bond when 10% is on offer, so its price falls until a buyer would earn 10% on it too. The longer you are stuck with the low coupon, the more years of missing interest the buyer must be paid for, so the bigger the fall. If rates fall instead, your bond becomes the attractive one and its price rises.

Hold to maturity and your return is locked at the YTM; sell early and you are exposed to whatever yields are then.

Definition · interest-rate risk

The risk that a bond's price changes because market rates change. It hits even default-free government bonds.

Rates move a lot. 10-year US Treasury yields fell from about 8–9% in 1990 to under 1% in 2020, then jumped to about 4–5% from 2022; Hong Kong 10-year yields track them closely.

Try it · maturity and coupon effects

1 year10 years30 years

Price change from the price at an 8% yield. Longer bonds swing more. Switch to the 2% coupon: the 10-year bond swings more than the 8%-coupon one (−37% vs −31% at 14%).

Why: later cash flows are more sensitive \[\begin{aligned}\text{Price}={}&\frac{C}{1+r}+\frac{C}{(1+r)^2}\\&+\dots+\frac{C+F}{(1+r)^t}\end{aligned}\]

Each term is discounted by \((1+r)\) raised to its own date, so the later the cash flow, the more a change in \(r\) moves its PV.

  • A long bond's value sits mostly in far-off terms → longer maturity, more interest-rate risk.
  • A low coupon puts more value in the distant face value → lower coupon, more interest-rate risk (same maturity).
Going further · duration

The value-weighted average date of a bond's cash flows is its duration; to a first approximation, % price change ≈ −duration × change in yield. Both rules above are “higher duration, more risk”.

Case: Silicon Valley Bank, March 2023

  • Founded 1983 in California; by 2022 the 16th-largest US bank (about US$209 billion of assets). The tech world's bank: start-ups and venture funds kept cash there, mostly far above the US$250,000 deposit-insurance limit.
  • A bank is a bond investor with borrowed money: deposits fund the assets, and depositors can leave at any time. The story below comes from SVB's own quarterly regulatory filings.
  • Deposits went into long-term bonds. Deposits rose from about US$40 billion (2017) to over US$180 billion (early 2022); about US$100 billion went into “held-to-maturity” bonds. When rates rose in 2022, their market value fell about US$15 billion below cost by end-2022, roughly the bank's whole equity (about US$16 billion). (Figures read off the lecture chart.)
DayWhat happened
Wed 8 MarSells US$21 billion of bonds at a US$1.8 billion loss; announces a US$2.25 billion equity raise

The feedback loop

Rates rise → bonds lose value → depositors fear for uninsured money and withdraw → the bank sells bonds to pay them (forced sale) → paper losses become real, equity shrinks → bad news travels → more withdrawals → … Rising rates start it; withdrawals and forced sales keep it going. Holding to maturity only protects you if no one forces you to sell.

You can now: explain why bond prices fall when yields rise; say which bonds carry more interest-rate risk (longer maturity, lower coupon); explain how rising rates and forced sales brought down SVB.

5Inflation and real rates

The nominal rate grows your money; the real rate grows what your money buys.

Hong Kong inflation (consumer prices, change on the year): about 9–11% in the early 1980s and early 1990s; prices fell for six years after 1998 (1999–2004, down to about −4%); since 2005 mostly 0–5%.

Result · the Fisher effect \[1+R=(1+r)(1+h)\] \[r=\frac{1+R}{1+h}-1\approx R-h\]

\(R\) = nominal rate (growth of your money), \(r\) = real rate (growth of what it buys), \(h\) = inflation (growth of prices).

In words: grow your money by \(1+R\), divide by how much prices grew, and what remains is real growth. \(R\approx r+h\) is fine when both rates are small; it drifts when they are not.

Examples
  • Eggs (practice question below): earn 10% while eggs go from $1.80 to $2.00: \(1+r=1.10/1.1111=0.99\), \(r=-1\%\).
  • Deposit pays 10%, prices rise 4%: \(r=1.10/1.04-1=5.77\%\) (approximation 6%).
  • Want 10% real, expect 8% inflation: \(R=1.10\times1.08-1=18.8\%\) (approximation 18%).
  • Bond yields are nominal: a 3% government bond with 2% inflation earns about 1% real.
Watch · be consistent

Discount nominal cash flows (dollars) at the nominal rate, real cash flows (today's purchasing power) at the real rate. Never mix.

Example · one basket a year for 3 years

A basket costs $100 today. You receive enough money to buy one basket at the end of years 1, 2, 3. \(R=10\%\), \(h=5\%\). PV and year-3 FV?

Nominal route: dollars at \(R\). Each basket costs \(100\times1.05^t\): $105.00, $110.25, $115.76.

FV\(_3=273.54\times1.10^3=\$364.09\) (year-3 dollars).

Real route: baskets at \(r\). \(1+r=1.10/1.05=1.0476\), \(r=4.76\%\). In baskets the flows are level, so use the annuity:

\[\begin{aligned}PV&=1\times AF(4.76\%,3)\\&=2.7354\text{ baskets}\\&=2.7354\times\$100=\$273.54\end{aligned}\]

FV\(_3=2.7354\times1.0476^3=3.1451\) baskets: $314.51 at today's prices, or \(\times\$115.76=\$364.09\) at year-3 prices. Both routes agree.

Hong Kong inflation-linked government bonds

  • Silver Bond (residents aged 60+): 3-year, HK$10,000 units; interest every six months at the past six months' average inflation or 4.25%, whichever is higher. 2026 batch: HK$50 billion, issued 15 Sep 2026.
  • iBond (everyone): same design with a 2% floor; eight series 2011–2021, traded on HKEX; none since.
  • The coupon floats with the CPI, so the real return is protected; the floor is a free extra when inflation is low.
  • Silver Bonds are not traded: holders sell back to the Government at par, so no interest-rate risk, by design.
  • Compare: a 2-year Exchange Fund Note yielded about 3.2% in Aug 2026.

6Term structure

Default-free loans of different lengths carry different rates; corporate yields add premia on top.

Definition · term structure of interest rates

The yields of default-free pure-discount bonds (one payment at maturity, no coupons) across maturities: the pure time value of money by horizon. In practice: the US Treasury yield curve; in Hong Kong, Exchange Fund Bills and Government Bonds (nearly default-free and liquid).

Shape: usually upward sloping; occasionally downward sloping (an inverted yield curve); occasionally humped.

Today's curves: US Treasuries (14 Sep 2026) rise from about 3.9% (shortest) to about 5.4% at 20 years, slightly lower at 30; Hong Kong paper (31 Aug 2026) rises from about 2.7% to about 4.1% at 15 years. Both upward sloping, HK lower.

What sets the term structure
ComponentCompensates forEffect on the curve
1. Real rateforgoing the use of moneymostly the overall level; shape only via expected changes in the real rate
2. Inflation premiumloss of purchasing powerlargely the shape: expected inflation rising → upward, falling → downward
3. Interest-rate risk premiumbearing interest-rate riskincreasing in maturity: long bonds react more, far-future rates are more uncertain

Upward curve: real rate flat, inflation premium and risk premium both growing with maturity. Downward curve: inflation premium falls steeply with maturity (inflation expected to drop), enough to outweigh the rising risk premium.

What else is in a yield

  • Default-risk premium: for possible losses if the issuer does not pay.
  • Liquidity premium: for the chance you cannot sell when you want, or only at a poor price.
  • Taxability premium: for tax on interest. US municipal bonds are tax-exempt; Treasuries and corporates are taxable. In HK individuals pay no tax on bond interest.
Result · building a corporate yield

Corporate yield = government yield of the same maturity + default, liquidity and tax premia.

Evergrande's US-dollar bonds traded at yields far above 20% through 2021, months before its December default: the default-risk premium in plain sight.

7Summary and formula card

  • Price = PV of coupons (annuity) + PV of face value. Par, discount or premium as the yield equals, exceeds or falls short of the coupon rate; prices pull to par over time.
  • Semi-annual: halve coupon and yield, double the periods.
  • YTM solves the price equation (trial and error); current yield = coupon / price; holding-period return if you sell early.
  • Interest-rate risk: prices fall when yields rise, more for long maturities and low coupons (SVB).
  • Fisher: \(1+R=(1+r)(1+h)\); nominal flows at the nominal rate, real at the real rate.
  • Term structure: real rate + inflation premium + interest-rate-risk premium; then default, liquidity and tax premia.

8Common mistakes

  • Discounting at the coupon rate. The coupon rate sets \(C\); the yield is the discount rate.
  • Forgetting the face value, or adding it with no discounting.
  • Semi-annual bonds with annual inputs. Coupon/2, yield/2, periods × 2.
  • “Higher yield, higher price.” The opposite: price and yield move inversely.
  • Thinking the YTM is the return whatever happens. Only if you hold to maturity (and no default); selling early depends on the resale yield.
  • Mixing nominal and real: dollar flows at the real rate, or basket flows at the nominal rate.
  • Using \(R\approx r+h\) when rates are large. Use the exact Fisher equation.

Practicewhat is this bond worth?

Last year a firm issued a bond paying $80 a year for 10 years and $1,000 at the end of year 10. Investors now require 9% on bonds like it. Its value today: A. exactly $1,000 · B. more than $1,000 · C. less than $1,000 · D. depends on the firm's profits

Hint

Compare the 8% coupon rate with the 9% yield.

Answer

Yield above coupon rate → discount bond → C. With 9 years left:

\[\begin{aligned}&80\,AF(9\%,9)+\tfrac{1{,}000}{1.09^9}\\&=\boxed{\$940.05}\end{aligned}\]

D is wrong: the payments are fixed by contract.

“Issued last year … for 10 years” could mean 9 or 10 years remain; the answer is C either way ($940.05 with 9 left, $935.82 with 10).
