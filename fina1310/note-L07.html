FINA1310 · Corporate Finance · Lecture 7

NPV and other investment criteria

How a firm decides whether a long-term investment is worth taking: the NPV rule, and why IRR, payback, AAR and the profitability index sometimes give the wrong answer.

0Where this sits

L3 taught how to discount a stream of cash flows. This lecture turns that into a decision rule for projects.

Recap from L3 · PV of a stream \[\begin{aligned}PV={}&\frac{CF_1}{1+r}+\frac{CF_2}{(1+r)^2}\\&+\dots+\frac{CF_T}{(1+r)^T}\end{aligned}\]

Annuity factor: \(AF(r,t)=\dfrac{1-(1+r)^{-t}}{r}\), the PV of $1 a period for \(t\) periods (L3 §2).

Sign convention

Money the firm receives is +, money it pays is −. \(CF_0\) at \(t=0\) (today) is usually the negative initial investment; \(CF_t\) arrives at the end of year \(t\).

1Capital budgeting

Take a long-term investment only if it is worth more to the firm than it costs.

Capital budgeting = planning and evaluating long-term investments. Examples: should a developer build flats or offices on a site; should an airline buy or lease aircraft; should a bakery add an oven line? These are big, long-lived and hard to reverse, so the decision rule matters.

StepQuestionWhere
① Cash flowsWhat comes in and goes out, and when?L8 (Ch. 10, 2.4)
② Discount rateWhat return do investors require?L10 (Ch. 14)
③ Investment criteriaIs it worth more than it costs?this lecture (Ch. 9)
④ Additional analysisWhat if the forecasts are wrong?L9 (Ch. 11)

The course teaches step ③ first, taking ① and ② as given.

A good investment criterion
  • adjusts for the time value of money;
  • adjusts for risk;
  • tells us whether the investment creates value for the firm.

Every rule below is tested against these three.

2Net present value (NPV)

NPV = what the investment is worth minus what it costs, both in today's money.

What is this?

Imagine you could sell the project the moment you start it. A buyer who could earn the required return elsewhere, on something equally risky, would pay at most the present value of its future cash flows: that is what the same cash would cost them elsewhere. NPV is that price minus what you spent to set the project up. It is the wealth the project creates today, on day one, before any of its cash has arrived.

Positive NPV: you built something worth more than it cost. Zero: you only matched what the market already offers. Negative: you overpaid.

Definition · NPV \[\begin{aligned}NPV={}&CF_0+\frac{CF_1}{1+r}+\frac{CF_2}{(1+r)^2}\\&+\dots+\frac{CF_T}{(1+r)^T}\end{aligned}\]

In words: NPV = market value − cost, where market value = PV of all cash inflows and cost = PV of all cash outflows. \(r\) is the required return: what investors could earn elsewhere on something equally risky.

Reading the formula
\(CF_0\)
today's cash flow, usually the negative set-up cost. It is not discounted: it is already in today's money.
\(CF_t\)
the net cash the project brings in year \(t\): what the day-one buyer is paying for.
\(\dfrac{1}{(1+r)^t}\)
what $1 in year \(t\) is worth today. It is smaller for later years and for a higher \(r\).
\(r\)
the required return: the buyer's next-best use of the money at the same risk.
the sum
market value minus cost: the day-one gain in wealth.

Three steps: (1) estimate the cash flows; (2) estimate the required return for the project's risk; (3) discount every cash flow to today and add. Steps 1–2 (forecasts and a rate) are the hard part; step 3 is just L3.

Example · a chocolate factory

Costs $500,000 today; net inflows $150,000, $200,000, $160,000 at the end of years 1–3; sold for $300,000 at the end of year 3. Required return 20%. Start it?

Year 3 carries two flows: \(160{,}000+300{,}000=460{,}000\). (Timeline in $ thousands.)

\[\begin{aligned}NPV&=-500{,}000+125{,}000\\&\quad+138{,}889+266{,}204\\&=\$30{,}093\gt0\end{aligned}\]

Positive → start it.

The NPV rule
  • Independent projects (taking one does not affect the others): accept if NPV > 0, reject if NPV < 0.
  • Mutually exclusive projects (taking one rules out the others): take the one with the highest positive NPV.

A positive-NPV project adds value, so it raises shareholders' wealth by its NPV.

Where positive NPVs come from

In an efficient market (one where prices reflect all available information), a share or bond's price equals the PV of its expected cash flows, so buying it has NPV \(=PV-\text{price}=0\): competition among investors removes positive NPVs. Real projects can have NPV > 0 when the firm has an edge rivals lack: a brand, a technology, a location, a licence. Capital budgeting is the search for these.

Real world · Hong Kong's Northern Link
  • Part 1 (July 2025): MTR finances, builds, owns and runs the line and bears its risks. Main Line: 10.7 km underground, open by 2034 or earlier; capital cost HK$31.4 billion (July 2025 prices).
  • Without support the line may be “financially non-viable”: NPV may be negative at MTR's required return.
  • The Government bridges the gap with land: Rail-plus-Property sites, HK$39.05 billion off their land premium, “not more than the minimum necessary”, i.e. enough to lift NPV to about 0.

Higher rates, fewer positive-NPV projects. A higher \(r\) lowers the PV of every future cash flow. The share of US firms not planning to invest that cite unfavourable financing rose from about 2% (late 2021) to about 24% (2023–24) as the Fed funds rate went from near 0 to over 5%, then fell as rates were cut.

Example · a bakery's oven line (last year differs)

Ovens $500,000 plus $50,000 of extra inventory today. Net working capital (NWC) = cash tied up in inventory and similar short-term items; it comes back when the project ends. Operating cash flow $150,000 a year for 5 years; at the end of year 5 the ovens sell for $40,000 after tax and the $50,000 NWC is recovered. Required return 12%.

\(CF_0=-550{,}000\). Year 5 carries three flows:

\[\begin{aligned}CF_5&=150{,}000+40{,}000+50{,}000\\&=240{,}000\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}NPV&=-550{,}000\\&\quad+150{,}000\times AF(12\%,4)\\&\quad+240{,}000/1.12^5\\&=-550{,}000+455{,}602+136{,}183\\&=\$41{,}785\gt0\end{aligned}\]

Invest. Treat years 1–4 as a 4-year annuity and year 5 separately.

You can now: say what NPV measures (value created today); compute it for an uneven stream, putting salvage and recovered NWC in the final year; apply the NPV rule to independent and mutually exclusive projects; and say where positive NPVs come from.

3Internal rate of return (IRR)

IRR is the discount rate that makes NPV exactly zero: the project's own rate of return.

What is this?

Think of the project as a savings account. You pay the set-up cost in today and take the later cash flows out. The IRR is the interest rate that account would have to pay so that your withdrawals empty it exactly at the end, no more and no less.

Then compare: if the project's “account” pays more than you could get elsewhere at the same risk (the required return), take it.

Definition · IRR \[\begin{aligned}0={}&CF_0+\frac{CF_1}{1+IRR}+\frac{CF_2}{(1+IRR)^2}\\&+\dots+\frac{CF_T}{(1+IRR)^T}\end{aligned}\]

In words: the rate at which the PV of the inflows exactly pays back the cost. “Internal” because it depends only on the project's own cash flows, not on market rates. It is the project's expected return, and the break-even rate: the highest required return at which you would still accept.

There is no formula for IRR beyond two periods: find it by trial and error (or a calculator).

The IRR rule

Accept if IRR > required return. It gives the same decision as NPV when both hold:

  • cash flows are conventional: only the first is negative (one sign change);
  • the project is independent.

Chan: coffee IRR 54.70% > 12%, sandwich IRR 9.64% < 12%. Same decisions as NPV.

Oven line: solve

\[\begin{aligned}0={}&-550{,}000+150{,}000\,AF(IRR,4)\\&+240{,}000/(1+IRR)^5\end{aligned}\]

IRR = 14.83% > 12%. NPV ($41,785 > 0) and IRR both say invest.

Tools

Calculator (covered in tutorials): enter cash flows by stages: CF0 = −550,000; C01 = 150,000, F01 = 4 (four equal flows); C02 = 240,000, F02 = 1. NPV with I = 12 → $41,784.85; IRR → 14.83%.

Excel: NPV(rate, value1, …) treats value1 as year 1, so add CF0 outside: =CF0+NPV(12%, CF1:CF5). IRR(values, [guess]) includes CF0. (Lecture file: Excel_template_Lecture_07.xlsx.)

You can now: explain IRR as the project's own rate of return; find it by trial and error or with the calculator's cash-flow entries; apply the IRR rule and say when it agrees with NPV; and enter NPV and IRR in Excel correctly.

4When IRR misleads

When NPV and another rule disagree, always use NPV. IRR fails with nonconventional cash flows and with mutually exclusive projects.

What is this?

The savings-account picture of IRR assumes you pay in first and take money out later. Take the money first and pay later, and you are the borrower: now a high rate is bad for you. Switch signs more than once, and two different rates can balance the account, or none can.

A rate also ignores size: 100% on $10 earns $10, while 20% on $1,000 earns $200. NPV counts the dollars created today, so it has none of these problems.

Nonconventional cash flows (a later cash flow is negative)

  • I · Money now, payment later (a financing-type project, like borrowing): +$100 today, −$120 next year. IRR = 20%, but NPV rises with \(r\): at 10%, \(NPV=100-120/1.1=-\$9.09\); at 30%, \(+\$7.69\). The rule flips: accept only if IRR < required return. Blindly applying “IRR > r” gets it backwards.
  • II · A plant with a clean-up cost. Build for $500,000, earn $200,000 a year for 8 years, and removal at the end of year 8 costs a further $1,200,000. Year 8 net: \(200{,}000-1{,}200{,}000=-1{,}000{,}000\). Flows: −500,000, +200,000 (years 1–7), −1,000,000. Two sign changes → two IRRs, 7.8% and 16.9%. NPV > 0 only between them (peak about $8,900 near 12%): build only if the required return is between 7.8% and 16.9%. “IRR > r” has no clear meaning here.
  • III · No IRR at all. A: −105, +250, −150 has NPV < 0 at every rate (−$5 at 0%, about −$0.83 at its best near 20%). B, the same flows with signs reversed, has NPV > 0 at every rate. Neither curve ever crosses zero, yet NPV still decides at once: reject A, accept B.
Going further · counting IRRs

A cash-flow stream can have at most as many IRRs as it has sign changes (Descartes' rule of signs). One sign change (conventional) → exactly one IRR. Two → zero, one or two.

6Average accounting return and profitability index

Average accounting return (AAR)

Definition · AAR \[AAR=\frac{\text{average net income}}{\text{average book value}}\]

In words: accounting profit per dollar of (accounting) investment. Accept if the AAR exceeds a target. Book value = cost minus accumulated depreciation, as on the balance sheet.

$500,000 of assets, straight-line to 0 over 5 years, net income $100,000 a year: average book value \((500{,}000+0)/2=\$250{,}000\); AAR \(=100{,}000/250{,}000=40\%\).

Example · Chan's AAR: the wrong yardstick

Depreciation \(20{,}000/4=5{,}000\) a year; average book value \((20{,}000+0)/2=10{,}000\). Net income = cash flow − depreciation (no taxes).

  • Coffee: average CF 11,500 → average net income 6,500 → AAR 65%.
  • Sandwich: average CF 6,250 → average net income 1,250 → AAR 12.5%.

Against the 12% required return, AAR accepts the sandwich shop, whose NPV is −$979. AAR uses book values and accounting income, not cash flows or market values, and ignores timing.

Profitability index (PI)

What is this? (profitability index)

PI is value for money: dollars of value today for every dollar put in. It helps when money, not ideas, is the bottleneck, because then you want the most value from each scarce dollar.

When you can pick only one project, though, the question is total dollars, not dollars per dollar. A small stall giving $1.82 per $1 on $10,000 adds less value than a shop giving $1.64 per $1 on $20,000.

Definition · PI \[PI=\frac{\text{PV of future cash flows}}{\text{initial investment}}\]

In words: value created per dollar invested. Accept if PI > 1, which for a single project is the same decision as NPV > 0.

Chan: coffee \(36{,}510/20{,}000=1.83\); sandwich \(19{,}021/20{,}000=0.95\).

Useful when capital is limited: rank projects by value per dollar invested.

But PI ranks mutually exclusive projects wrongly (scale again). At 10%: A (−10,000, +20,000) has PI 1.82, NPV $8,181.82; B (−20,000, +36,000) has PI 1.64, NPV $12,727.27. PI prefers A; NPV correctly prefers B. The increment B − A has PI 1.45 > 1, so the extra investment pays.

You can now: compute AAR and say why it is the wrong yardstick; compute PI and use it when capital is limited; and explain why PI, like IRR, can rank mutually exclusive projects wrongly.

8Summary and formula card

  • Capital budgeting: take investments worth more than they cost. Steps: cash flows (L8), discount rate (L10), criteria (here), what-if analysis (L9).
  • NPV is the only rule that measures value created; positive NPVs come from an edge over rivals.
  • IRR agrees with NPV only for conventional, independent projects; it fails for financing-type flows, multiple or no IRRs, and mutually exclusive projects (timing via the crossover rate, scale).
  • Payback ignores time value and late flows; discounted payback fixes time value only; AAR uses book numbers; PI fails on scale.

9Common mistakes

  • Excel's NPV() discounts its first value by one period. Add CF0 outside: =CF0+NPV(r, CF1:CFT).
  • Missing last-year flows: salvage value and recovered working capital sit in the final year alongside the operating cash flow.
  • Picking the higher IRR (or PI) among mutually exclusive projects. Compare NPVs, or use the crossover rate.
  • Applying “IRR > r” to nonconventional flows. Check the signs first; draw the NPV profile.
  • Payback with later outflows, or forgetting the fraction of the final year.
  • AAR uses net income and book value, not cash flows; comparing it with a market required return is apples and oranges.

Practicecrossover rate

Mutually exclusive: P = −1,000 now, +1,300 in year 1. Q = −1,000 now, +1,500 in year 2. Find each IRR, the crossover rate, and the choice at 10%.

Hint

Crossover = IRR of P − Q: 0, +1,300, −1,500.

Answer

IRR: P 30%; Q \(\sqrt{1.5}-1=22.47\%\). Crossover: \(1{,}300/(1+r)=1{,}500/(1+r)^2\) gives \(1+r=1{,}500/1{,}300\), so \(r=15.38\%\).

At 10% (below 15.38%): NPV P \(=\$181.82\), Q \(=\$239.67\) → \(\boxed{\text{take Q}}\), despite its lower IRR.
