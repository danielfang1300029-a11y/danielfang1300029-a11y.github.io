FINA1310 · Corporate Finance · Lecture 2

Time value of money

One cash flow, two dates: moving money forward (compounding) and back (discounting), and solving the one equation that links them for the rate or the time.

0Where this sits

Valuing anything means valuing its cash flows, which arrive at different times and with different risk. This lecture handles timing only (no uncertainty; risk comes in L6). Every later lecture uses today's two operations: compounding and discounting.

Recap from L1 · the bridge

Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macau Bridge: cost ≈ RMB 110 billion, then ≈ RMB 1 billion a year for 150 years. The naïve sum \(150-110=+\text{RMB }40\) billion is wrong: RMB 1 in 2168 is not worth RMB 1 in 2018. This lecture is the fix.

Sign convention

Unless stated otherwise: \(t=0\) is today, cash flows occur at the end of each period, money you receive is + and money you pay is −.

1Why money has a time value

A dollar today beats a dollar next year; the interest rate is the exchange rate between the two.

Three reasons $1 today is worth more than $1 next year:

  • Interest: money in hand can be invested and grow.
  • Inflation (prices rising over time) erodes purchasing power: next year's dollar buys less.
  • Risk: a promise of later money may not be kept (set aside until L6).

So we need to answer: what is next year's dollar worth today, and what amount today is equivalent to a dollar next year? Example: HK$1,000,000 now or HK$1,050,000 in a year? You cannot compare them until you know the rate (Practice Q1).

Timelines

A timeline puts every cash flow \(CF_t\) at its date. Date \(t\) is the end of period \(t\) and the beginning of period \(t+1\) (the end of year 1 is the start of year 2). Example: invest 100 today, receive 30 at the end of each of the next four years.

Different dates are different “currencies”

$100 at \(t=0\) and $120 at \(t=4\) cannot be added or compared directly, just as you cannot add $150 and £300. Convert first. At HK$10.50 per pound: \(150+300\times10.50=\text{HK}\$3{,}300\), or in pounds \(150/10.50+300=£314.29\). Either currency works as the common unit; likewise, either date works.

Definitions · used all semester
  • Present value (PV): value of a cash flow at an earlier date, usually today.
  • Future value (FV): value of a cash flow at a later date.
  • Interest rate \(r\): the rate linking them. Also called the discount rate, required return, cost of capital or opportunity cost of capital (the return you give up by not investing elsewhere).
  • Principal: the amount originally invested or borrowed.
  • Compounding = moving value forward in time; discounting = moving it back.

Periods can be years, months or days, but \(r\) must be quoted per period: use the matching “exchange rate”.

2Future value: compounding

Each period multiply by \((1+r)\); interest earns interest.

One period. $100 at 10% for a year: \(FV_1=100\times1.10=\$110\). The $10 is interest, the $100 is principal. In general \(FV_1=PV(1+r)\).

Formula\(FV_t=PV(1+t\,r)\)\(FV_t=PV(1+r)^t\)
Result · compound growth \[FV_t=PV\times(1+r)^t\]

In words: future value = today's amount × (1 + rate) once for every period. \((1+r)^t\) is the future value interest factor: the exchange rate between a dollar today and a dollar at date \(t\).

At the same \(r\), compound beats simple after the first period, and the gap widens every period. Almost every rate you meet is compound: deposits, loans, bond yields, growth of sales, dividends and GDP.

Two Hong Kong stories
  • The old IOU. Your great-great-grandmother lent $100 in 1900 at 1% a month. The borrower's tycoon descendant offers an HK$8 million flat to settle. Accept? 126 years = 1,512 months: \(100\times1.01^{1512}\approx\$342\) million. No.
  • The online loan. A lending app lends $1,000 at 1% a day, rolled over for a year. Simple: \(1{,}000(1+365\times0.01)=\$4{,}650\). Compound: \(1{,}000\times1.01^{365}=\$37{,}783\). This is why usury rules (legal caps on lending rates) exist, and why you must ask how a rate compounds.

All amounts in the IOU example are in HK$.

Try it · future value of $1: compound against simple

compound, chosen rsimple, chosen rcompound at 0, 5, 10, 15, 20%

Higher \(r\) raises FV; longer \(t\) raises FV; the curves pull apart over time. At 10%, $1 becomes $2.59 after 10 years (simple: $2.00).

Compound growth is everywhere. The FV formula is also a growth formula: value at \(t\) = value today × \((1+g)^t\), with \(g\) a growth rate.

  • Savings accounts grow compoundly.
  • A firm growing sales 10% a year doubles in about 7 years (\(1.10^7=1.95\)).
  • GDP (the total output of an economy) and inflation compound too: prices rising 2% a year are 22% higher after ten years (\(1.02^{10}=1.219\)), not 20%.

3Present value: discounting

What is this?

Compounding asks: if I put money in the bank today, how big will the balance be in three years? Discounting asks the same question backwards: how much must I put in today so that the balance shows $100 in three years? Each year the bank multiplies the balance by \((1+r)\), so to step back one year you undo that by dividing. Money that is further away has more years of growth ahead of it, so you need to put in less today; a higher rate means faster growth, so again less today.

Discounting is compounding run backwards: divide by \((1+r)\) once per period.

Back to the bridge: 150 years of tolls cannot simply be added, for the same reason as $150 + £300. Convert every flow to one date first.

One period. How much today at 10% gives $100 in a year? \(PV\times1.10=100\), so \(PV=100/1.10=\$90.91\). In general \(PV=FV_1/(1+r)\).

Two periods. \(PV=100/1.10^2=100/1.21=\$82.64\).

Result · present value \[PV=\frac{FV_t}{(1+r)^t}\]

In words: present value = future amount ÷ (1 + rate) once for every period. \(1/(1+r)^t\) is the discount factor: the value today of $1 at date \(t\).

Unless we say otherwise, the “value” of something means its present value.

Applications
  • Down payment. Need $10,000 in one year; an ETF (exchange-traded fund: a basket of shares traded like one share) is expected to earn 7%. Invest \(10{,}000/1.07=\$9{,}345.79\) today.
  • Trust fund. Worth $19,671.51 today after 10 years at 7%. Original investment: \(19{,}671.51/1.07^{10}=\$10{,}000\).
  • School fees. HK$180,000 needed in 17 years, earning 8%: \(180{,}000/1.08^{17}=\text{HK}\$48{,}648.41\) today.

The further away and the higher the rate, the less you need today.

Higher \(r\) lowers PV; longer \(t\) lowers PV; PV goes to zero as \(t\) grows. At 10%, $1 in 10 years is worth $0.39 today.

One equation, four variables

\(FV_t=PV(1+r)^t\) and \(PV=FV_t/(1+r)^t\) are the same equation. It links PV, FV, \(r\) and \(t\): given any three, find the fourth (that is the whole of Chapter 5). Compare at date 0 or at date \(t\): the ranking is the same.

Example · an investment contract, three ways

Pay $10,000 today, receive $12,000 in three years. Your bank pays 6.5%. Sign?

Compare future values (at t = 3) · $k = thousands

Compare present values (at t = 0)

  • FV: at the bank, \(10{,}000\times1.065^3=\$12{,}079.50\), more than the $12,000 offered.
  • PV: the $12,000 is worth \(12{,}000/1.065^3=\$9{,}934.19\lt\$10{,}000\) asked.
  • Rate: the contract earns \((12{,}000/10{,}000)^{1/3}-1\) \(=6.27\%\), below 6.5% (section 4).

Three routes, one answer: decline. Always compare at the same date, or compare rates.

You can now: find the present value of a single future amount; explain why PV falls when the rate rises or the wait gets longer; decide a deal by comparing values at the same date or by comparing rates.

4Solving for the rate and the number of periods

Result · implied rate \[r=\Big(\frac{FV_t}{PV}\Big)^{1/t}-1\]

In words: the growth multiple, \(t\)-th root, minus 1. (From \(FV_t/PV=(1+r)^t\): take the \(t\)-th root of both sides.)

  • $1,000 today → $1,200 in five years: \(1.2^{1/5}-1=3.71\%\) a year.
  • “Double your money in six years”: \(2^{1/6}-1=12.25\%\) a year.

This implied rate is how we will read yields (the return implied by a price) off bond prices in L4.

Example · borrow from a friend or the bank?

Need $1,000. Friend: repay $1,250 in three years. Bank: 8% a year.

  • Friend's implied rate: \(1.25^{1/3}-1=7.72\%\lt8\%\).
  • Check by FV: the bank would want \(1{,}000\times1.08^3=\$1{,}259.71\gt\$1{,}250\).

Borrow from the friend (and thank them).

Result · number of periods \[t=\frac{\ln(FV_t/PV)}{\ln(1+r)}\]

In words: log of the growth multiple ÷ log of one period's growth factor. (Take natural logs of \(FV_t/PV=(1+r)^t\); \(\ln\) turns the power \(t\) into a multiplier.)

  • Apple Watch: costs $3,000, you have $2,000, earn 5% a month. \(t=\ln1.5/\ln1.05=8.31\) months: you can buy it after the 9th monthly interest payment.
  • Car: have $15,000, car costs $20,000, earn 10% a year. \(t=\ln(4/3)/\ln1.10=3.02\) years.

5The Rule of 72, Excel and the calculator

Rule of 72 · quick doubling \[\text{periods to double}\approx\frac{72}{\text{rate in }\%}\]
  • 9% a year: about \(72/9=8\) years (exactly 8.04).
  • To double in 12 years: about \(72/12=6\%\) (exactly 5.95%).
  • Halving works too: at 4% inflation, purchasing power halves in about \(72/4=18\) years (exactly 17.67).

Good for rates of about 2%–15%. In exams use the exact formulas from section 4.

Going further · why 72?

Exact doubling time is \(\ln2/\ln(1+r)\approx0.693/r\), i.e. 69.3 ÷ rate in %. Because \(\ln(1+r)\) is a bit below \(r\), 72 fits better at typical rates, and it divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12.

Tools

Excel (pmt = 0 for a single cash flow; pmt is used from L3):

  • FV(rate, nper, pmt, pv): future value
  • PV(rate, nper, pmt, fv): present value
  • RATE(nper, pmt, pv, fv): interest rate
  • NPER(rate, pmt, pv, fv): number of periods

Financial calculator: keys N, I/Y, PV, PMT, FV. Enter three, compute the fourth.

  • Signs: money out −, money in +. PV = −1,000, FV = 1,200, N = 5 gives I/Y = 3.71.
  • Enter the rate as a percent (3.71, not 0.0371), and N and I/Y in the same period.
  • Only models on the Exam Office approved list are allowed in exams.
Going further · Excel signs and rate format

Excel uses the same sign convention: FV(0.05,2,0,-1000) returns 1,102.50, while FV(0.05,2,0,1000) returns −1,102.50. Unlike the calculator, Excel takes the rate as a decimal (0.05 or 5%).

6Summary and formula card

  • Money has a time value (interest, inflation, risk). Cash flows at different dates are different currencies: convert to one date before comparing.
  • Compounding moves value forward, discounting moves it back. Compound interest beats simple interest, enormously over long horizons.
  • PV falls as \(r\) or \(t\) rises; FV rises with both.
  • One equation, four variables: given three, find the fourth. Rule of 72 for quick estimates; Excel and calculator for exact ones.

\(r\) is per period; \(t\) counts the same periods.

7Common mistakes

  • Comparing amounts at different dates. Move them to one date (or compare rates) first.
  • Rate and period mismatch. Monthly periods need a monthly rate: 1% a month for 12 months, not 12% for 12 months.
  • Simple instead of compound. Unless told “simple interest”, compound: \(1.05^2\), not \(1+2\times0.05\).
  • Percent vs decimal. Formulas use 0.05; the calculator's I/Y key takes 5.
  • Calculator signs. PV and FV entered with the same sign → error.
  • Rounding \(t\). If interest is credited monthly, \(t=8.31\) means you need 9 months.
  • Rule of 72 in exams. It is an approximation; give the exact answer.

8Practice

Practicenow or in a year?

HK$1,000,000 now or HK$1,050,000 in one year? What do you need to know to decide?

Hint

Move the HK$1,000,000 forward one year at the going rate.

Answer

You need the interest rate (the exchange rate between the two dates).

  • At 3%: \(1{,}000{,}000\times1.03=1{,}030{,}000\), less than 1,050,000 → wait.
  • At 8%: \(1{,}080{,}000\gt1{,}050{,}000\) → take the money now.

\(\boxed{\text{Depends on }r\text{; break-even }r=5\%}\)

Practice$1,000 at 5% for two years

$1,000 in an account paying 5% a year. Balance after two years? A. $1,050 · B. $1,100 · C. $1,102.50 · D. $1,125. Answer with simple and with compound interest.

Hint

Simple: \(PV(1+t\,r)\). Compound: \(PV(1+r)^t\).

Answer

Simple: \(1{,}000(1+2\times0.05)=\$1{,}100\) → B. Compound: \(1{,}000\times1.05^2=\$1{,}102.50\) → C. The extra $2.50 is 5% on year 1's $50 of interest.

Over five years: simple $1,250, compound $1,276.28. Small gap over two years, bigger over five, enormous over decades.

\(\boxed{\text{B (simple)},\ \text{C (compound)}}\)

Practicefinancial literacy (a worldwide survey; most adults miss one)

1. Borrow US$100. Less to repay: US$105, or US$100 plus 3%?
2. A bank adds 15% a year. Does it add more in year 2 than in year 1, or the same?
3. US$100 at 10% a year for five years: more than US$150, exactly, or less?

Answer

1. US$100 + 3% = US$103, less than US$105.
2. More: 15% of a larger balance (compound interest).
3. More: \(100\times1.10^5=\text{US}\$161.05\); only simple interest gives exactly 150.
