FINA1310 · Corporate Finance · Lecture 3

Discounted cash flow valuation

Valuing a stream of cash flows: add up present values, with shortcuts for level and growing streams, how interest rates are quoted, and how loans are repaid.

0Where this sits

Lecture 2 valued one cash flow. This lecture values many. Every later valuation (bonds L4, stocks L5, projects L7–L8) is this lecture plus a story about where the cash flows come from. Textbook: Chapter 6.

Recap from L2 · one cash flow \[FV_t=PV\,(1+r)^t\qquad PV=\frac{FV_t}{(1+r)^t}\]

In words: moving money one period later multiplies it by \((1+r)\); moving it one period earlier divides by \((1+r)\). Given any three of \(PV, FV_t, r, t\) you can find the fourth.

Sign convention

Money you receive is +, money you pay is −. On a financial calculator, PV and PMT therefore usually have opposite signs (you receive the loan, you pay the instalments). Time \(t=0\) is today; \(t=1\) is the end of period 1.

Why it matters: the standard answer to “How do you value a company?” is discounted cash flow (DCF): forecast the cash flows, discount each one, add them up. The value of any productive asset is the present value of the cash flows it generates. Real assets produce many flows (a mall built once, rent every year; a 30-year mortgage: one inflow, 360 monthly outflows), so we need to value streams.

1Many cash flows: move each one, then add

You can only add cash flows that sit at the same date. So move every flow to one date, then add.

Future value of a stream

Example · two deposits

$100 today and $100 in one year at 10%. Value at \(t=2\)?

\[\begin{aligned}FV_2&=100(1.10)^2+100(1.10)\\&=121+110=\$231\end{aligned}\]
Example · inflows and outflows

+100, +200, −300 at \(t=0,1,2\); 10%. Value at \(t=4\)? Outflows keep their minus sign.

\[\begin{aligned}FV_4&=146.41+266.20-363.00\\&=\$49.61\end{aligned}\]

If the same three flows sat at \(t=2,3,4\) instead: \(121+220-300=\$41.00\) (the flow already at \(t=4\) is not compounded).

Result · FV of a stream \[\begin{aligned}FV_t={}&CF_0(1+r)^t+CF_1(1+r)^{t-1}\\&+\dots+CF_t\end{aligned}\]

In words: compound each cash flow for the number of periods between its date and date \(t\), then add. A date with no cash flow adds zero.

Present value of a stream

Example · $100, $200, $300 at t = 1, 2, 3; 10%

\(PV=90.91+165.29+225.39=\$481.59\)

  • First flow today (same flows at \(t=0,1,2\)): today's flow is not discounted: \(PV=\$529.75\) \((100+181.82+247.93)\).
  • Outflows first (−100, −200, +300 at \(t=2,3,4\)): \(PV=-\$28.00\) \((-82.64-150.26+204.90)\). A PV can be negative.
Result · PV of a stream \[\begin{aligned}PV={}&CF_0+\frac{CF_1}{1+r}+\frac{CF_2}{(1+r)^2}\\&+\dots+\frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}\end{aligned}\]

In words: discount each cash flow back to today, then add.

Link between the two: \(PV=FV_t/(1+r)^t\), exactly as for one cash flow, because every PV term times \((1+r)^t\) is the matching FV term. So you may value a stream at any convenient date and move the total afterwards. Check: \(FV_2\) of +100, +200, +300 at \(t=0,1,2\) is \(121+220+300=641\), and \(641/1.21=529.75\). ✓

Net present value (NPV)

Definition · NPV

Net present value = PV of what you receive − what you pay today. The most you should pay for a stream is its PV: pay less and you earn more than your required rate; pay more and you earn less.

Rule (built on in L7): take positive-NPV deals, reject negative-NPV deals.

Example · broker's offer

Pay $100 today for $40 in one year and $75 in two years; you require 15%.

\[\begin{aligned}NPV&=-100+\tfrac{40}{1.15}+\tfrac{75}{1.15^2}\\&=-100+34.78+56.71=-\$8.51\end{aligned}\]

Negative → reject.

Example · deferred pension

Five annual payments of $25,000, the first in 40 years; 12% required.

\[\begin{aligned}PV&=\tfrac{25{,}000}{1.12^{40}}+\dots+\tfrac{25{,}000}{1.12^{44}}\\&=\$1{,}084.71\end{aligned}\]

$125,000 of payments is worth about $1,000 today: 40 years at 12% is a long way off. Five terms is fine to add by hand; 360 mortgage payments is not, hence the formulas below.

2Annuities: the same cash flow every period

Definition · annuity

An annuity is a fixed number of equal cash flows \(C\), one per period.

  • Ordinary annuity: first payment at the end of period 1 (\(t=1,\dots,t\)). Loan and mortgage payments, bond coupons, most pensions. “Annuity” alone means ordinary.
  • Annuity due: first payment today (\(t=0,\dots,t-1\)). Rent, insurance premiums, leases.
Result · annuity present value \[PV=C\times\underbrace{\frac{1-\dfrac{1}{(1+r)^t}}{r}}_{AF(r,\,t)}\]

In words: PV = payment × annuity factor. The annuity factor \(AF(r,t)\) is the PV of $1 a period for \(t\) periods.

Show the calculation: where the formula comes from

(1)

\[PV=\tfrac{C}{1+r}+\tfrac{C}{(1+r)^2}+\dots+\tfrac{C}{(1+r)^t}\]

(2) multiply by \((1+r)\):

\[(1+r)PV=C+\tfrac{C}{1+r}+\dots+\tfrac{C}{(1+r)^{t-1}}\]

(2) − (1): every middle term cancels, leaving \(r\,PV=C-\dfrac{C}{(1+r)^t}\). Divide by \(r\): \(PV=\dfrac{C}{r}\Big(1-\dfrac{1}{(1+r)^t}\Big)\).

Check: $100 a year for 4 years at 10% by hand is \(90.91+82.64+75.13+68.30=\$316.99\); by formula \(AF(10\%,4)=3.1699\), and \(100\times3.1699=\$316.99\). ✓

Reading the formula. \(C\uparrow\) → PV up proportionally. \(r\uparrow\) → PV down. \(t\uparrow\) → PV up, but each extra payment adds less. Four variables (PV, \(C\), \(r\), \(t\)): given three, find the fourth.

Tools

Calculator: N, I/Y, PMT, PV with FV = 0. Excel: PV(rate, nper, pmt), PMT(rate, nper, pv), NPER(rate, pmt, pv), RATE(nper, pmt, pv).

Watch · where the answer lands

The formula gives the value one period before the first payment. Payments from age 65 → the formula values them at 64.

Example · which retirement plan? 6% a year

A: $12,000 a year for 15 years from 65. B: $10,000 a year for 20 years from 65. C: $10,000 a year for 20 years from 60.

  • A vs B (both valued at 64): \(A=12{,}000\,AF(6\%,15)=\$116{,}547\); \(B=10{,}000\,AF(6\%,20)=\$114{,}699\). Choose A: more money sooner beats five extra years.
  • C vs A, both at 59: \(C=10{,}000\,AF(6\%,20)=\$114{,}699\) (at 59); A at 59 \(=116{,}547/1.06^5=\$87{,}091\). Choose C: starting five years earlier is worth a lot.
Example · the HK$10 million sweepstake

30 year-end instalments of HK$333,333.33 at 5%:

\[\begin{aligned}PV&=333{,}333.33\times15.3725\\&=\text{HK}\$5{,}124{,}150\end{aligned}\]

About half the headline. Lotteries, sports contracts and settlements are often quoted as undiscounted sums.

Solving for the payment, the term, or the rate

Payment\[C=\frac{PV}{AF(r,t)}\]
  • Car loan: HK$100,000, 24 months, 12% APR = 1% a month. \(AF(1\%,24)=21.2434\), so \(C=\text{HK}\$4{,}707.35\) a month.
  • Mortgage: HK$5,000,000, 30 years, 3.5% APR = 0.2917% a month: \(C=\text{HK}\$22{,}452\) (\(=5{,}000{,}000/AF(0.2917\%,360)\)). Undiscounted total of 360 payments: HK$8.08 million.

Rate quoted per year but paid monthly? Convert to the rate per payment period first (section 5).

Number of payments\[t=-\frac{\ln\!\big(1-PV\cdot r/C\big)}{\ln(1+r)}\]

Car loan at 1% a month, but you can afford only HK$2,500: \(t=-\ln(0.6)/\ln(1.01)=51.3\) → 52 payments, the last one smaller.

If \(PV\cdot r/C\ge1\) there is no answer: the payment does not even cover the interest, so the loan never ends.

Rate: trial and error

No formula exists for \(r\); the calculator and RATE() search, and so can you. Pay $10,000 now, get $1,000 a month for 12 months:

  • 2%: \(1{,}000\,AF(2\%,12)=\$10{,}575\) → too high, raise \(r\).
  • 3%: $9,954 → too low, lower \(r\).
  • 2.92%: $10,002 → \(r\approx2.92\%\) a month.

That is 35% a year as an APR, 41% as an EAR (section 5): “$1,000 a month for a year” is not modest.

Annuity future value and annuity due

Result · annuity FV \[FV_t=C\times\frac{(1+r)^t-1}{r}\]

In words: the annuity PV compounded forward \(t\) periods; it lands on the date of the last payment.

Saving $2,000 a year for 40 years at 7.5%: \(FV=2{,}000\times227.26=\$454{,}513\). You deposit $80,000; compounding supplies the other $374,513.

Result · annuity due \[PV_{\text{due}}=PV_{\text{ord}}\times(1+r)\]\[FV_{\text{due}}=FV_{\text{ord}}\times(1+r)\]

In words: every payment comes one period earlier, so each is worth \((1+r)\) times more.

Saving for a flat: $10,000 a year for 3 years at 8%, first deposit today. Balance at end of year 3:

\[\begin{aligned}FV&=10{,}000\times\tfrac{1.08^3-1}{0.08}\times1.08\\&=32{,}464\times1.08=\$35{,}061.12\end{aligned}\]

3Perpetuities: level cash flows forever

Result · perpetuity \[PV=\frac{C}{r}\qquad PV_{\text{due}}=\frac{C}{r}(1+r)\]

In words: let \(t\to\infty\) in the annuity formula; \(1/(1+r)^t\to0\), leaving \(C/r\). There is no future value: the stream never ends.

  • Preferred stock pays a fixed dividend forever: $1 a year at 10% is worth \(1/0.10=\$10\). IBM preferred paying $2 a quarter priced at $50 implies \(2/50=4\%\) a quarter.
  • British consols were the classic perpetuity; the UK redeemed the last of them in 2015.

An annuity is the difference of two perpetuities

Perpetuity from 1 − perpetuity from t+1 = t-period annuity

A · perpetuity from 1

B · perpetuity from t + 1 (subtract)

A − B · ordinary annuity

Perpetuity from \(t=1\) minus perpetuity from \(t+1\) leaves exactly the payments at \(1,\dots,t\):

\[\frac{C}{r}-\frac{C/r}{(1+r)^t}=C\times\frac{1-\frac{1}{(1+r)^t}}{r}\]

The second perpetuity is valued at \(t\) (one period before its first payment) as \(C/r\), then discounted \(t\) periods. Exam use: build any odd stream from perpetuities and annuities that start and stop at the right dates.

4Growing streams

Cash flows start at \(C\) at \(t=1\) and grow at a constant rate \(g\) a period: \(C,\ C(1+g),\ C(1+g)^2,\dots\). Each term is the previous one × \(\frac{1+g}{1+r}\): growth partly offsets discounting.

Result · growing annuity and growing perpetuity (need g < r) \[PV_{\text{g. annuity}}=C\times\frac{1-\left(\frac{1+g}{1+r}\right)^{t}}{r-g}\] \[PV_{\text{g. perpetuity}}=\frac{C}{r-g}\]

In words: as before, but the effective discount factor is \(\frac{1+g}{1+r}\) instead of \(\frac{1}{1+r}\). With \(g=0\) these become the level annuity and perpetuity. If \(g\ge r\) the sum never converges: no finite value.

Show the calculation: the geometric series

\(PV=\dfrac{C}{1+r}\Big[1+q+q^2+\dots+q^{t-1}\Big]\) with \(q=\dfrac{1+g}{1+r}\).

The bracket sums to \(\dfrac{1-q^t}{1-q}\), and

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{1}{1+r}\cdot\frac{1}{1-q}&=\frac{1}{(1+r)-(1+g)}\\&=\frac{1}{r-g}\end{aligned}\]

So \(PV=C\,\dfrac{1-q^t}{r-g}\). As \(t\to\infty\), \(q^t\to0\) only if \(q\lt1\), i.e. \(g\lt r\).

Example · growing perpetuity (a stock)

Next year's dividend $1.30, growing 5% forever, required return 10%: \(PV=1.30/(0.10-0.05)=\$26.00\).

If $1.30 is the dividend just paid, next year's is \(1.30\times1.05\): \(PV=1.365/0.05=\$27.30\).

Example · growing annuity (a pension)

$20,000 next year, rising 3% a year for 40 years, at 10%:

\[\begin{aligned}PV&=20{,}000\times\tfrac{1-(1.03/1.10)^{40}}{0.07}\\&=\$265{,}122\end{aligned}\]

A level $20,000 for 40 years is worth \(20{,}000\,AF(10\%,40)=\$195{,}581\); the 3% escalator adds about a third.

Try it · the growing-perpetuity price is very sensitive to g

Price \(=1.30/(0.10-g)\). Going from \(g=5\%\) to 7% raises the price by 67%; at 9% it is $130 (+400%). As \(g\) approaches \(r\), \(r-g\) shrinks toward zero and the price explodes.

Three formulas used again in L4–L5

Perpetuity \(C/r\); annuity \(C\,AF(r,t)\); growing perpetuity (Gordon model) \(C/(r-g)\). Known, fixed \(C\) → bonds (L4). Uncertain \(C\) → stocks (L5).

5Quoting interest rates: APR and EAR

Definitions
  • Period rate: the rate actually applied each compounding period (e.g. 1% a month).
  • APR (annual percentage rate): period rate × \(m\), where \(m\) = periods per year. 12% APR monthly = 1% a month; quarterly = 3% a quarter; semi-annual = 6% per half-year. Period rate = APR / \(m\).
  • EAR (effective annual rate): what you actually earn or pay in a year, including compounding within the year.
Result · EAR \[EAR=\Big(1+\frac{APR}{m}\Big)^m-1\]\[APR=m\big[(1+EAR)^{1/m}-1\big]\]

In words: compound the period rate \(m\) times, subtract the 1 you started with. EAR > APR whenever \(m\gt1\); they are equal only with annual compounding.

Example · 20% a month on a $100 loan

APR \(=20\%\times12=240\%\). After a year you owe \(100\times1.20^{12}=\$891.61\), so the true annual cost is 791.6%. The APR ignores compounding within the year; the EAR includes it.

Try it · EAR at 10% APR as compounding gets more frequent

Annual 10.00%, quarterly 10.38%, monthly 10.47%, weekly 10.51%, daily 10.52%. EAR levels off fast at the continuous limit \(e^{0.10}-1=10.517\%\).

Example · which savings account?

5.25% APR daily vs 5.30% APR semi-annual. EARs: \((1+0.0525/365)^{365}-1=5.39\%\) vs \((1+0.053/2)^2-1=5.37\%\). $100 grows to $105.39 vs $105.37: the lower quoted rate wins.

Watch

Compare EARs, never APRs with different compounding. Banks quote deposits as EAR (also called APY) and loans as APR, so each looks better. In any annuity calculation use the rate per payment period.

Partial amortisation · the balloon payment

Common in commercial mortgages: payments are set as if the loan ran for many years, but the remaining balance (the balloon) is due after a few.

HK$100,000 at 12% APR, monthly payments as if over 20 years, balloon after 5 years:

  • Payment: \(100{,}000/AF(1\%,240)=\text{HK}\$1{,}101.09\)
  • Balloon = PV of the 180 payments not made: \(1{,}101.09\times AF(1\%,180)=\text{HK}\$91{,}744\)

In words: the balance owed on any loan = PV of the remaining payments. After five years you still owe 92% of the principal: early payments are mostly interest.

7Summary and formula card

  • Many cash flows: move each to one date, add. PV and FV of a stream are linked by \((1+r)^t\).
  • Annuity/perpetuity formulas, due versions, growing versions; given three of PV, \(C\), \(r\), \(t\), find the fourth.
  • APR = period rate × \(m\); EAR = \((1+APR/m)^m-1\). Always match the rate to the payment period.
  • Loans: pure discount, interest-only, amortised (fixed principal or fixed payment); schedules and balloons.

\(C\) = first cash flow at \(t=1\) (at \(t=0\) for “due”); \(r\), \(g\) per period; \(g\lt r\).

8Common mistakes

  • Annual rate with monthly payments. Divide the APR by 12 and use months for \(t\).
  • Forgetting where the formula lands. Annuity PV sits one period before the first payment; annuity FV sits on the last payment. Deferred streams need an extra discount.
  • “Just paid” vs “next” dividend. \(C/(r-g)\) needs the next cash flow; multiply a just-paid one by \((1+g)\).
  • Comparing APRs with different compounding. Convert to EAR.
  • Calculator signs. PV and PMT entered with the same sign → error or nonsense.
  • Using \(C/(r-g)\) with \(g\ge r\): no finite value.
