FINA1310 · Corporate Finance · Lecture 1

Overview of corporate finance

What finance is about (time and risk), the three decisions every financial manager makes, the legal forms a business can take, what the firm should aim for, and why managers may not aim for it.

1Why finance: time and risk

Finance is about moving money across time and under uncertainty. A dollar later is not a dollar now, and a risky dollar is not a safe dollar.

  • Two sides: valuation (what is an asset worth; how does the market price it?) and management (what to save, spend, buy, sell, when, and how to pay). The same tools serve a household and a company.
  • The circuit: investors put money into financial markets → firms raise it and invest → the investments' cash flows return to investors.
Challenge 1 · time

HK$10,000 today or HK$10,100 in a month? You need to know (a) what a bank pays for a month (the deposit rate) and (b) whether the promise is certain. Cash today ≠ cash later: the time value of money (L2–L3).

Challenge 2 · risk

Bank deposit or an index fund (a fund that simply holds every stock in an index such as the Hang Seng or S&P 500)? Over long periods stocks have returned roughly 7–10% a year on average versus 0–4% for deposits, but with big crashes (1987, 1997–98, 2000–02, 2007–09, 2020). Certain cash ≠ uncertain cash: risk and return (L6).

Even experts misjudge risk: LTCM, the hedge fund whose partners included Nobel laureates Scholes and Merton, earned 21%, 43% and 41% in 1995–97, then lost US$4.6 billion within months in 1998.

Going further · per year

Over 22 years 7 months, +93% is about \(1.93^{1/22.58}-1\approx3.0\%\) a year and +582% is about \(8.9\%\) a year (price only, dividends excluded). Compounding turns a 6-point yearly gap into 3.5 times as much money: each 2004 dollar became \(\$6.82\) vs \(\$1.93\).

Careers: a corporation asks whether to build a plant (cost now, future profits, their uncertainty); an investment bank asks how to pay for it (cash, loan, bonds, shares); a consultancy asks how to run its inventories, receivables and supplier credit. The material also appears in job interviews and CFA Level I.

2Corporate finance: three questions

The three decisions
  1. Capital budgeting: which long-term investments should the firm make? (Should Tesla build the new plant?)
  2. Capital structure: how should it pay for them? (Cash, bank loans, bonds or shares?)
  3. Working capital management: how should it manage day-to-day finances? (Inventories, receivables, payables, short-term borrowing.)

They map onto the balance sheet you know from ACCT1101:

3Capital budgeting: size, timing, risk

Take an investment if its value to the firm exceeds its cost. Valuing it is hard for three reasons: which cash flows count, when they arrive, and how certain they are.

The idea \[\text{Value to the firm}\gt\text{Cost to take}\] \[\text{Net cash flow}=\text{Inflows}-\text{Outflows}\]

In words: a good investment is worth more than it costs; its worth comes from the net cash it produces.

Size: which cash flows count?

Apple weighing a new headset: direct cash flows (production, sales, marketing, distribution) count; side effects count (iPhone and Mac sales may change; the brand may gain or lose); sunk costs (money already spent and unrecoverable whatever you decide, e.g. past R&D) do not. Only incremental cash flows matter: those that happen because of the decision (L8).

Timing: the Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macau Bridge

Example · a naïve sum

Built for about RMB 110 billion, opened 2018. Yearly tolls minus maintenance ≈ RMB 150 × 20 million cars − RMB 2 billion = RMB 1 billion. Suppose it lasts 150 years.

Cash flows, RMB billion

Naïve: \(1\times150-110=+\text{RMB }40\) billion. Wrong: RMB 1 in 2168 is not worth RMB 1 in 2018, and on the naïve sum it takes 110 years just to get the money back. Cash flows at different dates must be discounted (moved to a common date) before they are added (L2–L3).

Key insight · dates are currencies

\(\$150+\pounds300\) is not “450 of something”: convert to one currency first. Cash at different dates is the same: convert to one date first.

Going further · the bridge discounted

At a 5% discount rate, RMB 1 billion a year for 150 years is worth about RMB 20.0 billion today (L3's annuity formula), so the value is about \(20.0-110=-\text{RMB }90\) billion. A bridge is justified by wider benefits, not tolls.

Risk: future cash flows are uncertain

Uncertainty comes from the economy (recessions 2008–09, 2020), the industry (airlines in 2020) and the firm (will the product sell?). Two investments with the same expected cash flows but different uncertainty have different values: riskier cash flows are worth less. The course shows how to measure and price risk (L6) and build it into the discount rate (L10).

4Capital structure and working capital

The firm's value is a pie; capital structure decides how it is sliced between lenders (fixed claim, paid first) and shareholders (whatever is left).

Debt vs equity
  • Debt (bank loans, corporate bonds): a fixed claim, paid first.
  • Equity (common and preferred stock, retained earnings): the residual claim, what is left after everyone else is paid.

Debt financing

  • Syndicated bank loan: a lead arranger plus participating banks share a loan too large or risky for one bank. MTR, 2016: HK$25 billion from 21 banks: a 3-year term loan of HK$12.5bn at HIBOR + 0.54% and a 5-year revolving loan (borrow, repay, re-borrow up to a limit) of HK$12.5bn at HIBOR + 0.64%. HIBOR = the Hong Kong interbank rate, so the loan rate floats.
  • Corporate bonds: many investors each lend a little; the bonds then trade in the market. Amazon, 2017: bought Whole Foods for US$13.7 billion and issued US$16 billion of bonds in seven maturities from 3 to 40 years; the 10-year bond yielded about 3.17%.

Equity financing

  • IPO (initial public offering): a private company sells shares to the public for the first time. Tencent, 2004: 420 million shares at HK$3.70 raised HK$1.55 billion.
  • SEO (seasoned equity offering): a listed company sells new shares. Tesla, 2016: 6.8 million shares raised US$1.46 billion.
  • Private equity / venture capital: no public exchange; money from institutions and accredited (wealthy, professional) investors. ByteDance's shareholders include large asset managers and venture funds.

Working capital management

Goal: enough short-term resources (cash, receivables, inventory vs payables and short-term borrowing) to keep operating without costly interruptions. A profitable firm can still fail if it runs out of cash. Northern Rock: record profit in 2006, but it funded long-term mortgages with short-term market borrowing; when markets froze in September 2007 it needed Bank of England support, suffered the first UK bank run in 150 years, and was nationalised in February 2008. Mostly beyond this course.

The course plan: value cash flows at different dates and risks, apply that to bonds, stocks and projects (capital budgeting, L2–L10); then how the debt–equity mix affects value and how firms raise capital (L11). Throughout: does the decision add value for the owners?

5Forms of business organisation

Corp., Inc., Ltd., LLC, LP, LLP, plc, 有限公司 all come down to one question: if the business can't pay its debts, can creditors take the owners' personal assets?

Two legal concepts
  • Unlimited liability: the owner is responsible for all the business's debts; if business assets fall short, creditors can take the owner's personal assets.
  • Limited liability: the owner can lose at most what they invested; personal assets are safe.
  • Sole proprietorship (one owner). + Easiest and cheapest to start, least regulated; owner keeps all profit; taxed once as personal income. − Unlimited liability; life limited to the owner's, and transfer means selling the whole business; equity limited to the owner's wealth, so it stays small. Home services, freelancers, small shops.
  • Partnership (two or more owners), three kinds:
    • General partnership (GP): every partner has unlimited liability. Small, proprietorship-like businesses.
    • Limited partnership (LP): general partners run it with unlimited liability; limited partners don't manage and have limited liability. E.g. real-estate agencies, medical practices.
    • Limited liability partnership (LLP): all partners have limited liability, and no partner is liable for another's misconduct or negligence. Accounting, law, architecture firms.
    + More capital than one person; easy to start, an agreement divides gains and losses; taxed once as general partners' personal income. − Unlimited liability for general partners; dissolves when a general partner dies or sells out; hard to transfer; hard to grow large.
  • Corporation: a legal person separate from its owners. + Shareholders have limited liability; unlimited life; ownership transferred by selling shares; easier to raise large capital; professional management. − Harder to set up (articles of incorporation, bylaws, share certificates, registration); possible double taxation (corporate tax on profit, then personal tax on dividends; varies by country, and Hong Kong has no dividend tax); separating ownership from management creates agency problems (section 7).

Cash flows between the firm and the markets

(A) The firm issues debt and equity to investors → (B) invests the cash in current and fixed assets → (C) the assets generate cash flow → (D) some goes to government (taxes) and other stakeholders → (E) some is reinvested → (F) the rest returns to creditors (interest, principal) and shareholders (dividends, buybacks). Hence: total value of the assets = total value of the firm to its investors.

6The goal: maximise the market value of equity

Profit, market share and “long-run value” are all vague; today's share price is the one target that already counts timing and risk.

  • Maximise profit? Now or later? Under which accounting rules? At what risk?
  • Increase market share? A larger share may just mean lower prices, not more value.
  • Increase long-run value? When is the long run, and which value?
  • Maximise the current market value of equity: the only plausible financial objective, because today's share price already reflects the present value of all expected future cash flows, adjusted for timing and risk.
The goal, measured

Public firm: maximise the current price per share of existing stock. Private firm: maximise what a buyer would pay, or what the owners could get by selling. This goal takes profit, survival and growth (and the trade-offs among them) into account.

Every tool in the course answers one question: does this decision add value? Good decision → market value ↑; bad decision → market value ↓.

Caveats
  • The market is not always right: investors can be irrational (dot-com bubble), under-informed (limited disclosure) or misled (Enron).
  • Other stakeholders (creditors, employees, suppliers, customers, society): shareholders are residual claimants, paid after everyone else, so maximising the residual usually means the prior claims are met, but not always (not covered in this course).
  • Ethics: value is pursued within the law and ethical norms: product safety and honest advertising (customers), fairness and safety (employees), environment and lobbying (society).

7The agency problem

Shareholders own the firm but managers run it, and managers' interests are not the same as shareholders'.

Definitions

Agency relationship: a principal (shareholders) hires an agent (managers) to act on their behalf. Agency problem: the agent's interests differ. Shareholders want their shares' current value maximised; managers may want job security and a quiet life, a bigger empire for its own sake, perks (jets, offices, prestige projects), and pay tied to short-term results.

  • Direct agency costs: perks that benefit managers at shareholders' expense, and the cost of monitoring (auditors, boards).
  • Indirect agency costs: good investments not taken, bad ones taken.
Remedies
  • Pay tied to share value (stock and options), carefully designed.
  • Corporate control: the board can replace managers; proxy fights (outsiders collect shareholders' votes to replace the board); the threat of takeover.
  • Monitoring by large shareholders, creditors and other stakeholders.

Apple, 1985: the board sided with CEO John Sculley and Steve Jobs left. Control matters even for founders.

8Summary

  • Finance = moving money across time and under risk. Later cash and risky cash are worth less.
  • Three decisions: capital budgeting (what to invest in), capital structure (debt vs equity), working capital (day-to-day cash).
  • Capital budgeting: value exceeds cost, using incremental cash flows (ignore sunk costs), discounted to one date, adjusted for risk.
  • Debt = fixed claim paid first; equity = residual claim. Raised via loans, bonds, IPOs, SEOs, PE/VC.
  • Sole proprietorship and partnership: simple, but unlimited liability (for GPs) and hard to grow. Corporation: limited liability, unlimited life, easy transfer and capital; possible double tax and agency problems.
  • Goal: maximise the current market value of equity (share price), within the law and ethics.
  • Agency problem: managers' interests differ; contained by incentive pay, control and monitoring.

9Common mistakes

  • Counting sunk costs. Money already spent is gone whatever you decide; it is not a cash flow of the decision.
  • Ignoring side effects. Lost sales of existing products (or gains) are incremental cash flows.
  • Adding cash flows from different dates without discounting them to one date.
  • “The goal is maximum profit.” Profit ignores timing and risk; the goal is the current share price.
  • Mixing up LP and LLP. LP: general partners still have unlimited liability. LLP: every partner is limited.
  • “Corporations are always taxed twice.” It depends on the country; Hong Kong does not tax dividends.

10Practice

Practicetime

HK$10,000 today, or HK$10,100 one month from now?

Hint

What rate does waiting earn you, and how does it compare with a 1-month deposit?

Answer

Waiting earns \(100/10{,}000=1\%\) a month, about \(1.01^{12}-1=12.68\%\) a year. A 1-month HKD deposit pays far less (deposits 0–4% a year), so if the promise is certain, take the later payment.

If certain: \(\boxed{\text{wait for HK}\$10{,}100}\)

Practicerisk

Leave your savings in the bank, or buy an index fund tracking the Hang Seng or the S&P 500?

Hint

Compare average return and how much it swings, then your time horizon.

Answer

No single right answer. Stocks have averaged roughly 7–10% a year versus 0–4%, but can fall sharply (2008, 2020), and markets differ (Hang Seng +93% vs S&P 500 +582% since 2004). \(\boxed{\text{higher return, higher risk}}\): long horizon and able to sit through crashes → index fund; money needed soon → bank. L6 makes this precise.

Practicethree questions

Classify each: (a) Should MTR build a new railway line? (b) Should it pay for the line with bonds or new shares? (c) Should it give its contractors 60 days to be paid instead of 30?

Answer

(a) \(\boxed{\text{capital budgeting}}\) (b) \(\boxed{\text{capital structure}}\) (c) \(\boxed{\text{working capital}}\): it is about payables, a short-term liability.

Practicerelevant cash flows

A phone maker considers a new model. Which count: (i) HK$50m of R&D already spent; (ii) expected sales of the new model; (iii) lost sales of its current model; (iv) marketing for the launch?

Answer

\(\boxed{\text{(ii), (iii), (iv) count; (i) does not}}\). (i) is sunk; (iii) is a side effect and counts as a negative cash flow.

Practiceliability

You put HK$200,000 into a limited partnership as a limited partner. It fails owing creditors HK$5 million more than its assets. What is the most you can lose? What if you were a general partner?

Answer

Limited partner: \(\boxed{\text{HK}\$200{,}000}\), your investment. General partner: you can lose your investment plus personal assets toward the HK$5 million shortfall.

Practiceagency

A CEO rejects a risky project that would raise the share price because a failure might cost her job. What kind of agency cost is this, and name one remedy.

Answer

\(\boxed{\text{Indirect agency cost}}\) (a good investment not taken). Remedy: pay tied to share value, e.g. stock options, so she gains when the project adds value.
