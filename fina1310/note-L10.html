FINA1310 · Corporate Finance · Lecture 10

Cost of capital

Where the discount rate comes from: the costs of equity, preferred stock and debt, their weighted average (WACC), when not to use it, and the cost of raising the money.

0Where this sits

Capital budgeting has four steps: ① cash flows (L8), ② the discount rate (this lecture), ③ investment criteria (L7), ④ what-if analysis (L9).

Recap · three tools reused here
  • Gordon model (L5): \(P_0=D_1/(R_E-g)\).
  • Yield to maturity (L4): the rate that makes the PV of a bond's payments equal its price.
  • CAPM (L6): required return \(=R_f+\beta\times\) market risk premium.
Sign convention

Received +, paid −. Rates are per year unless stated; a bond paying semi-annually is solved per half-year, then doubled.

1The big picture

The discount rate is one number seen from two sides: investors' required return = the firm's cost of capital.

\[NPV=CF_0+\frac{CF_1}{1+r}+\dots+\frac{CF_T}{(1+r)^T}\]
  • For investors, \(r\) is the required return: what they could earn elsewhere at the same risk.
  • For the firm, \(r\) is the cost of capital: what it must offer investors to raise the money.
  • Invest only if the project beats it: NPV > 0, or IRR > \(r\).

Cost of capital = time value + risk premium. Time value (inflation and waiting) is the same for every investment: the risk-free rate. The risk premium pays for uncertain cash flows and differs with risk: the CAPM of L6.

What we can observe. A firm's cost of capital mixes its costs of equity and debt, each weighted by how much the firm uses it. Cost of equity = expected return on its stock (in an efficient market, where prices reflect available information, investors' average return equals their required return). Cost of debt = YTM on its bonds.

Use of funds, not source

Key idea

The risk of the cash flows (what the money is used for) sets the required return. Firm A with high-risk investments faces high required returns from creditors and shareholders; firm B with low-risk investments faces low ones. How the money is raised does not change what a project is worth.

Example · project B and the 8% loan

Project B costs $1,000 now and pays $1,100 in a year (IRR 10%). Same-risk securities return 15%. The firm can borrow at 8%. Value: \(1{,}100/1.15=\$956.52\), whatever the financing.

Who puts in the $1,000Puts inClaim worth todayGain
All equity: shareholders$1,000$956.52−$43.48
Half debt: lenders at 8%$500\(540/1.08=\$500.00\)$0.00
Half debt: shareholders$500\(956.52-500=\$456.52\)−$43.48

Lenders get a fair deal either way; shareholders lose $43.48 either way. Cheap borrowing does not rescue a project that fails its own risk-adjusted rate.

2Cost of equity

\(R_E\), the return shareholders require, is not observed directly; estimate it from the stock price (dividend growth model) or from risk (CAPM).

Dividend growth model

Result · rearrange the Gordon model \[R_E=\frac{D_1}{P_0}+g=\frac{D_0(1+g)}{P_0}+g\]

In words: required return = next year's dividend yield + growth rate. \(P_0\) and \(D_0\) are observed; \(g\) must be estimated.

Example · g from past dividends; price $10
Year20212022202320242025
Dividend$1.10$1.20$1.35$1.40$1.55
Growth9.09%12.50%3.70%10.71%
\[\begin{aligned}g&=\tfrac{9.09+12.50+3.70+10.71}{4}=9.0\%\\R_E&=\tfrac{1.55\times1.09}{10}+0.09=25.9\%\end{aligned}\]

A second source of \(g\): analysts' forecasts. They differ, so average several.

Pros: easy to understand and use. Cons: needs steadily growing dividends; very sensitive to \(g\); risk enters only through the price.

CAPM

Result · CAPM \[R_E=R_f+\beta_E\times\big[E(R_M)-R_f\big]\]

In words: required return = risk-free rate + the stock's beta × market risk premium. \(\beta_E\) = the stock's systematic risk (the part diversification cannot remove) relative to the market. Three inputs to estimate: \(R_f\), the premium, \(\beta_E\).

Reading the formula
\(R_f\)
what shareholders could earn with no risk: here, the 10-year government bond yield.
\(\beta_E\)
how much the stock tends to move when the market moves 1%: its amount of risk that diversification cannot remove.
\(E(R_M)-R_f\)
the extra return investors want per unit of beta (6% in this lecture).
\(R_E\)
the return shareholders give up elsewhere by funding this firm, so it is the firm's cost of equity.

Risk-free: free from what risk? From default: use a government bill or bond. But “risk-free” also depends on horizon:

  • Over one period, a short-term bill is safe; a 10-year bond's price moves with interest rates.
  • Over many years, rolling over bills faces unknown future rates; a 10-year bond locks in its yield.
  • So match the horizon of the cash flows: for projects and firms, the 10-year government bond yield is reasonable, with a market risk premium measured over that same bond.
  • Hong Kong, Oct 2026: 10-year bond about 3.8% vs 3-month bill 2.8%.

Market risk premium: from history or forecasts; long-run averages and surveys say about 4–7%. This lecture uses 6%. Beta: estimated from past returns against the market.

Hong Kong costs of equity · \(R_E=3.8\%+\beta\times6\%\)
CompanyBusinessBetaCost of equity
CLP HoldingsElectricity0.285.5%
HSBC HoldingsBanking0.667.8%
TencentInternet, games1.2911.5%

Betas from five years of monthly returns. A steady utility needs far less return than a cyclical tech firm.

Example · CAPM

\(\beta_E=1.2\), 10-year yield 4%, premium 6%: \(R_E=4\%+1.2\times6\%=11.2\%\).

Pros: adjusts for risk explicitly; works for any firm with a beta; no dividends needed. Cons: beta and premium are estimates, both change over time, and past returns stand in for the future.

Practice: in a 1999 survey of 392 CFOs, three in four always or almost always used the CAPM:

Method% of CFOs

You can now: estimate the cost of equity with the dividend growth model (including \(g\) from past dividends) and with the CAPM; choose a sensible risk-free rate and market risk premium; give the pros and cons of each method.

3Costs of preferred stock and debt

Result · preferred stock = a perpetuity \[P_0=\frac{D}{R_P}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_P=\frac{D}{P_0}\]

In words: cost of preferred = fixed dividend ÷ price. The dividend is often a % of par: 7% preferred with $100 par pays \(D=\$7\); at a price of $87.50, \(R_P=7/87.50=8\%\).

Cost of debt: the YTM

\(R_D\), the return lenders require, is set by the risk of the firm's debt. Public debt: the YTM on the firm's bonds. Private debt: the YTM on similar bonds (maturity, rating). The coupon rate is not the cost of debt: it was fixed when the bond was issued.

Example · 20 years, 10% coupon paid semi-annually, price $919.77

Calculator: N = 40, PV = −919.77, PMT = 50, FV = 1,000 → CPT I/Y = 5.5 (per half-year). Excel: =RATE(40, 50, -919.77, 1000) = 5.5%. Annual YTM \(=2\times5.5\%=11\%\). A bond quoted at 105% of par: PV = −1,050 per $1,000.

After-tax cost of debt

What is this? (after-tax cost of debt)

Interest is a business expense: it is paid before tax is worked out, so every dollar of interest lowers the firm's taxable profit by a dollar. With a 34% tax rate, a dollar of interest cuts the tax bill by 34 cents, so the firm itself bears only 66 cents and the government in effect pays the rest. Lenders still receive the full interest; only the firm's net cost is lower. Dividends are paid out of after-tax profit, so equity gets no such discount.

Result · interest is tax-deductible \[\text{After-tax cost of debt}=R_D\times(1-T_C)\]

In words: each dollar of interest cuts taxable income by a dollar, so the government pays \(T_C\) of it.

Borrow $1 million at 9%, tax 34%: interest $90,000 saves \(0.34\times90{,}000=\$30{,}600\) of tax, so after-tax interest is $59,400 = 5.94% of the loan \((=9\%\times0.66)\).

\(T_C\): ideally the marginal tax rate (on the last dollar earned). Dividends are not deductible: no tax adjustment to \(R_E\) or \(R_P\).

You can now: find the cost of preferred stock as dividend ÷ price; find the cost of debt as the YTM on the firm's bonds (not the coupon rate) by calculator or Excel; turn it into an after-tax cost with \((1-T_C)\).

4Weighted average cost of capital (WACC)

The firm's overall cost of capital = the costs of its sources, averaged with market-value weights.

What is this? (WACC)

Picture the firm as one big project funded from two pockets: shareholders, who want a high return because they are paid last, and lenders, who accept less because they are paid first. The WACC is the blended rate the whole firm must earn to give each pocket its required return at the same time. How much each pocket counts depends on how much money it has in the firm. A project of the same risk that earns less than this blend leaves someone short of what they could earn elsewhere.

Step 1 · weights at market value

What is this? (why market values)

The weights must show how much money each group has tied up in the firm today. A shareholder who could sell her shares for $5 each wants a fair return on $5, not on what the firm got for them years ago; the same holds for a bondholder whose bond now trades at $960. Book values record history. Market values show what investors give up right now by staying invested, and that is the amount their required return is earned on.

  • \(E\) = shares outstanding × share price.
  • \(D\) = number of public bonds × bond price. Private debt: value it at the YTM of similar public debt. Short-term debt: book value ≈ market value.
  • \(V=E+D\); weights \(E/V+D/V=100\%\).
Example · weights

100 million shares at $5; 250,000 bonds ($1,000 face) at $960.

\[\begin{aligned}E&=5\times100\text{m}=\$500\text{m}\\D&=960\times250{,}000=\$240\text{m}\\V&=500+240=\$740\text{m}\end{aligned}\]

\(E/V=500/740=67.57\%\), \(D/V=240/740=32.43\%\).

Step 2 · average the costs

Result · WACC \[WACC=\frac{E}{V}R_E+\frac{D}{V}R_D(1-T_C)\]

In words: each source's cost times its share of firm value, using the after-tax cost of debt.

With preferred stock (\(V=E+P+D\)), add \(\frac{P}{V}R_P\), with no tax adjustment:

\[\begin{aligned}WACC={}&\tfrac{E}{V}R_E+\tfrac{P}{V}R_P\\&+\tfrac{D}{V}R_D(1-T_C)\end{aligned}\]
Reading the formula
\(\frac{E}{V},\ \frac{D}{V}\)
the share of today's firm value funded by each pocket, at market values.
\(R_E\)
what shareholders require (CAPM or dividend growth model).
\(R_D\)
what lenders require: the YTM on the firm's debt.
\((1-T_C)\)
the tax discount on interest: the government in effect pays \(T_C\) of every dollar of interest, so the firm's net cost is lower.
no \((1-T_C)\) on \(R_E\), \(R_P\)
dividends come out of after-tax profit, so they bring no tax saving.
Example continued · WACC = 10.42%

Same firm: \(\beta_E=1.5\); bonds 20 years, 8% coupon semi-annual (at $960); \(R_f=4\%\), premium 6%, \(T_C=40\%\).

  • \(R_E=4\%+1.5\times6\%=13\%\)
  • \(R_D\) = YTM at $960: N = 40, PMT = 40, FV = 1,000 → 4.21% a half-year → 8.42%.
\[\begin{aligned}WACC&=0.6757\times13\%\\&\quad+0.3243\times8.42\%\times0.60\\&=10.42\%\end{aligned}\]

WACC, step by step (used in the comprehensive example below):

  1. Do: market value of each source: \(E\) = shares × price, \(P\) = preferred shares × price, \(D\) = bonds × price (private debt at the YTM of similar bonds, short-term debt at book value). The weights must reflect what each group's claim is worth today, the money it could take out and invest elsewhere.
  2. Do: \(V=E+P+D\), then the weights \(E/V\), \(P/V\), \(D/V\). Each weight is the share of the firm that one group funds; together they make 100%.
  3. Do: cost of each source: \(R_E\) by CAPM and/or dividend growth model, \(R_P\) = preferred dividend ÷ price, \(R_D\) = YTM (per half-year × 2 for semi-annual bonds). These are the returns each group could get elsewhere at the same risk, read from market prices.
  4. Do: multiply \(R_D\) by \((1-T_C)\); leave \(R_E\) and \(R_P\) alone. Only interest is tax-deductible, so only debt's cost to the firm is cut by tax.
  5. Do: WACC = sum of weight × cost. It is the blended rate that pays every group its required return at once.

Comprehensive example

Data · \(T_C=40\%\)
  • Common: 1bn shares at $5; \(\beta\) 1.5, \(R_f\) 4%, premium 6%; \(D_0=\$0.55\), \(g=2\%\).
  • Preferred: 100m shares at $10, paying $0.80 a year forever.
  • Bonds: 1m bonds at $980; 10 years, 5% coupon, semi-annual.
  • Private loan: one payment of $1bn in 10 years; same risk as the bonds.
  • Short-term debt: $500m (book), 4%.

Face value of the bonds is not stated; $1,000 is assumed (as in the earlier example). This gives a 5.26% YTM.

Equity and preferred
  • \(E=5\times1\text{bn}=\$5\text{bn}\)
  • CAPM: \(R_E=4\%+1.5\times6\%=13.00\%\)
  • DGM: \(R_E=0.55\times1.02/5+2\%=13.22\%\)
  • Average: \(R_E=13.11\%\). Why average? Each model's weaknesses partly hedge the other's.
  • \(P=10\times100\text{m}=\$1\text{bn}\); \(R_P=0.80/10=8\%\)
Three kinds of debt
  • Bonds: \(D_1=980\times1\text{m}=\$0.98\text{bn}\); YTM at $980 (N = 20, PMT = 25, FV = 1,000) → \(R_{D1}=5.26\%\).
  • Private loan: no price, so value it at the bonds' yield; \(R_{D2}=5.26\%\).
  • Short-term: \(D_3=\$0.5\text{bn}\), \(R_{D3}=4\%\).
  • \(D=0.98+0.595+0.5=\$2.075\text{bn}\). Overall \(R_D\): each cost weighted by value:
\[\begin{aligned}R_D={}&\tfrac{0.98}{2.075}(5.26\%)+\tfrac{0.595}{2.075}(5.26\%)\\&+\tfrac{0.5}{2.075}(4\%)=4.96\%\end{aligned}\]
Result · WACC = 9.87%

\(V=5+1+2.075=\$8.075\text{bn}\)

\[\begin{aligned}WACC={}&\tfrac{5}{8.075}(13.11\%)+\tfrac{1}{8.075}(8\%)\\&+\tfrac{2.075}{8.075}(4.96\%)(0.6)\\={}&8.12\%+0.99\%+0.76\%\\={}&9.87\%\end{aligned}\]

Where the 9.87% comes from

weight in firm value (%)

contribution to WACC (% points)

Equity is 62% of value and supplies 8.12 of the 9.87 points. Debt is 26% of value but, at a low after-tax 2.98%, adds only 0.76 points.

You can now: compute market-value weights for equity, preferred stock and several kinds of debt; combine the costs into a WACC with the after-tax cost of debt; say which source drives the result.

5Divisional and project costs of capital

WACC is the right rate only for projects as risky as the firm's existing business.

Use WACC for the same business, or the same business in a new place. Do not use it for a new line of business, or for one division of a conglomerate (a firm running several unrelated businesses).

One WACC for every project: two kinds of mistake (all-equity firm, β = 1, R_f = 4%, premium 6%)

Solid line: SML, required return \(=4\%+\beta\times6\%\). Dashed: the firm's WACC of 10%. Grey dots: projects A (β 0.6, IRR 9%) and B (β 1.5, IRR 12%); orange: your project (starts on A). A project above the SML is good; one above the dashed line merely looks good.

Project A · β 0.6, IRR 9%

Required \(4\%+0.6\times6\%=7.6\%\). IRR 9% > 7.6% → NPV > 0, take. With WACC: 9% < 10% → reject. Low-risk projects look worse than they are.

Project B · β 1.5, IRR 12%

Required \(4\%+1.5\times6\%=13\%\). IRR 12% < 13% → NPV < 0, reject. With WACC: 12% > 10% → take. High-risk projects look better than they are, so a one-rate firm drifts towards riskier projects.

Pure play approach

A pure play is a company in a single line of business. Use the cost of capital of pure plays in the project's business (average several if available). Video streaming: Netflix, not Google (YouTube is a small part of Google). A power station: a utility such as CLP.

Subjective approach

No good pure play? Put projects into risk classes and adjust the WACC. Example with WACC = 14%:

Risk classExampleDiscount rate
Very lowReplacing equipment14% − 8% = 6%
LowCost-saving project14% − 3% = 11%
Same as the firmExpanding the business14%
HighNew product14% + 5% = 19%
Very highNew market or technology14% + 10% = 24%

The adjustments are judgement calls, but rough risk adjustment beats none.

6Flotation costs

Issuing securities costs fees, so the firm must raise more than the project needs.

Flotation costs: fees for issuing new bonds or stock (underwriting, legal, registration).

Result · gross up \[\text{Amount raised}=\frac{\text{Amount needed}}{1-f}\]

In words: only \((1-f)\) of each dollar raised reaches the project. Need $100m with \(f=10\%\): raise \(100/0.90=\$111.11\)m; flotation cost $11.11m.

Equity costs far more to issue than debt (% of money raised; a third of CFOs weigh issuing costs when choosing debt):

IssueCostMeasure
US IPOs: underpricing (indirect)19.0%first-day return, 1980–2025

Hong Kong IPOs: underwriting fee2.3%base fee, 2000–25

Underpricing: IPO shares sold below what they trade at on day one, a hidden cost to the issuing firm. A seasoned offering is a new issue by an already-listed firm.

Result · weighted average flotation cost \[f_A=\frac{E}{V}f_E+\frac{D}{V}f_D\]

In words: average the stock and bond flotation costs with the target weights, not the mix of this particular issue (the firm will rebalance towards its target over time).

Example · flotation turns NPV negative

Target D/E = 1 (so weights 50/50). Project with the firm's risk: costs $500,000, pays $72,000 a year forever. \(T_C=34\%\), \(R_E=20\%\), \(R_D=10\%\); flotation 10% for stock, 6% for debt.

\[\begin{aligned}WACC&=0.5(20\%)+0.5(10\%)(0.66)\\&=13.3\%\\NPV&=-500{,}000+\tfrac{72{,}000}{0.133}\\&=\$41{,}353\end{aligned}\]

With flotation: \(f_A=0.5(10\%)+0.5(6\%)=8\%\). Raise \(500{,}000/0.92=\$543{,}478\); flotation cost $43,478.

\[\begin{aligned}&41{,}353-43{,}478=\boxed{-\$2{,}125}\end{aligned}\]

Positive NPV, but not enough to pay for raising the money: reject.

7Summary and formula card

  • The cost of capital depends on the use of funds, not the source.
  • \(R_E\): dividend growth model or CAPM (or average them). \(R_f\): default-free, matching the cash flows' horizon.
  • \(R_P=D/P_0\); \(R_D\) = YTM, after tax \(R_D(1-T_C)\).
  • WACC with market-value weights; use it only for projects with the firm's risk; otherwise a pure play or a risk class.
  • Flotation: raise need ÷ \((1-f_A)\), with target weights.

Cost of equity (CAPM)\(R_E=R_f+\beta_E[E(R_M)-R_f]\)
Cost of preferred\(R_P=D/P_0\)
Cost of debtYTM; after tax \(R_D(1-T_C)\)

Flotation\(f_A=\tfrac{E}{V}f_E+\tfrac{D}{V}f_D\); raise \(\tfrac{\text{need}}{1-f_A}\)

8Common mistakes

  • Using the borrowing rate as the discount rate. The project's risk sets the rate, not the cheapest source.
  • Coupon rate as cost of debt. Use the current YTM.
  • Forgetting the tax shield on debt, or applying \((1-T_C)\) to equity or preferred.
  • Book-value weights for equity or bonds. Use market values (book is fine only for short-term debt).
  • \(D_0\) instead of \(D_1\) in the dividend growth model.
  • D/E vs D/V. D/E = 1/2 means D/V = 1/3, not 1/2.
  • One WACC for every project: rejects good safe projects, accepts bad risky ones.
  • Flotation weights from this issue's mix rather than the target.
