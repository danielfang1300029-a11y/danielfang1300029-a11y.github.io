FINA1310 · Corporate Finance · Lecture 8

Making capital investment decisions

Which cash flows belong in a project's NPV, how to build them year by year from operations, working capital and capital spending, and three special cases: replacement, bid price and equivalent annual cost.

0Where this sits

Capital budgeting (deciding which long-term projects to invest in) has four steps. L7 gave the rule; this lecture supplies its input, the cash flows.

Recap from L7 · NPV rule

\(NPV=\) PV of all future cash flows − cost today. Take the project if \(NPV\gt0\); equivalently if \(IRR\gt r\) for a normal project. PV of an annuity: \(C\times AF(r,t)\), with \(AF(r,t)=\frac{1-(1+r)^{-t}}{r}\) (L3 §2).

Sign convention

Cash into the project is +, cash out is −. Year 0 = today; year \(t\) = end of year \(t\). All cash flows are after tax.

1Relevant cash flows

Count a cash flow only if it happens with the project and not without it.

What is this?

Picture two futures for the firm side by side: one where it takes the project and one where it doesn't. A cash flow belongs in the NPV only if it differs between the two.

A cost already paid (sunk) is the same in both futures, so it drops out. An asset the firm already owns is not free: in the “without” future it could be sold or rented out, so the project is charged what that would bring in (the opportunity cost). Sales the project takes from, or adds to, the firm's other products also differ between the two futures, so they count.

Definitions
  • Incremental cash flows: all changes in the firm's cash flows caused by the project, i.e. cash flows that occur only if the project is taken.
  • Stand-alone principle: treat the project as a “mini firm” and value it on its own incremental cash flows.

Opportunity cost

Opportunity cost = the value of the best alternative given up. An asset the firm already owns is not free: it could be sold or rented out, so charge the project its current market value. Idle land, an empty floor, spare machine time all have a value.

Example · coffee shop next to HKU

The firm bought the shopfront last year for $20 million; it is worth $25 million today. The project is charged $25 million (what the firm gives up by not selling), not the $20 million price paid (that is history).

Side effects

  • Erosion (negative): the new product takes sales from existing ones. A new iPhone cuts sales of older models; Shanghai Disneyland drew visitors from Hong Kong Disneyland.
  • Cross-selling (positive): the new product lifts sales of existing ones. Printers lift ink-cartridge sales; a new savings account brings credit-card customers.

Sunk costs

Sunk cost = a cost already paid or committed; accepting or rejecting cannot change it, so leave it out. Examples: R&D spent before the launch decision; money already spent on a project you are deciding whether to continue.

Example · install the anti-pollution device?

A factory was just built for $1 million and generates $0.2 million a year forever; \(r=10\%\). The regulator unexpectedly requires a $1.5 million device, or the factory shuts down.

Install it. The $1 million is spent either way, so it does not affect the choice.

The table lists the sunk −$1m in both rows but leaves it out of the NPV of the decision. Including it in both gives −$0.5m vs −$1m: the same $0.5m difference, so the decision is unchanged.

Financing costs

Interest, repayments, dividends and buybacks are how the assets' cash flows are split between creditors and stockholders (capital structure, L11), not cash the project generates. Value the cash flow from assets only; the cost of financing enters through the discount rate (L10).

You can now: decide whether a cash flow is incremental by comparing the firm with and without the project; charge an owned asset at its current market value; include erosion and cross-selling; and leave out sunk costs and financing costs.

2Cash flow from assets and operating cash flow

Result · cash flow from assets \[CFFA=OCF-NCS-\Delta NWC\]
  • OCF (operating cash flow): cash from day-to-day operations.
  • NCS (net capital spending) = fixed assets bought − fixed assets sold = ending net fixed assets − beginning net fixed assets + depreciation.
  • ΔNWC = ending NWC − beginning NWC, where net working capital NWC = current assets − current liabilities.

In words: cash from operations, minus cash put into fixed assets, minus cash tied up in working capital. A project uses the same formula with project OCF, project NCS and project ΔNWC.

Operating cash flow

What is this? (operating cash flow)

OCF is the cash that running the business actually puts in the till in a year, after the tax bill. Accounting profit is not quite that: it subtracts depreciation, which spreads the cost of a machine bought earlier over its life, so no cash leaves this year. OCF puts it back.

Interest is left out because it is about who funded the project, not what the project earns. The four routes in the table below are four ways of doing the same bookkeeping, so they always give the same number.

Definition · OCF\[OCF=EBIT+D-\text{Taxes}\]
  • EBIT (earnings before interest and taxes): interest is financing, not operations, so start before it.
  • + Depreciation \(D\): an expense in the income statement, but no cash leaves.
  • − Taxes: paid in cash.

\(OCF\lt0\) means operations do not cover everyday cash outflows.

Four equivalent ways to compute OCF (\(T\) = tax rate):

What is this? (depreciation tax shield)

Depreciation is a deduction the tax office lets you take without spending any cash this year. Each $1 of depreciation lowers taxable profit by $1, so (as long as the firm has taxable profit to set it against) it cuts the tax bill by the tax rate times $1. That saving is real cash, and it is the only cash effect depreciation has.

So OCF splits into two parts: the after-tax cash profit, as if there were no depreciation, plus the tax that depreciation saves.

Result · tax-shield form of OCF \[\begin{aligned}OCF&=(\text{sales}-\text{costs})(1-T)+D\,T\\&=\big[(P-v)Q-FC\big](1-T)+D\,T\end{aligned}\]

\(P\) = price, \(v\) = variable cost a unit, \(Q\) = units, \(FC\) = fixed costs.

In words: after-tax cash profit, plus the tax that depreciation saves. \(D\times T\) is the depreciation tax shield: each dollar of depreciation saves \(T\) dollars of tax.

4Net working capital

More NWC = cash stuck in the business (an outflow); less NWC = cash released (an inflow). Hence the minus sign on ΔNWC.

What is this?

Net working capital works like a rental deposit. To start trading you must put cash into stock on the shelves and into sales that customers have not paid for yet (less the bills you have not paid yet). That cash is not spent, just parked. When the project ends, the stock is sold off and customers pay up, so it comes back.

Like a deposit returned in full, it costs you only the return you could have earned on the money in the meantime. That is why NWC cash flows sum to zero over the project yet still lower its NPV.

NWC = current assets − current liabilitiesCash flow
NWC ↑ (ΔNWC > 0): receivables or inventory ↑ (cash tied up in unpaid sales and unsold goods), or payables ↓ (cash paid out to settle bills)CF < 0
NWC ↓ (ΔNWC < 0): receivables or inventory ↓ (customers paid, goods sold), or payables ↑ (bills not yet paid, cash kept for now)CF > 0

Over a project's life: at year 0, NWC goes from 0 to its initial level (outflow); during the project it moves with sales (if NWC = 20% of sales, ΔNWC = 20% × Δsales); at the end all NWC is recovered (inflow). Over the whole life the NWC cash flows sum to zero; they cost only the time value of money.

5Depreciation and salvage

Depreciation is a non-cash expense (added back bottom-up, never subtracted top-down) whose only cash effect is the tax shield \(D\times T\).

Definitions
  • Tax life: years until the book value reaches 0 for tax purposes.
  • Economic life: years the asset is actually in service.
  • Book value (BV): cost − accumulated depreciation.
  • Salvage value: what the asset sells for when it is disposed of.

Straight-line depreciation

\[D=\frac{\text{Initial cost}}{\text{Tax life}}\]

$80,000 machine, tax life 8 years: \(D=\$10{,}000\) a year. Sold after 5 years: \(BV=80{,}000-5\times10{,}000=\$30{,}000\). If the tax life is longer than the project, book value is left at the sale.

MACRS

MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System, the US tax schedule) depreciates a fixed percentage of the original cost each year, front-loaded so tax savings come sooner.

Selling the asset: tax on the gain or loss

Selling above book value means the asset was over-depreciated: it gave more tax shield than it should, so the gain is taxed. Selling below book value means under-depreciation: the loss earns a tax saving. Tax 34%, the equipment above sold at the end of year 2 (BV $22,220):

Result · after-tax salvage \[\begin{aligned}&\text{After-tax salvage}\\&\quad=MV-(MV-BV)\times T\end{aligned}\]

\(MV\) = market (sale) value, \(BV\) = book value at the sale.

In words: sale price minus tax on the gain over book value (a negative gain, i.e. a loss, adds a tax saving). At the end of the tax life \(BV=0\), so it is \(MV(1-T)\). It belongs in capital spending, in the year of the sale.

Reading the formula
\(MV\)
the cash the buyer pays you for the asset.
\(MV-BV\)
the gain over book value: how much more you deducted over the years than the asset really lost in value (negative = a loss).
\(\times T\)
tax on that gain, paid back to the tax office (a tax saving when the gain is negative).
the whole
the cash you keep from the sale.
  1. Do: Find book value at the sale: cost − accumulated depreciation (straight-line or MACRS). Book value is the part of the cost not yet deducted; the sale price is measured against it.
  2. Do: Gain \(=MV-BV\); tax on it \(=(MV-BV)\times T\). A gain means you deducted too much over the years, so tax is due; a loss earns a tax saving.
  3. Do: After-tax salvage = MV − that tax, entered as an inflow in capital spending in the year of the sale. You receive the price and pay (or save) the tax in the same year.

You can now: compute straight-line and MACRS depreciation and book value; compute after-tax salvage for a sale above or below book value; and put it in capital spending in the year of the sale.

7Special case I: replacing an old machine

Why firms invest (survey of US firms planning to invest in the next six months, Q3 2026): share citing each purpose for equipment: replace or repair 83%, increase capacity 46%, reduce reliance on labour 24%, new product 19%, cut costs 16%, health and safety 12%, good financing 3% (structures or land follow a similar order). Replacement is the most common case.

Setting
  • Old machine: bought 5 years ago for $100,000, straight-line over a 10-year tax life; could be sold for $60,000 now; worth $10,000 in 5 years.
  • New machine: costs $120,000 today, straight-line over 5 years; worth $20,000 in 5 years. It saves $20,000 a year before tax and frees up $10,000 of NWC.
  • Tax 34%; required return 15%.

Every cash flow is with replacement − without replacement.

Capital spending

  • Year 0. Buy new: −$120,000. Old machine's BV \(=100{,}000-5\times10{,}000=\$50{,}000\); selling it gives \(60{,}000-10{,}000\times0.34\) \(=+\$56{,}600\) (tax on the $10,000 gain). Overall \(-120{,}000+56{,}600=-\$63{,}400\). Without replacement: nothing. Incremental: −$63,400.
  • Year 5. With: sell the new machine, \(20{,}000(1-0.34)=\$13{,}200\). Without: sell the old one, \(10{,}000(1-0.34)=\$6{,}600\) (both BV = 0). The $6,600 is an opportunity cost: cash you would otherwise receive. Incremental: \(13{,}200-6{,}600=\) $6,600.

Operating cash flow

Take the tax-shield formula with and without, and subtract:

\[\Delta OCF=(\Delta\text{sales}-\Delta\text{costs})(1-T)+\Delta D\,T\]
  • \(\Delta\text{sales}=0\); \(\Delta\text{costs}=-\$20{,}000\) (a saving).
  • \(\Delta D=120{,}000/5-100{,}000/10\) \(=24{,}000-10{,}000=\$14{,}000\).
\[\begin{aligned}\Delta OCF&=20{,}000\times0.66+14{,}000\times0.34\\&=\$17{,}960\text{ a year}\end{aligned}\]

8Special case II: setting a bid price

Suppliers often compete for a contract by bidding (e.g. a government buying desktop computers). The lowest bid usually wins, but bid too low and you lose money. The base bid price is the price at which \(NPV=0\): bid at or above it, never below.

Setting
  • Sell 5 trucks a year for 4 years at bid price \(P\) a truck.
  • Fixed costs $24,000 a year; variable cost $14,000 a truck.
  • Equipment $60,000, straight-line to 0 over 4 years; sold for $5,000 at the end.
  • NWC $40,000 at the start, recovered at the end. Tax 39%; required return 20%.

Costs \(=24{,}000+5\times14{,}000=94{,}000\). After-tax salvage \(=5{,}000\times(1-0.39)=\$3{,}050\).

9Special case III: equivalent annual cost

Machines with different lives can't be compared by total PV of costs; compare the cost per year.

What is this?

EAC turns each machine's costs into a level yearly charge with the same present value, like a rent. It is like comparing two phone contracts of different lengths by their monthly price, not their total bill: a 2-year contract has a smaller total than a 3-year one simply because it is shorter.

If you will keep replacing the machine as it wears out, the one with the lower cost per year is the cheaper machine.

Definition · equivalent annual cost (EAC)

The equal annual cost over the machine's life with the same PV as its actual costs.

Machine A's costs and its EAC have the same PV (76.20)

Actual cash flows of A

Equivalent: a level cost each year

\[\begin{aligned}A&:\ EAC\times\tfrac{1-1/1.10^2}{0.1}=76.20\\&\Rightarrow EAC=\$43.90\text{ a year}\\B&:\ EAC\times\tfrac{1-1/1.10^3}{0.1}=93.58\\&\Rightarrow EAC=\$37.63\text{ a year}\end{aligned}\]

B is cheaper per year, so choose B if the machine will be replaced each time it wears out.

Result · EAC, general case \[EAC=\frac{\text{PV of costs}}{AF(r,t)}\]

In words: spread the PV of costs over the life as a \(t\)-period annuity. Compare machines by EAC when each is replaced at the end of its life (a repeating chain); for a one-off choice, compare PVs.

You can now: compute each machine's PV of costs and its EAC; and choose by EAC when machines are replaced at the end of their lives, or by PV for a one-off choice.

10Summary and formula card

  • Relevant cash flows are incremental: include opportunity costs, side effects, NWC and taxes; leave out sunk costs and financing costs.
  • Project CF = OCF − NCS − ΔNWC, year by year, after tax; then NPV/IRR as in L7.
  • NWC goes in at the start and comes back at the end.
  • Asset sales: tax the gain over book value (or save tax on the loss).
  • Special cases: replacement (with − without), bid price (solve NPV = 0 for \(P\)), different lives (EAC).

11Common mistakes

  • Including sunk costs (past R&D, money already spent) or leaving out opportunity costs (an owned building at its market value, not its purchase price).
  • Subtracting interest in project cash flows. Financing is in the discount rate, not the cash flows.
  • Forgetting to add back depreciation (bottom-up), or subtracting it in the top-down approach.
  • Wrong sign on NWC: an increase is an outflow. And forgetting to recover NWC at the end.
  • Salvage before tax: use \(MV-(MV-BV)T\), with the book value at the date of sale.
  • Replacement: forgetting the old machine's sale today and its lost salvage later (an opportunity cost).
  • Comparing PVs of machines with different lives when they will be replaced: compare EACs.
