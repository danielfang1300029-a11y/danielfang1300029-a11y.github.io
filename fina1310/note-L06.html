FINA1310 · Corporate Finance · Lecture 6

Risk and return

Where the discount rate R comes from: measuring return and risk, why diversification removes some risk, and why only the rest (measured by beta) earns a reward: the CAPM.

0Where this sits

L4–L5 discounted at a required return \(R\) that was simply given. This lecture finds it: \(R\) = risk-free rate + a reward for the risk that cannot be diversified away.

Conventions

Returns are per year unless stated. In formulas, write returns as decimals (12% = 0.12); variance is then in decimal² (0.0090), and standard deviation is back in % (9.49%).

Opening question: CLP Holdings' share price moved about 15% a year (standard deviation), Alibaba's about 54%. Which should investors require the higher return on? (Answer at the end of section 8, and as the first practice question.)

1Statistics primer

Five numbers describe returns: the mean (typical value), variance and standard deviation (spread), covariance and correlation (moving together). No statistics background is assumed.

SymbolMeaningFirst used
\(R_t,\ T\)return in period \(t\); number of periodss3
\(\bar R\)average of past returnss3
\(\sigma^2,\ \sigma\)variance, standard deviations3
\(p_s,\ R_s,\ N\)probability of state \(s\), return in state \(s\), number of statess5
\(E(R)\)expected returns5
\(w_i\)portfolio weight of asset \(i\)s6
\(\text{Cov},\ \rho\)covariance, correlations6
\(\beta,\ R_f,\ R_M\)beta, risk-free rate, market returns8

\(\Sigma\) means “add up”. \(\sum_{t=1}^{T}R_t=R_1+R_2+\dots+R_T\) and \(\sum_{s=1}^{N}p_sR_s=p_1R_1+\dots+p_NR_N\). The letter under \(\Sigma\) is only a counter.

The five measures · one formula each
  • Mean (average): the typical return. Past data: \(\bar R=\frac1T\sum R_t\). Future: weight each outcome by its probability, \(E(R)=\sum p_sR_s\).
  • Variance: the average squared distance from the mean; squaring stops ups and downs cancelling. \(\sigma^2=\sum p_s\big(R_s-E(R)\big)^2\). For a sample of past returns, divide by \(T-1\) instead (section 3).
  • Standard deviation (SD) \(\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}\): the same spread, back in % like the returns. Bigger \(\sigma\) = riskier.
  • Covariance: do two returns move together? Multiply their deviations state by state and average: \[\begin{aligned}\text{Cov}(R_A,R_B)=\sum p_s&\big(R_{A,s}-E(R_A)\big)\\[-2pt]\times&\big(R_{B,s}-E(R_B)\big)\end{aligned}\] Positive: same direction; negative: opposite.
  • Correlation: covariance rescaled to lie between −1 and +1, so its size means something: \(\rho_{AB}=\dfrac{\text{Cov}(R_A,R_B)}{\sigma_A\sigma_B}\). +1 = always together, −1 = always opposite, 0 = unrelated.

Two more built from these, explained later: beta \(\beta_i=\text{Cov}(R_i,R_M)/\sigma_M^2\), the slope of a stock's returns on the market's (s8); and the portfolio rules: expected return and beta are weighted averages, variance is not, it picks up a covariance term (s6).

The normal distribution: how far from the average?

If returns follow the bell-shaped normal distribution, about 68% of outcomes lie within 1\(\sigma\) of the mean, 95% within 2\(\sigma\), 99.7% within 3\(\sigma\). US large stocks 1928–2025 (mean 11.85%, \(\sigma\) = 19.40%) fit well:

2Returns: dollar and percentage

Definitions

Total dollar return = income + capital gain (or loss). Income: dividends on a stock, coupons on a bond. Capital gain: the price change \(P_{t+1}-P_t\); it counts whether you sell or keep the asset.

\[R_{t+1}=\underbrace{\frac{D_{t+1}}{P_t}}_{\text{dividend yield}}+\underbrace{\frac{P_{t+1}-P_t}{P_t}}_{\text{capital-gain yield}}\]

In words: percentage return = income per dollar invested + price change per dollar invested.

Both met before: for a bond the dividend yield is the current yield and \(R\) is the one-period holding-period return (L4); for a stock \(R=D_1/P_0+g\) (L5).

Example · 1,000 shares bought at $5, $0.20 dividend each
  • Price ends at $5.50: \(200+500=\$700\) dollar return; \(700/5{,}000=14\%\) (4% dividend yield + 10% capital gain).
  • Price ends at $4.60: \(200-400=-\$200\); \(-4\%\) (4% − 8%).
Example · bought at $25, $2 dividend

Ends at $35: \(\frac{2}{25}+\frac{10}{25}=8\%+40\%=48\%\). Ends at $20: \(8\%-20\%=-12\%\).

3Capital-market history: average returns and risk

Over a century, riskier assets earned more on average, but swung more from year to year.

  • $1 invested at the end of 1927 (income reinvested): stocks grew by far the most, then bonds, then Treasury bills.
  • US large stocks year by year (1928–2025): returns swing widely; T-bill returns are a smooth line.
  • Hang Seng Index (1987–2025, price only): Hong Kong swings even more.

Two ways to average

Arithmetic and geometric average \[\bar R=\frac{R_1+R_2+\dots+R_T}{T}\] \[\begin{aligned}(1+R_G)^T=(1+R_1)&(1+R_2)\\\times\dots\times&(1+R_T)\end{aligned}\]

In words: arithmetic = the return in a typical year; geometric = the constant yearly return that would have produced what you actually ended with. Geometric is below arithmetic unless every return is the same; the bigger the swings, the bigger the gap.

Example · Hang Seng Index 2021–2025

Annual changes: −14.08%, −15.46%, −13.82%, +17.67%, +27.77%.

Arithmetic:

\[\begin{aligned}&\tfrac{-14.08-15.46-13.82+17.67+27.77}{5}\\&=0.42\%\end{aligned}\]

Geometric:

\[\begin{aligned}&0.8592\times0.8454\times0.8618\\&\times1.1767\times1.2777=0.9411\end{aligned}\]

so \(0.9411^{1/5}-1=-1.21\%\) a year.

Up 0.42% on average, yet $100 at the end of 2020 was worth $94.11 at the end of 2025: you lost money.

Variance and standard deviation of past returns

Sample variance \[\sigma^2=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(R_t-\bar R\big)^2\]

In words: the average squared deviation from the average, but dividing by \(T-1\), not \(T\), because the data are a sample of past returns. \(\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}\), in %.

What is this? (risk premium)

A Treasury bill is like a bank deposit: you know almost exactly what you will get back. A share might pay a lot or lose money. If shares offered only the same average return as bills, nobody would take the extra risk, so share prices settle low enough that they offer more on average. That extra average return is the risk premium: the pay for putting up with the swings. It is an average over many years, not a promise for any one year.

Two lessons

Risk-free asset: a short-term government bill. Little default risk, little interest-rate risk, so its return is mostly pure time value of money: about 2.7% on a 3-month HK Exchange Fund Bill (Aug 2026).

Risk premium = return on a risky asset − risk-free rate.

  1. Risky assets earn a risk premium on average.
  2. The greater the risk, the greater the reward on average.

Open question, answered in section 7: which kind of risk earns the reward?

You can now: compute the arithmetic and geometric averages and the sample variance and SD of past returns; read a risk premium over T-bills from a table; explain why riskier assets must offer a higher average return.

4Capital-market efficiency

Definition · efficient market

An efficient capital market is one where prices fully reflect available information. Why: many investors research and trade the moment news arrives; buying on good news pushes the price up, selling on bad news pushes it down, so prices adjust quickly and correctly. The efficient markets hypothesis (EMH): large, well-organised markets (HKEX, the NYSE) are efficient.

How prices should react to news. Efficient: one immediate jump on the announcement day, then flat. Not efficient: a delayed reaction (price drifts up over the following days) or an overreaction (jumps too far, then falls back).

Only the surprise moves the price. Announcement = expected part + surprise; the expected part is already in the price. Profits up 20% when the market expected 30% → the price falls. A price falling on “good” news is no evidence against efficiency.

Three forms: what is already in the price?

Each form contains the one before: weak ⊂ semistrong ⊂ strong.

FormPrices reflectNo extra return fromEvidence
Weakpast prices and trading volumetechnical analysis (reading price charts)largely holds
Semistrong+ all public information (statements, announcements, news)fundamental analysis of public datamostly holds, debated
Strong+ private information (insiders' plans, unannounced results)even inside informationfails: insiders profit
  • Weak form → prices follow a random walk: past changes do not predict the next one, so chart reading earns only the normal risk premium. Evidence: HSI monthly changes 1987–2026, this month vs next month, correlation −0.003 (essentially zero).
  • Semistrong → only news not yet public could earn an extra return. Evidence: takeover targets jump on the announcement day, then stay flat.
  • Strong fails: insiders who trade before news is public earn excess returns. That is why insider dealing is illegal (in Hong Kong, under the Securities and Futures Ordinance, SFO).

Evidence and interpretation

  • Professionals rarely beat the market after costs: mutual funds earned no risk-adjusted abnormal return (Carhart, 1997); 79% of active US large-cap funds trailed the S&P 500 in 2025, about 90% over 15 years.
  • Some anomalies remain: prices underreact to news about a firm's customers (Cohen and Frazzini, 2008). Tracking insiders' reported trades earned more (strong form fails).
  • “Efficiently inefficient” (Pedersen, 2015): prices are inefficient enough to pay active managers' research and trading costs, efficient enough that profits after costs attract no new money. With perfectly efficient prices no one would pay to research, and prices could not stay efficient. The book covers hedge-fund strategies (equity, macro, arbitrage) with interviews of Asness, Chanos, Griffin, Paulson, Scholes, Soros and others.
What efficiency does and does not mean
  • It does not mean you cannot make money: on average you earn a return that matches the risk you bear.
  • It does not mean prices are right after the fact: they are the best guess with today's information.
  • It does not protect you from bad choices: an undiversified portfolio bears risk with no extra reward (section 7).
  • It does mean no free lunch: higher expected return only with more risk.

5Expected return and variance

Looking forward, replace “years of data” with “states of the economy, each with a probability”.

Result · expected return and variance \[E(R)=p_1R_1+p_2R_2+\dots+p_NR_N\] \[\sigma^2=\sum_{s=1}^{N}p_s\big(R_s-E(R)\big)^2\]

In words: the probability-weighted average return, and the probability-weighted squared deviation. Probabilities sum to 1. Same ideas as section 3: history weights each year equally (\(1/T\), or \(1/(T-1)\) for variance); here each state gets its probability. History is our estimate of the future.

6Portfolios

A portfolio's expected return is the weighted average of its assets'. Its risk is not: it depends on how the assets move together.

Definition · portfolio weight

\(w_i\) = share of your money in asset \(i\); weights sum to 1. 100 shares of A at $30 ($3,000) + 500 of B at $4 ($2,000): \(w_A=60\%\), \(w_B=40\%\).

Weights can be negative. Short selling = borrowing shares and selling them now, to buy back later; borrowing cash is a negative weight on the risk-free asset. With $1,000 of your own money: borrow $400 (−40%), short 20 A at $30 (−$600, −60%), buy 500 B at $4 ($2,000, +200%). Total $1,000 = 100%.

Expected return: a weighted average

\[E(R_P)=w_AE(R_A)+w_BE(R_B)\]

60% A, 40% B (from section 5), two routes to the same answer:

State\(p\)ABPortfolio \(0.6R_A+0.4R_B\)
Boom0.320%15%18.0%
Normal0.510%10%10.0%
Recession0.25%8%6.2%
  • State by state: \(0.3(18)+0.5(10)+0.2(6.2)=11.64\%\)
  • Weighted average: \(0.6(12.0)+0.4(11.1)=11.64\%\)

Variance: treat the portfolio as one asset

Use the portfolio's return in each state and apply the section 5 formula:

\[\begin{aligned}\sigma_P^2&=0.3(0.0636)^2+0.5(-0.0164)^2\\&\quad+0.2(-0.0544)^2\\&=0.0012135+0.0001345\\&\quad+0.0005919=0.00194\end{aligned}\]

\(\sigma_P=4.40\%\). Not the weighted average of the two variances: what matters is how the assets move together.

Same risk alone, very different risk together

Three stocks, each with \(E(R)=10\%\) and \(\sigma^2=0.005\). Boom (0.25) / normal (0.5) / recession (0.25):

Example · two stocks moving in opposite directions

A: 30% in a boom (prob. 0.4), −10% in a bust (0.6). B: −5% in a boom, 25% in a bust. 50% in each.

  • \(E(R_A)=0.4(30)+0.6(-10)=6\%\); \(E(R_B)=0.4(-5)+0.6(25)=13\%\)
  • Portfolio: 12.5% in a boom, 7.5% in a bust; \(E(R_P)=9.5\%\) (= average of 6 and 13).
  • SDs: \(\sigma_A=19.6\%\), \(\sigma_B=14.7\%\), \(\sigma_P=2.45\%\):
\[\begin{aligned}\sigma_A&=\sqrt{0.4(0.24)^2+0.6(0.16)^2}\\\sigma_P&=\sqrt{0.4(0.03)^2+0.6(0.02)^2}\end{aligned}\]

The portfolio earns the average return with far less risk than either stock alone.

Covariance and correlation

What is this? (covariance and correlation)

Picture two stalls on the same beach: one sells umbrellas, the other ice cream. On rainy days the umbrella stall does well and the ice-cream stall badly; on sunny days it is the other way round. Their takings move in opposite directions: negative covariance. Own both and your total takings barely change with the weather, which is stocks 1 and 3 in the table above. Two ice-cream stalls move together (positive covariance), so owning both gives no protection: stocks 1 and 2. Covariance says which way two returns move together; correlation rescales it to between −1 and +1 so you can also tell how strongly.

Definitions in section 1: covariance = probability-weighted product of the two deviations; correlation \(\rho=\text{Cov}/(\sigma_A\sigma_B)\) between −1 and +1; \(\rho\gt0\) together, \(\rho\lt0\) opposite, \(\rho=0\) unrelated.

Result · two-asset portfolio variance \[\begin{aligned}\sigma_P^2={}&w_A^2\sigma_A^2+w_B^2\sigma_B^2\\&+2w_Aw_B\rho_{AB}\sigma_A\sigma_B\end{aligned}\]

In words: each asset's own variance, weighted by its weight squared, plus a co-movement term \(2w_Aw_B\,\text{Cov}(R_A,R_B)\). Co-movement adds risk; opposite movement (negative covariance) removes it. Gives the same answer as the state-by-state route.

Check, 60/40 in A and B: the four cells of the 2 × 2 table are \(0.6^2(0.00310)=0.001116\), two cross cells \(0.6\times0.4\times0.00148=0.000355\) each, and \(0.4^2(0.000709)=0.000113\). Sum \(0.00194\), \(\sigma_P=4.40\%\), as before. ✓

Reading the formula
\(w_A^2\sigma_A^2\)
the umbrella stall's own ups and downs, scaled by how much of your money is in it (squared, because variance is in squared units).
\(w_B^2\sigma_B^2\)
the same for the ice-cream stall.
\(2w_Aw_B\rho_{AB}\sigma_A\sigma_B\)
the moving-together term. Positive \(\rho\) adds risk (bad days come together); negative \(\rho\) takes risk away (one stall's bad day is the other's good day).
the 2
the pair appears twice in the 2 × 2 table: A with B and B with A.
Result · n assets \[\begin{aligned}\sigma_P^2=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i^2\sigma_i^2+\sum_{i\ne j}w_iw_j\rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j\end{aligned}\]

In words: add every cell of an \(n\times n\) table: variances on the diagonal (\(n\) terms), covariances off it (\(n(n-1)\) cells: each of the \(n(n-1)/2\) pairs appears twice, hence the 2 in the two-asset formula).

Portfolio expected return and SD, step by step (as in the example below):

  1. Do: find each holding's value (number × price; a short sale is negative) and divide by your own money to get the weights. A weight says what share of your money rides on each asset; a short position is money riding against it.
  2. Do: \(E(R_P)=\sum w_iE(R_i)\). Expected returns simply average: each dollar earns its own asset's expected return.
  3. Do: variance terms \(w_i^2\sigma_i^2\), one per asset. Each asset's own swings, scaled by how much money is in it.
  4. Do: covariance terms \(2w_iw_j\rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j\), one per pair, keeping the signs of the weights. This is where diversification shows up: pairs that move oppositely, or a short against a stock that moves with another, cancel risk.
  5. Do: add all the terms to get \(\sigma_P^2\), then take the square root. Variance adds up cell by cell; the square root puts the answer back in %, like the returns.
Example · three stocks with a short sale

Stock 1: $20, \(E(R)=20\%\), \(\sigma^2=0.25\). Stock 2: $5, 12%, 0.16. Stock 3: $10, 18%, 0.2025. \(\rho_{12}=0.3\), \(\rho_{23}=0.1\), \(\rho_{13}=0.6\). Buy 500 of Stock 1 and 1,000 of Stock 2; short 500 of Stock 3.

  • Values $10,000, $5,000, −$5,000; total $10,000 → \(w=1,\ 0.5,\ -0.5\).
  • \(E(R_P)=20+6-9=17\%\) (weights × expected returns).
  • SDs: \(\sigma=0.5,\ 0.4,\ 0.45\).
Show the calculation: the variance

Variance terms \(w_i^2\sigma_i^2\):

\[\begin{aligned}&1^2(0.25)+0.5^2(0.16)\\&+(-0.5)^2(0.2025)=0.3406\end{aligned}\]

\[\begin{aligned}1,2:&\ 2(1)(0.5)(0.3)(0.5)(0.4)\\&=0.06\\2,3:&\ 2(0.5)(-0.5)(0.1)(0.4)(0.45)\\&=-0.009\\1,3:&\ 2(1)(-0.5)(0.6)(0.5)(0.45)\\&=-0.135\end{aligned}\]

7Diversification and systematic risk

Diversification removes each firm's own risk; the risk all firms share remains, and only that risk is rewarded.

What is this? (diversification)

Think of eggs in baskets. Put every egg in one basket and one trip can break them all. Spread them over many baskets and one trip costs only a few: accidents that hit one basket at a time (a factory fire, a CEO leaving) mostly cancel out across many firms. But if the lorry carrying every basket crashes, all the baskets fall together, and no amount of spreading helps. That shared accident is a recession or an interest-rate jump: the risk that remains however many stocks you hold.

Result · n equally weighted stocks

Each stock has SD \(\sigma\), every pair correlation \(\rho\), weights \(1/n\):

\[\sigma_P^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}+\Big(1-\frac1n\Big)\rho\sigma^2\]

In words: as \(n\) grows, the first term vanishes (each stock's own risk is diversified away) and the second tends to \(\rho\sigma^2\) (shared risk cannot be). If \(\rho=1\) there is no benefit at all: \(\sigma_P=\sigma\).

Show the calculation: from the n-asset formula

Diagonal: \(n\) terms of \((1/n)^2\sigma^2\) → \(\sigma^2/n\).

Off-diagonal: \(n(n-1)\) terms of \((1/n)^2\rho\sigma^2\) → \(\frac{n-1}{n}\rho\sigma^2\).

Reading the formula
\(\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\)
the dropped-basket risk: each firm's own accidents. Each stock is only \(1/n\) of your money, so this part shrinks towards zero as \(n\) grows.
\(\Big(1-\dfrac1n\Big)\rho\sigma^2\)
the lorry-crash risk that every stock shares. It does not shrink; it grows towards \(\rho\sigma^2\).
\(\rho\)
how much the stocks share: \(\rho=0\) means no common accident (the floor is zero); \(\rho=1\) means every stock is the same bet (no benefit at all).

Try it · more stocks, less risk, down to a floor (each σ = 30%)

Grey curves: \(\rho=1,\ 0.5,\ 0.2,\ 0\). The floor is \(\sigma\sqrt{\rho}\): 21.2% at \(\rho=0.5\), 13.4% at 0.2, zero only if \(\rho=0\). Most of the drop happens in the first 10–20 stocks.

The principle of diversification
  • Spreading money across assets cuts risk by more than it cuts expected return: bad news for one firm is offset by good news for another.
  • The less correlated the assets, the stronger the effect. Many assets is not enough: 50 internet stocks are not diversified.
  • Some risk remains however many assets you hold.

Systematic and unsystematic risk

What is this? (systematic and unsystematic risk)

Split every surprise in a stock's return into two parts. The lorry crash: news that hits nearly every firm at once, such as a rate rise or a recession. That is systematic (market) risk. The dropped basket: news about this firm alone, such as a lawsuit or a factory fire. That is unsystematic (firm-specific) risk. A diversified investor hardly feels the second kind, because other firms' good and bad luck cancel it, so the market pays no reward for bearing it. Only the first kind is left to be paid for.

Every return = expected part + surprise: \(R=E(R)+U\), and the surprise splits into \(U=m+\varepsilon\):

Total risk = systematic risk + unsystematic risk.

The systematic risk principle

The reward for bearing risk depends only on systematic risk. Unsystematic risk can be removed at almost no cost, so the market does not pay you for bearing it.

In words: expected return = risk-free rate + (amount of systematic risk) × (reward per unit). An asset's expected return does not depend on its total risk \(\sigma\). Next: measure the amount.

8Beta and the CAPM

What is this? (beta)

Beta measures how much a stock tends to move when the whole market moves. If the market rises 1%, a stock with beta 1.5 tends to rise about 1.5%, and a stock with beta 0.3 about 0.3%; when the market falls, they tend to fall by those amounts. Beta counts only this moving-with-the-market part: the lorry-crash risk of section 7. A stock can swing wildly on its own news and still have a low beta, if those swings have little to do with the market. That is why beta, not total risk, is the amount of risk that earns a reward.

Definition · beta \[\beta_i=\frac{\text{Cov}(R_i,R_M)}{\sigma_M^2}=\rho_{iM}\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_M}\]

In words: asset \(i\)'s systematic risk relative to the market portfolio (all risky assets, e.g. the HSI): if the market moves 1%, the stock moves \(\beta\)% on average. \(\beta_M=1\); \(\beta\gt1\): more systematic risk than the market; \(\beta\lt1\): less. Through \(\rho_{iM}\), beta keeps only the part of \(\sigma_i\) that moves with the market.

Reading the formula
\(\text{Cov}(R_i,R_M)\)
how much the stock and the market move together.
\(\div\,\sigma_M^2\)
scales that by the market's own movement, so the answer reads “% move in the stock per 1% move in the market” (the market measured against itself gives exactly 1).
\(\rho_{iM}\)
how closely the stock follows the market, from 0 (not at all) to 1 (perfectly). The part it does not follow is its own news, which diversifies away.
\(\sigma_i/\sigma_M\)
how big the stock's swings are compared with the market's.

Result · portfolio beta \[\beta_P=w_1\beta_1+w_2\beta_2+\dots+w_n\beta_n\]

In words: unlike SD, beta does average out: a portfolio's systematic risk is the weighted sum of its holdings'.

Same reward per unit of beta

Reward-to-risk ratio \[\text{reward-to-risk}=\frac{E(R_i)-R_f}{\beta_i}\]

In words: risk premium per unit of systematic risk.

\(R_f=3\%\), \(E(R_M)=9\%\). Asset A: \(E(R)=15\%\), \(\beta=1.6\), ratio \((15-3)/1.6=7.5\%\). Market: \((9-3)/1=6\%\). A pays more per unit of beta, so investors buy it; its price rises and its expected return falls until its ratio is 6% too. In equilibrium every asset offers the same ratio.

What is this? (CAPM and the security market line)

Think of a taxi meter. Every ride starts with a fixed flag fall, then adds the same charge for every kilometre. In the CAPM the flag fall is the risk-free rate (the reward for waiting), the kilometres are the asset's beta (how much market risk it carries), and the charge per kilometre is the market risk premium. Every asset is priced off the same meter. The security market line is that meter drawn as a graph: required return against beta. A stock offering more than the line is a bargain, so investors buy it until its price rises and its expected return falls back onto the line.

Result · capital asset pricing model (CAPM) \[E(R_i)=R_f+\beta_i\big(E(R_M)-R_f\big)\]

In words: expected return = reward for waiting (\(R_f\), pure time value of money) + amount of systematic risk (\(\beta_i\)) × reward per unit of beta (the market risk premium \(E(R_M)-R_f\)). Holds for single assets and portfolios; \(\beta=1\) gives the market's return, \(\beta=0\) the risk-free rate.

Reading the formula
\(R_f\)
the flag fall: what you earn just for waiting, with no risk (\(\beta=0\)).
\(\beta_i\)
the kilometres: how much market risk the asset carries (1 for the market itself).
\(E(R_M)-R_f\)
the charge per kilometre: the market risk premium, the extra return per unit of beta.
\(\beta_i\big(E(R_M)-R_f\big)\)
the asset's own risk premium. Its firm-specific news does not appear anywhere: that risk earns nothing.

The security market line (SML): \(E(R)=3\%+\beta\times6\%\)

The SML plots the CAPM: required return against beta, starting at \(R_f=3\%\) when \(\beta=0\) and passing through the market at \(\beta=1\). Above the line = underpriced; below = overpriced.

  • B: \(\beta=0.8\) requires \(3+0.8(6)=7.8\%\) but offers 10%. Above the line, underpriced: investors buy, its price rises, its expected return falls to 7.8%.
  • C: \(\beta=1.4\) requires \(3+1.4(6)=11.4\%\) but offers 8%. Below, overpriced: investors sell, its price falls, its expected return rises to 11.4%.

The asset on the SML at \(\beta=1.5\), 12% is a different asset from the reward-to-risk example's A (\(\beta=1.6\), 15%), so it is labelled A* on the chart.

Example · using the CAPM (\(R_f=3\%\), \(E(R_M)=9\%\))
  1. \(\beta=1.5\): \(E(R)=3+1.5(9-3)=12\%\).
  2. \(E(R)=10.5\%\): \(\beta=(10.5-3)/6=1.25\). Run the CAPM backwards: the asset's risk premium of 7.5% divided by 6% per unit of beta.

Portfolio beta and required return, step by step:

  1. Do: find each holding's weight: its value ÷ the portfolio's total value. Each stock counts in proportion to the money in it.
  2. Do: \(\beta_P=\sum w_i\beta_i\). How much the portfolio moves when the market moves 1% is the money-weighted sum of how much each holding moves; there is no covariance term, because beta already keeps only the moving-with-the-market part.
  3. Do: \(E(R_P)=R_f+\beta_P\big(E(R_M)-R_f\big)\). The CAPM holds for portfolios too: the same meter, with the portfolio's beta as the kilometres.
  4. Do: to check, take the weighted average of each holding's own CAPM required return: it gives the same answer. The SML is a straight line, so averaging before or after using it makes no difference.

What the CAPM gives

Investors: the required return that compensates systematic risk. Firms: the cost of capital, what investors expect for funding them. It is the \(R\) used to discount dividends in stock valuation (L5). About three in four CFOs use the CAPM to estimate the cost of equity; L10 builds on it.

9Summary and formula card

  • Return = dividend yield + capital-gain yield.
  • History: arithmetic vs geometric average; sample variance divides by \(T-1\). Risky assets earned a risk premium, larger for more risk.
  • Efficient markets: weak, semistrong (both largely hold), strong (fails). No free lunch.
  • Portfolios: expected return and beta average; variance does not (covariance term).
  • Diversification removes unsystematic risk; only systematic risk, measured by \(\beta\), is rewarded: CAPM.

10Common mistakes

  • Dividing by \(T\) for a sample of past returns. Use \(T-1\) (Excel VAR.S, STDEV.S). With probabilities, no division at all.
  • Averaging variances or SDs for a portfolio. Only expected return and beta average.
  • Forgetting the 2 in \(2w_Aw_B\text{Cov}\), or the sign of a negative weight or covariance.
  • Quoting the arithmetic average as what you earned over several years. That is the geometric average.
  • Judging risk by \(\sigma\) when asking about required return. The CAPM uses \(\beta\).
  • Using the market return, not the market risk premium, as the slope: \(3\%+\beta\times9\%\) is wrong when \(E(R_M)=9\%\); it is \(3\%+\beta\times6\%\).
  • Above the SML = overpriced. No: above means it offers more than required, so it is underpriced.
