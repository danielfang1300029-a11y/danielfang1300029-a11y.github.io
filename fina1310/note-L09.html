FINA1310 · Corporate Finance · Lecture 9

Project analysis and evaluation

An NPV is only as good as its forecasts. Tools to see how much it depends on them: scenarios, sensitivity, simulation, break-even points, operating leverage; plus what to do when there is not enough money for every good project.

0Where this sits

Capital budgeting in four steps: ① cash flows (L8) · ② discount rate (L10) · ③ investment criteria (L7) · ④ additional analysis: what if the forecasts are wrong? (this lecture).

Recap from L8 · operating cash flow (tax-shield form) \[OCF=\big[(P-v)\,Q-FC\big](1-T)+D\,T\]

In words: after-tax profit before depreciation, plus the tax saved by depreciation. \(P\) price, \(v\) variable cost per unit, \(Q\) unit sales, \(FC\) fixed costs a year, \(D\) depreciation, \(T\) tax rate. With a level OCF for \(t\) years, \(NPV=-I+OCF\times AF(r,t)\) (annuity factor, L3).

Sign convention

Money in +, money out −; \(t=0\) is today. Throughout, \(AF(12\%,5)=3.6048\).

1Problems with NPV

NPV uses the average forecast; the firm gets one actual outcome.

  • Projected cash flows = the average over possible outcomes; actual cash flows depend on which outcome occurs.
  • NPV > 0 on projected flows → a good decision ex ante (before the fact). NPV < 0 on actual flows → a bad investment ex post (after the fact). A good decision can still turn out badly.
Definition · forecasting risk

The risk of a bad decision because cash flows were misestimated. Sources: states of the world you did not anticipate; wrong probabilities for the states you did; wrong cash flows within a state. The rest of this lecture: tools to see how much NPV depends on the forecasts.

2Scenario analysis

Recompute NPV in a few internally consistent “states of the world”.

What is this?

Think of the forecast as a panel of dials: price, unit sales, variable cost, fixed costs, the investment. The base-case NPV is what you get with every dial on its best guess.

Scenario analysis sets all the dials to match one story at once (a recession: fewer units and a lower price together) and reads off the NPV. Sensitivity analysis (§3) turns one dial and leaves the rest alone; simulation (§4) spins them all at random, thousands of times.

Base case = NPV from the projected cash flows. What-if analysis = NPV if sales, price, costs, etc. differ. Three tools: scenario analysis (a few consistent states: a recession, a new competitor, a cost plan that fails), sensitivity analysis (one input at a time, s3), simulation (many inputs, many random values, s4).

Running example · the base case

Equipment $200,000, straight-line to 0 over 5 years (\(D=\$40{,}000\), no salvage). 6,000 units a year at $80; variable cost $60 a unit; fixed costs $50,000 a year; tax 34%; required return 12%; no net working capital.

\[\begin{aligned}OCF&=[20\times6{,}000-50{,}000](0.66)\\&\quad+0.34\times40{,}000\\&=46{,}200+13{,}600=\$59{,}800\end{aligned}\]

What scenarios can and cannot tell you
  • Any number of scenarios: 3 (worst, base, best), 5 (worst, bad, base, good, best) or more.
  • Reality can be worse than the worst case or better than the best.
  • It shows what happens in a state, not whether to invest. Many scenarios with NPV < 0 is a warning sign.

You can now: compute base, worst and best-case OCF and NPV, setting each input (the investment included) at the bound that lowers or raises NPV; and say what scenario analysis can and cannot tell you.

3Sensitivity analysis

Hold everything at base case, move one input, and see how steeply NPV moves.

A steep line = NPV is sensitive to that input → spend effort forecasting that input well.

Try it · NPV against one input (others at base case)

Slopes: $4,758 per 100 units; $14,275 per $1 of price; −$14,275 per $1 of variable cost; −$2,379 per $1,000 of fixed costs. NPV = 0 at 5,673 units (the financial break-even, s5).

Result · one input changes

Only OCF changes, and it changes by the same amount every year, so

\[\Delta NPV=\Delta OCF\times AF(r,t)\]

In words: the NPV change is the annuity value of the yearly OCF change. From \(OCF=[(P-v)Q-FC](1-T)+DT\):

Input moves\(\Delta OCF\)
Unit sales \(\Delta Q\)\(\Delta Q\,(P-v)(1-T)\)
Price \(\Delta P\)\(\Delta P\,Q\,(1-T)\)
Variable cost \(\Delta v\)\(-\Delta v\,Q\,(1-T)\)
Fixed costs \(\Delta FC\)\(-\Delta FC\,(1-T)\)

100 more units: \(100\times20\times0.66\times3.6048=\$4{,}758\).

4Simulation

Scenario + sensitivity combined: let several inputs vary at once, at random, thousands of times.

Draw a price from $75–85 and unit sales from 5,500–6,500, compute the NPV, repeat 100,000 times. Read off the average NPV, its spread (standard deviation, SD: the typical distance from the average) and the chance of NPV < 0. Going further · correlation

Correlation (between −1 and 1) measures how much two inputs move together. Positive correlation makes good inputs arrive with good inputs, so extremes are more likely and the spread widens.

5Break-even analysis

How low can sales go before the project loses on a given measure?

What is this?

Picture three finishing lines on the same track, each asking more of the project. The cash break-even is where the year's operating cash in just covers the cash out. The accounting break-even is further on: sales must also cover depreciation, the machine's cost spread over its life, so the investment comes back but earns nothing.

The financial break-even is furthest: sales must return the investment plus the required return on it, which is exactly what NPV = 0 means.

Definitions · the unit sales at which…
  • Accounting break-even: net income = 0
  • Cash break-even: OCF = 0
  • Financial break-even: NPV = 0

Sales below a break-even → the project loses on that measure.

Accounting break-even

With no fixed costs or depreciation, net income \(=(80-60)Q(0.66)=13.2Q\): a line through the origin, so break-even at 0. Bring back \(FC\) and \(D\):

\[\begin{aligned}NI&=(20Q-50{,}000-40{,}000)(0.66)\\&=13.2Q-59{,}400\end{aligned}\]
Result · accounting break-even \[Q=\frac{FC+D}{P-v}\]\[Q=\frac{50{,}000+40{,}000}{80-60}=4{,}500\]

In words: each unit contributes \(P-v\); sell enough units to cover all fixed costs, depreciation included.

  • The same at any tax rate (tax is a fraction of zero).
  • OCF = NI + D = \(D\) every year, so payback = the project's life.
  • You only get the investment back, so IRR = 0 → NPV < 0 at any positive required return.
Reading the formula
\(P-v\)
what each unit sold contributes: its price minus the variable cost of making it.
\(FC\)
the fixed costs to cover each year, whatever you sell.
\(D\)
the machine's cost spread over its life. Covering it means the investment comes back, with nothing earned on it.
\(Q\)
how many unit contributions it takes to cover both: the accounting finishing line.

Cash break-even

\[\begin{aligned}OCF&=NI+D=13.2Q-59{,}400+40{,}000\\&=13.2Q-19{,}400\end{aligned}\]

OCF = 0 at \(Q=19{,}400/13.2=1{,}470\) units. Without taxes: \(Q=FC/(P-v)=50{,}000/20=2{,}500\).

When is cash break-even useful?

At the cash break-even, NI < 0 (as \(D\gt0\)) and NPV < 0: none of the initial investment comes back. But once the project is running, the investment is sunk (spent and unrecoverable, L8). Above the cash break-even, continuing still brings in cash, so don't shut it down just because it is losing on paper.

Financial break-even

  1. Do: Find the OCF that makes NPV = 0. This yearly cash, received as an annuity, just repays the investment with the required return on it: the furthest finishing line, in dollars.
  2. Do: Then find the sales that produce it. OCF rises with unit sales, so solve the OCF formula for the units that deliver it.
\[\begin{aligned}0&=-200{,}000+OCF^*\times3.6048\\OCF^*&=200{,}000/3.6048=\$55{,}482\end{aligned}\]

\(13.2Q-19{,}400=55{,}482\) → \(Q=5{,}673\) units. Below 5,673, NPV < 0. Without taxes:

\[\begin{aligned}Q&=(FC+OCF^*)/(P-v)\\&=105{,}482/20=5{,}274\end{aligned}\]
Reading the formula
\(OCF^*\)
the yearly OCF that makes NPV = 0.
\(FC+OCF^*\)
with no tax, sales must cover the fixed costs and still leave \(OCF^*\) over.
\(P-v\)
each unit's contribution, as in the accounting break-even.
Example · financial break-even with salvage and NWC

Same project, plus: equipment sold for $20,000 at the end of year 5; net working capital (NWC) of $10,000 at the start, recovered at the end. Book value at year 5 is 0, so salvage after tax \(=20{,}000(1-0.34)=\$13{,}200\). Year 5 adds \(13{,}200+10{,}000=\$23{,}200\).

\[\begin{aligned}0&=-210{,}000+OCF^*\times3.6048\\&\quad+23{,}200/1.12^5\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}OCF^*&=\frac{210{,}000-13{,}164}{3.6048}=\$54{,}604\\Q&=\frac{54{,}604+19{,}400}{13.2}=5{,}606\end{aligned}\]

Terminal flows lower the break-even (5,606 vs 5,673): always include them.

Financial > accounting > cash break-even

You can now: compute the cash, accounting and financial break-evens, with and without taxes; include salvage and NWC in the financial one; and explain why cash < accounting < financial and when the cash break-even matters.

6Operating leverage

Fixed costs magnify forecast errors: a small miss in sales becomes a large miss in OCF.

What is this?

Think of a bus company and a taxi driver. The bus costs about the same to run whether 10 or 40 passengers ride, so each extra passenger is nearly pure profit and each lost one nearly pure loss. The taxi's costs (fuel, time) mostly rise and fall with its trips, so its profit moves more gently.

High fixed costs make a project like the bus: they magnify any miss in the sales forecast.

Operating leverage = how much a project relies on fixed costs. Higher fixed costs → OCF swings more when sales change → higher forecasting risk. Renting equipment or outsourcing turns fixed costs into variable ones and lowers it.

Result · degree of operating leverage \[\begin{aligned}DOL&=\frac{\%\text{ change in }OCF}{\%\text{ change in }Q}\\&=\frac{\Delta OCF/OCF}{\Delta Q/Q}\end{aligned}\] \[DOL=1+\frac{FC(1-T)-DT}{OCF}\]

In words: a 1% change in sales changes OCF by DOL %. Without taxes (\(T=0\)): \(DOL=1+FC/OCF\).

Derivation

Rewrite OCF so \(Q\) appears once:

\[\begin{aligned}OCF={}&(P-v)(1-T)\,Q\\&-FC(1-T)+DT\end{aligned}\]

Change \(Q\) by \(\Delta Q\): only the first term moves, so \(\Delta OCF=(P-v)(1-T)\Delta Q\). From the line above,

\[\begin{aligned}&(P-v)(1-T)Q\\&=OCF+FC(1-T)-DT\end{aligned}\]

Hence

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\Delta OCF}{OCF}&=\frac{\Delta Q}{Q}\cdot\frac{(P-v)(1-T)Q}{OCF}\\&=\frac{\Delta Q}{Q}\Big(1+\tfrac{FC(1-T)-DT}{OCF}\Big)\end{aligned}\]

Divide by \(\Delta Q/Q\) to get DOL.

  1. Do: Compute OCF at the current sales level. DOL depends on where you stand: the same fixed costs weigh more when OCF is small.
  2. Do: \(DOL=1+FC/OCF\) without taxes; with taxes, use \(1+[FC(1-T)-DT]/OCF\). It compares the fixed costs with the cash left after them: the bus's running cost against its takings.
  3. Do: Multiply the % change in sales by DOL to get the % change in OCF. That is what DOL means: the factor that turns a sales miss into an OCF miss.
Example · our project, ignoring taxes
  • 6,000 units: OCF \(=20\times6{,}000-50{,}000=\$70{,}000\); DOL \(=1+50{,}000/70{,}000=1.71\). Sales 10% higher → OCF up \(1.71\times10\%=17.1\%\).
  • 5,500 units: OCF \(=\$60{,}000\); DOL \(=1+50{,}000/60{,}000=1.83\).

DOL rises as sales fall towards the cash break-even (where OCF → 0 and DOL → ∞).

You can now: compute DOL with and without taxes; use it to turn a % change in sales into a % change in OCF; and explain why it rises as sales fall towards the cash break-even.

7Capital rationing

More positive-NPV projects than money: pick the set with the most total NPV.

  • Capital rationing: positive-NPV projects the firm cannot fund.
  • Soft rationing: a division gets a fixed budget; the firm as a whole is not short of capital. Ask for more, or choose the projects with the most total NPV.
  • Hard rationing: the firm cannot raise capital at all (financial distress, or a loan contract forbids it).

8Summary and formula card

  • NPV rests on forecasts; project analysis measures forecasting risk, it does not decide.
  • Scenarios: worst, base and best NPV (worst case: least favourable inputs, investment included). Sensitivity: one input at a time. Simulation: many at once, a distribution of NPVs.
  • Break-evens: cash < accounting < financial; include terminal flows in the financial one.
  • Operating leverage: fixed costs magnify forecast errors. Capital rationing: maximise total NPV within the budget.

9Common mistakes

  • Mixing directions in a worst case. Costs go to their higher bound, sales and price to the lower; investment to the higher (and its depreciation shield up with it).
  • Forgetting the depreciation tax shield when the investment changes, or when going from NI to OCF (add \(D\) back).
  • Sensitivity without \((1-T)\) or without the annuity factor.
  • Calling accounting break-even “safe”. It earns IRR = 0: NPV < 0 at any positive required return.
  • Ignoring salvage and NWC in the financial break-even.
  • DOL at the wrong sales level: use OCF at the level you are asked about.
  • Ranking by PI when the budget is not used up exactly.

A. $15,566, because NPV uses the expected cash flows · B. More, because sales can reach 6,500 · C. Less, because forecasts are optimistic · D. Unknown until sales are known, and it could be negative

Hint

Projected vs actual cash flows; the financial break-even is 5,673 units.

Answer

→ D. $15,566 is the ex-ante average; the actual NPV depends on actual sales, and any sales below 5,673 give \(\boxed{NPV\lt0}\).

PracticeDOL at a new sales level

Fixed costs are $60,000 a year and OCF is $90,000 at 10,000 units. Ignore taxes. DOL at the new sales level of 11,000 units? A. 1.36 · B. 1.40 · C. 1.57 · D. 1.67

Hint

First find \(P-v\) from OCF at 10,000 units, then OCF at 11,000.

Answer

\(90{,}000=(P-v)\times10{,}000-60{,}000\) → \(P-v=\$15\). At 11,000: OCF \(=165{,}000-60{,}000=\$105{,}000\).

\[DOL=1+\tfrac{60{,}000}{105{,}000}=\boxed{1.57}\]

→ C.

Option D (1.67) is the DOL at the old level, \(1+60/90\), which also equals the % change in OCF from 10,000 to 11,000 units (16.7%) over the % change in sales (10%). “At the new sales level” means OCF at 11,000, so C.
