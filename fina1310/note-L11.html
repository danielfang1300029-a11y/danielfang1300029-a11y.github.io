FINA1310 · Corporate Finance · Lecture 11

Capital structure

Does the mix of debt and equity change what a firm is worth? Not in a perfect market (MM); yes once taxes and distress costs enter; and in practice firms also follow a pecking order.

0Where this sits

L7–L10 answered “which projects?” (capital budgeting). This lecture answers corporate finance's second question: “how to pay for them?” (capital structure = the firm's mix of debt and equity). Day-to-day finances (working capital) are not in this course. Textbook: Chapter 16.

Recap from L10 · WACC \[\text{WACC}=\frac{E}{V}R_E+\frac{D}{V}R_D(1-T_C)\]

In words: the average return the firm's investors require, weighted by market values, with debt's cost cut by tax because interest is deductible. \(V=E+D\).

Notation

\(D, E, V\): market values of debt, equity, firm. \(R_A\) (= \(R_U\)): required return on the assets = cost of equity of an all-equity (unlevered) firm. Levered = has debt. \(T_C\): corporate tax rate. Cash flows are level perpetuities unless stated.

1Capital structure and firm value

Lenders are paid first; shareholders get what is left. Firm value is the PV of the asset cash flows at the WACC.

What is this? (financial leverage)

Think of buying a flat with a mortgage. The bank's loan is fixed: you owe the same amount whatever happens to the flat's price. So every rise or fall in the price lands on your own deposit. With a 20% deposit, a 10% rise in the flat's price is a 50% gain on your money, and a 10% fall is a 50% loss (ignoring interest). Debt in a firm works the same way: lenders get a fixed amount, so shareholders take all the swings, magnified. That is why more debt makes the return on equity swing more, and why shareholders then require a higher return.

Equity absorbs every swing; debt does not move (until the firm is worth less than 50).

Goal · maximise firm value \[V=\frac{CF_1}{1+\text{WACC}}+\dots+\frac{CF_T}{(1+\text{WACC})^T}\]

In words: if debt is fairly priced, what maximises \(V=E+D\) also maximises shareholders' value. Cash flows are set by capital budgeting, so the only lever left is the WACC. The question of this lecture: does the debt/equity mix change the WACC?

Key idea: the cost of capital depends on the use of funds (how risky the assets' cash flows are), not the source. Firm A with risky investments makes its creditors and shareholders demand high returns; firm B with safe investments, low returns, whoever supplies the money.

You can now: show how a fixed debt claim makes equity absorb every swing in firm value; explain why maximising \(V\) also maximises shareholders' value when debt is fairly priced; state that the cost of capital depends on the use of funds, not the source.

2Modigliani and Miller (MM) without taxes

In a perfect market, debt only slices the pie; it does not make it bigger. Firm value and WACC do not depend on capital structure.

Franco Modigliani (Nobel 1985) and Merton Miller (Nobel 1990). The lecture builds three cases, each relaxing one assumption of MM:

AssumptionI: MM, no taxesII: MM, taxesIII: static theory
Complete markets (any claim trades at a fair price), efficient markets, same information for managers and investors, no transaction costs, investment policy fixedYesYesYes
No corporate taxesYesNoNo
No bankruptcy costsYesYesNo
A fixed amount of debt forevernot neededYesYes
What is this? (MM Proposition I)

A pie is the same size however you cut it. The firm's assets produce a cash flow (the pie); debt and equity are just two ways of slicing it between lenders and shareholders. Without taxes or bankruptcy costs, changing the slices cannot make the pie bigger, so the firm is worth the same whatever its mix. And because investors can borrow or lend on their own, they will not pay extra for a firm that borrows for them.

MM Proposition I · no taxes \[V_L=V_U\]

In words: a levered firm L and an unlevered firm U with the same assets have the same value.

  • Debt and equity only slice the cash flow from assets; the slices always add up to the whole.
  • Issuing debt or equity is a zero-NPV deal: borrow $100 today, owe payments worth $100.
  • Investors can borrow and lend on their own, so the firm's leverage gives them nothing they can't do themselves (below).

Same value, same WACC

With perpetual cash flows, no taxes, no bankruptcy costs: \(V_U=\text{EBIT}/\text{WACC}_U\) and \(V_L=\text{EBIT}/\text{WACC}_L\). Same EBIT and \(V_L=V_U\) ⇒ \(\text{WACC}_L=\text{WACC}_U=R_A\).

In words: the WACC does not depend on capital structure; it equals \(R_A\), the required return on the firm's assets.

MM Proposition II · no taxes \[R_E=R_A+(R_A-R_D)\times\frac{D}{E}\]

In words: cost of equity = business risk (\(R_A\)) + financial risk (\((R_A-R_D)\,D/E\)). More debt makes the shareholders' claim riskier, so \(R_E\) rises in a straight line with \(D/E\).

Show the calculation: from WACC = R_A

\(R_A=\frac{E}{V}R_E+\frac{D}{V}R_D\). Multiply by \(V/E\), using \(V/E=1+D/E\):

\[\begin{aligned}R_E&=R_A\Big(1+\frac{D}{E}\Big)-R_D\frac{D}{E}\\&=R_A+(R_A-R_D)\frac{D}{E}\end{aligned}\]
Reading the formula
\(R_A\)
the return the assets themselves require: business risk, the risk of the whole pie.
\(R_A-R_D\)
how much more the assets are expected to earn than lenders are paid. On borrowed money, shareholders keep this gap.
\(\dfrac{D}{E}\)
dollars of debt per dollar of equity: the size of the mortgage compared with the deposit.
\((R_A-R_D)\dfrac{D}{E}\)
financial risk: the extra return shareholders require because their slice now takes the swings of the whole pie.
The same in CAPM betas \[\beta_E=\beta_A+(\beta_A-\beta_D)\times\frac{D}{E}\]

Investment-grade debt has \(\beta_D\approx0\), giving \(\beta_E=\beta_A(1+D/E)\). \(\beta_A\) = systematic risk of the assets (business risk); \(D/E\) adds financial risk.

Try it · two effects offset (no taxes) or don't quite (taxes)

Orange dashed: \(R_E\). Blue: WACC. Grey dash-dot: cost of debt (after tax in case II). More debt puts more weight on cheap debt but raises \(R_E\). Case I: exactly offsetting, WACC flat at 10%. Case II: \(R_E\) rises more slowly and the WACC falls.

Levered cost of equity, step by step (as in the example below):

  1. Do: find \(R_A\): given, or by the CAPM with the asset beta \(\beta_A\). This is the risk of the business itself, which no financing choice changes.
  2. Do: find \(R_D\) (riskless debt: \(R_D=R_f\)). Lenders are paid first, so they bear little or none of the swings.
  3. Do: find \(E\) at market value, e.g. \(E=V-D\) with \(V\) = perpetual asset cash flow ÷ \(R_A\). \(D/E\) must compare what the two claims are worth today, as in the WACC.
  4. Do: \(R_E=R_A+(R_A-R_D)\,D/E\). Business risk plus the extra risk of holding the slice that takes all the swings.
  5. Do: check with betas: \(\beta_E=\beta_A(1+D/E)\) when \(\beta_D=0\), then the CAPM. The CAPM and MM II describe the same risk, so both routes must agree.
Example · cost of equity under MM

Assets earn an expected $100 a year forever, \(\beta_A=1.0\); $200 of riskless debt; \(R_f=4\%\), market risk premium 6%.

\[\begin{aligned}R_A&=4\%+1.0\times6\%=10\%\\R_D&=4\%+0\times6\%=4\%\\E&=\tfrac{100}{0.10}-200=800\\R_E&=10\%+\tfrac{200}{800}(10\%-4\%)\\&=11.5\%\end{aligned}\]

Check by beta: \(\beta_E=1.0(1+0.25)=1.25\), \(4\%+1.25\times6\%=11.5\%\). ✓

The pie and the lesson

Firm U: equity $8m. Firm L: debt $4m + equity $4m. Same assets, same $8m pie, however it is cut.

MM assumes perfect capital markets, no taxes, no bankruptcy costs; none holds exactly. The lesson: financing only slices the pie, so capital structure matters only through what MM leaves out. The next two sections add those things back.

3MM with corporate taxes

Interest is tax-deductible, so debt shrinks the government's slice and grows the investors'.

Debt (loans, bonds)Equity (shares)
A legal claim, no votesOwnership, with votes
Interest is tax-deductibleDividends are not deductible
Unpaid debt → bankruptcyNo promised payments

Case II: add corporate taxes, still no bankruptcy costs.

What is this? (interest tax shield)

Now the pie has a third slice: the government's tax. Interest is paid before tax is worked out, so a firm with debt reports a smaller taxable profit and hands the government less. Each year it saves interest × tax rate. The saving is not created from nothing: it is cash moved from the government's slice to the investors' slices. For debt kept in place forever the saving is a perpetuity, and its present value works out to simply tax rate × debt.

Interest tax shield \[\text{tax shield per year}=R_D\times D\times T_C\]

With a fixed amount of debt forever (perpetual or always rolled over), the shield lasts forever and is as risky as the debt, so discount it at \(R_D\):

\[\text{PV(tax shield)}=\frac{R_D\,D\,T_C}{R_D}=T_C\times D\]

In words: each year the firm pays less tax by interest × tax rate; the PV of that saving forever is simply tax rate × debt.

Example · $20 million perpetual debt at 6%, T_C = 40%

Interest \(=6\%\times20=\$1.2\)m a year; shield \(=1.2\times40\%=\$0.48\)m a year; PV \(=0.48/0.06=40\%\times20=\$8\)m.

MM Proposition I · with taxes \[V_L=V_U+T_C\times D\]

In words: firm value rises by the PV of the interest tax shield, $1 of debt adds \(T_C\) dollars. In the running example (\(V_U=\$72\)m), \(D=\$20\)m gives \(V_L=\$80\)m; at \(D=\$100\)m, \(\$112\)m.

Reading the formula
\(V_U\)
the investors' share of the pie with no debt.
\(T_C\times D\)
the PV of the interest tax shield: the part of the government's slice that debt moves to investors.
\(V_L\)
the levered firm's value: bigger because the government's slice is smaller, not because the pie grew.

WACC with taxes

Perpetual cash flows, no bankruptcy costs: \(V_U=\text{EBIT}(1-T_C)/\text{WACC}_U\) and \(V_L=\text{EBIT}(1-T_C)/\text{WACC}_L\). With \(V_L=V_U+T_C D\):

\[\begin{aligned}\text{WACC}_L&=\frac{V_U}{V_L}\,\text{WACC}_U\\&=\text{WACC}_U-\frac{D}{V_L}\,\text{WACC}_U\,T_C\end{aligned}\]

In words: the WACC falls as debt rises. \(\text{WACC}_U=R_U\), the unlevered firm's cost of equity.

MM Proposition II · with taxes \[\text{WACC}_L=\frac{E}{V_L}R_E+\frac{D}{V_L}R_D(1-T_C)\]

Set this equal to \(R_U-\frac{D}{V_L}R_U T_C\) and solve for \(R_E\):

\[R_E=R_U+(R_U-R_D)(1-T_C)\frac{D}{E}\]

In words: \(R_E\) still rises with \(D/E\), but more slowly (slope × \((1-T_C)\)): the tax shield cushions shareholders. See the chart in section 2, case II.

Show the calculation: solving for R_E

Multiply both expressions for \(\text{WACC}_L\) by \(V_L/E\), using \(V_L/E=1+D/E\):

\[\begin{aligned}R_E&+R_D(1-T_C)\tfrac{D}{E}\\&=R_U\big(1+\tfrac{D}{E}\big)-R_U T_C\tfrac{D}{E}\end{aligned}\]

Move the \(R_D\) term across and group the \(D/E\) terms: \(R_E=R_U+(R_U-R_D)(1-T_C)\frac{D}{E}\).

Reading the formula
\(R_U\)
business risk, as before: the all-equity firm's cost of equity.
\((R_U-R_D)\dfrac{D}{E}\)
financial risk from the mortgage-like debt, as without taxes.
\((1-T_C)\)
the cushion: the tax shield is worth \(T_C D\) and is as risky as the debt, so it offsets part of the debt. Shareholders' slice is less geared than \(D/E\) suggests, and \(R_E\) rises more slowly.
Example · an all-equity firm borrows to buy back shares

EBIT $12m a year forever, no depreciation; \(R_U=10\%\), \(T_C=40\%\), 1 million shares. Plan: issue $20m perpetual debt at 6%, use it to buy back shares.

Before:

\[\begin{aligned}V_U&=\tfrac{12(1-0.40)}{0.10}=\$72\text{m}\\P_0&=72\text{m}/1\text{m}=\$72\end{aligned}\]

After: \(V_L=72+20\times0.40=\$80\)m; \(E=80-20=\$60\)m.

Price \(P\) after the announcement, shares bought back \(N\): buyback \(N\times P=20\)m; remaining equity \((1\text{m}-N)P=60\)m. Adding: \(1\text{m}\times P=80\)m, so \(P=\$80\) and \(N=20\text{m}/80=250{,}000\). The whole $8m gain goes to shareholders when the plan is announced, before the buyback.

Cost of equity and WACC:

\[\begin{aligned}R_E&=10\%+\tfrac{20}{60}(10\%-6\%)(0.6)\\&=10.8\%\\\text{WACC}_L&=\tfrac{60}{80}(10.8\%)+\tfrac{20}{80}(6\%)(0.6)\\&=8.1\%+0.9\%=9.0\%\end{aligned}\]

Check from value: \(\text{WACC}_L=12\times0.6/80=9.0\%\). ✓ (On the chart: \(D/E=1/3\).)

4Financial distress and the static theory

Debt saves tax but raises expected distress costs. Borrow until the two balance at the margin.

Bankruptcy: the assets are worth no more than the debt, so equity is worth zero and control passes from shareholders to lenders. In a perfect world nothing is lost (lenders take assets worth what they were). In practice distress destroys value:

  • Direct costs: lawyers, accountants, courts. Lehman Brothers (bankrupt 2008): almost $6 billion, ~1% of total assets, about half professional and consulting fees. Large in dollars, small relative to a large firm; a bigger share of value for small firms.
  • Indirect costs: customers and suppliers walk away, managers spend time fighting bankruptcy, good projects are passed up to save cash. China Evergrande (HK-listed, in distress from 2021): sales fell from RMB 723 billion (2020) to RMB 32 billion (2022); suppliers were paid in unfinished flats.
Expected distress cost \[\begin{aligned}\text{expected cost}={}&\text{prob. of distress}\\&\times\text{cost if distressed}\end{aligned}\]

In words: more debt raises both factors. Investors price the expected cost in today, so it lowers firm value now, before any distress happens.

Static (trade-off) theory · Case III

Benefit of debt: the interest tax shield. Cost: expected distress costs. Borrow until the last dollar of debt adds as much tax shield as distress cost; that debt level maximises firm value.

\[V_L=V_U+T_C D-\text{PV(distress costs)}\]

Who should borrow less?

  • Little taxable profit (past losses, tax holidays): less tax to shield.
  • Volatile earnings: distress more likely.
  • Intangible value (brands, research, growth options): little left in distress.

5Pecking order

Outside money is costly, so firms use internal funds first, then debt, and new equity only as a last resort.

Why outside money costs more: flotation costs (fees for issuing securities, L10) and outside investors know less than managers (so they suspect a firm that sells shares thinks they are overpriced).

The order · move down only when the step before runs out

① Internal funds (retained earnings) → not enough? → ② Debt (bank loans, bonds) → not enough? → ③ New equity, a last resort.

Implications: no target debt-equity ratio (debt depends on how much internal cash there is); profitable firms borrow less (more internal cash, the opposite of what the tax-shield logic suggests); firms build up financial slack (cash saved to move fast on good projects).

What CFOs say (392 CFOs, 1999; % rating a factor important): financial flexibility 59.4%, credit rating 57.1%, earnings and cash-flow volatility 48.1%, interest tax deduction 44.9%, costs of issuing debt 33.5%, other firms' debt levels 23.4%, bankruptcy or distress costs 21.4%. Flexibility first, distress costs far down.

6Summary and formula card

  • Case I (MM, no taxes): financing only slices the pie; \(V_L=V_U\), WACC \(=R_A\); \(R_E\) rises with \(D/E\).
  • Case II (MM, taxes): debt's tax shield adds \(T_C D\); WACC falls with debt.
  • Case III (static theory): borrow until the marginal tax gain = marginal expected distress cost.
  • Pecking order: internal funds, then debt, then equity; no target ratio.

7Common mistakes

  • “Debt is cheaper, so borrowing lowers the WACC.” Without taxes, \(R_E\) rises exactly enough to offset it.
  • Using D/V instead of D/E in Proposition II. Weights in the WACC are \(D/V\); the slope term is \(D/E\).
  • Dropping \((1-T_C)\) in Proposition II with taxes, or in the debt term of the WACC.
  • Buyback at the old price. The price jumps to \(V_L/\text{shares}\) at the announcement; the buyback happens at the new price.
  • PV of shield = \(T_C D\) for any debt. Only for a fixed amount of debt forever.
  • Taking Case II literally (“100% debt”). Distress costs (Case III) and the pecking order explain why firms don't.
