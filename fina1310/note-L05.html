FINA1310 · Corporate Finance · Lecture 5

Stock valuation

What a share gives you, where it trades, and what it is worth: the present value of its expected dividends, with shortcuts for zero, constant and nonconstant growth, plus the quick alternative of pricing it like its peers.

0Where this sits

L4 valued a bond: promised cash flows, known end date. A share is the same calculation with dividends that are expected, not promised, and that run forever. Textbook: Chapter 8.

Recap from L3 · the two formulas this lecture reuses \[\text{Perpetuity: }PV=\frac{C}{r}\]\[\text{Growing perpetuity: }PV=\frac{C}{r-g}\]

In words: \(C\) is the first cash flow, one period from now; \(g\lt r\). See L3 §3 and L3 §4.

Sign convention

\(t=0\) is today; \(D_t\) = dividend per share expected at the end of year \(t\); \(P_t\) = share price at \(t\) (just after that year's dividend unless stated). \(R\) = required return a year, \(g\) = dividend growth a year.

1Stock features

Equity is ownership: votes, discretionary dividends, and whatever is left after everyone else is paid.

Debt (loans, bonds)Equity (shares)
Ownershipno; creditors do not voteyes; common shareholders elect the board
Paymentsinterest: a cost, tax-deductibledividends: paid from after-tax profit
Obligationlegal; a missed payment is defaultnone; a firm cannot default on a dividend
Risk to the firmtoo much can cause financial distressan all-equity firm cannot go bankrupt from its financing

Common stock

  • Votes: elect the board, approve major decisions (mergers, issuing new shares), in person or by proxy (someone you authorise to vote for you).
  • Dividends: the board decides when and how much, in cash or in new shares.
  • Residual claim: in liquidation (the firm is wound up and its assets sold), shareholders are paid last: (1) secured creditors, from their collateral; (2) priority claims such as wages and taxes; (3) unsecured creditors; (4) shareholders, preferred then common, from what is left.
  • Dividend tax varies by place; Hong Kong does not tax dividends received by individuals.
One share, one vote? Not always

Dual-class shares: founders' shares carry more votes each than public shares. HKEX has allowed these weighted voting rights since 2018 (Xiaomi and Meituan among the first; founders' shares carry 10 votes, others 1). In the US, Alphabet's and Meta's founders control the vote with a minority of the shares. Trade-off: founders can pursue long-term plans, but outside shareholders cannot replace them.

Preferred stock: between debt and equity

  • A fixed dividend, paid before any common dividend; often cumulative: missed dividends pile up and must be paid before common shareholders get anything.
  • Paid before common shareholders in liquidation, up to a stated value. Usually no vote.
  • Unlike interest, a skipped preferred dividend is not a default.
Example · preferred stock is a perpetuity

$5 a year forever, 8% required return: \(P=5/0.08=\$62.50\).

2Stock markets

  • Primary market: the firm sells new shares and receives the money. An IPO (initial public offering) is a private firm's first sale to the public; an SEO (seasoned equity offering) is more shares from a firm already listed. HKEX raised the most IPO money of any exchange in 2025, about HK$275 billion.
  • Secondary market: investors trade existing shares with each other; the firm receives nothing. It still matters to the firm: its prices reveal investors' required return, and it lets IPO buyers sell later.

How shares trade in Hong Kong
  • HKEX is order-driven: buy and sell orders meet on an electronic order book. Investors trade through brokers; most stocks have no designated dealer.
  • Shares trade in board lots set by each company (e.g. 100 or 500 shares).
  • Trades settle two business days later (T+2).
  • Compare: NYSE is an auction market with one designated market maker per stock; Nasdaq has many competing dealers.

3The general case: price = PV of dividends

Same rule as a bond, different cash flows: price = PV of what you expect to receive.

Bond vs stock: the cash flows you discount

Bond: coupons C, plus face value F at maturity t

Stock: expected dividends, forever

BondStock
Cash flowscoupons and face value, promised by contractdividends, expected but not promised
Timinguntil maturityforever
Discount ratethe yield, set by the marketthe required return \(R\), set by the market

Why the selling price drops out

Hold for one year: you get \(D_1\) and sell at \(P_1\), so \(P_0=\dfrac{D_1+P_1}{1+R}\). The buyer at \(t=1\) values the share the same way, \(P_1=\dfrac{D_2+P_2}{1+R}\). Substitute:

\[P_0=\frac{D_1}{1+R}+\frac{D_2+P_2}{(1+R)^2}\]

Keep substituting. A price far in the future is divided by a huge \((1+R)^t\), so its weight goes to zero and only dividends are left. Your holding period does not matter: a one-year holder and a buy-and-hold investor pay the same price.

Result · dividend discount model \[\begin{aligned}P_0={}&\frac{D_1}{1+R}+\frac{D_2}{(1+R)^2}\\&+\frac{D_3}{(1+R)^3}+\cdots\end{aligned}\]

In words: a share is worth the present value of all its expected dividends, discounted at the required return.

Firms that pay no dividends are not worth zero
  • Growth stocks reinvest their earnings instead of paying them out. Their value is in dividends expected later, from a bigger firm.
  • Amazon has never paid a dividend since its 1997 IPO. Microsoft listed in 1986, first dividend 2003. Meta listed in 2012, first dividend 2024.
  • Other ways to pay shareholders: share buybacks (the firm buys its own shares), or a takeover at a premium.

Infinitely many dividends cannot be forecast one by one. Three patterns make the sum easy: zero growth (a perpetuity), constant growth (a growing perpetuity), and nonconstant growth (anything for a while, then constant growth: the two combined).

4Zero and constant growth

Zero growth: a perpetuity

Result · zero growth\[P_0=\frac{D}{R}\]

In words: the same dividend \(D\) forever, starting next year, is worth \(D/R\) today.

Cum-dividend and ex-dividend prices

\(D/R\) is the price just after a dividend is paid (ex-dividend). Just before it is paid, the price also includes that dividend (cum-dividend, “with dividend”).

Example · $2 a year forever, R = 20%

Ex-dividend: \(2/0.20=\$10\). Cum-dividend: \(P_{\text{cum}}=2+10=\$12\), which also equals \(10\times1.20\): a year's return on the $10.

The price path of the $2 share: a saw-tooth

Between dividends the price grows at \(R=20\%\) from $10 to $12; on each dividend date it drops by the $2 paid out. A holder earns 20% a year either way: price gain, then cash.

Ex-dividend date: who gets the dividend?
  • The board declares a dividend and a record date: holders on the register that day are paid.
  • Buy before the ex-dividend date and you get the dividend; buy on or after it and the seller does. In Hong Kong, with T+2 settlement, the ex-date is one business day before the record date.
  • CLP Holdings, Sep 2026: HK$0.63 a share; ex-date 2 Sep, record date 3 Sep, paid 15 Sep.
  • On the ex-date the price drops by roughly the dividend.

Constant growth: the Gordon model

What is this? (the dividend growth model)

Picture an orchard whose harvest grows by the same percentage every year, forever. Its value today is every future harvest, each discounted back. Growth makes each harvest bigger; discounting makes it count for less today. As long as the required return beats the growth rate, each later harvest is worth a little less today than the one before, so the total is finite. The closer growth gets to the required return, the more slowly far-off harvests fade, and the higher the price.

Result · dividend growth (Gordon) model, need g < R \[P_0=\frac{D_1}{R-g}=\frac{D_0(1+g)}{R-g}\]

In words: next year's dividend divided by (required return − growth). \(D_0\) is the dividend just paid; the formula needs the next one, \(D_1\).

Reading the formula
\(D_1\)
next year's dividend: the first one a buyer today will receive
\(D_0(1+g)\)
the same \(D_1\), built from the dividend just paid
\(R\)
the return shareholders require, set by the market
\(R-g\)
the gap between discounting and growth; a smaller gap means far-off dividends fade more slowly and the price is higher
Example · Gordon Growth Co.

Next year's dividend $4, \(R=16\%\), \(g=6\%\) forever. Price today, and in four years?

  • Today: \(P_0=\dfrac{4}{0.16-0.06}=\$40\).
  • In four years the next dividend is \(D_5=4\times1.06^4=5.05\), so \(P_4=\dfrac{5.05}{0.10}=\$50.50\).
  • Same answer from \(P_4=40\times1.06^4=\$50.50\).
Result · the price grows at g\[P_t=P_0(1+g)^t\]

In words: every dividend is \((1+g)\) times the one before, so the price one year on is the same formula with a dividend \((1+g)\) times bigger.

Why g must be below R

The PV of the dividend at \(t\) is \(D_0\left(\dfrac{1+g}{1+R}\right)^t\).

  • \(g\lt R\): the terms shrink towards zero and the sum is \(D_1/(R-g)\).
  • \(g\ge R\): the terms never shrink, the sum is infinite, and the formula gives nonsense (a negative or infinite price).
  • No firm can grow faster than \(R\) forever; growth above \(R\) is only ever temporary. That is the next section.

You can now: price a zero-growth share as a perpetuity; tell cum-dividend from ex-dividend prices; price a constant-growth share with the Gordon model, using \(D_1\) not \(D_0\); explain why \(g\) must be below \(R\).

5Nonconstant growth

Value the early dividends one by one; value everything from \(T+1\) on as a single Gordon price \(P_T\); discount both to today.

Result · nonconstant growth \[\begin{aligned}P_0={}&\frac{D_1}{1+R}+\cdots+\frac{D_T}{(1+R)^T}\\&+\frac{P_T}{(1+R)^T}\end{aligned}\] \[P_T=\frac{D_{T+1}}{R-g}\]

In words: \(P_T\) sits at \(T\), one period before the first growing dividend, so it is discounted \(T\) periods, the same as \(D_T\).

  1. Do: List the forecast dividends \(D_1,\dots,D_T\) one by one, up to the date \(T\) after which growth is constant. Before \(T\) the dividends follow no pattern, so each must be valued on its own.
  2. Do: Find the first dividend of the constant-growth phase, \(D_{T+1}\), and price the rest with Gordon: \(P_T=D_{T+1}/(R-g)\). From \(T+1\) on the dividends are a growing perpetuity, and its value lands one period before its first payment, at \(T\).
  3. Do: Add \(P_T\) to \(D_T\), since both sit at date \(T\). They are at the same date, so they can be added like any two amounts.
  4. Do: Discount every amount to today at \(R\) and add. Today's price is the PV of everything the share will pay, early dividends and later ones alike.
Example · three forecast dividends, then 5% growth

Analysts forecast $1.00, $2.00 and $2.50 at the end of years 1–3; after that the dividend grows 5% a year forever. \(R=10\%\).

Step 1, price at \(T=3\): \(P_3=\dfrac{D_4}{R-g}=\dfrac{2.50\times1.05}{0.05}=\$52.50\).

Step 2, discount to today. At \(t=3\) you receive \(D_3+P_3=2.50+52.50=\$55.00\):

Where the $43.88 comes from

PV of each piece. The discounted price \(P_3\) is $39.44, 90% of the value: even with explicit forecasts, the long-run growth assumption dominates.

Example · dividends skipped, growth carries on (Tutorial 4)

SQ just paid \(D_0=\$3\); dividends grow 5% forever; \(R=8\%\). Normally \(P_0=3.15/0.03=\$105\). Now SQ pays nothing in years 1 and 2, but the growth still applies to the skipped years, so \(D_3=3\times1.05^3=3.473\).

\[\begin{aligned}P_2&=\tfrac{D_3}{R-g}=\tfrac{3.473}{0.03}=\$115.76\\P_0&=\tfrac{115.76}{1.08^2}=\boxed{\$99.25}\end{aligned}\]

The Gordon price lands one period before its first dividend: at \(t=2\), not \(t=3\). (The tutorial slide prints $77.89; \(115.76/1.08^2\) is $99.25.)

Two-stage growth

Growth \(g_1\) until \(T\), then \(g_2\) forever. The first stage is a growing annuity (L3), the second a growing perpetuity:

\[\begin{aligned}P_0={}&D_1\,\frac{1-\left(\frac{1+g_1}{1+R}\right)^T}{R-g_1}\\&+\frac{P_T}{(1+R)^T}\end{aligned}\] \[P_T=\frac{D_T(1+g_2)}{R-g_2}\]

In words: growing-annuity PV of the first \(T\) dividends, plus the discounted Gordon price at \(T\). Here \(g_1\) may exceed \(R\) (the annuity has only \(T\) terms); only \(g_2\) must be below \(R\).

Why nonconstant growth is the realistic case

  • Growth stage: many investment opportunities, rapid expansion; \(g\) may exceed \(R\), dividends may be zero.
  • Transition stage: competition arrives; growth and margins fall.
  • Mature stage: growth settles below \(R\) and the growth model applies.

Valuing a young firm therefore means forecasting when it reaches the mature stage.

You can now: value a share whose dividends grow unevenly at first and then settle; place the Gordon price at the right date; handle skipped dividends and two-stage growth.

6Required return

Result · solve the Gordon model for R \[R=\frac{D_1}{P_0}+g\]

In words: required return = dividend yield \(D_1/P_0\) (the cash part) + capital-gain yield \(g\) (the price part, since the price grows at \(g\)).

Gordon Growth Co.: \(4/40+6\%=10\%+6\%=16\%\). The same split as a bond's current yield plus capital-gain yield (L4).

Hong Kong · CLP Holdings, a slow-growing utility
  • Dividends per share: HK$3.10 for 2023, 3.15 for 2024, 3.20 for 2025: about 1.6% growth a year.
  • Price HK$76.25 (25 Sep 2026); dividend yield \(3.20/76.25=4.2\%\).
  • If the dividend keeps growing at 1.6%, next year's is \(3.20\times1.016\approx3.25\): \(R\approx3.25/76.25+1.6\%\approx5.9\%\).
  • A low \(R\) for a stable, regulated business; most of the return comes as dividends.

The 4.2% uses the dividend just paid (HK$3.20, a “trailing” yield); the \(R\) estimate correctly uses next year's HK$3.25 (4.3% yield). The slide's numbers are kept.

The slide's question, a young tech firm: the opposite split. A tiny or zero dividend yield, so almost all of \(R\) is expected capital gain.

7Peer multiples

Skip the dividend forecast: price the share like similar firms.

Definition · P/E ratio

\(P/E=\) price per share ÷ earnings per share (EPS = net income ÷ number of shares). Estimated price = peers' average P/E × the firm's EPS.

Peers trade at 15 times earnings and the firm expects EPS of $2: price \(\approx15\times2=\$30\).

  • A high P/E means investors expect fast growth, or the share is overpriced.
  • P/E is the most widely quoted multiple and the quickest valuation.
  • When earnings do not work: price-to-sales for young firms with small or negative earnings; price-to-cash-flow, less sensitive to accounting choices and useful across markets. Same recipe: peers' average multiple × the firm's own figure.
  • Limits: good peers are hard to find; peers' multiples vary widely; a multiple is relative, so if the whole sector is overpriced, so is your estimate.
Beyond the handouts · why P/E reflects growth

If a firm pays out a fraction \(b\) of next year's earnings \(E_1\), Gordon gives \(P_0/E_1=b/(R-g)\): higher expected \(g\) means a higher P/E.

8Summary and formula card

  • Debt vs equity: votes, dividends, residual claim. Preferred stock sits between debt and common stock. Primary vs secondary markets.
  • Price = PV of all expected dividends; the selling price drops out.
  • Zero growth \(D/R\); constant growth \(D_1/(R-g)\) with \(g\lt R\), and the price grows at \(g\).
  • Nonconstant growth: discount the early dividends, plus the discounted \(P_T\).
  • \(R=D_1/P_0+g\): dividend yield + capital-gain yield.
  • Multiples: price relative to peers (P/E, P/S, P/CF).

9Common mistakes

  • Just paid vs next dividend. \(D_1/(R-g)\) needs the next dividend; multiply a just-paid \(D_0\) by \((1+g)\).
  • Discounting \(P_T\) one period too many. \(P_T=D_{T+1}/(R-g)\) sits at \(T\): divide by \((1+R)^T\), not \((1+R)^{T+1}\).
  • Using \(P_T=D_T/(R-g)\). The Gordon price at \(T\) needs the dividend at \(T+1\).
  • Gordon with \(g\ge R\). No finite value; use nonconstant growth with a lower long-run \(g\).
  • Valuing a non-payer at zero. Its value is the PV of later dividends.
  • Cum vs ex. Just before a dividend, the price includes it.

Lecture quiza share that pays no dividend

Amazon has never paid a dividend since its 1997 IPO, yet it is one of the most valuable firms in the world. A share of a firm like this is worth: A. nothing: no cash flows, no value · B. the PV of the dividends it is expected to pay one day · C. whatever the next buyer pays: price is set by resale · D. the accounting value of its assets per share

Hint

What is the next buyer's price based on?

Answer

B. Resale price (C) is itself the PV of the dividends after you sell, so it drops out (section 3). D ignores future growth.

Lecture quiza constant $2 dividend

A firm pays $2 a share at the end of every year, forever; \(R=20\%\). What is a share worth (A) just after this year's dividend, (B) just before it, (C) in four years, just after that year's dividend?

Hint

Zero growth: the price just after a dividend is always \(D/R\).

Answer

(A) \(2/0.20=\boxed{\$10}\) · (B) \(2+10=\boxed{\$12}\) · (C) \(\boxed{\$10}\): with no growth, the ex-dividend price never changes.

Lecture quizthe $4 dividend was just paid

Gordon Growth Co., but the $4 dividend was just paid; \(R=16\%\), \(g=6\%\). Price today? A. $37.74 · B. $40.00 · C. $42.40 · D. $50.50

Hint

Find \(D_1\) first.

Answer
\[\frac{4\times1.06}{0.10}=\frac{4.24}{0.10}=\boxed{\$42.40}\]

→ C. ($40 forgets the growth; $37.74 \(=40/1.06\) grows the wrong way.)

Lecture quizsupernormal growth

Dividends grow 30% a year for the next three years, then 10% forever. The firm has just paid total dividends of $5 million; \(R=20\%\). Equity value? A. $55.00m · B. $69.93m · C. $87.57m · D. $120.84m

Hint

\(D_1,D_2,D_3\) at 30%; \(P_3=D_4/(R-g)\) with \(g=10\%\).

Answer

Dividends ($m): 6.50, 8.45, 10.985. Then

\[\begin{aligned}P_3&=\tfrac{10.985\times1.10}{0.20-0.10}=120.84\\P_0&=\tfrac{6.50}{1.20}+\tfrac{8.45}{1.20^2}+\tfrac{10.985+120.84}{1.20^3}\\&=5.42+5.87+76.28\\&=\boxed{\$87.57\text{m}}\end{aligned}\]

→ C. Two-stage formula check: growing-annuity part \(17.64\) plus \(120.84/1.20^3=69.93\). B is that second piece alone (dividends forgotten); D is \(P_3\) undiscounted.

Lecture quizfirst dividend in five years

No dividends now. The first, $0.50 a share, is expected in five years; then 10% growth forever; \(R=20\%\). Price today? A. $0 · B. $2.01 · C. $2.41 · D. $5.00

Hint

\(D_5/(R-g)\) is a price at \(t=4\).

Answer
\[\begin{aligned}P_4&=\tfrac{0.50}{0.20-0.10}=5.00\\P_0&=\tfrac{5.00}{1.20^4}=\boxed{\$2.41}\end{aligned}\]

→ C. B discounts five years (one too many); D forgets to discount.

Lecture quizrequired return

A share sells for $20. The next dividend, $1, will grow 10% a year forever. Required return? A. 5.0% · B. 10.0% · C. 15.0% · D. 15.5%

Answer

\(1/20+10\%=5\%+10\%=\boxed{15.0\%}\) → C. (15.5% wrongly grows the $1, which is already the next dividend.)

Lecture quizimplied growth

A share sells for $25. The next dividend is $2; \(R=20\%\). What constant growth does the price imply? A. 8% · B. 12% · C. 20% · D. 28%

Hint

\(g=R-D_1/P_0\).

Answer

\(20\%-2/25=20\%-8\%=\boxed{12\%}\) → B.

Tutorial 4the next dividend is paid this afternoon

A preferred share pays $50 a year forever; required return 6.5%. Price today if the next dividend is a year away? If it is paid this afternoon?

Answer
