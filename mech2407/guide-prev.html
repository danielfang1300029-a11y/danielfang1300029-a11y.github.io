C1.5Lagrange multipliers: largest solid inside an ellipsoid

2 parts · 2 May sittings (May 2026, May 2022) · 25 marks · never in a December paper · Learn it · note C1 §8

Decision procedure
  1. Draw the solid and name it by symmetry. Box centred at the origin with a corner \((x,y,z)\) on the surface → sides \(2x,2y,2z\), \(V=8xyz\). Cylinder about the \(z\)-axis → radius \(r\), from \(z=-h\) to \(z=h\), \(V=2\pi r^2h\).
  2. Write the constraint \(g=c\): the touching point lies on the surface. If the horizontal cross-section is an ellipse but the cylinder is circular, the circle touches first in the narrow direction (the larger coefficient), so use that one.
  3. Write \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\), one equation per variable, plus \(g=c\).
  4. Eliminate \(\lambda\): for a box multiply each equation by its own variable (every left side becomes \(8xyz\)); for a cylinder divide one equation by the other. Discard \(\lambda=0\) or a zero side (volume 0).
  5. Substitute into the constraint to get the point, then \(\lambda\) if asked.
  6. Say why it is a maximum: \(V=0\) whenever a dimension is 0, \(V\gt0\) at the only critical point, and the constraint set is closed and bounded.
  7. State the full dimensions (\(2x\), \(2y\), \(2z\); diameter \(2r\), height \(2h\)) and the volume.

Cues: “maximise”, “largest box / cylinder that fits inside”, an ellipsoid equation, a company name and a product (gift box in an egg, figurine in a snow globe).

Traps:

  • Giving half-sides \(x,y,z\) as the box dimensions.
  • For a circular cylinder in an elliptical globe, using the wide direction: the cylinder then pokes out.
  • Leaving \(\lambda\) in the answer: it is a helper and must be eliminated (or evaluated).
  • Writing \(V=\pi r^2h\) when \(h\) is the half-height.
Ready answer · largest box and cylinder in an ellipsoid

Ellipsoid \(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}+\dfrac{z^2}{c^2}=1\). Largest box (edges parallel to the axes):

\[\begin{aligned}&\text{corner }\Big(\frac a{\sqrt3},\frac b{\sqrt3},\frac c{\sqrt3}\Big),\\ &V_{\max}=\frac{8abc}{3\sqrt3}.\end{aligned}\]

Largest circular cylinder about the \(z\)-axis (with \(a\le b\), so \(x\) is the narrow direction):

\[\begin{aligned}r&=a\sqrt{\tfrac23},\qquad h=\frac c{\sqrt3},\\ V_{\max}&=2\pi r^2h=\frac{4\pi a^2c}{3\sqrt3}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the best box puts its corner at \(\frac1{\sqrt3}\) of the way along each semi-axis; the best cylinder reaches \(\sqrt{2/3}\) of the narrow semi-axis sideways and \(\frac1{\sqrt3}\) of the height semi-axis up and down. Quote these only to check; the marks are for the Lagrange working.

Past-paper parts (May format, newest first)

May 2026 Q215 marksMay format

ABC Design House is creating a decorative snow globe. A figurine is enclosed by a circular cylinder, and this cylinder is placed inside a hollow snow globe. The inner surface of the snow globe is modelled by \(9x^2+4y^2+z^2=36\). To maximise the space for the figurine, the designer wants the cylinder to be symmetric about the \(z\)-axis. Determine the dimensions and maximum volume of the cylinder that can fit entirely inside the globe. You may leave your answers in terms of \(\lambda\).

Reading used: a circular cylinder of radius \(r\) from \(z=-h\) to \(z=h\). The globe's horizontal sections are ellipses, narrower in \(x\) (semi-axis 2) than in \(y\) (semi-axis 3), so the rim touches the globe on the \(x\)-axis: constraint \(9r^2+h^2=36\). “In terms of \(\lambda\)” is odd wording (\(\lambda\) is the multiplier, normally eliminated): the exact answer is given, and the \(\lambda\) form at the end.

Hint

Maximise \(V=2\pi r^2h\) subject to \(g=9r^2+h^2=36\) (the rim point \((r,0,h)\) on the globe). Two Lagrange equations; divide to eliminate \(\lambda\).

Solution
  1. Globe: \(\frac{x^2}4+\frac{y^2}9+\frac{z^2}{36}=1\), semi-axes 2, 3, 6. The top rim \((r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,h)\) needs \(9r^2\cos^2\theta+4r^2\sin^2\theta+h^2\le36\); the worst case is \(\theta=0\): \(g=9r^2+h^2=36\).
  2. \(f=V=2\pi r^2h\). \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\): \[\begin{aligned}4\pi rh&=18\lambda r,\\ 2\pi r^2&=2\lambda h.\end{aligned}\]
  3. \(r\ne0\): first equation gives \(\lambda=\frac{2\pi h}9\). Second: \(2\pi r^2=\frac{4\pi h^2}9\Rightarrow r^2=\frac{2h^2}9\).
  4. Constraint: \(2h^2+h^2=36\Rightarrow h^2=12\), \(h=2\sqrt3\); \(r^2=\frac83\), \(r=\frac{2\sqrt6}3\approx1.63\); \(\lambda=\frac{4\sqrt3\pi}9\).
  5. Maximum: \(V=0\) at the ends of the constraint (\(r=0\) or \(h=0\)) and the only critical point has \(V\gt0\). On the \(y\)-axis the rim has room to spare: \(4r^2+h^2=\frac{68}3\lt36\).
  6. \(V=2\pi\cdot\frac83\cdot2\sqrt3=\frac{32\sqrt3\pi}3\).

Marks go to the constraint (with a reason for using the \(x\)-direction), the two Lagrange equations, eliminating \(\lambda\), and stating the full height \(2h\), not \(h\).

\[\begin{aligned}&\text{radius }r=\tfrac{2\sqrt6}3\approx1.63,\\ &\text{height }2h=4\sqrt3\approx6.93,\\ &V_{\max}=\tfrac{32\sqrt3\,\pi}{3}\approx58.0\end{aligned}\]

In terms of \(\lambda\) (if that is what was wanted): \(h=\frac{9\lambda}{2\pi}\), \(r=\frac{3\sqrt2\,\lambda}{2\pi}\), \(V=\frac{81\lambda^3}{2\pi^2}\), with \(\lambda=\frac{4\sqrt3\pi}9\). Ready answer with \(a=2\), \(c=6\): \(V=\frac{4\pi\cdot4\cdot6}{3\sqrt3}\) ✓.

May 2022 Q1(c)10 marksMay format

ABC Wonder egg, a competitor of Kinder Surprise egg, is planning to put a rectangular gift box inside a chocolate shelled ellipsoid. Your help is needed to maximise this box. For your information, the 3D ellipsoid is modelled by \(4x^2+4y^2+z^2=4\), with the thickness of chocolate shell coating negligible. Identify the dimension and volume of the required box.

Hint

Corner \((x,y,z)\) on the ellipsoid, \(V=8xyz\), \(g=4x^2+4y^2+z^2=4\). Multiply each Lagrange equation by its own variable.

Solution
  1. Box centred at the origin, edges parallel to the axes, corner \((x,y,z)\), \(x,y,z\gt0\): maximise \(V=8xyz\) subject to \(g=4x^2+4y^2+z^2=4\).
  2. \(\nabla V=\lambda\nabla g\): \[\begin{aligned}8yz&=8\lambda x,\\ 8xz&=8\lambda y,\\ 8xy&=2\lambda z.\end{aligned}\]
  3. Multiply by \(x\), \(y\), \(z\): each left side is \(8xyz\), so \(8\lambda x^2=8\lambda y^2=2\lambda z^2\). \(\lambda\ne0\) (else \(V=0\)): \(y^2=x^2\), \(z^2=4x^2\).
  4. Constraint: \(12x^2=4\Rightarrow x=y=\frac1{\sqrt3}\), \(z=\frac2{\sqrt3}\) (and \(\lambda=\frac2{\sqrt3}\)).
  5. Maximum: \(V=0\) when any side is 0, and this is the only critical point with \(x,y,z\gt0\).

“Dimension” means the full edges \(2x,2y,2z\): writing \(\frac1{\sqrt3}\times\frac1{\sqrt3}\times\frac2{\sqrt3}\) loses the mark.

\[\begin{aligned}&\text{box }\tfrac2{\sqrt3}\times\tfrac2{\sqrt3}\times\tfrac4{\sqrt3}\\ &V_{\max}=\tfrac{16}{3\sqrt3}=\tfrac{16\sqrt3}9\approx3.08\end{aligned}\]

Ready answer with \(a=b=1\), \(c=2\): \(\frac{8\cdot2}{3\sqrt3}\) ✓. Agrees with the note's exam taster.

C3c.6Green's theorem in the plane

6 parts · 6 May sittings (May 2025, 2019, 2018, 2017, 2016, 2015) · 40 marks · never in a December paper · Learn it · note C3c §8 Stokes · note §6

Type card · predictable · which form?
Looks like
“Evaluate \(\oint_CM\,dx+N\,dy\) with the help of Green's theorem”, “verify Green's theorem”, “the circulation of \(\vec F\) around …”, with \(C\) a circle, ellipse, half-disc or the edge of a region between two curves.
Which form?
  • Circulation, \(\oint M\,dx+N\,dy\) → tangential form: \(\iint_R(N_x-M_y)\,dA\).
  • Outward flux across \(C\), \(\oint M\,dy-N\,dx\) → normal form: \(\iint_R(M_x+N_y)\,dA\).
  • “Verify” → do both sides: the double integral, and the line integral piece by piece (each piece its own parametrisation and direction).
  • “With the help of Green” → the double integral only. Ugly terms (\(e^{\sin x}\) in \(M\), \(\sqrt{y^4+1}\) in \(N\)) vanish from \(N_x-M_y\).
  • Circle or ellipse with \(N_x-M_y\) constant → constant × area (\(\pi a^2\), \(\pi ab\)): the area shortcut \(\iint_R dA\).
Do
1. Read off \(M\), \(N\); compute \(N_x-M_y\) and simplify. 2. Sketch \(R\) and set limits (polar for discs; \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=br\sin\theta\), \(dA=b\,r\,dr\,d\theta\) for \(x^2+\frac{y^2}{b^2}\le1\)). 3. Integrate. 4. Verifying: parametrise each piece of \(C\) anticlockwise and add.
Watch
Orientation: anticlockwise (region on your left) unless told otherwise; say so when the question does not. An odd integrand over a symmetric region is 0: say why in one line.
Ready answer · Green's theorem (on the May 2015–2019 sheet, not since)

\(C\) a simple closed curve, anticlockwise, enclosing \(R\); \(M,N\) with continuous partial derivatives on \(R\):

\[\begin{aligned}\text{tangential: }&\oint_CM\,dx+N\,dy\\&=\iint_R\Big(\pd Nx-\pd My\Big)dx\,dy,\\ \text{normal: }&\oint_CM\,dy-N\,dx\\&=\iint_R\Big(\pd Mx+\pd Ny\Big)dx\,dy,\\ \text{area: }&\iint_RdA=\tfrac12\oint_C(x\,dy-y\,dx).\end{aligned}\]

Ellipse \(\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1\): area \(\pi ab\); \(x=a\cos t\), \(y=b\sin t\) on \(C\). Green = Stokes with \(\vb n=\kk\) (guide C3c.5).

In words: circulation round the edge of a flat region = the total 2D curl inside; outward flux across the edge = the total divergence inside.

May 2025 Q410 marksMay format

Determine the circulation of \(\vec F=(x^2y^2)\,\hat i-x^2\,\hat j\) around the left semi-circle bounded by \(x^2+y^2=1\) and the \(y\)-axis.

The direction is not stated: we take the standard counter-clockwise direction (region on the left). Clockwise gives the negative, \(-\frac43\).

Hint

Tangential Green with \(M=x^2y^2\), \(N=-x^2\) over the left half-disc: polar, \(\frac\pi2\le\theta\le\frac{3\pi}2\). One term of \(N_x-M_y\) is odd in \(y\).

Solution
  1. \(M=x^2y^2\), \(N=-x^2\); continuous derivatives everywhere. \(N_x-M_y=-2x-2x^2y\).
  2. \(R\): \(0\le r\le1\), \(\frac\pi2\le\theta\le\frac{3\pi}2\); \(C\) = arc from \((0,1)\) through \((-1,0)\) to \((0,-1)\), then up the \(y\)-axis.
  3. \(\iint_R-2x^2y\,dA=0\): \(R\) is symmetric about the \(x\)-axis and the integrand is odd in \(y\).
  4. \[\begin{aligned}\iint_R-2x\,dA&=\int_{\pi/2}^{3\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^1-2r^2\cos\theta\,dr\,d\theta\\&=-\tfrac23\big[\sin\theta\big]_{\pi/2}^{3\pi/2}=\tfrac43.\end{aligned}\]
  5. Check directly: on the \(y\)-axis \(M=N=0\); on the arc \(x=\cos t\), \(y=\sin t\), \(t:\frac\pi2\to\frac{3\pi}2\): \(\int(-\cos^2t\sin^3t-\cos^3t)\,dt=0+\frac43\).

State the orientation you assume and that \(\vec F\) has continuous partials (so Green applies).

May 2019 Q3(b)6 marksMay format

With the help of Green's Theorem, evaluate \(\displaystyle\oint_Cy(4x^2+y^2)\,dx+x(2x^2+3y^2)\,dy\) where \(C\) is the boundary of the ellipse \(x^2+\dfrac{y^2}4=1\).

Hint

\(N_x-M_y\) collapses to one term. Ellipse: \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=2r\sin\theta\), \(dA=2r\,dr\,d\theta\). Take \(C\) anticlockwise.

Solution
  1. \(M=4x^2y+y^3\), \(N=2x^3+3xy^2\): \(N_x=6x^2+3y^2\), \(M_y=4x^2+3y^2\), so \(N_x-M_y=2x^2\).
  2. \(C\) anticlockwise; \(R\): \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=2r\sin\theta\), \(0\le r\le1\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\), Jacobian \(2r\).
  3. \[\begin{aligned}\iint_R2x^2\,dA&=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^12r^2\cos^2\theta\cdot2r\,dr\,d\theta\\&=4\cdot\tfrac14\cdot\pi=\pi.\end{aligned}\]

\(\displaystyle\oint_C\dots=\pi\)

May 2017 Q3(b)6 marksMay format

Verify Green's Theorem in the plane for \(\displaystyle\oint_C(2xy-x^2)\,dx+(x+y^2)\,dy\) where \(C\) is the closed curve of the region bounded by \(y=x^2\) and \(y^2=x\).

Hint

The curves meet at \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\). Double integral: \(x^2\le y\le\sqrt x\). Line integral: out along \(y=x^2\) (parameter \(x\)), back along \(x=y^2\) (parameter \(y\), from 1 to 0).

Solution
  1. \(M=2xy-x^2\), \(N=x+y^2\): \(N_x-M_y=1-2x\).
  2. Double integral: \[\begin{aligned}&\int_0^1\!\!\int_{x^2}^{\sqrt x}(1-2x)\,dy\,dx\\&=\int_0^1(1-2x)(\sqrt x-x^2)\,dx\\&=\tfrac23-\tfrac13-\tfrac45+\tfrac12=\tfrac1{30}.\end{aligned}\]
  3. \(C_1\): \(y=x^2\), \(x:0\to1\), \(dy=2x\,dx\): \[\begin{aligned}&\int_0^1\big[(2x^3-x^2)+(x+x^4)2x\big]dx\\&=\int_0^1(2x^5+2x^3+x^2)\,dx=\tfrac76.\end{aligned}\]
  4. \(C_2\): \(x=y^2\), \(y:1\to0\), \(dx=2y\,dy\): \[\begin{aligned}&\int_1^0\big[(2y^3-y^4)2y+2y^2\big]dy\\&=-\big(\tfrac45-\tfrac13+\tfrac23\big)=-\tfrac{17}{15}.\end{aligned}\]
  5. Total: \(\frac76-\frac{17}{15}=\frac{35-34}{30}=\frac1{30}\), equal to the double integral ✓.

The return piece must run from \((1,1)\) to \((0,0)\): limits 1 to 0. With 0 to 1 you get \(\frac76+\frac{17}{15}\) and no agreement.

\(\displaystyle\oint_C\dots=\iint_R(1-2x)\,dA=\frac1{30}\)
May 2016 Q3(b)6 marksMay format

Verify Green's Theorem by evaluating the circulation for the vector field \(\vb F(x,y)=(-x^2y)\,\ii+(xy^2)\,\jj\). Take the domain of integration to be the disk \(R\): \(x^2+y^2\le a^2\) and its bounding circle \(C\) as the closed path.

Hint

Tangential form. \(N_x-M_y=x^2+y^2=r^2\) in polar. On \(C\): \(x=a\cos t\), \(y=a\sin t\).

Solution
  1. \(M=-x^2y\), \(N=xy^2\): \(N_x-M_y=y^2+x^2\).
  2. Double integral: \(\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^ar^2\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\cdot\frac{a^4}4=\frac{\pi a^4}2\).
  3. Circulation, \(x=a\cos t\), \(y=a\sin t\), \(t:0\to2\pi\): \[\begin{aligned}M\,dx+N\,dy&=\big(a^4\cos^2t\sin^2t\\&\quad+a^4\cos^2t\sin^2t\big)dt.\end{aligned}\]
  4. \[\begin{aligned}&2a^4\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2t\sin^2t\,dt\\&=\frac{a^4}2\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^22t\,dt=\frac{a^4}2\cdot\pi.\end{aligned}\] Both sides \(\frac{\pi a^4}2\) ✓.

Both sides must be shown and compared in a closing sentence: “hence Green's theorem is verified”.

Verify the tangential form of Green's Theorem

\[\oint_CM\,dx+N\,dy=\iint_R\Big(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\Big)\,dx\,dy\]

using the integral \(\displaystyle\oint_C(2x+y^2)\,dx+(2y+x^2)\,dy\) where \(C\) is the closed path that goes from \((-1,0)\) to \((1,0)\) on the \(x\)-axis and returns from \((1,0)\) to \((-1,0)\) on the parabola \(y=1-x^2\).

Hint

\(N_x-M_y=2x-2y\); the \(2x\) part integrates to 0 by symmetry. Line integral: the segment (\(y=0\), \(dy=0\)) and the parabola with \(x\) from 1 to \(-1\).

  2. Double integral over \(-1\le x\le1\), \(0\le y\le1-x^2\): the \(2x\) term is odd in \(x\) on a symmetric region, so 0. \[\begin{aligned}&-\int_{-1}^1\!\!\int_0^{1-x^2}2y\,dy\,dx\\&=-\int_{-1}^1(1-x^2)^2dx\\&=-\big(2-\tfrac43+\tfrac25\big)=-\tfrac{16}{15}.\end{aligned}\]
  3. Segment \(y=0\), \(x:-1\to1\): \(\int_{-1}^12x\,dx=0\).

C5.6Heat equation with a decay (or growth) term

4 parts · 3 sittings (Dec 2021 Q5(a), Dec 2016 Q5(b), May 2016 Q5) · 50 marks · Learn it · note C5 §9 insulated ends · note §5

Type card · predictable
Looks like
A heat equation with an extra term in \(u\): \(u_t+ku=a^2u_{xx}\), \(u_t=a^2u_{xx}-ku\), or \(U_{xx}=kU_t-U\); so far always with insulated ends \(u_x=0\).
Do
1. Rearrange to \(u_t=\alpha u_{xx}+\beta u\). 2. Put \(u=e^{\beta t}v\): then \(v_t=\alpha v_{xx}\), with the same BCs and IC (as \(e^0=1\)); say so. 3. Solve for \(v\) exactly as an insulated-ends rod (guide C5.2). 4. Multiply by \(e^{\beta t}\). If the question says “by separation of variables, show …”, separate directly instead: \(X''-cX=0\), \(T'+(k-ca^2)T=0\).
Watch
The sign of \(\beta\): \(u_t+ku=\dots\) gives \(\beta=-k\) (decay), but Dec 2016's \(U_{xx}=kU_t-U\) gives \(\beta=+\frac1k\) (growth). Keep the constant mode: with the extra term it becomes \(a_0e^{\beta t}\), not \(a_0\).
Ready answer · heat with decay, insulated ends

\(u_t=a^2u_{xx}-ku\), \(0\lt x\lt L\); \(u_x(0,t)=u_x(L,t)=0\); \(u(x,0)=f(x)\):

\[\begin{aligned}u&=e^{-kt}\Big[a_0+\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\cos\frac{n\pi x}L\,e^{-\left(\frac{n\pi a}L\right)^2t}\Big],\\ a_0&=\frac1L\int_0^Lf\,dx,\\ a_n&=\frac2L\int_0^Lf\cos\frac{n\pi x}L\,dx.\end{aligned}\]

Type card · predictable
Looks like
“Solve the PDE \(\dfrac{\partial^2U}{\partial x\partial y}=a\dfrac{\partial U}{\partial x}+g(x,y)\)” (or \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(U_y-aU\big)=b\)) with two boundary conditions: one on a line \(y=\) const, one on \(x=0\).
Do
1. Integrate with respect to one variable: name \(w=U_x\) (the derivative in the lower-order term), so \(w_y-aw=g\). 2. Solve this first-order linear ODE in \(y\) with the integrating factor \(e^{-ay}\) (or trial \(w_p\)); the “constant” becomes an arbitrary function \(F(x)\). 3. Integrate in \(x\): \(U=\int w_p\,dx+H(x)e^{ay}+G(y)\). 4. Apply the BCs to fix \(H\) and \(G\); fix the last number at the corner \(y=0\). 5. Substitute back.
Watch
The constants of integration are functions of the other variable, not numbers. Unknown numbers such as \(G(0)\) must cancel at the end; if they do not, the algebra has slipped. Read the variable order: \(U_{yx}=aU_y+\dots\) means \(w=U_y\) and an ODE in \(x\).
Ready answer · \(u_{xy}=a\,u_x+g\)

With \(w=u_x\): \(w_y-aw=g(x,y)\), so

\[\begin{aligned}u_x&=e^{ay}F(x)+w_p(x,y),\\ u&=\int w_p\,dx+H(x)e^{ay}+G(y).\end{aligned}\]
\(g\)particular \(w_p\)
\(p(x)\)\(-\dfrac{p(x)}a\)
\(p(x)\,y+q(x)\)\(-\dfrac pa\,y-\dfrac p{a^2}-\dfrac qa\)
other\(e^{ay}\displaystyle\int e^{-ay}g\,dy\)

\(u_{xy}=0\) alone gives \(u=F(x)+G(y)\). Typical BCs: \(u(x,0)=\varphi(x)\) gives \(H(x)\) in terms of \(G(0)\); \(u_y(0,y)=\psi(y)\) gives an ODE for \(G(y)\); then \(y=0\) fixes its constant.

In words: undo one derivative at a time; each time, the constant of integration is any function of the variable you did not integrate over, and the two boundary conditions decide those functions.

Type card · predictable
Looks like
“\(u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0\) inside the rectangular domain \(0\le x\le a\), \(0\le y\le b\)” with a condition on each of the four edges, one or two of them non-zero; then “find \(u\) at the point …”.
Do
1. \(u=X(x)Y(y)\): \(\frac{X''}X=-\frac{Y''}Y=\lambda\). 2. The other variable gets \(\cosh/\sinh\): use its zero edge to keep \(\sinh\) (insulated pair: also the \(n=0\) mode \(Y=A+By\)). 4. Match the non-zero edge(s) by a Fourier series, or read off if already a shape. 5. Evaluate at the point.
Watch
No time here: the second variable gives \(\sinh\)/\(\cosh\), not a decaying exponential. Divide by \(\sinh(\dots)\) at the hot edge so the factor is 1 there. A point on an edge just returns the edge data: use it as a check.
Ready answer · one non-zero edge

\(0\le x\le a\), \(0\le y\le b\); \(u=0\) on three edges, \(u(x,b)=f(x)\):

\[\begin{aligned}u&=\sum_{n=1}^\infty B_n\frac{\sinh\frac{n\pi y}a}{\sinh\frac{n\pi b}a}\sin\frac{n\pi x}a,\\ B_n&=\frac2a\int_0^af(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}a\,dx.\end{aligned}\]

Hot edge \(x=a\) instead, \(u(a,y)=f(y)\): swap the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) (and \(a\), \(b\)):

\[\begin{aligned}u&=\sum_{n=1}^\infty B_n\frac{\sinh\frac{n\pi x}b}{\sinh\frac{n\pi a}b}\sin\frac{n\pi y}b,\\ B_n&=\frac2b\int_0^bf(y)\sin\frac{n\pi y}b\,dy.\end{aligned}\]

Zero at the hot edge's opposite side gives \(\sinh\) measured from that side; a mixed pair \(u=0\), \(u_x=0\) gives \(\sin\frac{(2n-1)\pi x}{2a}\).

In words: write the hot edge's temperature as a sine series; each sine shape fades into the plate like a \(\sinh\), equal to 1 on the hot edge and 0 on the opposite edge.

  4. \(n=0\): \(Y_0(0)=0\), \(Y_0(1)=\frac{a^2}2\Rightarrow Y_0=\frac{a^2}2y\).

  6. \(n=2\): \(Y_2(0)=0\), \(Y_2(1)=\frac{a^2}2\Rightarrow Y_2=\frac{a^2}2\frac{\sinh2y}{\sinh2}\). All other \(n\): 0.
  7. (ii) \(a=3\), \((0,1)\): \(9\big[\frac12+0+\frac12\big]=9\) (also \(9\cos^20\) from the top edge ✓).

The \(n=0\) mode (\(Y=A+By\), straight line) is the mark most often lost; show the \(\lambda=0\) case.

\[\begin{aligned}u=a^2\Big[&\frac y2+\frac{\sinh(1-y)}{\sinh1}\cos x\\&+\frac{\sinh2y}{2\sinh2}\cos2x\Big]\\ \text{(ii) }u&(0,1)=9\end{aligned}\]

Agrees with the note's exam taster. \(\sinh(1-y)\) is the shape that is 0 at \(y=1\), needed because \(\cos x\) appears only on the bottom edge.

May 2018 Q5(i)–(ii)17 + 3 marksMay format

Consider \(\dfrac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}+\dfrac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}=0\) inside the rectangular domain \(0\le x\le2\), \(0\le y\le2\), with boundary conditions \(u(0,y)=0\), \(\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}(2,y)=0\), \(0\le y\le2\); \(u(x,0)=0\), \(u(x,2)=\sin\dfrac{\pi x}4\), \(0\le x\le2\).

(i) By separation of variables, find the solution \(u(x,y)\). (17 marks)

(ii) Hence, find the value of the solution \(u(x,y)\) at \((x,y)=(2,2)\). (3 marks)

Hint

Homogeneous pair in \(x\): \(X(0)=0\), \(X'(2)=0\), giving quarter-wave shapes \(\sin\frac{(2n-1)\pi x}4\). Zero at \(y=0\) → \(\sinh\). The top edge is the \(n=1\) shape.

Solution
  1. \(u=XY\): \(\frac{X''}X=-\frac{Y''}Y=\lambda\); \(X(0)=0\), \(X'(2)=0\).
  2. \(\lambda=0\): \(X=A+Bx\), \(A=0\), \(B=0\). \(\lambda=p^2\): \(X=B\sinh px\), \(X'(2)=Bp\cosh2p=0\Rightarrow B=0\). \(\lambda=-p^2\): \(X=B\sin px\), \(Bp\cos2p=0\Rightarrow p=\frac{(2n-1)\pi}4\).
  3. \(Y''=p^2Y\), \(Y(0)=0\Rightarrow Y=\sinh py\). So \[u=\sum_{n\ge1}c_n\sin\tfrac{(2n-1)\pi x}4\sinh\tfrac{(2n-1)\pi y}4.\]
  4. Top edge: \(\sin\frac{\pi x}4=\sum c_n\sinh\frac{(2n-1)\pi}2\sin\frac{(2n-1)\pi x}4\): only \(n=1\), \(c_1=\frac1{\sinh(\pi/2)}\).
  5. (ii) \(u(2,2)=\sin\frac\pi2\cdot\frac{\sinh(\pi/2)}{\sinh(\pi/2)}=1\) (the corner of the top edge: \(\sin\frac{2\pi}4=1\) ✓).

The mixed pair \(u=0\), \(u_x=0\) gives odd quarter-waves; using \(\sin\frac{n\pi x}2\) fails \(u_x(2,y)=0\).

\[\begin{aligned}u(x,y)&=\frac{\sinh(\pi y/4)}{\sinh(\pi/2)}\sin\frac{\pi x}4\\ \text{(ii) }u(2,2)&=1\end{aligned}\]
May 2015 Q514 + 6 marksMay format

Find the general solution of the two-dimensional Laplace's equation in the \(xy\)-plane inside a domain \(0\le x\le a\), \(0\le y\le b\), \(\dfrac{\partial^2U}{\partial x^2}+\dfrac{\partial^2U}{\partial y^2}=0\) with boundary conditions \(U(0,y)=0\), \(U(a,y)=f(y)\), \(0\le y\le b\); \(U(x,0)=0\), \(U(x,b)=0\), \(0\le x\le a\). (14 marks)

Hence, solve the particular solution if \(f(y)=k\sin\dfrac{\pi y}b\), where \(k\) is a non-zero constant. Make use of the particular solution to compute the value of \(U\), in terms of \(k\), at the point \((x,y)=\big(a,\frac b2\big)\) inside the domain. (6 marks)

\(\big(a,\frac b2\big)\) is on the edge \(x=a\), not inside, so \(U=f\big(\frac b2\big)=k\) directly; we compute it from the solution as asked and use this as the check.

Hint

The homogeneous pair is now top and bottom: \(Y(0)=Y(b)=0\) → \(\sin\frac{n\pi y}b\). Then \(X''=\big(\frac{n\pi}b\big)^2X\), \(X(0)=0\) → \(\sinh\frac{n\pi x}b\).

Solution
  1. \(U=XY\): \(\frac{X''}X=-\frac{Y''}Y=\lambda\); \(Y(0)=Y(b)=0\).
  2. \(Y''=-\lambda Y\): \(\lambda=0\) and \(\lambda\lt0\) give only \(Y=0\); \(\lambda=p^2\): \(Y=\sin py\), \(\sin pb=0\), \(p=\frac{n\pi}b\).
  3. \(X''=p^2X\), \(X(0)=0\Rightarrow X=\sinh\frac{n\pi x}b\).
  4. General solution and the edge \(x=a\): \[\begin{aligned}U&=\sum_{n\ge1}c_n\sinh\frac{n\pi x}b\sin\frac{n\pi y}b,\\ c_n&=\frac{2}{b\sinh\frac{n\pi a}b}\int_0^bf(y)\sin\frac{n\pi y}b\,dy.\end{aligned}\]
  5. \(f=k\sin\frac{\pi y}b\) is the \(n=1\) shape: \(c_1\sinh\frac{\pi a}b=k\), all other \(c_n=0\).
  6. At \(\big(a,\frac b2\big)\): \(U=k\cdot1\cdot\sin\frac\pi2=k\).

The general solution must include the coefficient formula (a sine series in \(y\) on \([0,b]\)), not just the sum.

\[\begin{aligned}U&=k\,\frac{\sinh(\pi x/b)}{\sinh(\pi a/b)}\sin\frac{\pi y}b,\\ U\big(a,\tfrac b2\big)&=k\end{aligned}\]
