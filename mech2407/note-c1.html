1Functions of several variables, level curves and level surfaces (handout §1.1, pp. 1–4) ~11 min

What is this?

A function of several variables is a machine with several input dials and one output display. Think of a hill drawn on a map: you tell it where you are standing (how far east, how far north) and it tells you how high the ground is. Two inputs, one output. A room's temperature is the same idea with four dials: three for position and one for the time of day.

Many quantities in the physical world depend on more than one variable. Example 1.1.1 gives four:

Example 1.1.1
  1. The height of the ground at map coordinates \((x,y)\): \(\;\text{height}=h(x,y)\).
  2. The temperature in a lecture room, which varies with position and time: \(\;\text{Temperature}=T(x,y,z,t)\).
  3. The volume of a rectangular box of length \(x\), width \(y\) and height \(z\): \(\;V=xyz\).
  4. The magnitude of the electric potential due to a uniformly charged spherical shell with total charge \(Q\): \[\begin{gathered}\varphi=\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}},\\ r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}\gt R,\end{gathered}\] where \(R\) is the radius of the sphere and \(\epsilon_0\) is the permittivity (a constant of the material between the charges).

In words: potential = (charge ÷ 4π·permittivity) × 1 ÷ (distance from the centre), valid outside the shell.

In each case you choose the inputs and one number comes out. These are all scalar-valued functions of real variables: the output is a single number (a scalar), not a vector. In general we write

\[\begin{gathered}z=f(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)\equiv f(\vb x),\\ \vb x=\begin{bmatrix}x_1&x_2&\cdots&x_n\end{bmatrix}^T\end{gathered}\]
Definitions
  • \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) are the independent variables: the inputs you are free to choose.
  • \(z\) is the dependent variable: the output, whose value depends on the inputs.
  • \(f\) is a scalar field: a rule that attaches one number to every point of a region of space. "Field" just means "something defined at every point", like the temperature at every point of a room.
  • The superscript \(T\) means transpose: the row of inputs is turned into a column vector. It is only a way of packing the inputs into one vector \(\vb x\).

Picturing a function of two variables

What is this?

A level curve is a contour line on a hiking map. Walk along it and you never go up or down: every point on the line is at the same height. Draw contour lines for several heights on one sheet and you get the whole hill on flat paper, which is much easier to read than a 3D drawing.

For one variable, the graph \(y=f(x)\) is a curve. For two variables, the graph \(z=f(x,y)\) is a surface in 3D space: above each point \((x,y)\) of the floor, the surface sits at height \(z=f(x,y)\). Drawing a surface on flat paper is hard, so we use a second picture, borrowed from hiking maps.

Definition (level curve, contour map)

A level curve of \(f(x,y)\) is the set of points \((x,y)\) for which

\[f(x,y)=c,\qquad c \text{ a constant.}\]

A collection of level curves (one for each of several values of \(c\)) forms a contour map.

In words: a level curve joins all the places on the floor where the surface has the same height \(c\).

Example 1.1.2 (contour maps)

1. A hill. Picture a single hill rising from flat ground at height 0 m to a summit a little above 1000 m. Slice it horizontally at heights 0 m, 500 m and 1000 m. Each slice cuts the hill in a closed loop: the 0 m loop is the outline of the hill's base, the 500 m loop is smaller, and the 1000 m loop is a small ring just below the summit. Now look straight down and draw all three loops on one flat map, each labelled with its height. That drawing is the contour map: three nested loops labelled 0 m, 500 m and 1000 m.

2. \(f(x,y)=x^2+y^2\). The surface is a bowl (a paraboloid). Its level curves \(x^2+y^2=c\) are circles centred at the origin with radius \(\sqrt c\). The levels \(c=1^2,\,2^2,\,3^2\) give circles of radius 1, 2 and 3. Seen from the side, these circles are horizontal rings on the bowl at heights 1, 4 and 9. The rings are equally spaced in radius but not in height, because the bowl gets steeper as you move out.

3. \(f(x,y)=x^2-y^2\). Its level curves \(x^2-y^2=c\) are hyperbolas. For \(c\gt0\) they open left and right (crossing the \(x\)-axis at \(x=\pm\sqrt c\)); for \(c\lt0\) they open up and down; for \(c=0\) they are the two crossing straight lines \(y=\pm x\). The contour map looks like a set of nested "X"-shaped curves centred on the origin.

Analogy

This is exactly a hiking map. Loops crowded together mean steep ground; loops far apart mean gentle ground. A closed loop with nothing inside is a hilltop or the bottom of a dip.

Computer-generated surface plots usually draw the lines on which \(x\) is constant and the lines on which \(y\) is constant. Together they form a mesh that shows the shape of the surface. The 3D picture above works the same way: each small patch is bounded by lines \(x=\)const and \(y=\)const.

Example 1.1.3 (surface plots)

1. \(z=x^2-y^2\), plotted for \(-2\le x\le2,\ -2\le y\le2\). The mesh shows a saddle: along the \(x\)-direction the surface curves upward (like \(z=x^2\)), along the \(y\)-direction it curves downward (like \(z=-y^2\)), and the two meet at a flat point at the origin. Heights run from \(-4\) (at \(y=\pm2,\ x=0\)) to \(4\) (at \(x=\pm2,\ y=0\)).

2. \(z=\cos xy\), plotted for \(-3\le x\le3,\ -3\le y\le3\). Heights stay between \(-1\) and \(1\). Along both axes \(xy=0\), so \(z=1\): a flat ridge forms a cross through the origin. Moving away from the axes, \(z\) ripples down and up along the hyperbolas \(xy=\text{const}\) (troughs where \(xy=\pm\pi\), ridges where \(xy=\pm2\pi\)), and the ripples bunch together in the corners, where \(xy\) changes fastest.

Functions of three variables: level surfaces

What is this?

With three inputs (a point in a room, say) there is no "height" left to draw, so instead we collect all the points in the room where the function has the same value. These points form a surface, not a curve. Picture the air around a heater: all the points at exactly 25 °C form a closed shell around it, all the points at 22 °C form a bigger shell, and so on. Each shell is a level surface, the 3D cousin of a contour line.

A function of three variables \(f(x,y,z)\) would need four dimensions to graph, so we cannot sketch its graph. Instead we look at where it takes a fixed value.

Definition (level surface)

For any number \(c\), the set of points \((x,y,z)\) with \(f(x,y,z)=c\) is a level surface of \(f\). We name the surface by its equation \(f(x,y,z)=c\).

In words: a level surface is the 3D version of a level curve: all points in space where \(f\) has the same value.

Example. Take \(f(x,y,z)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}\), the distance from the origin. Its level surfaces \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}=1,2,3\) are spheres of radius 1, 2 and 3 nested inside each other, like the layers of an onion.

Level surfaces of \(f=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}\): nested spheres (cut-away)

The upper halves of the spheres \(f=1,2,3\), with a quarter cut away so the inner ones show (drag to rotate). Every point on one sphere has the same value of \(f\).

Two names from physics:

  • A surface on which the temperature is constantly \(c\) is an isothermal surface ("iso" = equal, "thermal" = heat).
  • A surface on which the voltage (potential) is constantly \(c\) is an equipotential surface. For the charged shell of Example 1.1.1, the equipotential surfaces are exactly these spheres, since \(\varphi\) depends only on \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}\).

You can now: say what the inputs and output of a function of several variables are; sketch the contour map of a simple function such as \(x^2+y^2\) or \(x^2-y^2\) by setting \(f=c\); and describe the level surfaces of a function of three variables.

2Partial differentiation (handout §1.2, pp. 4–8) ~11 min

What is this?

You are standing on a hill. Walk due east and you feel one slope; walk due north and you feel a different one. A partial derivative is the slope you feel when you walk in exactly one of those directions and do not move at all in the other.

Recall that for a function \(f(x)\) of one variable the derivative at \(x_0\) is

\[\frac{df}{dx}(x_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}.\]

In words: derivative = limit of (change in output) ÷ (change in input) as the change in input shrinks to zero; geometrically, the slope of the tangent line.

With two inputs there is no single "change in input": on the hill you could walk east, north, or in any mixture. The simplest choice is to move only one input and freeze all the others.

Definition (partial derivatives)

Hold \(y\) constant at \(y=y_0\). Then \(f(x,y_0)\) is a function of \(x\) alone, and its derivative at \(x_0\) is the partial derivative of \(f\) with respect to \(x\), provided the limit exists:

\[\pd{f}{x}(x_0,y_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_0+h,\,y_0)-f(x_0,\,y_0)}{h}.\]

Similarly, holding \(x=x_0\) fixed, the partial derivative with respect to \(y\) is

\[\pd{f}{y}(x_0,y_0)=\lim_{k\to0}\frac{f(x_0,\,y_0+k)-f(x_0,\,y_0)}{k}.\]

In words: \(\partial f/\partial x\) = how fast \(f\) changes when you nudge \(x\) and keep \(y\) fixed; \(\partial f/\partial y\) = how fast \(f\) changes when you nudge \(y\) and keep \(x\) fixed. The curly \(\partial\) ("partial", "del" or "dee") warns you that other variables are being held still.

Reading the formula
\(f(x_0,y_0)\)
your height where you stand now.
\(f(x_0+h,\,y_0)\)
your height after a small step \(h\) east, still on the same north line.
\(f(x_0+h,y_0)-f(x_0,y_0)\)
how much you rose (or fell) during that step.
\(\dfrac{\cdots}{h}\), then \(h\to0\)
rise per unit step, for a step so small that only the slope right at your feet counts.
\(y_0\) unchanged
you did not move north, so \(y\) is frozen. This is why the working rule is "treat \(y\) as a constant". The second formula is the same story walking north with a step \(k\), with \(x\) frozen.

Picture. Walking due east through \(P\) traces out a path on the hill. In symbols: cut the surface \(z=f(x,y)\) with the vertical plane \(y=y_0\). The cut is a curve \(z=f(x,y_0)\) lying in that plane, and the slope in the \(x\)-direction, \(f_x(x_0,y_0)\), is the slope of its tangent line at the point \(P(x_0,y_0,f(x_0,y_0))\). Cutting instead with the plane \(x=x_0\) gives the curve \(z=f(x_0,y)\), whose slope is the slope in the \(y\)-direction, \(f_y(x_0,y_0)\). Move the point below to see both slices.

Surface \(f(x,y)=1+0.5x^2-0.4y^2+0.3xy\). Teal curve: slice \(y=y_0\), with tangent line of slope \(f_x\) (orange). Purple curve: slice \(x=x_0\), with tangent line of slope \(f_y\) (red).

Notation

For \(z=f(x,y)\), all of these mean the same thing:

\[\begin{gathered}\pd{f}{x}(x,y)\equiv f_x(x,y)\equiv\pd{z}{x}(x,y)\\ \equiv z_x(x,y)\quad\text{or}\quad\left(\pd{f}{x}\right)_{y}\end{gathered}\]

The last form, with a subscript \(y\) outside the bracket, states explicitly that \(y\) is held constant. It matters when it is not obvious which variables are frozen (for example after a change of variables, §3).

How to compute one (no limits needed in practice):

  1. Do: Pick the variable that moves: the one on the bottom of \(\partial f/\partial(\cdot)\). This is choosing the direction you walk (east for \(x\), north for \(y\)).
  2. Do: Treat every other variable as a fixed number, as if it were 3 or 7. Terms with only frozen variables give 0; frozen factors stay as multipliers. You do not move in the other directions, so their coordinates stay at \(y_0\) (or \(x_0\)), exactly as in the limit.
  3. Do: Differentiate with the ordinary single-variable rules (power, product, quotient, chain). Along your walking line the surface is just a curve of one variable, and its slope is an ordinary derivative.
Example 1.2.1

1. \(f(x,y)=x^2y+y^3\sin x\). Treating \(y\) as a constant, then \(x\) as a constant:In \(\partial f/\partial x\), \(y\) and \(y^3\) are frozen multipliers; in \(\partial f/\partial y\), \(x^2\) and \(\sin x\) are.

\[\pd fx=2xy+y^3\cos x,\quad \pd fy=x^2+3y^2\sin x.\]

2. \(f(x,y,z)=e^{2z}\cos xy\). With two variables frozen each time (and the chain rule for \(\cos xy\)):Walking in \(x\), the inside \(xy\) changes at rate \(y\) (since \(y\) is frozen), so the chain rule brings out a factor \(y\).

\[\begin{aligned}f_x&=-e^{2z}\,y\sin xy,\\ f_y&=-e^{2z}\,x\sin xy,\\ f_z&=2e^{2z}\cos xy.\end{aligned}\]

3. (Function of a function.) If \(f=f(u)\) and \(u=u(x,y)\), the single-variable chain rule applies with the other variable frozen:

\[\pd fx=\frac{df}{du}\left(\pd ux\right)_y,\quad \pd fy=\frac{df}{du}\left(\pd uy\right)_x.\]

In words: outer derivative × inner partial derivative.

If \(f=\ln u\) and \(u=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), then

\[\begin{aligned}\pd fx&=\frac1u\times\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\times2x=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2},\\ \pd fy&=\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}.\end{aligned}\]

4. (Implicit function.) Sometimes \(z\) is not given as a formula but is tied to \(x\) and \(y\) by an equation, here

\[x^3-xy^2+yz^2-z^3=5.\]

The equation describes a surface: the points \((x,y,z)\) that satisfy it. We still regard \(z=z(x,y)\) as a function of \(x\) and \(y\) (an implicit function: defined by an equation rather than an explicit formula): it is the height of that surface above the point \((x,y)\). The method:

  1. Do: Keep \(z\) as an unknown function \(z(x,y)\), not a constant. You stay on the surface. When you step east, the height \(z\) must change to keep the equation true.
  2. Do: Differentiate every term of both sides partially with respect to \(x\), holding \(y\) fixed. Every term containing \(z\) gets the chain rule, with an extra factor \(\pd zx\). The constant on the right gives 0. \(z\) moves when \(x\) moves, because you stay on the surface, so \(z^2\) and \(z^3\) need the chain rule.
  3. Do: Collect the terms containing \(\pd zx\) on one side, factor it out and divide. \[\begin{gathered}3x^2-y^2+2zy\pd zx-3z^2\pd zx=0\\ \Longrightarrow\;\pd zx=\frac{y^2-3x^2}{2zy-3z^2},\end{gathered}\] \(\pd zx\) is the one unknown: the east-slope of the surface. It appears only to the first power, so solve for it.
  4. Do: State the condition that the denominator is not zero: here \(3z^2\ne2zy\). Where the denominator is 0 the surface stands vertically in that direction (or folds over), so its height has no finite slope there.
  5. Do: Repeat with respect to \(y\), now with \(x\) frozen. Watch for terms where \(y\) multiplies something containing \(z\): here \(-xy^2\) gives \(-2xy\), and \(yz^2\) needs the product rule. \[\begin{gathered}0-2xy+2yz\pd zy+z^2-3z^2\pd zy=0\\ \Longrightarrow\;\pd zy=\frac{2xy-z^2}{2yz-3z^2},\end{gathered}\] again provided \(3z^2\ne2zy\). Walking north, both \(y\) itself and the height \(z\) change, so a product of the two has two parts that change.
Show the calculation: both implicit derivatives, term by term

Differentiating with respect to \(x\) (\(y\) is a constant; \(z=z(x,y)\) changes with \(x\)):

  • \(x^3\to3x^2\): ordinary power rule.
  • \(-xy^2\to-y^2\): \(y^2\) is just a constant multiplier of \(x\).
  • \(yz^2\to y\cdot2z\,\pd zx\): \(y\) is a constant; \(z^2\) needs the chain rule (derivative of \(z^2\) with respect to \(z\) is \(2z\), times \(\partial z/\partial x\) because \(z\) moves when \(x\) moves).
  • \(-z^3\to-3z^2\,\pd zx\): same reason.
  • \(5\to0\): a constant.

Adding: \(3x^2-y^2+2yz\,z_x-3z^2\,z_x=0\). Collect the two terms containing \(z_x\) on one side and everything else on the other:

\[\begin{gathered}(2yz-3z^2)\,z_x=y^2-3x^2\\ \Longrightarrow\;z_x=\frac{y^2-3x^2}{2yz-3z^2}.\end{gathered}\]

Differentiating with respect to \(y\) (\(x\) is now the constant):

  • \(x^3\to0\): no \(y\) in it.
  • \(-xy^2\to-2xy\): power rule in \(y\), \(x\) a constant multiplier.
  • \(yz^2\to z^2+y\cdot2z\,\pd zy\): product rule, because both factors \(y\) and \(z^2\) change with \(y\): (derivative of \(y\))·\(z^2\) + \(y\)·(derivative of \(z^2\)).
  • \(-z^3\to-3z^2\,\pd zy\): chain rule.

Adding and collecting the \(z_y\) terms:

\[\begin{gathered}-2xy+z^2+(2yz-3z^2)\,z_y=0\\ \Longrightarrow\;z_y=\frac{2xy-z^2}{2yz-3z^2}.\end{gathered}\]

Both divisions need \(2yz-3z^2\ne0\), which is the condition \(3z^2\ne2zy\) stated above.

Second and higher partial derivatives

What is this?

The east-slope is not the same everywhere on the hill, so we can ask how it changes. Keep walking east and ask whether the path is getting steeper or flatter: that is the second derivative in \(x\), the bending of the ground in that direction, like a road that steepens towards the top of a climb. Or step north and ask how the east-slope has changed: that is a mixed second derivative, and it measures how the surface twists as you move across it.

The first partial derivatives are themselves functions of \(x\) and \(y\), so we can differentiate them again to get second partial derivatives:

\[\begin{aligned} f_{xx}&=\pd{^2f}{x^2}=\pd{}{x}\left(\pd fx\right)\\ f_{yy}&=\pd{^2f}{y^2}=\pd{}{y}\left(\pd fy\right)\\ f_{yx}&=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}=\pd{}{x}\left(\pd fy\right)\\ f_{xy}&=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}=\pd{}{y}\left(\pd fx\right) \end{aligned}\]

In words: in subscript notation, read left to right: \(f_{xy}\) means "first \(x\), then \(y\)". In \(\partial\) notation, read right to left: \(\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial y\,\partial x}\) means "first \(\partial x\) (nearest \(f\)), then \(\partial y\)". Both describe the same thing. \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) are the mixed partial derivatives.

Higher-order partial derivatives (third, fourth, …) are defined in the same way.

Example 1.2.2

Let \(f(x,y)=\sin xy^2\). Then \(f_x=y^2\cos xy^2\) and \(f_y=2xy\cos xy^2\), and

\[\begin{aligned} f_{xx}&=-y^4\sin xy^2\\ f_{xy}&=\pd{}{y}\big(y^2\cos xy^2\big)\\&=2y\cos xy^2-2xy^3\sin xy^2\\ f_{yx}&=\pd{}{x}\big(2xy\cos xy^2\big)\\&=2y\cos xy^2-2xy^3\sin xy^2\\ f_{yy}&=\pd{}{y}\big(2xy\cos xy^2\big)\\&=2x\cos xy^2-4x^2y^2\sin xy^2 \end{aligned}\]

(\(f_{xy}\) uses the product rule on \(y^2\cdot\cos xy^2\); \(f_{yy}\) uses it on \(2xy\cdot\cos xy^2\).)

Show the calculation: all six derivatives, step by step

Throughout, write \(w=xy^2\) for the inside of the sine, so \(\pd wx=y^2\) and \(\pd wy=2xy\). The chain rule gives \(\pd{}{x}\sin w=\cos w\cdot y^2\), \(\pd{}{x}\cos w=-\sin w\cdot y^2\), and similarly with \(2xy\) for \(y\).

  1. \(f_x=\cos xy^2\cdot y^2=y^2\cos xy^2\) (outer derivative × \(\partial w/\partial x\)).
  2. \(f_y=\cos xy^2\cdot2xy=2xy\cos xy^2\) (outer derivative × \(\partial w/\partial y\)).
  3. \(f_{xx}=\pd{}{x}\big(y^2\cos xy^2\big)\): \(y^2\) is a constant here, so only \(\cos xy^2\) is differentiated: \(y^2\cdot(-\sin xy^2)\cdot y^2=-y^4\sin xy^2\).
  4. \(f_{xy}=\pd{}{y}\big(y^2\cos xy^2\big)\): both factors contain \(y\), so use the product rule: \[\begin{aligned}&\underbrace{2y}_{(y^2)'}\cos xy^2+y^2\underbrace{(-\sin xy^2)(2xy)}_{(\cos xy^2)'}\\&=2y\cos xy^2-2xy^3\sin xy^2.\end{aligned}\]
  5. \(f_{yx}=\pd{}{x}\big(2xy\cos xy^2\big)\): product rule in \(x\): \[\begin{aligned}&\underbrace{2y}_{(2xy)'}\cos xy^2+2xy\underbrace{(-\sin xy^2)(y^2)}_{(\cos xy^2)'}\\&=2y\cos xy^2-2xy^3\sin xy^2.\end{aligned}\]
  6. \(f_{yy}=\pd{}{y}\big(2xy\cos xy^2\big)\): product rule in \(y\): \[\begin{aligned}&\underbrace{2x}_{(2xy)'}\cos xy^2+2xy\underbrace{(-\sin xy^2)(2xy)}_{(\cos xy^2)'}\\&=2x\cos xy^2-4x^2y^2\sin xy^2.\end{aligned}\]

Steps 4 and 5 give the same expression: \(f_{xy}=f_{yx}\).

In this example the two mixed derivatives came out equal, \(f_{xy}=f_{yx}\). This happens for most functions met in engineering and science, but it is not always true. The precise condition:

Theorem (equality of mixed partials, also called Clairaut's or Schwarz's theorem)

If \(f,\ f_x,\ f_y,\ f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) are all defined in a neighbourhood of \((x_0,y_0)\) (a small disc around the point) and are continuous at \((x_0,y_0)\), then

\[f_{xy}(x_0,y_0)=f_{yx}(x_0,y_0).\]

In words: for smooth functions, the order in which you take partial derivatives does not matter. Exam questions say "assume all partial derivatives are continuous" precisely so that you may use this.

\(f_{yx}\)
find the north-slope, then ask how it changes as you step east.

Beyond the handouts · when the order does matter

The standard counterexample is \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{xy(x^2-y^2)}{x^2+y^2}\) with \(f(0,0)=0\). One finds \(f_x(0,y)=-y\) and \(f_y(x,0)=x\), so \(f_{xy}(0,0)=-1\) but \(f_{yx}(0,0)=+1\). The theorem does not apply because the mixed partials are not continuous at the origin. You will not be asked to compute this; it is here only to show why the continuity condition is stated.

Show the calculation: why \(f_{xy}(0,0)=-1\) but \(f_{yx}(0,0)=+1\)

The formula breaks down at the origin, so use the limit definitions of §2 directly.

  1. \(f_x\) along the \(y\)-axis. \(f(0,y)=0\) for every \(y\) (the factor \(x\) is 0). So \[\begin{aligned}f_x(0,y)&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,y)-f(0,y)}{h}\\&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{hy(h^2-y^2)}{h(h^2+y^2)}\\&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{y(h^2-y^2)}{h^2+y^2}\\&=\frac{y(-y^2)}{y^2}=-y\end{aligned}\] (the \(h\) cancels before the limit; for \(y=0\) the same limit gives 0, which is also \(-y\)).
  2. \(f_y\) along the \(x\)-axis. \(f(x,0)=0\), so \[\begin{aligned}f_y(x,0)&=\lim_{k\to0}\frac{xk(x^2-k^2)}{k(x^2+k^2)}\\&=\frac{x\cdot x^2}{x^2}=x.\end{aligned}\]
  3. Mixed derivatives at the origin. \(f_{xy}\) means "first \(x\), then \(y\)": differentiate \(f_x(0,y)=-y\) with respect to \(y\), giving \(-1\). \(f_{yx}\) means "first \(y\), then \(x\)": differentiate \(f_y(x,0)=x\) with respect to \(x\), giving \(+1\).

You can now: explain a partial derivative as the slope walking in one direction; compute first and second partial derivatives by freezing the other variables; and find \(\partial z/\partial x\) and \(\partial z/\partial y\) for a surface given by an implicit equation.

3The chain rule (handout §1.3, pp. 8–10) ~15 min

What is this?

A hiker walks along a path on a hill. Her height changes for two reasons at once: she is moving east, and the hill rises or falls going east; and she is moving north, and the hill rises or falls going north. The chain rule says the total rate at which her height changes is the sum of these two contributions, and each contribution is "how steep the hill is in that direction" times "how fast she is moving in that direction".

Start from the one-variable version you already know. If \(y=f(x)\) and \(x=x(t)\), then \(y=f(x(t))\) and

\[\frac{dy}{dt}=\frac{df}{dx}\,\frac{dx}{dt}.\]

In words: rate of \(y\) per unit \(t\) = (rate of \(y\) per unit \(x\)) × (rate of \(x\) per unit \(t\)).

Now suppose \(z\) depends on two variables \(x\) and \(y\), and both of them change with time \(t\): the hiker is at position \((x(t),y(t))\) on a hill of height \(f(x,y)\). How fast does her height change?

Theorem 1.3.1 (The Chain Rule)

If \(z=f(x,y)\) and \(x=x(t),\ y=y(t)\), then

\[\frac{dz}{dt}=\pd fx\frac{dx}{dt}+\pd fy\frac{dy}{dt}.\]

In words: total rate of change of \(z\) = (effect of \(x\) moving) + (effect of \(y\) moving), where each effect = sensitivity of \(f\) to that variable × how fast that variable moves.

Reading the formula
\(\dfrac{dz}{dt}\)
how fast the hiker's height changes per unit time: the thing we want.
\(\pd fx\)
the slope of the hill going east (north frozen): how sensitive height is to an east step.
\(\dfrac{dx}{dt}\)
her east speed: how many east steps she takes per unit time.
\(\pd fx\dfrac{dx}{dt}\)
height gained per unit time from the east motion alone.
\(+\ \pd fy\dfrac{dy}{dt}\)
the same for the north motion; the two contributions simply add, because over a tiny time the hill looks like a flat tilted plane.

The tree diagram

With more variables it is easy to lose a term. A tree diagram is a bookkeeping picture: it shows every route by which a change at the bottom (time \(t\)) can reach the top (height \(z\)).

Tree diagram: one branch per route from \(z\) down to \(t\)

Multiply along each path from \(z\) to \(t\), then add the paths. Straight \(d\) is used where the variable depends on one thing only (\(x\) depends only on \(t\)); curly \(\partial\) where it depends on several (\(f\) depends on \(x\) and \(y\)).

  1. Do: write the quantity you want (\(z\)) at the top. Below it, write every variable it depends on directly (\(x\), \(y\)). Below each of those, write what they depend on, down to the variable you are differentiating with respect to (\(t\)). The tree is a map of the hiker's situation: height depends on position, position depends on time.
  2. Do: label each branch with the derivative of the upper variable with respect to the lower one: curly \(\partial\) if the upper variable depends on several things, straight \(d\) if on one only. Each label is one link of sensitivity: "how much the upper thing changes per unit change of the lower thing".
  3. Do: find every path from the top down to \(t\). Each path is one separate route by which a change in time reaches the height (here: through the east motion, and through the north motion).
  4. Do: multiply the labels along each path. Along one route the effects pass on like a chain: steepness × speed, exactly as in the one-variable rule.
  5. Do: add the products from all paths. The separate routes act at the same time, and for small changes their effects add up.
Proof of Theorem 1.3.1, explained line by line

By the definition of the derivative,

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dz}{dt}&=\lim_{\delta t\to0}\frac{z(t+\delta t)-z(t)}{\delta t}\\&=\lim_{\delta t\to0}\frac{1}{\delta t}\Big[f\big(x(t+\delta t),y(t+\delta t)\big) -f\big(x(t),y(t)\big)\Big].\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dz}{dt}&=\lim_{\delta t\to0}\frac{z(t+\delta t)-z(t)}{\delta t}\\&=\lim_{\delta t\to0}\frac{1}{\delta t}\Big[f\big(x(t+\delta t),y(t+\delta t)\big)\\&\qquad\qquad-f\big(x(t),y(t)\big)\Big].\end{aligned}\]

As \(t\) changes to \(t+\delta t\), \(x\) and \(y\) change by \(\delta x=x(t+\delta t)-x(t)\) and \(\delta y=y(t+\delta t)-y(t)\). The trick is to make the change in \(f\) happen in two steps: first move \(x\) only (walk east), then move \(y\) only (walk north). Adding and subtracting \(f(x+\delta x,y)\):

\[\begin{aligned}&f(x+\delta x,y+\delta y)-f(x,y)\\&=\big[f(x+\delta x,y)-f(x,y)\big] +\big[f(x+\delta x,y+\delta y)-f(x+\delta x,y)\big].\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}&f(x+\delta x,y+\delta y)-f(x,y)\\&=\big[f(x+\delta x,y)-f(x,y)\big]\\&\quad+\big[f(x+\delta x,y+\delta y)-f(x+\delta x,y)\big].\end{aligned}\]

Divide by \(\delta t\) and multiply-and-divide each bracket by \(\delta x\) and \(\delta y\) respectively:

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dz}{dt}&=\lim_{\delta x\to0}\left[\frac{f(x+\delta x,y)-f(x,y)}{\delta x}\right]\\&\qquad\times\lim_{\delta t\to0}\left[\frac{\delta x}{\delta t}\right]\\&\quad+\lim_{\substack{\delta y\to0\\\delta x\to0}}\left[\frac{f(x+\delta x,y+\delta y)-f(x+\delta x,y)}{\delta y}\right]\\&\qquad\times\lim_{\delta t\to0}\left[\frac{\delta y}{\delta t}\right]\\&=\pd fx\frac{dx}{dt}+\pd fy\frac{dy}{dt}.\end{aligned}\]

The first bracket is exactly the definition of \(f_x\); the second becomes \(f_y\) evaluated at \((x,y)\) because \(\delta x\to0\) too (this step quietly uses continuity of \(f_y\)). As \(\delta t\to0\), \(\delta x\to0\) and \(\delta y\to0\).

Example 1.3.1

If \(z=f(x,y)\) where \(f(x,y)=x^2+2xy\) and \(x=\sin t,\ y=\cos t\), find \(dz/dt\).

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dz}{dt}&=\pd fx\frac{dx}{dt}+\pd fy\frac{dy}{dt}\\&=(2x+2y)\cos t+2x(-\sin t)\\&=2(\sin t+\cos t)\cos t-2\sin^2 t\\&=2\sin t\cos t+2(\cos^2t-\sin^2t)\\&=\sin2t+2\cos2t.\end{aligned}\]
  • The first term \((2x+2y)\cos t\) is the east route: slope in the \(x\)-direction times the speed of \(x\). The second, \(2x(-\sin t)\), is the north route.
  • Replacing \(x,y\) by \(\sin t,\cos t\) at the end puts the answer in terms of the only true variable, time.

Check by direct substitution: \(z=\sin^2t+2\sin t\cos t=\sin^2t+\sin2t\), so \(\dfrac{dz}{dt}\) \(=2\sin t\cos t+2\cos2t\) \(=\sin2t+2\cos2t.\) ✓

Special case: \(y\) is a function of \(x\)

What is this?

Now the hiker's path is fixed in advance: her north position is tied to her east position by a rule, so walking east automatically drags her north too. Push her a little east and her height changes in two ways: directly, because the ground slopes east; and indirectly, because the rule has moved her north as well, where the ground slopes differently. The total derivative counts both.

Suppose \(z=f(x,y)\) and \(y=y(x)\). Then \(z\) is really a function of \(x\) only. Put \(x=t\) in Theorem 1.3.1 (so \(dx/dt=1\)):

\[\frac{dz}{dx}=\pd fx+\pd fy\frac{dy}{dx}.\]

In words: the total derivative \(dz/dx\) counts both the direct effect of \(x\) (\(\partial f/\partial x\), with \(y\) frozen) and the indirect effect through \(y\) (\(\partial f/\partial y\times dy/dx\)).

Reading the formula
\(\dfrac{dz}{dx}\)
height change per east step along the actual path (straight \(d\): along the path, \(z\) depends on \(x\) alone).
\(\pd fx\)
the direct effect: slope going east if she were not dragged north.
\(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\)
how far north the rule drags her per east step.
\(\pd fy\dfrac{dy}{dx}\)
the indirect effect: north slope × north distance gained per east step.
Watch out

\(\dfrac{dz}{dx}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\) are different here. The partial derivative pretends \(y\) stays still; the total derivative lets \(y\) follow its rule \(y(x)\). For instance, with \(f=xy\) and \(y=x^2\): the partial derivative is \(\partial f/\partial x=y=x^2\), but the total derivative is \(y+x\cdot2x=3x^2\), which matches differentiating \(z=x^3\) directly.

Example 1.3.2

\(z=f(x,y)=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac xy\right)\), \(y=\sin x\). Using \(\frac{d}{du}\tan^{-1}u=\frac1{1+u^2}\):

\[\begin{aligned}\pd fx&=\frac{1}{1+x^2/y^2}\cdot\frac1y\\&=\frac{y}{x^2+y^2},\\ \pd fy&=\frac{1}{1+x^2/y^2}\cdot\left(-\frac{x}{y^2}\right)\\&=-\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}.\end{aligned}\]

These two are the slopes of the hill in the east and north directions, found as if the path rule did not exist.

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dz}{dx}&=\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}-\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\cos x\\&=\frac{\sin x-x\cos x}{x^2+\sin^2x}.\end{aligned}\]

The first fraction is the direct effect; the second is the north slope times \(dy/dx=\cos x\), the indirect effect through the path rule \(y=\sin x\).

Show the calculation: the simplifications
  1. Let \(w=x/y\). Then \(\pd wx=\dfrac1y\) (\(y\) constant) and \(\pd wy=-\dfrac{x}{y^2}\) (\(x\) constant, derivative of \(y^{-1}\) is \(-y^{-2}\)).
  2. \(\pd fx=\dfrac{1}{1+w^2}\cdot\dfrac1y=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2/y^2}\cdot\dfrac1y\). Multiply top and bottom of the first fraction by \(y^2\) to clear the small fraction: \(\dfrac{y^2}{y^2+x^2}\cdot\dfrac1y=\dfrac{y}{x^2+y^2}\).
  3. \(\pd fy=\dfrac{y^2}{x^2+y^2}\cdot\left(-\dfrac{x}{y^2}\right)=-\dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2}\) (the \(y^2\) cancels).
  4. Total derivative: \(\dfrac{dz}{dx}=\pd fx+\pd fy\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) with \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\cos x\), giving \(\dfrac{y-x\cos x}{x^2+y^2}\) over a common denominator.
  5. Finally replace \(y\) by \(\sin x\) everywhere, so the answer is in terms of \(x\) only.

Implicit differentiation, again

What is this?

An equation like \(f(x,y)=0\) draws a curve on the map: it is the contour line at height zero. Walk along a contour line and your height does not change at all. So as you step east, the direct effect (the east slope) and the indirect effect (the north movement the curve forces on you) must exactly cancel. That cancellation tells you how steeply the curve must turn north, which is its slope \(dy/dx\).

If \(y\) is defined as an implicit function of \(x\) by the equation \(z=f(x,y)=0\) (meaning \(y\) is not given by its own formula, only by the equation linking it to \(x\)), then \(z\) is constantly zero along the curve, so \(dz/dx\equiv0\). The special case above gives \(0=f_x+f_y\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), so

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{\partial f/\partial x}{\partial f/\partial y}\qquad\text{whenever }\pd fy\ne0.\]

In words: slope of the curve \(f(x,y)=0\) = −(partial in \(x\)) ÷ (partial in \(y\)). Note the minus sign: it is not a cancellation of fractions.

Reading the formula
\(0=f_x+f_y\dfrac{dy}{dx}\)
"height does not change along a contour": direct effect + indirect effect = 0.
the minus sign
the indirect effect must undo the direct one. If the ground rises going east, the curve must turn towards the direction where the ground falls to stay level.
\(f_y\) on the bottom
the steeper the north slope, the smaller the north move needed to cancel a given east rise.
\(f_y\ne0\)
if the ground is flat going north, no north move can compensate, and the formula fails (the curve may go vertical there).

Quick check: on the circle \(x^2+y^2-25=0\) at \((3,4)\), \(-f_x/f_y=-2x/2y=-3/4\), the same as differentiating \(y=\sqrt{25-x^2}\) directly.

Example 1.3.3

The slope of the tangent at a point \((x,y)\) of the conic \(f(x,y)\) \(=ax^2+2hxy+by^2+2px+2qy+c\) \(=0\) is

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dy}{dx}&=-\frac{\partial f/\partial x}{\partial f/\partial y}\\&=-\frac{2ax+2hy+2p}{2hx+2by+2q}\\&=-\frac{ax+hy+p}{hx+by+q}.\end{aligned}\]

The curve is the zero contour of \(f\), so its slope is −(east slope)÷(north slope); each slope is a partial derivative with the other variable frozen.

(A conic is any curve given by a second-degree equation in \(x\) and \(y\): circle, ellipse, parabola or hyperbola.)

Change of variables

What is this?

Sometimes we describe positions on the map in a different way, for example by distance \(r\) and angle \(\theta\) from a lamp post instead of east and north. The hill has not changed; only the labels have. Changing variables asks: how fast does height change as I walk outward from the lamp post (angle fixed), or around it (distance fixed)? It is the same tree as before, except now the bottom row has two variables, and when you differentiate with respect to one you hold the other fixed.

Theorem 1.3.2 (Change of variables)

If \(z=f(x,y)\) and \(x=x(u,v),\ y=y(u,v)\), then

\[\begin{aligned}\pd zu&=\pd fx\pd xu+\pd fy\pd yu,\\ \pd zv&=\pd fx\pd xv+\pd fy\pd yv.\end{aligned}\]

In words: same tree as before, but now \(x\) and \(y\) each depend on two variables \(u,v\). To differentiate with respect to \(u\), hold \(v\) fixed and follow every path from \(z\) to \(u\).

Reading the formula
\(\pd zu\)
height change per unit step in the new \(u\)-direction, with \(v\) frozen (curly \(\partial\) because \(v\) is held fixed).
\(\pd xu,\ \pd yu\)
how far east and how far north one \(u\)-step carries you.
\(\pd fx\pd xu\)
the east route: east slope × east distance per \(u\)-step.
sum of two terms
the east route plus the north route, exactly as for the hiker; only "time" has been replaced by "a step in \(u\)".

Proof. Holding \(v\) fixed, \(x\) and \(y\) are functions of the single variable \(u\), so Theorem 1.3.1 applies with \(u\) in place of \(t\) (and \(d\) becoming \(\partial\) because \(v\) is held fixed). Likewise for \(v\).

Example 1.3.4

\(z=f(x,y)=e^{2xy}\) with the change of variables to polar coordinates \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\). Here \(f_x=2ye^{2xy}\), \(f_y=2xe^{2xy}\), \(\partial x/\partial r=\cos\theta\), \(\partial y/\partial r=\sin\theta\). Also \(2xy=2r^2\cos\theta\sin\theta=r^2\sin2\theta\). Walking outward (\(r\) increasing, \(\theta\) fixed) moves you \(\cos\theta\) east and \(\sin\theta\) north per unit step. So

\(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) are the lower branch labels of the tree for \(r\): how far east and north one outward step carries you.

\[\begin{aligned}\pd zr&=2ye^{2xy}\cos\theta+2xe^{2xy}\sin\theta\\&=2r\,e^{r^2\sin2\theta}\sin2\theta,\end{aligned}\]

(since \(2y\cos\theta+2x\sin\theta\) \(=4r\sin\theta\cos\theta\) \(=2r\sin2\theta\)), and with \(\partial x/\partial\theta=-r\sin\theta\), \(\partial y/\partial\theta=r\cos\theta\):

Walking around the circle (\(\theta\) increasing, \(r\) fixed) moves you sideways at speed proportional to \(r\): a bigger circle means more ground per unit of angle.

\[\begin{aligned}\pd z\theta&=2ye^{2xy}(-r\sin\theta) +2xe^{2xy}\,r\cos\theta\\&=2r^2e^{r^2\sin2\theta}\cos2\theta.\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}\pd z\theta&=2ye^{2xy}(-r\sin\theta)\\&\quad+2xe^{2xy}\,r\cos\theta\\&=2r^2e^{r^2\sin2\theta}\cos2\theta.\end{aligned}\]

Check: substitute first, \(z=e^{r^2\sin2\theta}\), and differentiate directly; both answers agree. ✓

Show the calculation: simplifying both derivatives, and the check
  1. The exponent. \(2xy\) \(=2(r\cos\theta)(r\sin\theta)\) \(=r^2(2\sin\theta\cos\theta)\) \(=r^2\sin2\theta\), using the double-angle formula \(\sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta\).
  2. \(\partial z/\partial r\). Factor out \(e^{2xy}\): \(e^{2xy}\,(2y\cos\theta+2x\sin\theta)\). Substitute \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\) in the bracket: \(2r\sin\theta\cos\theta+2r\cos\theta\sin\theta\) \(=4r\sin\theta\cos\theta\) \(=2r\sin2\theta\).
  3. \(\partial z/\partial\theta\). Factor out \(e^{2xy}\): \(e^{2xy}\,(-2yr\sin\theta+2xr\cos\theta)\). Substitute: \(-2r^2\sin^2\theta+2r^2\cos^2\theta\) \(=2r^2(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta)\) \(=2r^2\cos2\theta\), using \(\cos2\theta=\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta\).
  4. Check. With \(z=e^{r^2\sin2\theta}\): differentiating the exponent with respect to \(r\) (\(\theta\) fixed) gives \(2r\sin2\theta\); with respect to \(\theta\) (\(r\) fixed) gives \(r^2\cdot2\cos2\theta\). Multiplying each by \(e^{r^2\sin2\theta}\) (chain rule for \(e^{(\ )}\)) reproduces both answers.
Beyond the handouts · second derivatives after a change of variables (exam type C1.1)

The handout stops at first derivatives, but December Q1(a) almost always asks for second derivatives in new coordinates (e.g. "show that \(f_{xx}+f_{yy}=g_{rr}+\frac1rg_r+\frac1{r^2}g_{\theta\theta}\)"). In the picture: the same hill \(f\) is described in east/north labels and in distance/angle labels \(g\); the question asks you to prove that a "curvature" quantity comes out the same in both languages. Two facts make this work.

Fact 1. The chain rule is an operator. Theorem 1.3.2 run backwards (from \(f(x,y)=g(u,v)\) to \(x\)-derivatives) says, for any function \(\Box\):

\[\pd{\Box}{x}=\pd{\Box}{u}\pd ux+\pd{\Box}{v}\pd vx.\]

An operator is a rule that turns one function into another ("differentiate with respect to \(x\)" is one). The formula above is a recipe that works on whatever you feed it, not just on \(f\). To get \(f_{xx}\), apply it to \(\Box=f_x\), which you have already written in terms of \(g_u,g_v\) and \(u,v\). Remember that \(g_u\) and \(g_v\) are themselves functions of \(u\) and \(v\), so they need the operator too, and the coefficients (like \(\cos\theta\)) need the product rule.

Fact 2. Partial derivatives do not flip. \(\pd rx\ne1\Big/\pd xr\) in general, because different variables are held fixed (\(y\) on the left, \(\theta\) on the right). In the picture: \(\partial r/\partial x\) asks "step east with north frozen, how much further from the lamp post?", while \(\partial x/\partial r\) asks "step outward with angle frozen, how far east?". These are different walks, so there is no reason for one to be the reciprocal of the other. For polar coordinates, from \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), \(\theta=\tan^{-1}(y/x)\):

\[\begin{aligned}\pd rx&=\frac xr=\cos\theta, & \pd ry&=\sin\theta,\\ \pd\theta x&=-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}, & \pd\theta y&=\frac{\cos\theta}{r},\end{aligned}\]

whereas \(\pd xr=\cos\theta\) but \(\pd x\theta=-r\sin\theta\), and \(1/(\partial x/\partial\theta)\ne\partial\theta/\partial x\).

How to do a C1.1 "show that" question

  1. Do: find the derivatives of the new variables with respect to the old ones (\(r_x, r_y, \theta_x, \theta_y\)) by writing \(r,\theta\) as formulas in \(x,y\) and differentiating. These are the lower branch labels of the tree for "\(\partial/\partial x\) of something written in \(r,\theta\)". By Fact 2 you cannot get them by flipping \(x_r\), \(x_\theta\).
  2. Do: write the first derivatives \(f_x\), \(f_y\) with the tree: \(f_x=g_r\,r_x+g_\theta\,\theta_x\), and similarly for \(f_y\). Two routes from \(f\) to \(x\): through \(r\) and through \(\theta\). Multiply along each, add.
  3. Do: read off the operators \(\pd{}{x}=r_x\pd{}{r}+\theta_x\pd{}{\theta}\) and \(\pd{}{y}=r_y\pd{}{r}+\theta_y\pd{}{\theta}\). By Fact 1 the same tree works for any function, so this is a reusable machine for "differentiate in \(x\)".
  4. Do: feed \(f_x\) back into the \(\partial/\partial x\) operator: compute \(P=\pd{}{r}(f_x)\) and \(Q=\pd{}{\theta}(f_x)\) separately, then \(f_{xx}=r_x\,P+\theta_x\,Q\). \(f_x\) is a new function on the hill (its east slope) that you have written in \(r,\theta\); differentiating it again in \(x\) means following the same two routes. Doing \(P\) and \(Q\) as separate pieces keeps the bookkeeping clean.
  5. Do: inside \(P\) and \(Q\), use the product rule on every term, and differentiate \(g_r\), \(g_\theta\) as well as the coefficients. \(g_r\) and \(g_\theta\) are not constants: they are slopes that themselves change as you move, so they have their own \(r\)- and \(\theta\)-derivatives. The coefficients (\(\cos\theta\), \(1/r\)) also change as you move. Missing either loses terms.
  6. Do: use \(g_{r\theta}=g_{\theta r}\) to combine mixed terms, repeat steps 4–5 for \(f_{yy}\), then add and collect by \(g_{rr}, g_{r\theta}, g_r, g_\theta, g_{\theta\theta}\). Mixed partials agree for smooth functions (§2); collecting by derivative type makes the cancellations visible, and \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\) tidies the rest.

Worked example: Laplacian in polar coordinates. Let \(g(r,\theta)=f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\). The Laplacian of \(f\) is \(f_{xx}+f_{yy}\), the sum of the two unmixed second derivatives.

  1. First derivatives: \(f_x=\cos\theta\,g_r-\dfrac{\sin\theta}{r}\,g_\theta\) and \(f_y=\sin\theta\,g_r+\dfrac{\cos\theta}{r}\,g_\theta\).
  2. Apply the operator \(\pd{}{x}=\cos\theta\pd{}{r}-\dfrac{\sin\theta}{r}\pd{}{\theta}\) to \(f_x\). Name the two pieces: \[\begin{aligned}P&=\pd{}{r}(f_x)\\&=\cos\theta\,g_{rr}-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}g_{r\theta} +\frac{\sin\theta}{r^2}g_\theta,\\ Q&=\pd{}{\theta}(f_x)\\&=-\sin\theta\,g_r+\cos\theta\,g_{r\theta} -\frac{\cos\theta}{r}g_\theta-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}g_{\theta\theta}.\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}P&=\pd{}{r}(f_x)\\&=\cos\theta\,g_{rr}-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}g_{r\theta}\\&\quad+\frac{\sin\theta}{r^2}g_\theta,\\ Q&=\pd{}{\theta}(f_x)\\&=-\sin\theta\,g_r+\cos\theta\,g_{r\theta}\\&\quad-\frac{\cos\theta}{r}g_\theta-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}g_{\theta\theta}.\end{aligned}\] Then \[f_{xx}=\cos\theta\,P-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\,Q.\]
  3. Do the same for \(f_{yy}\) with \(\pd{}{y}=\sin\theta\pd{}{r}+\dfrac{\cos\theta}{r}\pd{}{\theta}\), then add. Every \(g_{r\theta}\) and \(g_\theta\) term cancels and \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\) leaves \[\boxed{\begin{aligned}&\pd{^2f}{x^2}+\pd{^2f}{y^2}\\&=\pd{^2g}{r^2}+\frac1r\pd gr+\frac1{r^2}\pd{^2g}{\theta^2}\end{aligned}}\]
Show the calculation: the full derivation, every term

Step A: derivatives of \(r\) and \(\theta\) with respect to \(x\) and \(y\). From \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\): \(r_x=\dfrac{2x}{2\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}=\dfrac xr=\cos\theta\) and likewise \(r_y=\dfrac yr=\sin\theta\). From \(\theta=\tan^{-1}(y/x)\), using \(\frac{d}{dw}\tan^{-1}w=\frac1{1+w^2}\):

\[\begin{aligned}\theta_x&=\frac{1}{1+y^2/x^2}\cdot\left(-\frac{y}{x^2}\right)\\&=-\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}=-\frac{\sin\theta}{r},\\ \theta_y&=\frac{1}{1+y^2/x^2}\cdot\frac1x\\&=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}=\frac{\cos\theta}{r}.\end{aligned}\]

(Multiply top and bottom by \(x^2\), then use \(y=r\sin\theta\), \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(x^2+y^2=r^2\).)

Step B: first derivatives. The chain rule (Theorem 1.3.2 with the roles of the variables swapped) gives

\[\begin{aligned}f_x&=g_r\,r_x+g_\theta\,\theta_x\\&=\cos\theta\,g_r-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}g_\theta,\\ f_y&=g_r\,r_y+g_\theta\,\theta_y\\&=\sin\theta\,g_r+\frac{\cos\theta}{r}g_\theta.\end{aligned}\]

So, as operators, \(\pd{}{x}=\cos\theta\pd{}{r}-\dfrac{\sin\theta}{r}\pd{}{\theta}\) and \(\pd{}{y}=\sin\theta\pd{}{r}+\dfrac{\cos\theta}{r}\pd{}{\theta}\).

Step C: \(f_{xx}\). Apply \(\partial/\partial x\) to \(f_x\). Two pieces are needed.

  • \(\pd{}{r}(f_x)\) \(=\cos\theta\,g_{rr}-\sin\theta\,\pd{}{r}\!\left(\dfrac{g_\theta}{r}\right)\) \(=\cos\theta\,g_{rr}-\dfrac{\sin\theta}{r}g_{r\theta}+\dfrac{\sin\theta}{r^2}g_\theta\). (\(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) are constants when \(\theta\) is held fixed; \(g_\theta/r\) needs the product rule, and \((1/r)'=-1/r^2\).)
  • \(\pd{}{\theta}(f_x)\) \(=-\sin\theta\,g_r\) \(+\:\cos\theta\,g_{r\theta}\) \(-\:\dfrac1r\big(\cos\theta\,g_\theta+\sin\theta\,g_{\theta\theta}\big)\). (Now \(r\) is constant; each product \(\cos\theta\,g_r\) and \(\sin\theta\,g_\theta\) needs the product rule in \(\theta\); \(g_{r\theta}=g_{\theta r}\) by the mixed-partials theorem.)

Then \(f_{xx}=\cos\theta\cdot\pd{}{r}(f_x)-\dfrac{\sin\theta}{r}\cdot\pd{}{\theta}(f_x)\). Multiplying out and collecting like terms:

\[\begin{aligned}f_{xx}&=\cos^2\theta\,g_{rr}-\frac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r}g_{r\theta} +\frac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r^2}g_\theta+\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r}g_r +\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2}g_{\theta\theta}.\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}f_{xx}&=\cos^2\theta\,g_{rr}-\frac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r}g_{r\theta}\\&\quad+\frac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r^2}g_\theta+\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r}g_r\\&\quad+\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2}g_{\theta\theta}.\end{aligned}\]

(The \(g_{r\theta}\) terms: \(\cos\theta\cdot(-\frac{\sin\theta}{r})\) plus \((-\frac{\sin\theta}{r})\cdot\cos\theta\). The \(g_\theta\) terms: \(\cos\theta\cdot\frac{\sin\theta}{r^2}\) plus \((-\frac{\sin\theta}{r})(-\frac{\cos\theta}{r})\).)

Step D: \(f_{yy}\). The same two pieces for \(f_y\):

  • \(\pd{}{r}(f_y)\) \(=\sin\theta\,g_{rr}+\dfrac{\cos\theta}{r}g_{r\theta}-\dfrac{\cos\theta}{r^2}g_\theta\),
  • \(\pd{}{\theta}(f_y)\) \(=\cos\theta\,g_r\) \(+\:\sin\theta\,g_{r\theta}\) \(+\:\dfrac1r\big(-\sin\theta\,g_\theta+\cos\theta\,g_{\theta\theta}\big)\).

Then \(f_{yy}=\sin\theta\cdot\pd{}{r}(f_y)+\dfrac{\cos\theta}{r}\cdot\pd{}{\theta}(f_y)\), which collects to

\[\begin{aligned}f_{yy}&=\sin^2\theta\,g_{rr}+\frac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r}g_{r\theta} -\frac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r^2}g_\theta+\frac{\cos^2\theta}{r}g_r +\frac{\cos^2\theta}{r^2}g_{\theta\theta}.\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}f_{yy}&=\sin^2\theta\,g_{rr}+\frac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r}g_{r\theta}\\&\quad-\frac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r^2}g_\theta+\frac{\cos^2\theta}{r}g_r\\&\quad+\frac{\cos^2\theta}{r^2}g_{\theta\theta}.\end{aligned}\]

Step E: add. Term by term: \(g_{rr}\) has \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\); the \(g_{r\theta}\) and \(g_\theta\) terms have equal and opposite coefficients and cancel; \(g_r\) has \(\frac{\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta}{r}=\frac1r\); \(g_{\theta\theta}\) has \(\frac1{r^2}\). This is the boxed result. (Both intermediate formulas were checked in sympy.)

In words: the Laplacian (sum of the two unmixed second derivatives) in polar coordinates = second \(r\)-derivative + (1/r)× first \(r\)-derivative + (1/r²)× second \(\theta\)-derivative.

4Taylor's theorem in two variables (handout §1.4, pp. 10–13) ~9 min

What is this?

Close to where you stand, a complicated hill can be replaced by a much simpler shape that copies it. The crudest copy only gets your height right (a flat floor at your level). A better copy also gets the tilt right (a sloping plank lying on the ground). A better one still also gets the bend right (a curved sheet that dips or bulges like the ground). Taylor's theorem is the recipe for these copies, and it tells you that each extra layer copies one more feature of the hill.

You met the one-variable version in first year: replace a curve near a point by a polynomial that matches its value and its derivatives there. Here it is restated, then extended to two variables.

Taylor's theorem, one variable

If \(f(x)\) has continuous derivatives \(f'(x),f''(x),\dots,f^{(N-1)}(x)\) in \([a,b]\) and \(f^{(N)}(x)\) exists in \((a,b)\), then

\[\begin{aligned}f(a+h)&=f(a)+\frac{h}{1!}f'(a)+\frac{h^2}{2!}f''(a)+\cdots +\frac{h^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}f^{(N-1)}(a)+R_N(h),\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}f(a+h)&=f(a)+\frac{h}{1!}f'(a)+\frac{h^2}{2!}f''(a)+\cdots\\&\quad+\frac{h^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}f^{(N-1)}(a)+R_N(h),\end{aligned}\]

where \(R_N(h)\) is the error term (also called the remainder): the difference between the true value and the polynomial.

In words: value a small step \(h\) away = value here + step × slope + (step²/2!) × curvature + … + error.

Writing \(h=x-a\) gives the equivalent form

\[\begin{aligned}f(x)&=f(a)+(x-a)f'(a) +\frac{(x-a)^2}{2!}f''(a)+\cdots+R'_N(x).\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}f(x)&=f(a)+(x-a)f'(a)\\&\quad+\frac{(x-a)^2}{2!}f''(a)+\cdots+R'_N(x).\end{aligned}\]

When \(a=0\) it is called Maclaurin's theorem.

Example 1.4.1: approximating \(y=e^x\) near \(x=0\)

Orange: \(e^x\). Teal: the Taylor polynomial. The first four are \(y=1\) (same height), \(y=1+x\) (same height and slope), \(y=1+x+x^2/2\) (same bend too), \(y=1+x+x^2/2+x^3/6\). Each extra term hugs the curve over a wider range.

Two variables

On a surface you can step in two directions at once: \(h\) in the \(x\)-direction and \(k\) in the \(y\)-direction. The idea is the same as in one variable, but each layer now has an \(x\)-part, a \(y\)-part and (from the second layer on) a mixed part.

Taylor's theorem, two variables

Suppose \(f(x,y)\) has continuous partial derivatives of all orders up to and including \(N-1\) inside and on the boundary of the rectangle with vertices \((a,b),(a+c,b),(a,b+d),(a+c,b+d)\), and all \(N\)-th order partial derivatives exist within this rectangle. Then

\[\begin{aligned}&f(a+h,b+k)\\&=f(a,b)+\big[hf_x(a,b)+kf_y(a,b)\big]\\&\quad+\frac1{2!}\big[h^2f_{xx}(a,b)+2hkf_{xy}(a,b)\\&\qquad\quad+k^2f_{yy}(a,b)\big]+\cdots+R_N(h,k)\\&=\sum_{r=0}^{N-1}\frac1{r!}\left(h\pd{}{x}+k\pd{}{y}\right)^{\!r}f(a,b)\\&\quad+R_N(h,k),\end{aligned}\]

where \(R_N(h,k)\) is the error term.

In words: value at a nearby point = value here + (first-order change: step in \(x\) × slope in \(x\) + step in \(y\) × slope in \(y\)) + ½ × (second-order change) + … + error.

Reading the formula
\(f(a,b)\)
your height where you stand: the "same height" layer.
\(hf_x+kf_y\)
tilt × step in each direction: the slope east times how far east you step, plus the slope north times how far north you step. This is the height you gain because the ground is tilted (the "same tilt" layer).
\(\tfrac12\big[h^2f_{xx}+\dots\big]\)
how the ground bends under your step: the extra height (or loss of height) because the ground curves up or down, the "same bend" layer. The \(\tfrac12\) is the same \(1/2!\) as in one variable.
\(R_N(h,k)\)
everything the copy has missed; it shrinks quickly as the step gets smaller.

Reading the compact form. \(\left(h\pd{}{x}+k\pd{}{y}\right)^r\) is expanded like a binomial \((A+B)^r\), with \(\partial/\partial x\) and \(\partial/\partial y\) acting on \(f\). For \(r=2\): \(h^2f_{xx}+2hkf_{xy}+k^2f_{yy}\) (using \(f_{xy}=f_{yx}\)). For \(r=3\) the coefficients would be \(1,3,3,1\). The generalisation to more than two variables works the same way.

Why the middle coefficient is 2. Squaring \((A+B)\) without letting the letters swap gives four pieces, \(A^2+AB+BA+B^2\). Here that means a bend measured "\(x\) then \(y\)" (\(hk\,f_{xy}\)) and a bend measured "\(y\) then \(x\)" (\(kh\,f_{yx}\)). The two orders give the same number, because mixed partials are equal (§2), so the two pieces add up to \(2hkf_{xy}\). In the picture: the mixed term measures how the east–west slope changes as you move north (a twist in the ground), and a diagonal step feels that twist from both directions.

Writing \(h=x-a,\ k=y-b\) gives the equivalent form

\[\begin{aligned}f(x,y)&=f(a,b)+\big[(x-a)f_x(a,b)\\&\qquad+(y-b)f_y(a,b)\big]\\&\quad+\frac1{2!}\big[(x-a)^2f_{xx}(a,b)\\&\qquad+2(x-a)(y-b)f_{xy}(a,b)\\&\qquad+(y-b)^2f_{yy}(a,b)\big]+\cdots+R'_N(x,y).\end{aligned}\]

If \(R'_N(x,y)\to0\) as \(N\to\infty\), the infinite sum is the Taylor series representation of \(f(x,y)\).

Three useful truncations

Stopping after different numbers of terms gives successive approximations, all meeting \(z=f(x,y)\) at the point \((a,b)\). Each one copies one more feature of the hill:

Constant approximation\(z=f(a,b)\)
copies the height only: a horizontal plane at the right level
Tangent plane approximation\(z\) \(=f(a,b)\) \(+\:(x-a)f_x(a,b)\) \(+\:(y-b)f_y(a,b)\)
copies height and tilt: a tilted plane with the right slopes in both directions (also called the linear approximation)
Quadratic surface approximationtangent plane \(+\dfrac1{2!}\big[(x-a)^2f_{xx}\) \(+\:2(x-a)(y-b)f_{xy}\) \(+\:(y-b)^2f_{yy}\big]\)
copies height, tilt and bend: a curved surface with the right curvatures

How to build a tangent-plane or quadratic approximation (the exam procedure):

  1. Do: write down the point \((a,b)\) and compute \(f(a,b)\). This is the "same height" layer: every copy must touch the hill where you stand.
  2. Do: find \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) and evaluate them at \((a,b)\). These are the slopes east and north; they give the tilt of the plank.
  3. Do: tangent plane \(z=f(a,b)\) \(+\,(x-a)f_x(a,b)\) \(+\,(y-b)f_y(a,b)\); stop here if only the linear approximation is asked. Height here + tilt × step in each direction.
  4. Do: for the quadratic, find \(f_{xx},f_{xy},f_{yy}\) and evaluate them at \((a,b)\). These measure how the ground bends east–west, with a twist, and north–south.
  5. Do: add \(\tfrac12\times\big[\) \((x-a)^2f_{xx}\) \(+\,2(x-a)(y-b)f_{xy}\) \(+\,(y-b)^2f_{yy}\) \(\big]\) to the tangent plane, remembering the 2 on the middle term. This is the "same bend" layer; the 2 counts the two orders \(xy\) and \(yx\).
  6. Do: to estimate a value, substitute the nearby point into the approximation (not into \(f\)). The copy is easy to evaluate; the closer the point, the smaller the error \(R_N\).
Example 1.4.2

Find the tangent plane and quadratic surface approximations given by Taylor's theorem at the point \((1,0)\) for the cone \(f(x,y)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). Hence estimate \(f(0.9,0.1)\).

The derivatives are

\[\begin{aligned}f_x&=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}, & f_y&=\frac{y}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}},\\ f_{xx}&=\frac{y^2}{\sqrt{(x^2+y^2)^3}}, & f_{xy}&=\frac{-xy}{\sqrt{(x^2+y^2)^3}},\\ f_{yy}&=\frac{x^2}{\sqrt{(x^2+y^2)^3}},\end{aligned}\]

which exist everywhere except at the origin \((0,0)\) (the tip of the cone). At \((1,0)\): \(f\) \(=1,\ f_x\) \(=1,\ f_y\) \(=0,\ f_{xx}\) \(=0,\ f_{xy}\) \(=0,\ f_{yy}\) \(=1\).Steps 1, 2 and 4: height 1; the cone rises at slope 1 going east (straight up its side) and is level going north; it does not bend east–west (the side of a cone is a straight line) but does bend north–south (around the circular cross-section).

Show the calculation: the five derivatives of \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\)

Write \(f=(x^2+y^2)^{1/2}\) and let \(s=x^2+y^2\) for short.

  1. \(f_x=\tfrac12s^{-1/2}\cdot2x=x\,s^{-1/2}\): chain rule (power rule on the outside, \(\partial s/\partial x=2x\) inside). By the same steps, \(f_y=y\,s^{-1/2}\).
  2. \(f_{xx}=\pd{}{x}\big(x\,s^{-1/2}\big)\): product rule, because both \(x\) and \(s^{-1/2}\) contain \(x\): \[\begin{aligned}f_{xx}&=1\cdot s^{-1/2}+x\cdot\left(-\tfrac12\right)s^{-3/2}\cdot2x\\&=\frac{s-x^2}{s^{3/2}}=\frac{y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^{3/2}}.\end{aligned}\] (Put both terms over the common denominator \(s^{3/2}\); then \(s-x^2=y^2\).)
  3. \(f_{xy}=\pd{}{y}\big(x\,s^{-1/2}\big)\): now \(x\) is a constant, so only \(s^{-1/2}\) is differentiated: \(x\cdot(-\tfrac12)s^{-3/2}\cdot2y=-\dfrac{xy}{s^{3/2}}\).
  4. \(f_{yy}\): the same as step 2 with \(x\) and \(y\) swapped, so \(\dfrac{x^2}{s^{3/2}}\).
  5. At \((1,0)\): \(s=1\), so \(f=1\), \(f_x=1\), \(f_y=0\), \(f_{xx}=0^2/1=0\), \(f_{xy}=0\), \(f_{yy}=1^2/1=1\).

Tangent plane:

\[\begin{aligned}z&=f(1,0)+(x-1)f_x(1,0)+(y-0)f_y(1,0)\\&=1+(x-1)+y\times0=x.\end{aligned}\]

Step 3: height 1, plus slope 1 times the step \(x-1\) east, plus slope 0 times the step north.

Quadratic surface:

\[\begin{aligned}z&=1+(x-1)+y\times0 +\frac1{2!}\Big[(x-1)^2\times0+2(x-1)y\times0 +y^2\times1\Big]\\&=x+\tfrac12y^2.\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}z&=1+(x-1)+y\times0\\&\quad+\frac1{2!}\Big[(x-1)^2\times0+2(x-1)y\times0\\&\qquad\quad+y^2\times1\Big]\\&=x+\tfrac12y^2.\end{aligned}\]

Step 5: only the north–south bend is non-zero, so the only correction is \(\tfrac12y^2\), which lifts the plane as you move sideways, just as the cone curves up away from the line \(y=0\).

Hence, using the tangent plane, \(f(0.9,0.1)\approx0.9\); using the quadratic surface, \(f(0.9,0.1)\approx0.9+0.1^2/2=0.905\). The correct value is \(f(0.9,0.1)=\sqrt{0.82}=0.9055\) (to 4 significant figures). Copying the bend as well as the tilt brought the estimate much closer. A bound for the error in approximating a function near a point \((a,b)\) can be obtained by summing over-estimates of the sizes (moduli) of the terms in \(R_N(x,y)\).

The cone and its approximations at \((1,0)\)

Colour-shaded surface: the cone \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). Orange: the chosen approximation. All touch at the dot \((1,0,1)\); switch between the three. The horizontal plane only matches the height; the tangent plane \(z=x\) also matches the slopes, so it lies along the cone in the \(x\)-direction; the quadratic surface \(z=x+y^2/2\) also bends upward in the \(y\)-direction, following the cone's sideways curvature, which no plane can do.

5Maxima, minima and saddle points (handout §1.5, pp. 13–18) ~15 min

What is this?

Walk around a hilly park. A local maximum is a hilltop: from there every small step takes you down, even if a taller hill stands somewhere else. A local minimum is the bottom of a dip: every small step takes you up. "Local" means you only compare with the ground right around you, like being the tallest person in your own row rather than in the whole stadium.

Definitions (local extrema)

A function \(f(x,y)\) has a local (relative) maximum at a point \((a,b)\) if, for all points \((x,y)\) close to \((a,b)\),

\[f(x,y)\le f(a,b).\]

It has a local (relative) minimum at \((a,b)\) if, for all points \((x,y)\) close to \((a,b)\),

\[f(x,y)\ge f(a,b).\]

In words: a local maximum is a hilltop: no nearby point is higher (though a different hill far away may be). A local minimum is the bottom of a dip: no nearby point is lower.

Local maxima and a local minimum on one surface

Two hilltops (red): each is a local maximum, since no point near it is higher, even though one peak is lower than the other. One dip (orange): a local minimum, since no point near it is lower. The small rings around each point are the level curves just below a peak or just above the dip.

Where to look: stationary points

What is this?

On a smooth hilltop the ground is level: put a marble there and it does not roll east, west, north or south. The same is true at the bottom of a smooth dip. So hilltops and dips can only be at flat spots, where the slope in the \(x\)-direction and the slope in the \(y\)-direction are both zero. Finding the flat spots is how you shortlist the candidates.

At a local maximum or minimum at \((a,b)\), the curves lying in the two vertical planes \(x=a\) and \(y=b\) must also have a maximum or minimum there (a hilltop is the top of every path across it). From single-variable calculus, their slopes are zero:

\[\pd fx(a,b)=\pd fy(a,b)=0.\]

At a hilltop, both slices are flat

At the top \((a,b)\): the teal curve \(g(x)=f(x,b)\) lies in the plane \(y=b\) and the purple curve \(h(y)=f(a,y)\) lies in the plane \(x=a\). Both have their highest point at the top, so both have a horizontal tangent (orange and red lines): \(\partial f/\partial x=0\) and \(\partial f/\partial y=0\). The dashed line drops to \((a,b,0)\) on the floor.

Definition (stationary point)

If \(f_x(a,b)=f_y(a,b)=0\), the point \((a,b)\) is a stationary point of \(f\).

In words: a stationary point is where the surface is flat in both the \(x\)- and \(y\)-directions, so its tangent plane is horizontal.

What is this? (saddle point)

Not every flat spot is a top or a bottom. Think of the middle of a horse saddle: along the horse's back the seat curves up in front and behind, so you sit at the lowest point; across the horse it curves down towards both stirrups, so you sit at the highest point. The seat is flat at the centre, but it is a top one way and a bottom the other.

Definition (saddle point)

A stationary point can be neither a local maximum nor a local minimum. If along one path through \((a,b)\) the point is a local minimum, while along a different path it is a local maximum, \((a,b)\) is a saddle point. There is no equivalent for functions of one variable. (The nearest one-variable cousin, a stationary point of inflection like \(x^3\) at 0, is different: there the function rises on one side and falls on the other along the same line.)

Analogy

A mountain pass. Walking along the ridge from one peak to the other, the pass is your lowest point. Walking across the ridge from one valley to the other, the pass is your highest point. The ground is flat at the pass, yet it is neither a top nor a bottom.

What is this? (global extrema)

Now fence off a region of the park and ask for the very highest and very lowest ground inside the fence. The highest point might be a smooth hilltop (a flat spot), a sharp peak where the slope is not defined (like the tip of a cone), or simply a spot on the fence itself where the ground is still rising as it leaves the region.

Global extrema on a domain. To find the largest (smallest) value of \(f(x,y)\) within a domain (these largest and smallest values are called extrema), we must consider all of:

  • the stationary points,
  • the points where \(f_x\) or \(f_y\) fails to exist (for example the tip of a cone),
  • the boundary points of the domain.

The first two sets together are collectively called critical points. (This definition is not universal: some books use "critical point" to mean the same as "stationary point". Recent May papers say "critical points" and mean the points where \(f_x=f_y=0\).)

Example 1.5.1

1. \(f(x,y)=x^2+2y^2\): \(f_x=2x,\ f_y=4y\). Setting both to zero gives \((x,y)=(0,0)\), the only stationary point. Since \(f(0,0)=0\) and clearly \(f(x,y)\ge0\) everywhere, this is a local minimum (also global).

2. \(f(x,y)=2x-x^2-2y^2\): \(f_x=2-2x,\ f_y=-4y\). Setting both to zero gives \((1,0)\), the only stationary point. Since

\[\begin{aligned}f(x,y)-f(1,0)&=2x-x^2-2y^2-1\\&=-(x-1)^2-2y^2\\&\lt0\quad\text{for all }(x,y)\ne(1,0),\end{aligned}\]

we have \(f(x,y)\le f(1,0)\), and \((1,0)\) is a local maximum (also global).

3. \(f(x,y)=x^2-2y^2\): \(f_x=2x,\ f_y=-4y\), so \((0,0)\) is the only stationary point. However, \(f\) may be positive or negative near it depending on the relative sizes of \(|x|\) and \(|y|\). Along \(y=0\), \(f(x,0)=x^2\ge0\) (minimum along this path). Along \(x=0\), \(f(0,y)=-2y^2\le0\) (maximum along this path). Thus \((0,0)\) is a saddle point.

The three surfaces of Example 1.5.1

The red dot is the stationary point. For the saddle, the teal curve (along \(y=0\)) bends up and the purple curve (along \(x=0\)) bends down.

The second-derivative test (derived from Taylor's theorem)

What is this?

At a flat spot the tilt is zero, so in Taylor's theorem (§4) the "tilt × step" layer vanishes. What is left is the bend: whether the ground curves up or down as you step away. If it curves up whichever way you step, you are in a bowl (minimum); if it curves down every way, you are on a dome (maximum); if it curves up some ways and down others, you are on a saddle. The second-derivative test is a quick way to read this from three numbers, \(f_{xx}\), \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yy}\).

Inspecting the sign of \(f(a+h,b+k)-f(a,b)\) for small \(h,k\) is not always easy. Instead we use Taylor's theorem to derive a test similar to the second-derivative test for one variable. At a stationary point \((a,b)\), Taylor's theorem gives

\[\begin{aligned}f(a+h,b+k)&=f(a,b)+h\underbrace{\pd fx(a,b)}_{0}+k\underbrace{\pd fy(a,b)}_{0}\\&\quad+\frac12\Big[h^2f_{xx}+2hkf_{xy}+k^2f_{yy}\Big]\\&\quad+R_3(h,k).\end{aligned}\]

For \(h\) and \(k\) sufficiently small we may assume \(R_3(h,k)\) is negligible compared with the other terms, so

\[\begin{aligned}&f(a+h,b+k)-f(a,b)\\&\approx\frac12\Big[h^2f_{xx}(a,b)+2hkf_{xy}(a,b)+k^2f_{yy}(a,b)\Big].\end{aligned}\]

Let \(q=h^2f_{xx}+2hkf_{xy}+k^2f_{yy}\). In the picture, \(q\) is how the ground bends for a step \((h,k)\): half of it is the height you gain (\(q\gt0\)) or lose (\(q\lt0\)) by taking that step. So the whole question is: does \(q\) have the same sign for every direction of step? Completing the square in \(h\) (assuming \(f_{xx}\ne0\)):

\[\begin{aligned}q&=f_{xx}\left[h^2+2\frac{kf_{xy}}{f_{xx}}h+\left(\frac{kf_{xy}}{f_{xx}}\right)^2\right] +k^2f_{yy}-f_{xx}\left(\frac{kf_{xy}}{f_{xx}}\right)^2\\&=f_{xx}\left(h+\frac{kf_{xy}}{f_{xx}}\right)^2 +\frac{k^2}{f_{xx}}\left(f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\right).\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}q&=f_{xx}\left[h^2+2\frac{kf_{xy}}{f_{xx}}h+\left(\frac{kf_{xy}}{f_{xx}}\right)^2\right]\\&\quad+k^2f_{yy}-f_{xx}\left(\frac{kf_{xy}}{f_{xx}}\right)^2\\&=f_{xx}\left(h+\frac{kf_{xy}}{f_{xx}}\right)^2\\&\quad+\frac{k^2}{f_{xx}}\left(f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\right).\end{aligned}\]
Reading the formula
\(f_{xx}\left(h+\tfrac{kf_{xy}}{f_{xx}}\right)^2\)
the bend along a first direction: a square (never negative) times the coefficient \(f_{xx}\). Completing the square has untangled the twist term so the bend splits into two separate pieces like this.
\(\dfrac{D}{f_{xx}}\,k^2\)
the bend along a second, independent direction: a square times the coefficient \(D/f_{xx}\), where \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\).
\(D=f_{xx}\times\dfrac{D}{f_{xx}}\)
\(D\) is the product of the two bend coefficients. A product is positive when both have the same sign, negative when they differ, and zero when one of them is zero.

That is the whole test in one sentence. \(D\gt0\): both pieces bend the same way, so the ground is a bowl or a dome, and \(f_{xx}\) (the bend in the \(x\)-direction) says which. \(D\lt0\): one piece bends up and the other down, a saddle. \(D=0\): the second piece vanishes, so along one line of steps the bend is zero and the test is blind there. (Note that \(D\gt0\) forces \(f_{xx}\ne0\), since \(f_{xx}=0\) would give \(D=-f_{xy}^2\le0\); so the assumption \(f_{xx}\ne0\) costs nothing in the first two rows.) Reading off signs (a square is never negative):

\(q\gt0\) always\(f_{xx}\gt0\) and \(f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\gt0\)local minimum

Show the calculation: completing the square, and why the signs come out this way

Completing the square (the same trick as \(x^2+2bx=(x+b)^2-b^2\)):

  1. Take \(f_{xx}\) out of the two terms containing \(h\): \(q=f_{xx}\Big[h^2+2\dfrac{kf_{xy}}{f_{xx}}h\Big]+k^2f_{yy}\). (Multiplying back gives \(h^2f_{xx}+2hkf_{xy}\), as before.)
  2. Inside the bracket, \(b=\dfrac{kf_{xy}}{f_{xx}}\). Add \(b^2\) inside the bracket to make a perfect square, and subtract \(f_{xx}b^2\) outside so nothing has changed: \(q=f_{xx}(h+b)^2+k^2f_{yy}-f_{xx}b^2\).
  3. Simplify the leftover: \(f_{xx}b^2=f_{xx}\dfrac{k^2f_{xy}^2}{f_{xx}^2}=\dfrac{k^2f_{xy}^2}{f_{xx}}\). So \(k^2f_{yy}-\dfrac{k^2f_{xy}^2}{f_{xx}}=\dfrac{k^2}{f_{xx}}\big(f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\big)\) (common denominator \(f_{xx}\)).

So \(q=f_{xx}\,(h+b)^2+\dfrac{k^2}{f_{xx}}\,D\) with \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\).

Reading off the signs. \((h+b)^2\ge0\) and \(k^2\ge0\).

  • \(D\gt0\). The first term has the sign of \(f_{xx}\) (or is 0); the second also has the sign of \(f_{xx}\) (since \(D\gt0\) and \(k^2\ge0\)), or is 0. Both are 0 only if \(k=0\) and then \(h=0\). So for every small step \((h,k)\ne(0,0)\), \(q\) has the sign of \(f_{xx}\): always positive (minimum) if \(f_{xx}\gt0\), always negative (maximum) if \(f_{xx}\lt0\).
  • \(D\lt0\). Step along \(k=0\), \(h\ne0\): \(q=f_{xx}h^2\), which has the sign of \(f_{xx}\). Now choose \(k\ne0\) and \(h=-b\) so the square is 0: \(q=\frac{k^2}{f_{xx}}D\), which has the opposite sign (since \(D\lt0\)). So \(q\) takes both signs near \((a,b)\): a saddle. (If \(f_{xx}=0\), then \(D=-f_{xy}^2\), and \(D\lt0\) means \(f_{xy}\ne0\); then \(q=k(2hf_{xy}+kf_{yy})\) changes sign when \(h\) changes sign with \(k\) small and fixed, again a saddle.)
  • \(D=0\). \(q=f_{xx}(h+b)^2\), which is 0 along the whole line \(h=-b\). Along that line the second-order terms tell you nothing and the neglected \(R_3\) decides, hence "no information".

If \(f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2=0\), higher-order terms in Taylor's theorem would need to be considered. The quantity \(f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\) is called the discriminant function of \(f\) (many books write \(D\) or \(\Delta\); it is the determinant of the matrix of second derivatives).

Summary: the second-derivative test

All partial derivatives are evaluated at \((a,b)\). First, \((a,b)\) is a stationary point of \(f(x,y)\iff f_x=0\) and \(f_y=0\). Then, with \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\):

  1. If \(D\gt0\): \((a,b)\) is a local maximum if \(f_{xx}\lt0\), a local minimum if \(f_{xx}\gt0\).
  2. If \(D\lt0\): \((a,b)\) is a saddle point.
  3. If \(D=0\): the test gives no information.

In words: positive discriminant means the surface curves the same way in every direction (a bowl or a dome), and \(f_{xx}\) tells you which; negative discriminant means it curves up in some directions and down in others (a saddle).

How to find and classify stationary points (the exam procedure):

  1. Do: find \(f_x\) and \(f_y\). These are the slopes east and north; a top, bottom or saddle needs both to be zero.
  2. Do: solve \(f_x=0\) and \(f_y=0\) simultaneously and list all combinations. If each equation involves only one variable, pair every \(x\)-value with every \(y\)-value; if they are linked, substitute one into the other and check each solution in both equations. A flat spot must be flat in both directions at the same point. Every missed combination is a missed flat spot (and lost marks).
  3. Do: find \(f_{xx}\), \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yy}\). With the tilt gone, these three numbers describe the bend \(q\).
  4. Do: at each point, evaluate them and \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\). \(D\) is the product of the two bend coefficients: does the ground bend the same way in both independent directions?
  5. Do: classify: \(D\gt0\) and \(f_{xx}\gt0\) minimum; \(D\gt0\) and \(f_{xx}\lt0\) maximum; \(D\lt0\) saddle; \(D=0\) use the method in the box below. Same bend both ways = bowl or dome, and the \(x\)-bend says which; opposite bends = saddle; zero = the test cannot see along one line.
  6. Do: present the results in a table, one row per point. It shows the marker every value and makes a missing point obvious.

Example 1.5.3 (when the test fails)

Consider \(f(x,y)=x^4+y^4\), which has \(f_x=4x^3,\ f_y=4y^3\), so \((0,0)\) is the only stationary point. The discriminant at \((0,0)\) is

\[\pd{^2f}{x^2}\pd{^2f}{y^2}-\left(\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x\,\partial y}\right)^2=0\cdot0-0^2=0,\]

so the second-derivative test is inconclusive. However, it is obvious that \((0,0)\) is a local minimum, because \(f(0,0)=0\) and \(f\ge0\) everywhere.

The ground here is so flat at the bottom that the bend \(q\) is zero in every direction; the rising happens only in the fourth-order terms, which the bend test ignores.

Beyond the handouts · what to do when \(D=0\)

The handout only says "higher-order terms need to be considered". When \(D=0\) the bend test is blind along at least one line, so stop relying on the bend and look at the heights themselves. In the past papers with \(D=0\) (Dec 2016 Q1(b)(ii), where a whole line of stationary points appears, and Dec 2023 Q2(a)) the reliable method is to look at \(f(a+h,b+k)-f(a,b)\) directly, which is exactly the "height difference" the definitions of maximum and minimum talk about:

  1. If it is \(\ge0\) for all nearby points (like \(x^4+y^4\)), the point is a minimum; if \(\le0\), a maximum.
  2. To prove a saddle, find two paths through the point with opposite behaviour (one going up from the point, one going down), just like the horse saddle and the mountain pass. Try the axes \(x=0\), \(y=0\) first, then lines \(y=mx\) or curves like \(x=y^2\). Example: \(f=x^4-y^4\) has \(D=0\) at the origin, but \(f(x,0)=x^4\gt0\) and \(f(0,y)=-y^4\lt0\), so it is a saddle.

Dec 2023 Q2(a) gives the hint "consider \(f\) along \(y=mx\)": along a line the function becomes a one-variable function of \(x\), which you can analyse with first-year methods.

You can now: find every stationary point of \(f(x,y)\) and classify each with the second-derivative test, explaining what \(D\) and \(f_{xx}\) say about the shape; list the three kinds of place to check for global extrema; settle a \(D=0\) case by looking at height differences along paths.

6Directional derivative and gradient (handout §1.6, pp. 18–23) ~14 min

What is this?

The two partial derivatives tell you the slope underfoot when you walk due east or due north. But a hiker can walk in any compass direction: north-east, south-south-west, anything. The directional derivative is the slope you feel underfoot when you walk in whichever direction you choose: how many metres you rise for each metre you walk.

The gradient is an arrow drawn on the map (the floor), not on the hill. It points in the direction that goes straight uphill, and its length is how steep that uphill is. As we will see, this one arrow already contains the slope in every other direction.

Start at the point \(P_0(x_0,y_0)\) and walk a distance \(s\) along a straight line \(l\) to the point \(P(x,y)\). The average slope along the walk is (change in height) ÷ (distance walked):

\[\frac{f(x,y)-f(x_0,y_0)}{s},\]

and the slope right at \(P_0\), in the direction from \(P_0\) to \(P\), is what this becomes as the walk shrinks to nothing:

Definition (directional derivative) \[\frac{df}{ds}(x_0,y_0)=\lim_{s\to0}\frac{f(x,y)-f(x_0,y_0)}{s},\]

provided the limit exists. This is the directional derivative of \(f(x,y)\) in the direction \(P_0P\).

In words: the directional derivative = the slope of the surface you feel underfoot when you walk from \(P_0\) in the chosen direction (rise per unit distance walked).

The directional derivative is the slope of a vertical slice

On the floor: the point \(P_0\) (red) and the line \(l\) through it in the chosen direction \(\vb u\) (dark arrow). Above it: the curve where the vertical plane through \(l\) cuts the surface (teal). The orange line is its tangent at the point above \(P_0\); its slope is \(df/ds\), the directional derivative. Surface \(f=1.2+0.6x-0.3y^2+0.2xy\) (an example), \(P_0=(0.4,0.5)\).

From the picture to a formula

Describe the direction by a unit vector (a vector of length 1) \(\vb u=u_1\ii+u_2\jj\). Its first component \(u_1\) is how far east each metre of walking takes you, and \(u_2\) is how far north. Walking a distance \(s\) along it takes you to

\[x=x_0+su_1,\qquad y=y_0+su_2.\]

The idea. A short step in direction \(\vb u\) is really two steps at once: a step east of length \(su_1\) and a step north of length \(su_2\). For a short step the hill is practically a flat tilted plane (the tangent plane of §4), so the rises simply add: the east part rises by (east slope × east distance), the north part by (north slope × north distance). Using Taylor's theorem (the tangent-plane part, since \(s\) is small):

\[\begin{aligned}&\frac{df}{ds}(x_0,y_0)\\&=\lim_{s\to0}\frac{f(x_0+su_1,y_0+su_2)-f(x_0,y_0)}{s}\\&=\lim_{s\to0}\frac{su_1f_x(x_0,y_0)+su_2f_y(x_0,y_0)}{s}\\&=u_1f_x(x_0,y_0)+u_2f_y(x_0,y_0).\end{aligned}\]

In words: slope in direction \(\vb u\) = (east slope × east part of a 1-metre step) + (north slope × north part of a 1-metre step).

This is a dot product of \(\vb u\) with the vector made of the two slopes, \((f_x,f_y)\). To keep a vector description, we give that vector a name.

Definition (gradient) \[\operatorname{grad}f=f_x\,\ii+f_y\,\jj\]

(also written \(\nabla f\), read "del f" or "nabla f")

is the gradient of \(f\). For a function of three variables \(f(x,y,z)\):

\[\operatorname{grad}f=\pd fx\ii+\pd fy\jj+\pd fz\kk.\]

In words: the gradient is the vector whose components are the partial derivatives. It lives in the floor (the \(xy\)-plane), not on the surface.

Directional derivative formula \[\begin{aligned}D_{\vb u}f(x_0,y_0)&=\frac{df}{ds}(x_0,y_0)\\&=\operatorname{grad}f(x_0,y_0)\cdot\vb u\qquad(|\vb u|=1)\end{aligned}\]

In words: rate of change in direction \(\vb u\) = gradient dot (unit vector in that direction). \(D_{\vb u}f\) is the more common notation.

Reading the formula
\(\operatorname{grad}f=(f_x,f_y)\)
the east slope and the north slope at the point, packed into one arrow on the map
\(\vb u=(u_1,u_2)\)
one metre of walking, split into its east part and its north part
\(\cdot\) (dot product)
multiply each slope by its matching part of the step and add: total rise for that metre
\(|\vb u|=1\)
we want rise per metre walked, so the step must be exactly one metre long

Why \(\vb u\) must have length 1. Say the east slope is 3 and the north slope is 1, and you walk north-east. If you lazily use the arrow \(\ii+\jj\), the dot product gives \(3+1=4\). But that arrow is \(\sqrt2\approx1.41\) metres long, so 4 is the rise over 1.41 metres, not over one metre. The true slope is \(4/\sqrt2=2\sqrt2\approx2.83\), which is what \(\vb u=\frac1{\sqrt2}(\ii+\jj)\) gives. A longer arrow would give a bigger number without the hill being any steeper.

Watch out

\(\vb u\) must be a unit vector. An exam saying "in the direction of \(\ii+\jj\)" means \(\vb u=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ii+\jj)\). Forgetting to divide by the length is the most common lost mark in this topic.

Which way is steepest?

Write the dot product with the angle \(\alpha\) between the gradient arrow and your walking direction \(\vb u\):

\[\begin{aligned}D_{\vb u}f(x_0,y_0)&=\operatorname{grad}f(x_0,y_0)\cdot\vb u\\&=|\operatorname{grad}f(x_0,y_0)|\,|\vb u|\cos\alpha\\&=|\operatorname{grad}f(x_0,y_0)|\cos\alpha\end{aligned}\]

(the last step since \(|\vb u|=1\)).

In words: slope in your direction = (steepness of the hill) × cos(angle between your direction and straight uphill).

The steepness \(|\operatorname{grad}f|\) is fixed at the point; only \(\cos\alpha\) changes as you turn. So every question about direction is a question about \(\cos\alpha\):

  1. Along which direction is the rate of change largest? \(\cos\alpha\) is largest (=1) when \(\alpha=0\), so \(\vb u\) must point along \(\operatorname{grad}f(x_0,y_0)\): face the way the arrow points.
  2. What is the maximum rate of change? At \(\alpha=0\) it is \[\begin{aligned}&|\operatorname{grad}f(x_0,y_0)|\\&=\sqrt{f_x^2(x_0,y_0)+f_y^2(x_0,y_0)}.\end{aligned}\] That is, the length of the arrow.
  3. Steepest descent. Turn round (\(\alpha=\pi\), \(\cos\alpha=-1\)): the fastest decrease is in the direction \(-\operatorname{grad}f\), at rate \(-|\operatorname{grad}f|\). This is the way water runs downhill.
  4. No change. Turn sideways (\(\alpha=\pi/2\), \(\cos\alpha=0\)): when \(\vb u\perp\operatorname{grad}f\) the rate is zero. You are walking level, along the contour line.

In other words, \(\operatorname{grad}f\) gives both the magnitude and direction of the greatest rate of change of \(f\). The directional derivative is simply the projection (the shadow) of \(\operatorname{grad}f\) onto \(\vb u\).

Turn the direction \(\vb u\) and watch \(D_{\vb u}f\)

Contours of \(\varphi=\ln\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) (Example 1.6.1). Orange: \(\operatorname{grad}\varphi\) at \((1,2)\). Teal: the unit vector \(\vb u\). The readout shows \(D_{\vb u}\varphi=|\operatorname{grad}\varphi|\cos\alpha\); it peaks when \(\vb u\) lines up with the gradient, is negative when it points the other way, and is zero when \(\vb u\) runs along the contour.

Exam recipes

(a) Directional derivative at a point in a given direction

  1. Do: find the gradient: differentiate partially with respect to each variable. This finds the slope in each axis direction, the building blocks of every other slope.
  2. Do: substitute the point into the gradient. The slopes depend on where you stand; we want them at this spot on the hill.
  3. Do: turn the given direction into a unit vector: divide it by its length. We want rise per metre walked, so the step must be exactly one metre long.
  4. Do: take the dot product of the gradient with the unit vector. This multiplies each axis slope by how much of the step goes that way, and adds up the rises.

(b) Maximum rate of change and its direction

  1. Do: find the gradient and evaluate it at the point. This is the uphill arrow on the map at that spot.
  2. Do: the direction of maximum increase is the gradient itself (as a unit vector, divide by its length if a unit direction is asked). Facing along the arrow makes \(\alpha=0\), so \(\cos\alpha=1\) is as large as it can be.
  3. Do: the maximum rate is the length of the gradient, \(\sqrt{f_x^2+f_y^2\,(+f_z^2)}\). With \(\cos\alpha=1\), the slope is just the steepness of the hill.
  4. Do: if asked, the fastest decrease is in the direction minus the gradient, at rate minus the length. Turning round gives \(\cos\alpha=-1\): straight downhill.

The gradient is normal to level curves

The idea. Walk along a contour line on the map. Your height does not change, so the slope in your walking direction is zero. By the formula above, zero slope means your step has zero dot product with the gradient, so your step is perpendicular to the gradient. Since your step runs along the contour, the gradient must be perpendicular to the contour.

The same argument with the chain rule: consider a particle moving along the level curve \(f(x,y)=c\), at position \((x(t),y(t))\) at time \(t\). Since \(f\) stays equal to \(c\),

\[\begin{gathered}\frac{df}{dt}=0=f_x\frac{dx}{dt}+f_y\frac{dy}{dt}\\ \Longrightarrow\quad0=\operatorname{grad}f(x,y)\cdot\vb v,\\ \vb v=\frac{dx}{dt}\ii+\frac{dy}{dt}\jj.\end{gathered}\]

The vector \(\vb v\) is the particle's velocity, which always points along (tangent to) the curve. A zero dot product means perpendicular, so

Result

\(\operatorname{grad}f(x,y)\) at a point on the curve \(f(x,y)=c\) is always normal (perpendicular) to the curve.

In words: the steepest way up a hill crosses the contour lines at right angles.

Consequences for the tangent line. The 2D gradient vector \((f_x,f_y)\) has slope \(\dfrac{f_y}{f_x}\), so the tangent line (perpendicular to it) has slope \(-\dfrac{f_x}{f_y}\) (since \(-\dfrac{f_x}{f_y}\cdot\dfrac{f_y}{f_x}=-1\), the condition for perpendicular lines). This agrees with the implicit-differentiation formula of §3. Hence the equation of the tangent line at \((x_0,y_0)\) is

\[\begin{gathered}\frac{y-y_0}{x-x_0}=-\frac{f_x}{f_y}\\ \Longrightarrow\quad(x-x_0)f_x+(y-y_0)f_y=0,\end{gathered}\]

with \(f_x,f_y\) evaluated at \((x_0,y_0)\).

In words: the tangent line is the set of points whose displacement from \((x_0,y_0)\) has zero dot product with the gradient.

Three variables. For \(f(x,y,z)\), the gradient vector at \((x_0,y_0,z_0)\) is normal to the level surface \(f(x,y,z)=c\) and to its tangent plane at that point. The velocity of any particle moving on the surface \(f(x,y,z)=c\) is tangential to the surface (and \(f\) does not change along it). Picture a point \(P_0\) on the level surface with its tangent plane resting on it: any two curves drawn on the surface through \(P_0\) have velocities \(\vb v_1,\vb v_2\) lying in the tangent plane, and \(\nabla f\) sticks straight out of the plane, perpendicular to both. The figure in §7 shows a tangent plane with its normal; the tangent plane equation follows there.

Example 1.6.1

An electrostatic potential field is given by \(\varphi=\log(x^2+y^2)^{1/2}\). Find the directional derivative of this field at the point \((1,2)\) in the direction \(\ii+\jj\). (Here \(\log\) means the natural log, \(\ln\).)

\[\begin{aligned}\vb u&=\frac1{\sqrt2}(\ii+\jj),\\ \operatorname{grad}\varphi&=\pd\varphi x\ii+\pd\varphi y\jj\\&=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\ii+\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}\jj.\end{aligned}\]

The arrow \(\ii+\jj\) has length \(\sqrt2\), so we divide by \(\sqrt2\) to get a one-metre step.

The directional derivative, evaluated at \((1,2)\), is

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d\varphi}{ds}&=\operatorname{grad}\varphi\cdot\vb u\\&=\left(\tfrac15\ii+\tfrac25\jj\right)\cdot\frac1{\sqrt2}(\ii+\jj)\\&=\frac{3}{5\sqrt2}\approx0.424.\end{aligned}\]

East slope \(\tfrac15\) times east part \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\), plus north slope \(\tfrac25\) times north part \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\).

The maximum rate of change at \((1,2)\) is determined by \(\operatorname{grad}\varphi=\frac15\ii+\frac25\jj\). Its magnitude is \(\frac1{\sqrt5}\approx0.447\) and its direction is \(\ii+2\jj\): directly away from the origin (as expected, since \(\varphi\) depends only on distance from the origin), and normal to the level curve through \((1,2)\), which is a circle centred at \((0,0)\).

The answer 0.424 is less than the maximum 0.447 because north-east is not quite the uphill direction \(\ii+2\jj\): \(\cos\alpha=0.424/0.447\approx0.95\).

Example 1.6.2

The temperature in a room is \(T(x,y,z)=T_0(x^2-y^2+xyz)+273\), \(T_0\gt0\). Find the maximum rate of change of temperature at \((1,1,1)\) and the direction in which it occurs.

\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{grad}T=T_0\big[&(2x+yz)\ii+(xz-2y)\jj +xy\,\kk\big].\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{grad}T=T_0\big[&(2x+yz)\ii+(xz-2y)\jj\\&+xy\,\kk\big].\end{aligned}\]

At \((1,1,1)\): \(\operatorname{grad}T=T_0(3\ii-\jj+\kk)\), with magnitude \(T_0\sqrt{9+1+1}=T_0\sqrt{11}\).

The "uphill" arrow now lives in 3D space: it points the way the room warms fastest, and its length is how fast.

Thus the maximum rate of change of \(T\) at \((1,1,1)\) is \(T_0\sqrt{11}\), in the direction \(\vb n=\frac1{\sqrt{11}}(3\ii-\jj+\kk)\). \(T\) increases at this rate in the direction \(\vb n\), and decreases at this rate in the direction \(-\vb n\).

Facing along the gradient gives \(\cos\alpha=1\); facing the opposite way gives \(\cos\alpha=-1\).

7Beyond the handouts: tangent planes and normal lines Beyond ~4 min

Beyond the handouts · why this section is here

The handout states that the gradient is normal to a level surface and to its tangent plane, but never writes the tangent plane's equation. Every December paper from 2014 to 2025 asks for one (type C1.3). Everything below follows directly from §4 and §6.

What is this?

Rest a flat sheet of card on a ball so that it touches at exactly one point without cutting in: that sheet is the tangent plane at that point. A pin pushed into the ball at the same point, sticking straight out, is the normal line.

From §6, the gradient of \(F\) at a point on the surface \(F(x,y,z)=c\) sticks straight out of that surface, so it is the direction of the pin. The tangent plane is then every point you can reach from the touching point by a displacement that is perpendicular to the pin.

Form 1: surface given as a level surface \(F(x,y,z)=c\)

A plane through the point \(P_0(x_0,y_0,z_0)\) with normal vector \(\vb n=(n_1,n_2,n_3)\) consists of the points whose displacement from \(P_0\) is perpendicular to \(\vb n\): \(\vb n\cdot(x-x_0,\,y-y_0,\,z-z_0)=0\). Taking \(\vb n=\nabla F(P_0)\):

Tangent plane to \(F(x,y,z)=c\) at \(P_0\) \[\begin{aligned}&F_x(P_0)(x-x_0)+F_y(P_0)(y-y_0) +F_z(P_0)(z-z_0)=0\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}&F_x(P_0)(x-x_0)+F_y(P_0)(y-y_0)\\&\quad+F_z(P_0)(z-z_0)=0\end{aligned}\]

In words: (gradient at the point) · (position − point) = 0. It is the 3D version of the tangent line \((x-x_0)f_x+(y-y_0)f_y=0\) from §6.

Reading the formula
\((F_x,F_y,F_z)\) at \(P_0\)
the pin: the direction sticking straight out of the surface
\((x-x_0,\,y-y_0,\,z-z_0)\)
the arrow from the touching point to a general point \((x,y,z)\)
sum of products \(=0\)
that arrow is perpendicular to the pin, so the point lies flat on the card

The normal line (the straight line through \(P_0\) perpendicular to the surface) is \(\vb r(t)=(x_0,y_0,z_0)+t\,\nabla F(P_0)\).

In words: start at the point and move any amount \(t\) along the pin.

Form 2: surface given as a graph \(z=f(x,y)\)

This is exactly the tangent plane approximation of §4:

\[\begin{aligned}z-z_0&=f_x(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0) +f_y(x_0,y_0)(y-y_0),\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}z-z_0&=f_x(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0)\\&\quad+f_y(x_0,y_0)(y-y_0),\end{aligned}\]

where \(z_0=f(x_0,y_0)\).

In words: height change on the plane = east slope × east distance + north slope × north distance.

It is also Form 1 applied to \(F(x,y,z)=f(x,y)-z=0\), whose gradient is \((f_x,f_y,-1)\). So a normal vector to a graph is \(f_x\ii+f_y\jj-\kk\) (or its negative, the "upward" normal \(-f_x\ii-f_y\jj+\kk\), which you will meet again for flux integrals in Chapter 3c).

Recipe: tangent plane and normal line

  1. Do: write the surface as \(F(x,y,z)=c\), with everything in \(F\). For a graph \(z=f(x,y)\), use \(F=f(x,y)-z\) and \(c=0\). The pin is the gradient of a function that stays constant on the surface, so we need the surface in that "level" form.
  2. Do: check the given point satisfies the equation (find \(z_0\) from it if only \(x_0,y_0\) are given). The card must touch the surface at a point that is actually on it.
  3. Do: find \(\nabla F=(F_x,F_y,F_z)\) and evaluate it at \(P_0\). This is the pin's direction at the touching point.
  4. Do: tangent plane: \(F_x(x-x_0)+F_y(y-y_0)+F_z(z-z_0)=0\); expand and simplify. Every point on the card is reached by an arrow perpendicular to the pin (dot product zero).
  5. Do: normal line: \((x,y,z)=(x_0,y_0,z_0)+t\,\nabla F(P_0)\). Start at the touching point and slide along the pin.
Worked example (written for these notes)

Find the tangent plane and normal line to the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=9\) at \((1,2,2)\).

  1. \(F=x^2+y^2+z^2\), \(\nabla F=(2x,2y,2z)\). Check the point is on the surface: \(1+4+4=9\) ✓.
  2. \(\nabla F(1,2,2)=(2,4,4)\). For a sphere the pin points straight out from the centre, along \((1,2,2)\) itself.
  3. Tangent plane: \(2(x-1)+4(y-2)+4(z-2)=0\), i.e. \(\boxed{x+2y+2z=9}\).
  4. Normal line: \((x,y,z)=(1,2,2)+t(2,4,4)\), which passes through the centre \((0,0,0)\) at \(t=-\tfrac12\), as it should for a sphere.

Surface \(z=2-x^2-\tfrac12y^2\) over a disc. Orange: the tangent plane at the red point. Arrow: the upward normal \(-f_x\ii-f_y\jj+\kk\).

What is this?

You must stay on a fixed path across the hill (the constraint), and you want the highest or lowest point of the path. Think of a footpath across a hillside: the highest point of the path is usually not the top of the hill, just the point where the path comes closest to it.

At that point the path just grazes a contour line instead of crossing it. Where two curves graze, they point the same way, so the uphill arrows of both (the two gradients) are parallel. The Lagrange multiplier is the number you multiply one arrow by to get the other.

The problem. Find the largest or smallest value of \(f(x,y)\) (or \(f(x,y,z)\)) when the variables are not free but must satisfy a constraint \(g=c\). Example: the largest box (volume \(f=8xyz\)) whose corners touch an ellipsoid (\(g=4x^2+4y^2+z^2=4\)).

The idea. Walk along the constraint curve \(g=c\) on a contour map of \(f\). While you cross contours of \(f\), the value of \(f\) is still changing, so you are not at the best point. At the best point the constraint curve just touches a contour of \(f\) without crossing it. Touching curves share a tangent line, so their normals are parallel. By §6, the normals are \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\):

Method of Lagrange multipliers

At a constrained maximum or minimum of \(f\) subject to \(g=c\) (with \(\nabla g\ne\vb 0\)),

\[\nabla f=\lambda\,\nabla g,\qquad g=c,\]

for some number \(\lambda\), called the Lagrange multiplier. In two variables that is three equations \(f_x=\lambda g_x,\ f_y=\lambda g_y,\ g=c\) for the three unknowns \(x,y,\lambda\); in three variables, four equations for \(x,y,z,\lambda\).

In words: at the best point, the direction of steepest increase of \(f\) is straight across the constraint, so moving along the constraint cannot improve \(f\).

Reading the formula
\(\nabla f\)
uphill arrow of the hill \(f\), perpendicular to its contour through the point
\(\nabla g\)
arrow perpendicular to the path \(g=c\)
\(\nabla f=\lambda\,\nabla g\)
the two arrows are parallel (one is a multiple of the other): the contour and the path graze
\(g=c\)
you must still be standing on the path
  1. Do: write \(f\) (what to optimise) and \(g=c\) (the constraint). In word problems, use symmetry to choose variables (for a box centred at the origin with a corner at \((x,y,z)\), the sides are \(2x,2y,2z\)). This separates the hill (\(f\)) from the path you are forced to walk (\(g=c\)).
  2. Do: solve \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\), \(g=c\). A good trick: divide equations to eliminate \(\lambda\), or multiply each equation by its variable and compare. This finds every point of the path where the path grazes a contour; the value of \(\lambda\) itself is rarely needed.
  3. Do: evaluate \(f\) at every solution; the largest is the maximum, the smallest the minimum (on a closed bounded constraint such as an ellipse or ellipsoid both exist). Grazing happens at the highest and the lowest points alike, so the equations alone cannot tell them apart; comparing heights does.
Worked example (written for these notes)

Find the maximum and minimum of \(f(x,y)=xy\) on the ellipse \(x^2+4y^2=8\).

  1. \(g=x^2+4y^2\), \(\nabla f=(y,x)\), \(\nabla g=(2x,8y)\).
  2. \(y=2\lambda x,\quad x=8\lambda y,\quad x^2+4y^2=8\). Two "arrows parallel" equations plus "stay on the ellipse".
  3. Multiply the first by \(x\) and the second by \(y\): \(xy=2\lambda x^2=8\lambda y^2\). If \(\lambda=0\) then \(x=y=0\), not on the ellipse. So \(x^2=4y^2\), and the constraint gives \(8y^2=8\): \(y=\pm1,\ x=\pm2\).
  4. \(f=xy=2\) at \((2,1),(-2,-1)\) and \(f=-2\) at \((2,-1),(-2,1)\). All four points are grazing points; comparing values sorts highest from lowest.

Maximum \(2\) at \((\pm2,\pm1)\) (same signs); minimum \(-2\) at \((\pm2,\mp1)\) (opposite signs).

The constraint touches a contour at the optimum

Coloured curves: contours \(xy=c\). Dark curve: the constraint \(x^2+4y^2=8\). Orange: \(\nabla f\); teal: \(\nabla g\) at the moving point. They line up (parallel) exactly at the four optimal points.

You can now: set up and solve \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g,\ g=c\) for a constrained maximum or minimum, and explain it as "the path grazes a contour".

◆Common mistakes

✗Using \(\ii+2\jj-\kk\) itself in \(\nabla f\cdot\vb u\). Fix: divide by its length \(\sqrt6\) first. ✗Classifying with \(f_{xx}\) alone. Fix: compute \(D\) first; \(f_{xx}\) only decides max vs min when \(D\gt0\). ✗Calling a point with \(D=0\) a saddle (or a minimum) without proof. Fix: say "test inconclusive", then examine \(f\) along paths. ✗Missing stationary points when solving \(f_x=f_y=0\), e.g. dividing by \(x\) in \(2xy=2x\). Fix: factorise: \(2x(y-1)=0\Rightarrow x=0\) or \(y=1\); follow both cases. ✗\(\pd rx=1/\pd xr\). Fix: different variables are held fixed; compute \(\partial r/\partial x\) from \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). ✗In second-order chain-rule work, treating \(g_r\) as a constant. Fix: \(g_r\) is a function of \(r,\theta\); apply the chain-rule operator to it and the product rule to its coefficient. ✗Giving the tangent plane with the gradient at a general point. Fix: evaluate \(\nabla F\) at \(P_0\) first; check \(P_0\) lies on the surface. ✓Always state the conclusion in words ("\((1,1)\) is a local minimum since \(D=36\gt0\) and \(f_{xx}=6\gt0\)"); markers look for the reason.

◆Find the mistake

Written for these notes. Each "solution" has one error. Find it before opening the answer.

1. Directional derivative

"\(f=x^2y\). The directional derivative at \((1,3)\) in the direction \(3\ii+4\jj\) is \(\nabla f\cdot(3\ii+4\jj)=(6,1)\cdot(3,4)=22\)."

Answer

\(3\ii+4\jj\) is not a unit vector (length 5). Correct: \(\vb u=\frac15(3,4)\), so \(D_{\vb u}f=\frac{18+4}{5}=4.4\).

2. Classification

"\(f=x^2+4xy+y^2\). Stationary point \((0,0)\); \(f_{xx}=2\gt0\), so \((0,0)\) is a local minimum."

Answer

\(D\) \(=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\) \(=2\cdot2-4^2\) \(=-12\) \(\lt0\), so \((0,0)\) is a saddle. Indeed \(f(t,-t)=-2t^2\lt0\) while \(f(t,0)=t^2\gt0\).

3. Polar chain rule

"With \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(\pd xr=\cos\theta\), so \(\pd rx=\dfrac1{\cos\theta}\)."

Answer

Partial derivatives cannot be flipped like this. From \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), \(\pd rx=\dfrac xr=\cos\theta\) (with \(y\) held fixed), not \(1/\cos\theta\) (which would hold \(\theta\) fixed).

◆Concept checks

Written for these notes.

1. On a contour map, contours are tightly packed near \(A\) and widely spaced near \(B\). Where is \(|\nabla f|\) larger?

Answer

At \(A\): \(f\) changes a lot over a short distance, so the maximum rate of change \(|\nabla f|\) is large.

2. If \(\nabla f(P)=\vb 0\), what is \(D_{\vb u}f(P)\) for any \(\vb u\)? What kind of point is \(P\)?

Answer

Zero in every direction; \(P\) is a stationary point (max, min, saddle or undetermined).

3. Can a directional derivative be larger than \(|\nabla f|\)?

Answer

No: \(D_{\vb u}f=|\nabla f|\cos\alpha\le|\nabla f|\).

4. At a stationary point, can \(D\gt0\) while \(f_{xx}=0\)?

Answer

No: \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\gt0\) needs \(f_{xx}f_{yy}\gt f_{xy}^2\ge0\), so \(f_{xx}\ne0\).

5. For \(z=f(x,y)\) with \(y=y(x)\), when is \(\dfrac{dz}{dx}=\pd fx\)?

Answer

When \(f_y\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0\): \(f\) does not depend on \(y\) there, or \(y\) is not changing.

6. Why is \(\nabla F\) at a point of \(F(x,y,z)=c\) a normal to the tangent plane, not a vector in it?

Answer

Every curve on the surface keeps \(F=c\), so \(\nabla F\cdot\vb v=0\) for every tangent velocity \(\vb v\): \(\nabla F\) is perpendicular to all tangent directions.

Dec 2025 Q1(a)4 + 6 marksGuide C1.1 · chain rule

Suppose \(f(x,y)\) is transformed to \(g(u,v)\) where \(u=x^2-y\) and \(v=x+y^2\).

(i) Use the Chain Rule to express \(\pd fx,\ \pd fy\) in terms of \(x,\ y,\ \pd gu,\ \pd gv\). (Do NOT attempt to express \(x\) and \(y\) in terms of \(u\) and \(v\).)

(ii) Use the results in (i) to express the PDE \(\;(1-2y)\pd fx+(1+2x)\pd fy=0\;\) in terms of the partial derivatives of \(g(u,v)\).

Hint

\(\pd fx=\pd gu\pd ux+\pd gv\pd vx\) with \(u_x=2x,\ v_x=1,\ u_y=-1,\ v_y=2y\). In (ii) substitute and collect the \(g_u\) and \(g_v\) terms.

Answer

(i) \(f_x=2x\,g_u+g_v,\qquad f_y=-g_u+2y\,g_v.\)

(ii) The left side becomes \((1+4xy)(g_v-g_u)\), so (where \(1+4xy\ne0\)) the PDE is \(\boxed{\pd gu=\pd gv}\), i.e. \(\pd gu-\pd gv=0\).

Show the calculation (outline)
  1. Partial derivatives of the new variables: \(u_x=2x,\ u_y=-1,\ v_x=1,\ v_y=2y\).
  2. Chain rule: \(f_x=g_uu_x+g_vv_x=2xg_u+g_v\); \(f_y=g_uu_y+g_vv_y=-g_u+2yg_v\).
  3. Substitute into the left side and expand: \((1-2y)(2xg_u+g_v)\) \(+\:(1+2x)(-g_u+2yg_v)\).
  4. Collect \(g_u\): \(2x(1-2y)-(1+2x)\) \(=2x-4xy-1-2x\) \(=-(1+4xy)\). Collect \(g_v\): \((1-2y)+2y(1+2x)\) \(=1-2y+2y+4xy\) \(=1+4xy\).
  5. So the left side is \((1+4xy)(g_v-g_u)\); dividing by \(1+4xy\) (where it is not zero) gives \(g_u=g_v\).

ABC Wonder egg, a competitor of Kinder Surprise egg, is planning to put a rectangular gift box inside a chocolate shelled ellipsoid. Your help is needed to maximize this box. For your information, the 3D ellipsoid is modelled by \(4x^2+4y^2+z^2=4\), with the thickness of chocolate shell coating negligible. Identify the dimension and volume of the required box.

Hint

A box centred at the origin with corner \((x,y,z)\) on the ellipsoid has volume \(V=8xyz\). Solve \(\nabla V=\lambda\nabla g\) with \(g=4x^2+4y^2+z^2\).

Answer

Corner at \(\left(\frac1{\sqrt3},\frac1{\sqrt3},\frac2{\sqrt3}\right)\): box \(\frac2{\sqrt3}\times\frac2{\sqrt3}\times\frac4{\sqrt3}\), volume \(\frac{16}{3\sqrt3}=\frac{16\sqrt3}{9}\approx3.08\).

Show the calculation (outline)

  2. \(\nabla V=\lambda\nabla g\): \(8yz=8\lambda x,\quad8xz=8\lambda y,\quad8xy=2\lambda z\).
  3. Multiply the three equations by \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) respectively: each left side becomes \(8xyz\), so \(8\lambda x^2=8\lambda y^2=2\lambda z^2\). (\(\lambda\ne0\), otherwise \(V=0\).) Hence \(y^2=x^2\) and \(z^2=4x^2\).

  5. \(V=8\cdot\frac1{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac1{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac2{\sqrt3}=\frac{16}{3\sqrt3}\).
