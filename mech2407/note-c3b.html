1Curves: parametrisation, simple and closed curves, orientation ~11 min

What is this? (line integrals)

Picture yourself walking along a winding footpath on a windy day. Along the way you might want to add something up: how heavy a cable laid along the path is, or how much the wind helped or hindered you. An ordinary integral adds things up along a straight stretch of the \(x\)-axis; a line integral adds them up along a bent path in space. Before we can add anything up, we need a way to describe the path itself, and that is what this section is about.

A line integral is an integral more general than the integral

\[\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\]

of one variable. Instead of integrating a function over a straight line segment along the \(x\)-axis, \([a,b]\), we integrate over a curve in space. Such an integral is called a line integral, although the term "curve integral" would be more appropriate. Before defining line integrals we look at how curves are described.

Parametrisation

What is this? (parametrisation)

Walk the path holding a stopwatch. At every reading of the stopwatch you are at exactly one spot, so "where am I at time \(t\)?" describes the whole path, together with the order in which you visit it. A parametrisation is exactly this: a rule that gives your position for each value of a "clock" variable \(t\). It is like the route trace a running app records, a list of (time, position) pairs, made continuous.

Definition (parametrisation of a curve)

Let

\[\vb r(t)=x(t)\,\ii+y(t)\,\jj+z(t)\,\kk,\qquad t\in[a,b]\]

describe a curve in space: as \(t\) runs from \(a\) to \(b\), the tip of the position vector \(\vb r(t)\) traces the curve, starting at the point \(\vb r(a)\) and ending at \(\vb r(b)\). This is called a parametrisation of the curve in the variable \(t\); \(t\) is the parameter.

In words: a parametrisation is a recipe "for each value of \(t\), here is a point \((x,y,z)\) on the curve". Think of \(t\) as time and \(\vb r(t)\) as the position of a bead sliding along a wire.

Reading the formula
\(t\)
the reading on your stopwatch (it need not be real time; any number that increases as you walk will do).
\(x(t),\ y(t),\ z(t)\)
how far east, north and up you are at that reading.
\(\vb r(t)\)
the arrow from the origin to where you stand at that reading.
\(t\in[a,b]\)
you set off when the watch reads \(a\) (at the point \(\vb r(a)\)) and stop when it reads \(b\) (at \(\vb r(b)\)).

For a plane curve simply drop the \(\kk\)-term (\(z=0\)). One curve has many parametrisations: you can walk the same path at different speeds, or in the opposite direction. Your velocity \(d\vb r/dt=x'(t)\ii+y'(t)\jj+z'(t)\kk\) points the way you are heading, so it is tangent to the curve (Chapter 3a §1); its length is your speed.

Simple, closed and open curves

What is this? (simple and closed)

Two plain questions about your walk. Did you finish where you started, like a lap of a running track? Then the path is closed. Did you ever cross your own footprints along the way? If not, the path is simple. These matter later: the big theorems of Chapter 3c (Green's and Stokes') are about laps of a track that never cross themselves.

Definitions
  • A curve is closed if it ends where it starts: \(\vb r(b)=\vb r(a)\). Otherwise it is open (it has two different end points).
  • A curve is simple if it does not cross itself: different values of \(t\) give different points (for a closed curve, apart from the start and end point, which are the same).

In words: closed = a loop; simple = no self-crossing. A circle is simple and closed; a figure-eight is closed but not simple; a straight segment is simple and open.

Smooth curves and orientation

What is this? (smooth and oriented)

A smooth path is one you can walk without ever having to stop and turn on the spot: your heading changes gradually. A path with a few sharp corners, such as walking round the three sides of a triangular field, is piecewise smooth: smooth along each side, with a turn at each corner. The orientation is simply which way you walk. For adding up the weight of a cable it makes no difference; for the wind's help it does, because a tailwind one way is a headwind the other way.

In this course we only work with smooth or piecewise smooth curves. A curve is smooth if it has a parametrisation whose derivative \(d\vb r/dt\) is continuous (and, strictly, never \(\vb 0\)), so the curve has a well-defined tangent direction at every point and no corners. A curve is piecewise smooth if it can be divided into a finite number of smooth pieces (for example, the three edges of a triangle: smooth along each edge, with corners where edges meet).

In words: smooth = no corners or cusps; piecewise smooth = finitely many smooth pieces joined end to end.

In certain situations curves are considered to have a direction: they are oriented. An oriented curve is a curve together with a chosen direction of travel, from an initial point to a terminal point. A parametrisation fixes an orientation: the direction of increasing \(t\). The same curve with the opposite direction is written \(-C\). Scalar line integrals (§2) do not care about orientation; work integrals (§3) do.

How to parametrise a path

Exam questions describe the path in words ("the segment from … to …", "the upper half of the circle …", "the parabola \(y=x^2\) from … to …"). Turning that sentence into \(\vb r(t)\) is the first step of every line integral.

  1. Do: Identify the shape of each piece: straight segment, graph \(y=g(x)\), circle or arc, helix. Split a path with corners into its smooth pieces. Each smooth piece is a stretch you can walk without turning on the spot, and each one gets its own stopwatch.
  2. Do: Write \(\vb r(t)\) for each piece from the ready-made list below. This is writing down "where am I at each reading of the watch" for that stretch.
  3. Do: Choose the \(t\)-interval and check it: put in the first value of \(t\) and confirm you get the start point, the last value and confirm you get the end point. The walk must begin and end at the right places; a wrong interval walks too far, too short, or round the wrong arc.
  4. Do: Check the direction: as \(t\) increases, do you move the way the question says (from \(P\) to \(Q\), anticlockwise, …)? If not, reverse it. The parametrisation fixes the orientation, which way you walk; for work in §3 the wrong way flips the sign of the answer.
Ready-made parametrisations · going further
  • Straight segment from \(P\) (position vector \(\vb p\)) to \(Q\) (position vector \(\vb q\)): \(\vb r(t)=\vb p+t(\vb q-\vb p)\), \(0\le t\le1\); then \(d\vb r/dt=\vb q-\vb p\) (constant). Example: \((1,4,2)\) to \((0,5,1)\): \(\vb r=(1-t)\ii+(4+t)\jj+(2-t)\kk\).
  • Graph \(y=g(x)\) from \(x=x_0\) to \(x=x_1\): \(\vb r(t)=t\,\ii+g(t)\,\jj\), \(x_0\le t\le x_1\) (use \(x\) itself as the parameter). Example: \(y=x^2\) from \((0,0)\) to \((2,4)\): \(\vb r=t\ii+t^2\jj\), \(0\le t\le2\).
  • Circle of radius \(R\) centred at \((x_0,y_0)\), anticlockwise: \(\vb r(t)=(x_0+R\cos t)\ii+(y_0+R\sin t)\jj\), \(0\le t\le2\pi\); upper semicircle from \((x_0+R,y_0)\): \(0\le t\le\pi\). Clockwise: replace \(\sin t\) by \(-\sin t\).
  • Helix of radius \(a\) and pitch \(2\pi c\) (rise per turn) about the \(z\)-axis: \(\vb r(t)=a\cos t\,\ii+a\sin t\,\jj+ct\,\kk\).
  • Reversing any parametrisation on \([a,b]\): \(\vb r(a+b-t)\), \(a\le t\le b\); for a segment, \(\vb q+t(\vb p-\vb q)\).

In words: segment = start + \(t\) × (end − start); graph = let \(x\) be the parameter; circle = centre + radius × \((\cos t,\sin t)\).

Reading the formula
\(\vb p+t(\vb q-\vb p)\)
start at \(P\), then walk a fraction \(t\) of the way along the arrow from \(P\) to \(Q\): at \(t=0\) you are at \(P\), at \(t=\tfrac12\) halfway, at \(t=1\) at \(Q\).
\(t\,\ii+g(t)\,\jj\)

\((x_0+R\cos t,\ y_0+R\sin t)\)

\(ct\) in the helix

\(a+b-t\)

You can now: describe a path in words as a parametrisation \(\vb r(t)\) with a checked \(t\)-interval and direction; tell whether a curve is simple, closed, smooth or piecewise smooth; and reverse the orientation of a parametrised curve.

2Scalar line integrals \(\int_C f\,ds\) and arc length ~13 min

What is this? (scalar line integral)

Imagine a cable laid along your winding footpath, thick in some places and thin in others. To weigh it without lifting it, you walk along it with a tape measure: for every short stretch you note "this stretch weighs about (kilograms per metre here) × (metres in the stretch)", and you add the notes up. A scalar line integral is exactly that running total: a quantity that varies along the path, multiplied by length walked, added up from start to end. There is no wind yet and no direction: only how much and how far.

Motivation: the mass of a wire

Suppose \(C\) is a smooth curve which describes a thin wire, and that the mass density (mass per unit length, e.g. in kg/m) at any point \((x,y,z)\) on \(C\) is \(f(x,y,z)\). If the density were constant, mass = density × length. Since it changes along the wire, we divide the curve \(C\) into \(n\) short sections: on each one the density is nearly constant. Pick a point \((x_k,y_k,z_k)\) in the \(k\)-th section, whose length is \(\Delta s_k\). Then the mass is approximately

\[\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(x_k,y_k,z_k)\,\Delta s_k .\]

In words: mass ≈ sum over the pieces of (density at a point of the piece) × (length of the piece).

The division points along the curve are \(P_0,P_1,\dots,P_n\): \(P_0\) is the start (position vector \(\vb r(a)\)), \(P_n\) the end (position vector \(\vb r(b)\)), and \(\Delta s_k\) is the length of the arc from \(P_{k-1}\) to \(P_k\).

Drag to rotate. The blue curve runs from \(P_0\) (position vector \(\vb r(a)\), green arrow) to \(P_n\) (position vector \(\vb r(b)\), green arrow). Red dots are the division points. The \(k\)-th arc, from \(P_{k-1}\) to \(P_k\), is drawn thick in orange; its sample point \((x_k,y_k,z_k)\) is the pink dot, with dashed pink lines dropping to the \(xy\)-plane to show its coordinates. The readout adds up \(f\,\Delta s_k\) for the density \(f=1+z\) along this wire: as \(n\) grows the sum settles on the line integral.

Definition

Definition (line integral of \(f\) over \(C\))

If \(f(x,y,z)\) is continuous at every point on \(C\), and \(\max\{\Delta s_k\}\to0\) as \(n\to\infty\) (the longest piece shrinks to zero), then

\[\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(x_k,y_k,z_k)\,\Delta s_k=\int_C f(x,y,z)\,ds,\]

which is called the line integral of \(f\) over \(C\) (also the scalar line integral, or the line integral with respect to arc length). Here \(ds\) stands for a tiny length of curve, the element of arc length.

In words: \(\int_C f\,ds\) = the limit of (value of \(f\)) × (length of piece), added over finer and finer pieces of the curve. If \(f\) is a density, it is the mass of the wire.

Reading the formula
\(\Delta s_k\), then \(ds\)
the length of one short stretch of the walk, measured along the path (a length, so always positive).
\(f(x_k,y_k,z_k)\)
the value of the quantity somewhere on that stretch, e.g. the kilograms per metre of cable there.
\(f\,\Delta s_k\)
the note you write down for that stretch: its share of the total.
\(\sum\), then \(\lim\) and \(\int_C\)
add every note from start to end, with stretches so short that \(f\) is constant on each one.

Computing it with a parametrisation

The definition talks about lengths along the path, but a parametrisation (§1) talks about readings of a stopwatch \(t\). The bridge between them is your speed: if you walk at speed 3 m per unit of \(t\), then a short interval \(dt\) on the watch covers \(3\,dt\) metres of path.

To compute \(\int_C f\,ds\) we turn it into an ordinary integral in \(t\). Since \(\vb r(t)=x(t)\ii+y(t)\jj+z(t)\kk\), the length travelled per unit of \(t\) is the speed:

\[\left|\frac{d\vb r}{dt}\right|=\sqrt{\Big(\frac{dx}{dt}\Big)^2+\Big(\frac{dy}{dt}\Big)^2+\Big(\frac{dz}{dt}\Big)^2}=\frac{ds}{dt}.\]

In words: rate of covering length = length of the velocity vector (speed); so a small step \(dt\) in the parameter covers a length \(ds=|d\vb r/dt|\,dt\).

Show the calculation: why \(ds/dt=|d\vb r/dt|\)
  1. Over a small step \(\Delta t\) the point moves from \(\vb r(t)\) to \(\vb r(t+\Delta t)\). The arc between them is almost straight, so its length is almost the length of the chord: \(\Delta s\approx|\Delta\vb r|\). Why: a short piece of a smooth curve is nearly a straight segment.
  2. Write the chord in components: \(\Delta\vb r=\Delta x\,\ii+\Delta y\,\jj+\Delta z\,\kk\), so \(|\Delta\vb r|=\sqrt{\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2+\Delta z^2}\). Why: Pythagoras in 3D.
  3. Divide by \(\Delta t\gt0\): \(\dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}\approx\sqrt{\Big(\dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\Big)^2+\Big(\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta t}\Big)^2+\Big(\dfrac{\Delta z}{\Delta t}\Big)^2}\). Why: \(\Delta t\) is positive, so it moves inside the square root as \(\Delta t^2\).
  4. Let \(\Delta t\to0\): the approximation becomes exact and each ratio becomes a derivative, giving \(\dfrac{ds}{dt}=\sqrt{x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2+z'(t)^2}=\Big|\dfrac{d\vb r}{dt}\Big|\).

Thus,

Formula (scalar line integral) \[\begin{aligned}&\int_C f(x,y,z)\,ds\\&=\int_a^b f(x(t),y(t),z(t))\left|\frac{d\vb r}{dt}\right|dt\\&=\int_a^b f(x(t),y(t),z(t)) \times\sqrt{x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2+z'(t)^2}\;dt.\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}&\int_C f(x,y,z)\,ds\\&=\int_a^b f(x(t),y(t),z(t))\left|\frac{d\vb r}{dt}\right|dt\\&=\int_a^b f(x(t),y(t),z(t))\\&\qquad\times\sqrt{x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2+z'(t)^2}\;dt.\end{aligned}\]

In words: substitute the parametrisation into \(f\), multiply by the speed \(|\vb r'(t)|\), and integrate over the parameter interval from \(a\) to \(b\) (always smaller limit at the bottom).

Reading the formula
\(f(x(t),y(t),z(t))\)
the kilograms per metre at the spot where you stand when the watch reads \(t\).
\(\left|\dfrac{d\vb r}{dt}\right|\)
your speed: metres of path per unit of watch time.
\(\left|\dfrac{d\vb r}{dt}\right|dt\)
the metres of path you cover in a short watch interval \(dt\); this is \(ds\).
\(\int_a^b\cdots dt\)
add the notes in the order the watch ticks, from setting off (\(t=a\)) to stopping (\(t=b\)).

Recipe. To compute \(\int_C f\,ds\):

  1. Do: Parametrise \(C\) by \(\vb r(t)\), \(a\le t\le b\) (§1). This puts a stopwatch on the walk so that every point of the path has a reading \(t\).
  2. Do: Compute \(\vb r'(t)\) and the speed \(|\vb r'(t)|\). The speed converts watch time into metres of path: \(ds=|\vb r'|\,dt\).
  3. Do: Write \(f\) in terms of \(t\) by substituting \(x=x(t)\), \(y=y(t)\), \(z=z(t)\). You only need \(f\) at the spots you actually walk through, not everywhere in space.
  4. Do: Integrate \(f\,|\vb r'|\) from \(a\) to \(b\), the smaller limit at the bottom. This adds up (amount per metre) × (metres walked) over the whole walk; lengths are positive, so the limits always run upwards.

Piecewise smooth curves

If the path has corners, walk it one straight-or-smooth stretch at a time, keep a separate running total for each stretch, and add the totals at the end.

For a piecewise smooth curve we divide the path into a number of smooth subcurves \(C_1,C_2,C_3,\dots\), joined end to end, parametrise each separately, and add:

\[\int_C f\,ds=\int_{C_1}f\,ds+\int_{C_2}f\,ds+\int_{C_3}f\,ds.\]

In words: the integral over a path made of pieces = the sum of the integrals over the pieces.

A piecewise smooth curve \(C\) made of three smooth pieces \(C_1\), \(C_2\), \(C_3\)

Grey dots mark the corners where the pieces join: the tangent direction jumps there, so the whole path is not smooth, but each piece is.

Show the calculation: the integral in Example 3.6.1
  1. Simplify the bracket: \(t+9t^2-4t=9t^2-3t\). Why: \((-3t)^2=9t^2\) and \(2(2t)=4t\).
  2. \(\sqrt{14}\) is a constant, so take it outside: \(\sqrt{14}\int_0^1(9t^2-3t)\,dt\).
  3. Integrate term by term: \(\big[3t^3-\tfrac32t^2\big]_0^1=3-\tfrac32=\tfrac32\).

Notice that reversing the direction of parametrisation does NOT change the value of \(\int_C f\,ds\). The reason: each \(\Delta s_k\) in the defining sum is a length, which is positive whichever way we walk; and in the formula, \(|d\vb r/dt|\) is positive and the limits are always taken from smaller \(t\) to larger \(t\).

Arc length

What is this? (arc length)

If every metre of the cable counts as 1, the running total is just the number of metres walked: the length of the path. This is the same as a car's odometer: it adds up speed × time, so the distance covered is the integral of the speed.

When \(f(x,y,z)\equiv1\) (density 1 everywhere), the "mass" is just the length:

\[\begin{aligned}\int_C f(x,y,z)\,ds&=\int_C ds\\&=\int_a^b\sqrt{x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2+z'(t)^2}\;dt\end{aligned}\]

gives the arc length of the curve \(C\) from \(t=a\) to \(t=b\).

In words: length of a curve = integral of its speed over the parameter interval (distance = ∫ speed dt).

Reading the formula
\(f\equiv1\)
every stretch "weighs" exactly its length, so the total is the length.
\(\sqrt{x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2+z'(t)^2}\)
the speed at watch time \(t\) (Pythagoras on the velocity).
\(\int_a^b\cdots dt\)
the odometer reading at the end of the walk.
Example 3.6.3

Consider the circular helix \(\vb r(t)=a\cos t\,\ii+a\sin t\,\jj+ct\,\kk\) (radius \(a\gt0\); it rises \(2\pi c\) per turn). Find the length of one coil.

One coil is \(0\le t\le2\pi\). The length of one coil is given by\(t\) is the angle turned, so one full turn round the staircase is \(t\) from 0 to \(2\pi\).

\[\begin{aligned}&\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{(-a\sin t)^2+(a\cos t)^2+c^2}\;dt\\&=\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{a^2+c^2}\;dt=2\pi\sqrt{a^2+c^2}.\end{aligned}\]

(Inside the root, \(a^2\sin^2t+a^2\cos^2t=a^2\), so the speed is the constant \(\sqrt{a^2+c^2}\).)You climb the spiral staircase at a steady pace: sideways speed \(a\) round the circle and upward speed \(c\), combined by Pythagoras.

Going further · the value does not depend on the parametrisation

Any two parametrisations of the same curve give the same \(\int_C f\,ds\), because the integral is a limit of sums of (value) × (length) that never mention \(t\). For example, the segment of Example 3.6.1 can also be written \(\vb r(u)=u^2\ii-3u^2\jj+2u^2\kk\), \(0\le u\le1\) (the bead starts slowly and speeds up). Then \(|\vb r'(u)|=2u\sqrt{14}\) and \(f=9u^4-3u^2\), and \(\int_0^1(9u^4-3u^2)\,2u\sqrt{14}\,du\) \(=\sqrt{14}\big[3u^6-\tfrac32u^4\big]_0^1\) \(=\tfrac32\sqrt{14}\) again. In general the substitution \(t=g(u)\) turns one formula into the other: \(|d\vb r/du|\,du=|d\vb r/dt|\,|dt/du|\,du=|d\vb r/dt|\,dt\).

In words: going faster along the curve makes \(|\vb r'|\) bigger but the parameter interval shorter, and the two effects cancel exactly.

Going further · mass and centre of mass of a wire

For a wire with density \(\rho(x,y,z)\): mass \(M=\int_C\rho\,ds\); centre of mass \(\bar x=\frac1M\int_C x\rho\,ds\), \(\bar y=\frac1M\int_C y\rho\,ds\), \(\bar z=\frac1M\int_C z\rho\,ds\). Example: a semicircular wire \(\vb r=a\cos t\,\ii+a\sin t\,\jj\), \(0\le t\le\pi\), density 1. Speed \(=a\), so \(M=\int_0^\pi a\,dt=\pi a\) and \(\bar y=\frac1{\pi a}\int_0^\pi(a\sin t)\,a\,dt=\frac{2a^2}{\pi a}=\frac{2a}{\pi}\); by symmetry \(\bar x=0\).

In words: centre of mass = (mass-weighted average position), each piece weighted by its mass \(\rho\,ds\).

You can now: explain \(\int_C f\,ds\) as a running total of (amount per length) × (length) along a path; compute it by parametrising, multiplying by the speed and integrating in \(t\); and find the length of a curve, knowing that the direction of travel does not affect either.

3Work integrals \(\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) ~19 min

From a constant force to a force field

What is this? (work along a path)

You walk along a winding path on a windy day. At every moment the wind pushes you somewhere, but only the part of the push that points along your path helps (or hinders) your walking; a push straight across the path just leans on you. The work integral adds up "how much the wind pushed me along my path" over the whole walk. A tailwind gives positive work, a headwind negative work, a crosswind none.

Another form of line integral is directly related to the work done in moving a particle in a force field. Start with the simplest case. Under a constant force \(\vb F\), the work done \(W\) in moving an object along a straight line from \(A\) to \(B\), that is along the vector \(\overrightarrow{AB}\), is given by

\[W=\vb F\cdot\overrightarrow{AB}.\]

In words: work = (component of the force along the motion) × (distance moved). A force at right angles to the motion does no work; a force against the motion does negative work.

For a force field \(\vb F(x,y,z)\) (a force that depends on position, like a wind that differs from street to street; see Chapter 3a §2) we consider the work done along a curved path in space. The force changes along the path and the path keeps changing direction, so we cut the path into short pieces again, exactly as in §2: on each piece both the wind and the direction are almost constant, so the straight-line rule above works there.

A force field acting along a path from \(P_0\) to \(P_n\)

Drag to rotate. The blue path runs from \(P_0\) (position vector \(\vb r(a)\)) to \(P_n\) (position vector \(\vb r(b)\)), green arrows from the origin. At each division point \(P_0,P_1,\dots,P_n\) (red dots) a dark red arrow shows the force \(\vb F\) there: it changes in size and direction along the path, and it is generally not along the path. The dashed pink lines locate one sample point \((x_k,y_k,z_k)\).

On the short piece from \(P_{k-1}\) to \(P_k\) the force is nearly constant, \(\vb F(x_k,y_k,z_k)\), and the piece is nearly a straight step of length \(\Delta s_k\) in the direction of the unit tangent vector \(\vb T(x_k,y_k,z_k)\) (a vector of length 1 pointing along the curve in the direction of travel, like an arrow showing which way you face as you walk). So the step is \(\Delta s_k\,\vb T\), and the work done from \(P_{k-1}\) to \(P_k\) is approximately

\[\vb F(x_k,y_k,z_k)\cdot\big[\Delta s_k\,\vb T(x_k,y_k,z_k)\big],\]

where \(\Delta s_k\) is the arc length between \(P_{k-1}\) and \(P_k\).

In words: work on one piece ≈ (force) · (step), with step = (length of the piece) × (unit direction of travel).

One short piece of the path: unit tangent \(\vb T\) and force \(\vb F\) at the sample point

The blue arc goes from \(P_{k-1}\) to \(P_k\); the curved black arrow marks its length \(\Delta s_k\). At the sample point \((x_k,y_k,z_k)\) the green arrow is the unit tangent vector \(\vb T\) and the red arrow the force vector \(\vb F\). Only the component of \(\vb F\) along \(\vb T\), namely \(\vb F\cdot\vb T\) (the dashed shadow of \(\vb F\) on the tangent line), does work. Turn \(\vb F\): at right angles to \(\vb T\) the work on this piece is zero; pointing backwards it is negative.

Adding the pieces, the approximation of the work done along \(C\) is

\[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big[\vb F(x_k,y_k,z_k)\cdot\vb T(x_k,y_k,z_k)\big]\Delta s_k .\]

Let \(n\to\infty\) with \(\max\{\Delta s_k\}\to0\); then

\[W=\int_C\vb F(x,y,z)\cdot\vb T(x,y,z)\,ds .\]

In words: work = the scalar line integral (§2) of the tangential component \(\vb F\cdot\vb T\) of the force.

Reading the formula
\(\vb F\cdot\vb T\)
how hard the wind pushes you along the path at that spot: positive for a tailwind, negative for a headwind, zero for a crosswind.
\(ds\)
the length of one tiny piece of the walk.
\((\vb F\cdot\vb T)\,ds\)

The unit tangent and \(d\vb r\)

What is this? (the tiny step \(d\vb r\))

Each tiny piece of the walk is really a small arrow: it has a length (how far you stepped) and a direction (which way you stepped). Writing it as one arrow, \(d\vb r\), packs both into one symbol, so the work on a piece is simply the wind dotted with the step arrow.

The unit tangent is the velocity divided by its length:

\[\vb T(x,y,z)=\frac{d\vb r/dt}{|d\vb r/dt|}.\]

In words: \(\vb T\) = the direction of the velocity, shrunk or stretched to length 1.

Since \(\vb T\,|d\vb r/dt|=d\vb r/dt\), multiplying by \(dt\) gives \(\vb T\,|d\vb r|=d\vb r\), and as \(|d\vb r|=ds\) (§2),

\[\vb T(x,y,z)\,ds=d\vb r .\]

Thus

\[\int_C\vb F(x,y,z)\cdot\vb T(x,y,z)\,ds=\int_C\vb F(x,y,z)\cdot d\vb r .\]

In words: \(d\vb r\) is a tiny step along the curve: its length is \(ds\) and its direction is \(\vb T\). The work integral adds up (force) · (tiny step).

Reading the formula
\(\vb T\,ds=d\vb r\)
(direction you face) × (distance stepped) = the step arrow itself.
\(\vb F\cdot d\vb r\)
the dot product keeps only the part of the wind along the step and multiplies it by the step length, in one operation.
Show the calculation: from \(\vb T\) to \(\vb T\,ds=d\vb r\)
  1. Start from the definition \(\vb T=\dfrac{d\vb r/dt}{|d\vb r/dt|}\) and multiply both sides by the number \(|d\vb r/dt|\): \(\vb T\left|\dfrac{d\vb r}{dt}\right|=\dfrac{d\vb r}{dt}\).
  2. Multiply by \(dt\) (positive, so it can go inside the modulus): \(\vb T\,\Big|\dfrac{d\vb r}{dt}\Big|dt=\dfrac{d\vb r}{dt}\,dt\), i.e. \(\vb T\,|d\vb r|=d\vb r\).
  3. From §2, \(|d\vb r/dt|\,dt=ds\), so \(|d\vb r|=ds\) and the left side is \(\vb T\,ds\). Hence \(\vb T\,ds=d\vb r\), and \(\vb F\cdot\vb T\,ds=\vb F\cdot d\vb r\).

Orientation matters

What is this? (direction of travel)

Walk the same street the other way and every tailwind becomes a headwind. So for work, the path must come with a direction of travel (an orientation), and reversing it flips the sign of the answer. A scalar line integral such as the mass of a wire (§2) does not care which way you walk; work does.

The above integral requires \(C\) to be oriented (§1). Notice that reversing the direction of integration changes the value of \(\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\). If we take the curve \(-C\) as the opposite orientation of \(C\), then, since the unit tangent vector at any point on \(-C\) is the negative of the unit tangent vector at the same point on \(C\), we have

\[\int_{-C}\vb F\cdot d\vb r=-\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r .\]

In words: walk the path backwards and the work changes sign (a wind that helps you one way hinders you the other way). Contrast \(\int_C f\,ds\), which does not change.

Reading the formula
\(-C\)
the same set of points, walked from the old end to the old start.
minus sign on the right
every step arrow \(d\vb r\) now points the other way, so every piece of work \(\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) changes sign, and so does their total.
Show the calculation: \(\int_{-C}=-\int_C\) with a parametrisation
  1. If \(\vb r(t)\), \(a\le t\le b\) traces \(C\), then \(\vb q(t)=\vb r(a+b-t)\), \(a\le t\le b\) traces \(-C\) (§1).
  2. Chain rule: \(\vb q'(t)=-\vb r'(a+b-t)\). Why: the inside function \(a+b-t\) has derivative \(-1\).
  3. \(\int_{-C}\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) \(=\int_a^b\vb F(\vb r(a+b-t))\) \(\cdot\big(-\vb r'(a+b-t)\big)\,dt\).
  4. Substitute \(u=a+b-t\), \(du=-dt\), \(t=a\to u=b\), \(t=b\to u=a\): \(=\int_b^a\vb F(\vb r(u))\cdot(-\vb r'(u))(-du)\) \(=\int_b^a\vb F(\vb r(u))\cdot\vb r'(u)\,du\).
  5. Swapping the limits gives a minus sign: \(=-\int_a^b\vb F(\vb r(u))\cdot\vb r'(u)\,du=-\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\). (For \(\int f\,ds\) the speed \(|\vb q'|=|\vb r'|\) has no minus sign, so there the value is unchanged.)

For plane curves, going anticlockwise around a closed path is taken as the positive direction. For a closed curve the integral is often written with a circle on the integral sign, \(\oint_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\), and called the circulation of \(\vb F\) around \(C\) (in the picture: the net push of the wind on a walker who goes once round a loop and ends where they started).

Ways to write and compute the work integral

What is this? (turning the walk into an ordinary integral)

To actually add up the pushes, describe the walk by a clock: at time \(t\) you are at \(\vb r(t)\). Then at each instant, work rate = (wind where you are) · (your velocity), and adding that over the time of the walk is an ordinary one-variable integral in \(t\).

Furthermore, if \(\vb r(t)=x(t)\ii+y(t)\jj+z(t)\kk\), then \(d\vb r(t)=\big(x'(t)\ii+y'(t)\jj+z'(t)\kk\big)\,dt\), and we have

Formula (work integral with a parametrisation) \[\begin{aligned}&\int_C\vb F(x,y,z)\cdot d\vb r\\&=\int_a^b\vb F\big(x(t),y(t),z(t)\big)\cdot\frac{d\vb r}{dt}\,dt .\end{aligned}\]

In words: substitute the path into the field, dot with the velocity \(\vb r'(t)\), and integrate from the start value \(a\) to the end value \(b\) of the parameter. No square root: the speed does not appear.

Reading the formula
\(\vb F\big(x(t),y(t),z(t)\big)\)
the wind felt at the spot where you are at time \(t\).
\(\dfrac{d\vb r}{dt}\,dt\)
the step arrow \(d\vb r\): velocity × time. Its length already contains the speed, which is why no square root is needed (compare \(\int f\,ds\) in §2).
\(\int_a^b\)
from the moment you set off (\(t=a\), the start of \(C\)) to the moment you arrive (\(t=b\), the end). The order of \(a\) and \(b\) encodes the direction of travel.
  1. Do: parametrise the path, \(\vb r(t)=x(t)\ii+y(t)\jj+z(t)\kk\), and note the start value \(a\) and end value \(b\) of \(t\). This is the clock for the walk; \(a\) must give the start point and \(b\) the end point, otherwise you are walking the wrong way and the sign flips.
  2. Do: differentiate to get the velocity \(\vb r'(t)\). The velocity times \(dt\) is the tiny step \(d\vb r\).
  3. Do: substitute \(x(t),y(t),z(t)\) into \(\vb F\). The wind depends on position, so you need the wind at the point where the walker is at time \(t\).
  4. Do: take the dot product \(\vb F(\vb r(t))\cdot\vb r'(t)\) and simplify. This keeps only the push along the direction of travel: the rate at which work is done at time \(t\).
  5. Do: integrate from \(t=a\) to \(t=b\). Adding the work rate over the whole time of the walk gives the total work.

Yet another form is obtained if \(\vb F(x,y,z)\) \(=M(x,y,z)\,\ii+N(x,y,z)\,\jj+P(x,y,z)\,\kk\) and \(d\vb r=dx\,\ii+dy\,\jj+dz\,\kk\); then

\[\int_C\vb F(x,y,z)\cdot d\vb r=\int_C M\,dx+N\,dy+P\,dz .\]

In words: the work integral is \(M\) times the small change in \(x\), plus \(N\) times the small change in \(y\), plus \(P\) times the small change in \(z\), added along the path. (The whole expression \(M\,dx+N\,dy+P\,dz\) is under one integral sign.) To compute it, replace \(dx\) by \(x'(t)\,dt\), etc.

Reading the formula
\(M\,dx\)
the east–west part of the wind times how far you moved east–west on this step.
\(N\,dy,\ P\,dz\)
the same for the north–south and up–down parts.
\(M\,dx+N\,dy+P\,dz\)
the dot product \(\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) written out in components: the work on one tiny step.
  1. Do: describe the path by one variable: a parameter \(t\), or \(x\) or \(y\) itself when the curve is given as \(y=g(x)\) or \(x=h(y)\). Along a path only one quantity is free; the shape of the path ties the others to it.
  2. Do: express \(dx\), \(dy\), \(dz\) in terms of the differential of that variable (e.g. \(dy=g'(x)\,dx\)); on a horizontal piece \(dy=0\), on a vertical piece \(dx=0\). This says how far you move in each direction when you take a tiny step along this particular path.
  3. Do: substitute the path into \(M\), \(N\), \(P\) as well. The wind must be the one at your position on the path.
  4. Do: integrate from the start value to the end value of the variable; for a path made of pieces, do each piece and add. The limits fix the direction of travel; the work over the whole route is the sum of the work over its stretches.

These are the many forms line integrals can take: \(\int_C\vb F\cdot\vb T\,ds\), \(\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\), \(\int_a^b\vb F\cdot\vb r'(t)\,dt\) and \(\int_C M\,dx+N\,dy+P\,dz\) are the same number.

Analogy

Cycling through a wind field. The wind \(\vb F\) changes from street to street. On each short stretch only the part of the wind along your direction of travel helps (tailwind, positive work) or hinders you (headwind, negative work); a pure crosswind does nothing. The work integral totals this help over the whole route. Ride the route backwards and every tailwind becomes a headwind: the total changes sign. Choose a different route between the same two places and, in general, the total changes too (Example 3.6.5).

Example 3.6.4

A particle moves upward along the circular helix \(C\) parametrised by \(\vb r(t)=\cos t\,\ii+\sin t\,\jj+t\,\kk\) for \(0\le t\le2\pi\), under a force given by \(\vb F(x,y,z)=-zy\,\ii+zx\,\jj+xy\,\kk\). Find the work done by the force.

Here we have \(x(t)=\cos t\), \(y(t)=\sin t\) and \(z(t)=t\), thus

\[\frac{d\vb r}{dt}=-\sin t\,\ii+\cos t\,\jj+\kk .\]

This is the walker's velocity: round the circle (the \(\ii,\jj\) part) while climbing steadily (the \(\kk\) part).

Therefore

\[\begin{aligned}W&=\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\\&=\int_0^{2\pi}\vb F(x(t),y(t),z(t))\cdot\frac{d\vb r}{dt}\,dt\\&=\int_0^{2\pi}\big(t\sin^2t+t\cos^2t+\cos t\sin t\big)\,dt\\&=\Big[\tfrac12t^2+\tfrac12\sin^2t\Big]_0^{2\pi}=2\pi^2 .\end{aligned}\]

The integrand simplifies to \(t+\sin t\cos t\): the push along the coil grows with height \(t\), so the force keeps helping more and more as the particle climbs, and the total is positive.

Show the calculation: the dot product and the integral in Example 3.6.4
  1. Substitute the path into the field: \(\vb F(\vb r(t))\) \(=-(t)(\sin t)\,\ii\) \(+\:(t)(\cos t)\,\jj\) \(+\:(\cos t)(\sin t)\,\kk\). Why: \(-zy=-t\sin t\), \(zx=t\cos t\), \(xy=\cos t\sin t\).
  2. Dot with \(\vb r'(t)=(-\sin t,\cos t,1)\): \((-t\sin t)(-\sin t)\) \(+\:(t\cos t)(\cos t)\) \(+\:(\cos t\sin t)(1)\) \(=t\sin^2t+t\cos^2t+\cos t\sin t\).
  3. Simplify: \(t(\sin^2t+\cos^2t)=t\), so the integrand is \(t+\sin t\cos t\).
  4. Antiderivatives: \(\int t\,dt=\tfrac12t^2\); \(\int\sin t\cos t\,dt=\tfrac12\sin^2t\) (substitute \(u=\sin t\), \(du=\cos t\,dt\)).
  5. Evaluate: \(\tfrac12(2\pi)^2+\tfrac12\sin^2(2\pi)-0\) \(=2\pi^2+0\) \(=2\pi^2\) \(\approx19.74\).

It is fairly obvious that when the path is reversed (the particle moves down the helix), we have

\[\int_{2\pi}^{0}\vb F(x(t),y(t),z(t))\cdot\frac{d\vb r}{dt}\,dt=-2\pi^2 .\]
Example 3.6.5 · the work depends on the path taken

Use the force of Example 3.6.4, but this time the particle moves along a straight line \(C'\) with the same initial and terminal points, \((1,0,0)\) and \((1,0,2\pi)\).

For such a straight line, we have

\[\vb r(t)=\ii+t\,\kk\qquad\text{for }0\le t\le2\pi .\]

Here \(x(t)=1\), \(y(t)=0\), \(z(t)=t\) and \(\dfrac{d\vb r}{dt}=\kk\). On this line \(\vb F=-t(0)\ii+t(1)\jj+(1)(0)\kk=t\,\jj\). Consequently,

\[\begin{aligned}W&=\int_{C'}\vb F\cdot d\vb r\\&=\int_0^{2\pi}(0\,\ii+t\,\jj+0\,\kk)\cdot\kk\,dt=0 .\end{aligned}\]

The force is at right angles to the straight path everywhere, so it does no work there; along the helix it did \(2\pi^2\). Same start, same end, different work: the work depends on the path. (In §4 we see which fields avoid this.)

4The Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals and path independence ~14 min

What is this?

Hike from a car park to a summit. However winding your route, your total gain in height is (height at the summit) − (height at the car park): every bit of climbing and descending along the way cancels down to that. Now imagine a special wind that at every point blows exactly "uphill" on some invisible landscape \(f\), with strength equal to the steepness. The work that wind does on you is then just the change in that landscape's height between your start and your end, and the route does not matter. That is the Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals.

Example 3.6.6 gave 16 along three different paths, while Examples 3.6.4–3.6.5 gave different answers along different paths. The theorem below explains the difference. Recall from Chapter 1 and Chapter 3a §3 that the gradient of a scalar function \(f(x,y,z)\) is the vector field \(\operatorname{grad}f=\nabla f=\pd fx\ii+\pd fy\jj+\pd fz\kk\) (at each point it points in the steepest uphill direction of \(f\), and its length is that steepest slope).

The Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals

Let \(C\) be an oriented curve with initial point \((x_0,y_0,z_0)\) and terminal point \((x_1,y_1,z_1)\). Let \(f(x,y,z)\) be a function that is differentiable at every point on \(C\), and assume that \(\operatorname{grad}f\) is continuous on \(C\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}\int_C\operatorname{grad}f\cdot d\vb r&=\int_C\nabla f\cdot d\vb r\\&=f(x_1,y_1,z_1)-f(x_0,y_0,z_0).\end{aligned}\]

In words: the work done by a gradient field along a path = (value of \(f\) at the end) − (value of \(f\) at the start). The shape of the path in between does not matter.

Reading the formula
\(\nabla f\)
the special wind: it blows uphill on the landscape \(f\).
\(\nabla f\cdot d\vb r\)
(uphill slope) · (tiny step) = how much height \(f\) you gain on that step. Its total is the work.
\(f(x_1,y_1,z_1)-f(x_0,y_0,z_0)\)
height at the end minus height at the start: all the small gains and losses on the way add up to just this.
no \(C\) on the right
only the two end points appear, so every path between them gives the same answer.

Proof. We first consider the case in which \(C\) is smooth, and let \(\vb r(t)=x(t)\ii+y(t)\jj+z(t)\kk\) be a smooth parametrisation of \(C\) with \(a\le t\le b\). Then, using the formula of §3 and the chain rule,

\[\begin{aligned}&\int_C\operatorname{grad}f\cdot d\vb r\\&=\int_a^b\operatorname{grad}f(x(t),y(t),z(t))\cdot\frac{d\vb r}{dt}\,dt\\&=\int_a^b\frac{d}{dt}f(x(t),y(t),z(t))\,dt\\&=f(x(b),y(b),z(b))-f(x(a),y(a),z(a))\\&=f(x_1,y_1,z_1)-f(x_0,y_0,z_0),\end{aligned}\]

where the second last step is due to the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. Hence the result.The key line is the chain rule: (uphill slope) · (velocity) is exactly the rate at which your height \(f\) changes as you walk, so integrating it over the walk gives the total change in height.

Show the calculation: every line of the proof
  1. Work-integral formula (§3): \(\int_C\nabla f\cdot d\vb r=\int_a^b\nabla f(\vb r(t))\cdot\vb r'(t)\,dt\).
  2. Write out the dot product: \(\nabla f\cdot\vb r'\) \(=\pd fx(x(t),y(t),z(t))\,x'(t)\) \(+\:\pd fy(x(t),y(t),z(t))\,y'(t)\) \(+\:\pd fz(x(t),y(t),z(t))\,z'(t)\). Why: components of \(\nabla f\) times components of \(\vb r'\).
  3. Recognise the chain rule (Chapter 1 §3): for \(F(t)=f(x(t),y(t),z(t))\), \(\dfrac{dF}{dt}=\pd fx\dfrac{dx}{dt}+\pd fy\dfrac{dy}{dt}+\pd fz\dfrac{dz}{dt}\), exactly the expression of step 2. So the integrand is \(\dfrac{d}{dt}f(x(t),y(t),z(t))\).
  4. Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: \(\int_a^b F'(t)\,dt\) \(=F(b)-F(a)\) \(=f(\vb r(b))-f(\vb r(a))\).
  5. \(\vb r(b)\) is the terminal point \((x_1,y_1,z_1)\) and \(\vb r(a)\) the initial point \((x_0,y_0,z_0)\).

If the curve \(C\) is piecewise smooth and can be decomposed into, say, two smooth portions \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) joined at \((x',y',z')\), then we can apply the theorem above to each piece and obtain

\[\begin{aligned}\int_C\operatorname{grad}f\cdot d\vb r&=\int_{C_1}\operatorname{grad}f\cdot d\vb r+\int_{C_2}\operatorname{grad}f\cdot d\vb r\\&=[f(x',y',z')-f(x_0,y_0,z_0)] +[f(x_1,y_1,z_1)-f(x',y',z')]\\&=f(x_1,y_1,z_1)-f(x_0,y_0,z_0)\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}\int_C\operatorname{grad}f\cdot d\vb r&=\int_{C_1}\operatorname{grad}f\cdot d\vb r+\int_{C_2}\operatorname{grad}f\cdot d\vb r\\&=[f(x',y',z')-f(x_0,y_0,z_0)]\\&\quad+[f(x_1,y_1,z_1)-f(x',y',z')]\\&=f(x_1,y_1,z_1)-f(x_0,y_0,z_0)\end{aligned}\]

as before (the value at the joint cancels: the height you gained up to the corner, plus the height you gained after it, is the total). The result is known as the Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals, and it is reminiscent of the formula

\[\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx=f(b)-f(a)\]

for functions of one variable: in both, integrating a derivative gives the change in the function between the end points. The gradient plays the role of the derivative.

In words: adding up the small changes \(\nabla f\cdot d\vb r=df\) of \(f\) along any path gives the total change of \(f\).

  1. Do: find a function \(f\) whose partial derivatives are the components of \(\vb F\) (guess from the first component, then adjust; the systematic method is in Chapter 3c §2). This finds the landscape that the wind is blowing uphill on; without it there is no shortcut.
  2. Do: check by differentiating: \(\pd fx\), \(\pd fy\), \(\pd fz\) must give back every component of \(\vb F\). If even one component fails, the wind is not purely "uphill on \(f\)" and the theorem does not apply.
  3. Do: make sure \(f\) and \(\nabla f\) behave (are defined and continuous) along the whole path. The landscape must exist everywhere you walk; a hole or a spike on the route (such as the origin in Example 3.6.7) breaks the argument.
  4. Do: work = \(f(\text{end})-f(\text{start})\), in that order. Height gained = finish height minus start height; swapping them gives the reversed walk and the opposite sign.

Back to Example 3.6.6. \(\vb F=y^2\ii+2xy\jj=\nabla(xy^2)\), since \(\partial(xy^2)/\partial x=y^2\) and \(\partial(xy^2)/\partial y=2xy\). So along any path from \((0,0,0)\) to \((4,2,0)\), \(\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r=4\cdot2^2-0=16\). Not a coincidence.

Example 3.6.7 · gravitational potential

Let \(C\) be a piecewise smooth curve from \((1,-2,3)\) to \((1,0,0)\) and \(\vb F\) the gravitational field of a point mass \(m\) at the origin, where

\[\begin{aligned}\vb F&=-\frac{Gm}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}(x\ii+y\jj+z\kk)\\&=-\frac{Gm}{|\vb r|^3}\vb r\end{aligned}\]

and \(\vb r=x\ii+y\jj+z\kk\) (\(G\) is the gravitational constant). Find the work \(W\) done by \(\vb F\) on a unit point mass that traverses \(C\).

We are not told the shape of \(C\), so we cannot parametrise it: we need the Fundamental Theorem. Consider

\[f(x,y,z)=\frac{Gm}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}}=\frac{Gm}{|\vb r|};\]

Gravity pulls towards the mass, and \(Gm/|\vb r|\) grows as you get closer to it, so its "uphill" direction points at the mass, just like the field. That makes it the natural guess for the landscape.

we have

\[\begin{aligned}\nabla f&=Gm\Big(\pd{}{x}\frac1{|\vb r|}\ii+\pd{}{y}\frac1{|\vb r|}\jj+\pd{}{z}\frac1{|\vb r|}\kk\Big)\\&=Gm\Big(-\frac{x}{|\vb r|^3}\ii-\frac{y}{|\vb r|^3}\jj-\frac{z}{|\vb r|^3}\kk\Big)\\&=-\frac{Gm}{|\vb r|^3}\vb r=\vb F .\end{aligned}\]
Show the calculation: \(\partial(1/|\vb r|)/\partial x=-x/|\vb r|^3\)
  1. Write \(\dfrac1{|\vb r|}=(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{-1/2}\).
  2. Chain rule (power rule on the outside, \(\partial/\partial x\) of the inside \(=2x\)): \(\pd{}{x}(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{-1/2}\) \(=-\tfrac12(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{-3/2}\cdot2x\).
  3. Simplify: \(=-x(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{-3/2}=-\dfrac{x}{|\vb r|^3}\). By symmetry the \(y\)- and \(z\)-derivatives are \(-y/|\vb r|^3\) and \(-z/|\vb r|^3\).

Hence, with \(|(1,0,0)|=1\) and \(|(1,-2,3)|=\sqrt{1+4+9}=\sqrt{14}\),

\[\begin{aligned}W=\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r&=f(1,0,0)-f(1,-2,3)\\&=Gm\Big(1-\frac1{\sqrt{14}}\Big).\end{aligned}\]

End height minus start height. The path must avoid the origin, where \(f\) is undefined.

The function \(f(x,y,z)\) is called a gravitational potential. The work is positive: the unit mass moves from distance \(\sqrt{14}\approx3.74\) to distance 1, closer to the attracting mass, i.e. "downhill" in the gravity field.

Scalar potentials and conservative fields

What is this? (potential and conservative field)

The hidden landscape \(f\) gets a name: the field's scalar potential. A wind that has such a landscape is called conservative: the work it does depends only on where you start and finish, never on the route. Walk any loop back to your starting point and such a wind has done zero net work, because you are back at the same height. A swirling wind, which keeps pushing you round a loop, can never have a potential.

Definition (scalar potential)

In general, if \(\vb F=\nabla f\), then \(f\) is called a scalar potential of the vector field \(\vb F\). (In physics, it is conventional to take \(f\) such that \(\vb F=-\nabla f\) as the scalar potential; then the potential energy decreases in the direction of the force. In this course \(\vb F=\nabla f\), as in Example 3.6.7.)

In words: a scalar potential is a function whose gradient is the field; the field is "the uphill direction" of its potential.

Another way to describe the Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals is: if \(\vb F\) is continuous and has a scalar potential \(f\), then \(\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) is independent of path, where \(C\) lies entirely in a region such that \(\nabla f=\vb F\). "Independent of path" means: any two paths with the same initial point and the same terminal point give the same value. A field with this property is called conservative (the name, and how to test for it, are developed in Chapter 3c §1).

Two paths from \(A\) to \(B\) make a closed loop

A closed oriented curve \(C\) through the points \(A\) (lower left) and \(B\) (upper right). Its upper part \(C_1\) (blue, arrow pointing right) goes from \(A\) to \(B\); its lower part \(C_2\) (purple, arrow pointing left) comes back from \(B\) to \(A\). Going round \(C\) once means \(C_1\) followed by \(C_2\).

Thus, if \(C\) is a closed oriented curve which is composed of \(C_1\) and \(C_2\), then we have

\[\oint_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r=\int_{C_1}\vb F\cdot d\vb r+\int_{C_2}\vb F\cdot d\vb r .\]

The property of independence of path implies that

\[\int_{C_1}\vb F\cdot d\vb r=f(B)-f(A)=-\int_{C_2}\vb F\cdot d\vb r\]

where \(\nabla f=\vb F\) (\(C_2\) runs from \(B\) to \(A\), so it gives \(f(A)-f(B)\)). That is:

Closed paths

If there exists \(f\) such that \(\vb F=\nabla f\), then

\[\oint_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r=0\]

where \(C\) is any closed path within the domain of \(\vb F\). Another way to describe this is: \(\displaystyle\int_{AB}\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) is independent of the path from \(A\) to \(B\).

In words: a gradient field does no net work around any loop: going out and coming back, the change in \(f\) is zero.

Reading the formula
\(\oint_C\)
walk once round a loop, ending where you started.
\(=0\)
start and end are the same point, so (height at end) − (height at start) is zero: the climbing on \(C_1\) is undone exactly by the descent on \(C_2\).
"zero round every loop" ⇔ "path independent"
two routes from \(A\) to \(B\), one walked forwards and the other backwards, form a loop; zero round the loop means the two routes give equal work.

Path independence test: a gradient field versus a swirling field

The four paths from \((0,0)\) to \((4,2)\): \(C_1\) straight (red), \(C_2\) parabola \(y^2=x\) (blue), \(C_3\) along the axis then up (green), and a wiggly path (purple). The readout gives \(\int\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) along each (numerical sum over small steps). For the gradient field all four are 16. For the swirl \(-y\ii+x\jj\) they are \(0\), \(-8/3\), \(8\) and something else again: no potential exists, and (§3) a loop such as \(C_3\) followed by \(-C_1\) has non-zero circulation \(8\).

Watch out

The theorem needs \(\vb F=\nabla f\) on a region containing the whole path. The helix field of Example 3.6.4, \(-zy\ii+zx\jj+xy\kk\), is not a gradient (its work depends on the path: \(2\pi^2\) versus 0), so there no shortcut exists and the path must be parametrised. Exam questions sometimes say "do NOT use the Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals": then parametrise, even if a potential exists (you may still use the theorem to check your answer).

Beyond this chapter · how to tell whether a potential exists

Since \(\nabla\times(\nabla f)=\vb 0\) for every \(f\) (Chapter 3a §6), a gradient field must have zero curl (a paddle wheel placed in it does not spin). The helix field fails: \(\nabla\times(-zy\ii+zx\jj+xy\kk)\) \(=0\,\ii-2y\,\jj+2z\,\kk\) \(\ne\vb 0\). The field of Example 3.6.6 passes: \(\nabla\times(y^2\ii+2xy\jj)=(2y-2y)\kk=\vb 0\). The converse (zero curl ⇒ a potential exists, on a suitable region) and the method for finding \(f\) are in Chapter 3c §1–§2.

◆Common mistakes

✗Leaving out the speed in \(\int_C f\,ds\), i.e. writing \(\int f(\vb r(t))\,dt\). Fix: \(ds=|\vb r'(t)|\,dt\); for a segment \(|\vb r'|\) is the length of \(\vb q-\vb p\) (e.g. \(\sqrt{14}\) in Example 3.6.1). ✗Putting the speed into the work integral, \(\int\vb F\cdot\vb r'\,|\vb r'|\,dt\). Fix: \(d\vb r=\vb r'(t)\,dt\) already contains the length; no square root appears in \(\int\vb F\cdot d\vb r\). ✗Parametrising a segment as \(\vb p+t\vb q\). Fix: \(\vb p+t(\vb q-\vb p)\); check \(\vb r(0)=\) start and \(\vb r(1)=\) end. ✗Running the parameter the wrong way in a work integral (e.g. clockwise when anticlockwise was asked) and not adjusting. Fix: the parameter must go from the start to the end of the oriented path; otherwise change the sign at the end, \(\int_{-C}=-\int_C\). ✗Substituting only some of \(x,y,z\) into \(\vb F\), or forgetting that \(z=0\) on a plane path. Fix: replace every \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) by its function of \(t\) before dotting; the result must contain only \(t\). ✗In \(\int M\,dx+N\,dy\), keeping both terms on a horizontal or vertical piece. Fix: on a horizontal piece \(dy=0\); on a vertical piece \(dx=0\) (Example 3.6.6, \(C_3\)). ✗Using one formula across a corner (e.g. one parametrisation for all three sides of a triangle). Fix: split a piecewise smooth path into its smooth pieces, parametrise each, add. ✗Using \(f(\text{end})-f(\text{start})\) for a field that is not a gradient, or when the question says not to. Fix: the Fundamental Theorem needs \(\vb F=\nabla f\); a field with non-zero curl has no potential. When told "do NOT use the Fundamental Theorem", parametrise and use it only to check.

◆Concept checks

1. You reverse the direction of a curve \(C\). What happens to \(\int_C f\,ds\)? To \(\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\)?

Answer

\(\int_C f\,ds\) is unchanged (lengths \(\Delta s_k\) are positive either way). \(\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) changes sign (every tangent \(\vb T\) is reversed).

2. A force is perpendicular to the path at every point. How much work does it do?

Answer

None: \(\vb F\cdot\vb T=0\) everywhere, so \(\int\vb F\cdot\vb T\,ds=0\). Example 3.6.5 is such a case (\(\vb F=t\jj\), motion along \(\kk\)).

3. What is \(\int_C ds\) when \(C\) is a circle of radius 3?

Answer

The length of \(C\): \(\int_0^{2\pi}3\,dt=6\pi\).

4. What is the unit tangent vector along the segment of Example 3.6.1, from \((0,0,0)\) to \((1,-3,2)\)?

Answer

\(\vb T=\vb r'/|\vb r'|=\dfrac{\ii-3\jj+2\kk}{\sqrt{14}}\), the same at every point of a straight segment.

6. Can the force of Example 3.6.4, \(-zy\ii+zx\jj+xy\kk\), be the gradient of some function? Answer using Examples 3.6.4 and 3.6.5 only.

Answer

No. Two paths with the same end points gave different work (\(2\pi^2\) on the helix, 0 on the line). For a gradient field the Fundamental Theorem would force both to equal \(f(1,0,2\pi)-f(1,0,0)\).

Dec 2024 Q4(a)(iii)5 marksGuide C3b.1 · line integral by parametrising

Consider a vector field defined in \(\R^3\) given by \(\vb F=y^a e^{xy}\,\ii+x^a e^{xy}\,\jj\), where \(a\gt0\) is a constant. (Earlier parts of the question: (i) find the value of \(a\) such that \(\vb F\) is irrotational, which gives \(a=1\); (ii) find a scalar potential of \(\vb F\).)

(iii) With \(a\) in Q4(a)(i), evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) where \(C\) is the straight line segment from \((0,0)\) to \((1,\pi)\) using a parametrisation of the line segment. (Do NOT use the Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals as no marks will be given.)

Hint

With \(a=1\), \(\vb F=y e^{xy}\ii+x e^{xy}\jj\). Parametrise \(\vb r(t)=t\,\ii+\pi t\,\jj\), \(0\le t\le1\), so \(xy=\pi t^2\). The integrand is of the form \(g'(t)e^{g(t)}\).

Answer

\(\displaystyle \int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) \(\displaystyle =\int_0^1 2\pi t\,e^{\pi t^2}\,dt\) \(\displaystyle =\boxed{e^{\pi}-1}\) \(\displaystyle \approx22.14\). (Check, not for marks: the potential \(e^{xy}\) gives \(e^{\pi}-e^0\).)

Show the calculation: outline
  1. \(\vb r(t)=t\ii+\pi t\jj\), \(\vb r'(t)=\ii+\pi\jj\); on the path \(x=t\), \(y=\pi t\), \(e^{xy}=e^{\pi t^2}\).
  2. \(\vb F(\vb r(t))=\pi t\,e^{\pi t^2}\ii+t\,e^{\pi t^2}\jj\).
  3. \(\vb F\cdot\vb r'=\pi t\,e^{\pi t^2}+\pi t\,e^{\pi t^2}=2\pi t\,e^{\pi t^2}\).
  4. Substitute \(u=\pi t^2\), \(du=2\pi t\,dt\): \(\int_0^\pi e^u\,du=e^\pi-1\).
Dec 2016 Q3(a)(iii)5 marksGuide C3b.1 · line integral by parametrising

Given a vector field in \(\R^3\): \(\vb F\) \(=(\alpha y^2+4zx)\,\ii\) \(+\:y(\beta x+2z)\,\jj\) \(+\:(\alpha y^2+2x^2)\,\kk\), where \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are constants. (Earlier parts: (i) find the conditions on \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) such that \(\vb F\) is irrotational; (ii) find the divergence of \(\vb F\).)

(iii) Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) in terms of \(\alpha,\beta\), where \(C\) is the semi-circular path \(\vb r(t)=\cos t\,\ii+\sin t\,\jj\) for \(t\in[0,\pi]\).

Hint

On \(C\), \(z=0\), so most terms vanish: \(\vb F=\alpha y^2\ii+\beta xy\jj+(\cdots)\kk\), and \(\vb r'(t)\) has no \(\kk\)-component. You need \(\int_0^\pi\sin^3t\,dt\) and \(\int_0^\pi\sin t\cos^2t\,dt\).

Answer

\(\displaystyle \int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) \(\displaystyle =\int_0^\pi\big(-\alpha\sin^3t+\beta\sin t\cos^2t\big)\,dt\) \(\displaystyle =\boxed{\tfrac{2\beta-4\alpha}{3}}\). (Check: the irrotational case of part (i) is \(\alpha=1,\ \beta=2\), which gives 0, as it must, since the potential \(xy^2+y^2z+2x^2z\) takes the same value 0 at both ends \((1,0,0)\) and \((-1,0,0)\).)

Show the calculation: outline
  1. On the path \(x=\cos t\), \(y=\sin t\), \(z=0\): \(\vb F\) \(=\alpha\sin^2t\,\ii\) \(+\:\beta\cos t\sin t\,\jj\) \(+\:(\alpha\sin^2t+2\cos^2t)\kk\); \(\vb r'=-\sin t\,\ii+\cos t\,\jj\).
  2. \(\vb F\cdot\vb r'=-\alpha\sin^3t+\beta\sin t\cos^2t\) (the \(\kk\)-part meets a 0).
  3. \(\int_0^\pi\sin^3t\,dt\) \(=\int_0^\pi(1-\cos^2t)\sin t\,dt\) \(=\big[-\cos t+\tfrac13\cos^3t\big]_0^\pi\) \(=\tfrac43\).
  4. \(\int_0^\pi\sin t\cos^2t\,dt\) \(=\big[-\tfrac13\cos^3t\big]_0^\pi\) \(=\tfrac23\).
  5. Total: \(-\tfrac43\alpha+\tfrac23\beta=\tfrac{2\beta-4\alpha}3\).
May 2024 Q3(a)10 marksMay formatGuide C3b.1 · line integral by parametrising

Determine the work done by \(\vec F=x\,\hat i+3xy\,\hat j-(x+z)\,\hat k\) over a straight path from \((1,4,2)\) to \((0,5,1)\).

Hint

Segment: start + \(t\)(end − start), \(0\le t\le1\). Work \(=\int_0^1\vb F(\vb r(t))\cdot\vb r'(t)\,dt\). The May format has no formula sheet: state the formula you use.

Answer

\(W=\displaystyle\int_0^1\big(14-10t-3t^2\big)\,dt=\boxed{8}\).

Show the calculation: outline
  1. \(\vb r(t)\) \(=(1,4,2)+t(-1,1,-1)\) \(=(1-t)\ii+(4+t)\jj+(2-t)\kk\), \(\vb r'=-\ii+\jj-\kk\).
  2. \(\vb F(\vb r(t))\) \(=(1-t)\ii\) \(+\:3(1-t)(4+t)\jj\) \(-\:(3-2t)\kk\) (since \(x+z=3-2t\)).
  3. \(\vb F\cdot\vb r'\) \(=-(1-t)\) \(+\:3(1-t)(4+t)\) \(+\:(3-2t)\) \(=-1+t+12-9t-3t^2+3-2t\) \(=14-10t-3t^2\).
