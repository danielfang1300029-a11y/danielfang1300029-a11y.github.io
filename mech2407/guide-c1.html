Functions of Several Variables: exam guide

December Q1 belongs to this chapter every year, and Q2 or Q3 usually has a C1 part as well. Three question types make up almost all of it, and all three are very predictable: a chain-rule “show that” (often the Laplacian in polar coordinates), a “find and classify the stationary points”, and a gradient → directional derivative → tangent plane chain.

◆Exam format and formula sheet for this chapter

  • December (main format): 3 hours, five 25-mark questions, answer any four. Q5 (PDEs) is compulsory (except Dec 2015, where any four of the five were allowed), so you choose three of Q1–Q4. Q1 is the C1 question in every paper we have, so it is a natural one to pick.
  • Where each type sits in December: C1.1 chain rule is Q1(a) every year (10–13 marks; the rest of Q1 is stationary points or a C2 Jacobian). C1.2 stationary points was Q1(b) or (c) in 2014–2022 (6–13 marks) and moved to Q2–Q3 in 2023–2025 (9–13 marks). C1.3 gradient → directional derivative → tangent plane is part of Q2, Q3 or Q4 (9–12 marks): a tangent plane has been asked in every December paper 2014–2025.
  • What the December formula sheet gives (same sheet 2014–2025): surface integral for \(z=f(x,y)\), line integrals, flux, Gauss, Stokes, Fourier series, reduction formulas. Nothing from this chapter.
  • What it leaves out (so learn the ready answers on this page): the chain rule and its tree, the polar derivatives \(r_x,\ \theta_x\dots\), \(f_x,f_y\) in terms of \(g_r,g_\theta\), the polar Laplacian (C1.1); the second-derivative test (C1.2); the directional derivative, max rate and tangent-plane formulas (C1.3); the linear approximation and implicit-differentiation formulas (C1.4).
  • Time budget: 25 marks ≈ 45 min, about 1.8 min a mark: a 12-mark stationary-points part ≈ 20 min, a 4-mark tangent plane ≈ 7 min.
  • Missing papers: Dec 2021 Q3 and Q4 are missing from the scans; they might have held another C1.3 part.
  • May format (other semester; useful practice, different rules): 2015–2019 five 20-mark questions, all compulsory, with a formula sheet; Q1 was always C1 in three parts (partial derivatives C1.4, stationary points C1.2, gradient C1.3). 2021–2024: attempt all five, no formula sheet, Q1 still C1. 2025–2026: attempt all eight 10-mark questions, no sheet; Q1 is a “find and classify all the critical points” question (and May 2026 Q2 was Lagrange, now out of syllabus). Only May papers ask the direct-partials type C1.4 and “maximum directional derivative”.
  • Words in the papers: “critical point” (May) = “stationary point” (December): both mean \(f_x=f_y=0\). “Determine the nature” = classify as local maximum, local minimum or saddle point. “Discriminant” = \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\).

C1.1Chain rule and change of variables

23 parts · 11 sittings (Dec 2025, 2024, 2023, 2022, 2021, 2018, 2016, 2015, 2014; May 2021, 2015; Dec 2017 repeats Dec 2022 (ii)) · 124 marks · Learn it · note §3

Type card · predictable · which method?
Looks like
“\(f(x,y)\) is transformed to \(g(u,v)\) where …; express / show that \(\pd fx=\dots\)”, then “transform the expression (or PDE) …” with second derivatives. Polar coordinates \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\) in about half the papers.
Which method?
  • New variables given as \(u(x,y),\ v(x,y)\) (Dec 2025, 2024) → draw the tree \(g\to u,v\to x,y\); \(f_x=g_uu_x+g_vv_x\), \(f_y=g_uu_y+g_vv_y\). Done in two lines.
  • Old variables given as \(x(u,v),\ y(u,v)\) (polar; \(x=u^2-v^2,\ y=2uv\)) → the tree runs the other way: \(g_u=f_xx_u+f_yy_u\), \(g_v=f_xx_v+f_yy_v\). Solve this 2×2 system for \(f_x,f_y\) (or, for polar, use \(r_x,\theta_x\dots\) from the ready answer).
  • Second derivatives → treat the first-derivative result as an operator, \(\pd{}{x}=u_x\pd{}{u}+v_x\pd{}{v}\), and apply it to \(f_x\); product rule on every coefficient (\(\cos\theta\), \(\frac1r\), \(v\)…); \(g_{uv}=g_{vu}\).
  • “Show LHS = RHS”, one side in old variables, one in new → differentiate the side whose coefficients are simplest (Dec 2016’s hint: start from \(g_{uu}+g_{vv}\)).
  • Radial function \(f=g(r)\), \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) (Dec 2023, 2021) → one path: \(f_x=g'(r)\,r_x\); then \(r_x^2+r_y^2=1\), \(r_{xx}+r_{yy}=\frac1r\) (ready answer).
  • Numbers given (May 2021) → work out the point \((x,y,z)\) first, then every partial at that point, then sum along the tree.
Do
1. Draw the tree; list the inner partials (\(u_x,u_y,\dots\)). 2. Write the chain rule in general form, then substitute. 3. For second derivatives, apply the operator, product rule, collect like terms. 4. Rewrite coefficients in the variables the question asks for.
Watch
\(\pd rx\ne1\Big/\pd xr\): different variables are held fixed. And \(g_u\) is itself a function of \(u,v\): differentiating it with respect to \(x\) needs the chain rule again (two terms, not one).

Tree diagrams: one term per path from the top to the variable you differentiate by

First tree: \(f(x,y)=g(u,v)\). The two highlighted paths to \(x\) give \(\pd fx=\pd gu\pd ux+\pd gv\pd vx\): multiply along each path, add the paths. Second tree: \(z=z(r)\), \(r=r(x,y)\) has only one path, so \(\pd zx=\dfrac{dz}{dr}\pd rx\). For polar coordinates the middle row is \(r,\theta\); when \(x,y\) are given in terms of \(u,v\), put \(f\) on top, \(x,y\) in the middle and \(u,v\) at the bottom.

Ready answer · chain rule (not on the sheet)

If \(f(x,y)=g(u,v)\) with \(u=u(x,y)\), \(v=v(x,y)\):

\[\begin{aligned}\pd fx&=\pd gu\pd ux+\pd gv\pd vx,\\ \pd fy&=\pd gu\pd uy+\pd gv\pd vy.\end{aligned}\]

If instead \(x=x(u,v)\), \(y=y(u,v)\):

\[\begin{aligned}\pd gu&=\pd fx\pd xu+\pd fy\pd yu,\\ \pd gv&=\pd fx\pd xv+\pd fy\pd yv,\end{aligned}\]

then solve for \(f_x,f_y\) (Cramer’s rule; the determinant is the Jacobian \(\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\)).

In words: the rate of change of \(f\) in \(x\) = (rate of \(g\) in \(u\)) × (rate of \(u\) in \(x\)) + (rate of \(g\) in \(v\)) × (rate of \(v\) in \(x\)), one product per path in the tree.

Ready answer · polar coordinates \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\)

From \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), \(\theta=\tan^{-1}(y/x)\):

\[\begin{aligned}r_x&=\frac xr=\cos\theta, & r_y&=\sin\theta,\\ \theta_x&=-\frac{\sin\theta}r, & \theta_y&=\frac{\cos\theta}r.\end{aligned}\]

So for \(g(r,\theta)=f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\):

\[\begin{aligned}\pd fx&=\cos\theta\pd gr-\frac{\sin\theta}r\pd g\theta,\\ \pd fy&=\sin\theta\pd gr+\frac{\cos\theta}r\pd g\theta,\end{aligned}\]

and the other way round:

\[\begin{aligned}\pd gr&=\frac xr\pd fx+\frac yr\pd fy,\\ \pd g\theta&=-y\pd fx+x\pd fy,\\ \nabla f&=\pd gr\,\vb u_r+\frac1r\pd g\theta\,\vb u_\theta.\end{aligned}\]

In words: moving out along the radius changes \(f\) at the rate \(g_r\); moving round the circle a distance \(r\,d\theta\) changes it at \(\frac1r g_\theta\) per unit length, which is why the \(\frac1r\) appears.

Ready answer · Laplacian results that keep coming back \[\begin{aligned}&\pd{^2f}{x^2}+\pd{^2f}{y^2}\\&=\pd{^2g}{r^2}+\frac1r\pd gr+\frac1{r^2}\pd{^2g}{\theta^2}\\&=\frac1r\pd{}r\Big(r\pd gr\Big)+\frac1{r^2}\pd{^2g}{\theta^2}.\end{aligned}\]

For \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\): \(r_x^2+r_y^2=1\) and \(r_{xx}+r_{yy}=\frac1r\) (since \(r_{xx}=\frac{y^2}{r^3}\), \(r_{yy}=\frac{x^2}{r^3}\)). Hence for a radial function \(f=g(r)\):

\[\begin{aligned}\nabla f&=g'(r)\,\frac{\vb r}r,\\ \nabla^2f&=g''+\frac1rg',\\ \nabla^4f&=g''''+\frac2rg'''-\frac1{r^2}g''+\frac1{r^3}g'.\end{aligned}\]

Second derivatives after any change of variables: apply the operator (for polar, \(\pd{}{x}\) \(=\cos\theta\pd{}r-\frac{\sin\theta}r\pd{}\theta\)) to \(f_x\), product rule on the coefficients. Full polar derivation: note §3.

In words: the Laplacian in polar form = second \(r\)-derivative + \(\frac1r\)× first \(r\)-derivative + \(\frac1{r^2}\)× second \(\theta\)-derivative; for a function of \(r\) alone the \(\theta\) term drops out.

Past-paper parts (December, newest first)

Dec 2025 Q1(a)(i)–(ii)4 + 6 marks

Suppose \(f(x,y)\) is transformed to \(g(u,v)\) where \(u=x^2-y\) and \(v=x+y^2\).

(i) Use the Chain Rule to express \(\pd fx\), \(\pd fy\) in terms of \(x,\ y,\ \pd gu,\ \pd gv\). (Do NOT attempt to express \(x\) and \(y\) in terms of \(u\) and \(v\).)

(ii) Use the results in (i) to express the PDE

\[\Big(1-2y\Big)\pd fx+\Big(1+2x\Big)\pd fy=0\]

in terms of the partial derivatives of \(g(u,v)\).

Hint

New variables given as functions of \(x,y\): tree \(g\to u,v\to x,y\). You need \(u_x,u_y,v_x,v_y\).

Solution
  1. \(u_x=2x,\ u_y=-1,\ v_x=1,\ v_y=2y\).
  2. (i) \(f_x=g_uu_x+g_vv_x=2x\,g_u+g_v\); \(f_y=g_uu_y+g_vv_y=-g_u+2y\,g_v\).
  3. (ii) Substitute: \((1-2y)(2x\,g_u+g_v)\) \(+(1+2x)(-g_u+2y\,g_v)\).
  4. Coefficient of \(g_u\): \(2x-4xy-1-2x=-(1+4xy)\). Coefficient of \(g_v\): \(1-2y+2y+4xy=1+4xy\).
  5. So the PDE is \((1+4xy)(g_v-g_u)=0\); dividing by \(1+4xy\) (where it is not 0) leaves an equation in \(g\) only.

Write the chain rule in its general form before substituting; that line carries the method mark in (i).

\[\begin{aligned}&\text{(i) }\pd fx=2x\pd gu+\pd gv,\\ &\phantom{\text{(i) }}\pd fy=-\pd gu+2y\pd gv\\ &\text{(ii) }\pd gu-\pd gv=0\end{aligned}\]

So \(g=\phi(u+v)\) for any differentiable \(\phi\), i.e. \(f=\phi(x^2+x-y+y^2)\) (checked: it satisfies the PDE).

Dec 2024 Q1(a)(i)–(ii)6 + 6 marks

Suppose \(f(x,y)\) is transformed to \(g(u,v)\) where \(u=x^2+y\) and \(v=2x\).

(i) Show that \(\pd fx=v\pd gu+2\pd gv\), \(\ \pd fy=\pd gu\).

(ii) Assuming that all partial derivatives are continuous, transform the expression \[\pd{^2f}{x^2}+2\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial y\,\partial x}+\pd{^2f}{y^2}\] in terms of the partial derivatives of \(g(u,v)\) with respect to \(u\) and \(v\).

Hint

(i) Tree as usual; replace \(2x\) by \(v\). (ii) Use (i) as operators: \(\pd{}x=v\pd{}u+2\pd{}v\), \(\pd{}y=\pd{}u\).

Solution
  1. (i) \(u_x=2x=v,\ u_y=1,\ v_x=2,\ v_y=0\), so \(f_x=g_u\cdot2x+g_v\cdot2=v\,g_u+2g_v\) and \(f_y=g_u\cdot1+g_v\cdot0=g_u\). \(\blacksquare\)
  2. (ii) \(f_{yy}=\pd{}y(g_u)=g_{uu}\).
  3. \(f_{xy}=\pd{}x(g_u)=v\,g_{uu}+2g_{uv}\) (continuous partials, so \(f_{xy}=f_{yx}\)).
  4. \(f_{xx}=\pd{}x(v\,g_u)+2\pd{}x(g_v)\). Product rule: \(\pd{}x(v\,g_u)=v_xg_u+v\pd{}x(g_u)\) \(=2g_u+v(v\,g_{uu}+2g_{uv})\); and \(2\pd{}x(g_v)=2(v\,g_{uv}+2g_{vv})\).
  5. So \(f_{xx}=2g_u+v^2g_{uu}+4v\,g_{uv}+4g_{vv}\).
  6. Add \(f_{xx}+2f_{xy}+f_{yy}\): \(g_{uu}\) has \(v^2+2v+1=(v+1)^2\); \(g_{uv}\) has \(4v+4\); \(g_{vv}\) has 4; \(g_u\) has 2.

The \(2g_u\) term comes from differentiating the coefficient \(v=2x\); it is the term most answers lose.

\[\begin{aligned}&(v+1)^2\pd{^2g}{u^2}+4(v+1)\frac{\partial^2g}{\partial u\,\partial v}\\&\quad+4\pd{^2g}{v^2}+2\pd gu\end{aligned}\]
Dec 2023 Q1(a)(i)–(ii)6 + 6 marks

(i) Let \(z=z(r)\) where \(r=r(x,y)\). Show that

\[\begin{aligned}\pd{^2z}{x^2}&=\frac{dz}{dr}\pd{^2r}{x^2}+\frac{d^2z}{dr^2}\Big(\pd rx\Big)^2,\\ \pd{^2z}{y^2}&=\frac{dz}{dr}\pd{^2r}{y^2}+\frac{d^2z}{dr^2}\Big(\pd ry\Big)^2.\end{aligned}\]

(ii) With \(r(x,y)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), show that if \(\pd{^2z}{x^2}+\pd{^2z}{y^2}=0\) then \(r\dfrac{d^2z}{dr^2}+\dfrac{dz}{dr}=0\).

Hint

(i) One path: \(z_x=z'(r)\,r_x\); differentiate again with the product rule, and \(z'(r)\) needs the chain rule. (ii) Compute \(r_x^2+r_y^2\) and \(r_{xx}+r_{yy}\).

Solution
  1. (i) \(z_x=\dfrac{dz}{dr}\,r_x\) (chain rule, one path).
  2. Product rule: \(z_{xx}\) \(=\Big(\pd{}x\dfrac{dz}{dr}\Big)r_x+\dfrac{dz}{dr}\,r_{xx}\), and \(\pd{}x\dfrac{dz}{dr}\) \(=\dfrac{d^2z}{dr^2}\,r_x\) (chain rule again). So \(z_{xx}\) \(=\dfrac{d^2z}{dr^2}r_x^2+\dfrac{dz}{dr}r_{xx}\). Same with \(y\). \(\blacksquare\)
  3. (ii) \(r_x=\dfrac xr,\ r_y=\dfrac yr\); \(r_{xx}\) \(=\dfrac{r-x\cdot\frac xr}{r^2}\) \(=\dfrac{y^2}{r^3}\), \(r_{yy}=\dfrac{x^2}{r^3}\).
  4. So \(r_x^2+r_y^2=\dfrac{x^2+y^2}{r^2}=1\) and \(r_{xx}+r_{yy}\) \(=\dfrac{x^2+y^2}{r^3}\) \(=\dfrac1r\).
  5. Add the two results of (i): \(z_{xx}+z_{yy}\) \(=\dfrac{d^2z}{dr^2}\cdot1+\dfrac{dz}{dr}\cdot\dfrac1r\) \(=0\); multiply by \(r\). \(\blacksquare\)

In (i), say why \(\pd{}x\big(\frac{dz}{dr}\big)\) \(=\frac{d^2z}{dr^2}\pd rx\) (it is a function of \(r\) only); that is the step being tested.

\[\begin{aligned}&z_{xx}+z_{yy}=\frac{d^2z}{dr^2}+\frac1r\frac{dz}{dr}=0\\ &\Rightarrow\ r\frac{d^2z}{dr^2}+\frac{dz}{dr}=0\end{aligned}\]

Its solution is \(z=A\ln r+B\): the only radial solutions of Laplace’s equation in the plane.

Dec 2022 Q1(a)(ii)–(iii)4 + 5 marks

Also set: Dec 2017 Q1(a)(ii) (4 marks; part (ii) only, same wording)

Let \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\). (Part (i), C3a.3: the polar unit vectors are \(\vb u_r=\cos\theta\,\ii+\sin\theta\,\jj\) and \(\vb u_\theta\) \(=-\sin\theta\,\ii+\cos\theta\,\jj\).)

(ii) Given \(f(x,y)\), let \(g(r,\theta)=f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\). Show that

\[\begin{aligned}\pd fx&=\cos\theta\pd gr-\frac{\sin\theta}r\pd g\theta,\\ \pd fy&=\sin\theta\pd gr+\frac{\cos\theta}r\pd g\theta.\end{aligned}\]

(iii) Show that \(\nabla f\) \(=\pd gr\,\vb u_r+\dfrac1r\pd g\theta\,\vb u_\theta\).

Hint

Here \(x,y\) are given in terms of \(r,\theta\): write \(g_r\) and \(g_\theta\) by the chain rule, then solve for \(f_x,f_y\). (iii) Substitute (ii) into \(\nabla f=f_x\ii+f_y\jj\) and regroup.

Solution
  1. (ii) \(x_r\) \(=\cos\theta\), \(y_r\) \(=\sin\theta\), \(x_\theta\) \(=-r\sin\theta\), \(y_\theta\) \(=r\cos\theta\). Chain rule: \(g_r=\cos\theta\,f_x+\sin\theta\,f_y\), \(g_\theta\) \(=-r\sin\theta\,f_x+r\cos\theta\,f_y\).
  2. \(\cos\theta\,g_r-\dfrac{\sin\theta}r\,g_\theta\) \(=(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)f_x\) \(+(\cos\theta\sin\theta-\sin\theta\cos\theta)f_y\) \(=f_x\). \(\blacksquare\)
  3. \(\sin\theta\,g_r+\dfrac{\cos\theta}r\,g_\theta\) \(=(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta)f_y\) \(=f_y\). \(\blacksquare\)
  4. (iii) \(\nabla f=f_x\ii+f_y\jj\) \(=g_r(\cos\theta\,\ii+\sin\theta\,\jj)\) \(+\dfrac{g_\theta}r(-\sin\theta\,\ii+\cos\theta\,\jj)\) \(=g_r\vb u_r+\dfrac1rg_\theta\vb u_\theta\). \(\blacksquare\)

Show how \(f_x\) is obtained (solving the system, or deriving \(r_x=\cos\theta\), \(\theta_x=-\frac{\sin\theta}r\)); quoting the formula scores little.

\[\nabla f=\pd gr\,\vb u_r+\frac1r\pd g\theta\,\vb u_\theta\]

Equivalent route for (ii): \(f_x=g_rr_x+g_\theta\theta_x\) with \(r_x=\cos\theta\), \(\theta_x=-\frac{\sin\theta}r\) (ready answer). Dec 2017 (iii)–(iv) go on to the divergence in polar form: C3a.2.

Dec 2021 Q1(a)(i)–(iii)3 + 5 + 5 marks

Suppose \(f(x,y)=g(r)\) where \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) and assume that all their partial derivatives are continuous. Let \(\vb r=x\ii+y\jj\).

(i) Show that, for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\), \(\nabla f=\dfrac{dg}{dr}\dfrac{\vb r}r\).

(ii) Show that \(\pd{^2f}{x^2}+\pd{^2f}{y^2}\) \(=\dfrac1r\dfrac{dg}{dr}+\dfrac{d^2g}{dr^2}\).

(iii) Find the terms \(A(r)\), \(B(r)\) and \(C(r)\) such that

\[\begin{aligned}(\nabla\cdot\nabla)^2f&=\frac{d^4g}{dr^4}+A(r)\frac{d^3g}{dr^3}\\&\quad+B(r)\frac{d^2g}{dr^2}+C(r)\frac{dg}{dr}.\end{aligned}\]
Hint

(i) One path: \(f_x=g'(r)\frac xr\). (ii) As Dec 2023 (i)–(ii). (iii) \(h=\nabla^2f\) is again a function of \(r\) only, so \(\nabla^2h=h''+\frac1rh'\).

Solution
  1. (i) \(f_x=g'(r)\,r_x=g'\dfrac xr\), \(f_y=g'\dfrac yr\), so \(\nabla f\) \(=\dfrac{g'}r(x\ii+y\jj)\) \(=g'\dfrac{\vb r}r\). \(\blacksquare\)
  2. (ii) \(f_{xx}=g''r_x^2+g'r_{xx}\) and likewise for \(y\); with \(r_x^2+r_y^2=1\), \(r_{xx}+r_{yy}=\frac1r\): \(\nabla^2f=g''+\frac1rg'\). \(\blacksquare\)
  3. (iii) Let \(h(r)=g''+\frac1rg'\). By (ii) applied to \(h\): \((\nabla\cdot\nabla)^2f=h''+\frac1rh'\).
  4. \(h'=g'''+\dfrac{g''}r-\dfrac{g'}{r^2}\); \(h''\) \(=g''''+\dfrac{g'''}r-\dfrac{2g''}{r^2}+\dfrac{2g'}{r^3}\).
  5. \(h''+\dfrac{h'}r\) \(=g''''+\dfrac{2}{r}g'''-\dfrac{1}{r^2}g''+\dfrac1{r^3}g'\).

In (iii) say that \(\nabla^2f\) depends on \(r\) only, so (ii) can be reused; that sentence saves a page of algebra.

\(\displaystyle A=\frac2r,\quad B=-\frac1{r^2},\quad C=\frac1{r^3}\)
Dec 2018 Q1(a)(i)–(iii)3 + 6 + 6 marks

Suppose \(f(x,y)\) \(=f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\) \(=g(r,\theta)\) and assume that all their partial derivatives are continuous.

(i) Show that \(\nabla\cdot\nabla f\) \(=\pd{^2f}{x^2}+\pd{^2f}{y^2}\).

(ii) Show that \(\nabla\cdot\nabla f\) \(=\dfrac1r\pd{}r\Big(r\pd gr\Big)+\dfrac1{r^2}\pd{^2g}{\theta^2}\).

(iii) Find the terms \(A(r),B(r),C(r),D(r)\) such that

\[\begin{aligned}&(\nabla\cdot\nabla)^2f=\pd{^4g}{r^4}+\frac2r\pd{^3g}{r^3}\\&\quad-\frac1{r^2}\pd{^2g}{r^2}+\frac1{r^3}\pd gr\\&\quad+A(r)\frac{\partial^4g}{\partial r^2\partial\theta^2}+B(r)\frac{\partial^3g}{\partial r\,\partial\theta^2}\\&\quad+C(r)\pd{^4g}{\theta^4}+D(r)\pd{^2g}{\theta^2}.\end{aligned}\]
Hint

(ii) The polar Laplacian; expand \(\frac1r(rg_r)_r\). (iii) Apply \(L\) \(=\pd{^2}{r^2}+\frac1r\pd{}r+\frac1{r^2}\pd{^2}{\theta^2}\) to \(Lg\); only the terms with \(\theta\)-derivatives are new.

Solution
  1. (i) \(\nabla f=f_x\ii+f_y\jj\); \(\nabla\cdot(P\ii+Q\jj)=P_x+Q_y\), so \(\nabla\cdot\nabla f=f_{xx}+f_{yy}\). \(\blacksquare\)
  2. (ii) Apply \(\pd{}x\) \(=\cos\theta\pd{}r-\frac{\sin\theta}r\pd{}\theta\) to \(f_x\) (ready answer) and collect: \[\begin{aligned}f_{xx}&=\cos^2\theta\,g_{rr}-\frac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}rg_{r\theta}\\&\quad+\frac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r^2}g_\theta+\frac{\sin^2\theta}rg_r\\&\quad+\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2}g_{\theta\theta}.\end{aligned}\]
  3. \(f_{yy}\) is the same with \(\sin\leftrightarrow\cos\) and the signs of the \(g_{r\theta}\), \(g_\theta\) terms flipped. Adding, those cancel: \(f_{xx}+f_{yy}\) \(=g_{rr}+\frac1rg_r+\frac1{r^2}g_{\theta\theta}\), and \(\frac1r(rg_r)_r=\frac1r(g_r+rg_{rr})\) is the first two terms. \(\blacksquare\)
  4. (iii) Write \(Lg=R+\frac{q}{r^2}\) with \(R=g_{rr}+\frac1rg_r\), \(q=g_{\theta\theta}\). The radial part of \(L\) on \(R\) gives the four printed terms (as in Dec 2021 (iii)).

C1.2Stationary points: find and classify

28 parts · 21 sittings (every Dec 2014–2025; May 2026, 2025, 2024, 2023, 2022, 2021, 2019, 2018, 2017, 2016, 2015) · 187 marks · Learn it · note §5

Why a procedure and not a card: the test itself is mechanical, but every paper’s \(f_x=f_y=0\) system needs a different route (factorise, substitute, linear system, integral-defined \(f\)), and about one paper in three has a \(D=0\) point or a parameter.

Decision procedure
  1. Write \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) and factorise both as far as possible (take out common factors: \(2x(y-1)\), \(8y(y^2-x)\), \(e^{-x}(\dots)\)).
  2. If one equation is a product = 0 → case-split: set each factor to 0 in turn, substitute into the other equation, solve. Write every case down, including the ones with no real solution (“\(y=-3\): \(4x^2=-49\), no real \(x\)”).
  3. If one equation gives one variable in terms of the other (\(y=x^3\), \(x=3y-2\), \(x=4y\)) → substitute into the other and factorise the one-variable polynomial. Never divide by \(x\): \(x=x^9\) means \(x(x^8-1)=0\), and \(x=0\) is a point.
  4. If both equations are linear (quadratic \(f\); an \(f\) defined by \(\int_0^1(\dots)^2du\)) → solve the 2×2 linear system: one point (unless the determinant is 0, see step 7). For the integral, differentiate under the integral sign or expand and integrate in \(u\) first (ready answer).
  5. If \(f=e^{h}\) or \(f=e^{-x}(\dots)\) → the exponential is never 0: cancel it from \(f_x=f_y=0\).
  6. Classify each point in a table: \(f_{xx},f_{yy},f_{xy}\), \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\), conclusion (ready answer). Add \(f\) at the point if asked for “extrema”.
  7. If \(D=0\) → say “the test is inconclusive”, then settle it by hand (ready answer): a whole line of stationary points → write \(f\) as a square; otherwise look at \(f\) along the axes and along \(y=mx\) (or \(x=y^2\)). Opposite signs on two paths → saddle.
  8. If there is a parameter (\(k\), \(a\), \(n\)) → find the points in terms of it, then split into cases where \(D\) changes sign or where points merge (\(a=\frac23\), \(k=0\)), and treat each special value separately.

Cues: “find and classify”, “determine the nature”, “critical points” (May), “extrema”; a hint such as “there are four stationary points” is a count to check your case-split against.

Traps:

  • Losing a point by dividing by a variable, or forgetting the \(\pm\) in \(y^2=x\).
  • Using \(D\gt0\) alone: you also need the sign of \(f_{xx}\) to say max or min.
  • Writing “\(D=0\) so it is a saddle”: \(D=0\) says nothing until you check paths.
  • “Find the extrema”: saddle points are not extrema; say which points are maxima or minima and give \(f\) there.
Ready answer · second-derivative test (not on the sheet)

At a stationary point (\(f_x=f_y=0\)), with \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\):

\(D\)\(f_{xx}\)Nature
\(D\gt0\)\(f_{xx}\gt0\)local minimum
\(D\gt0\)\(f_{xx}\lt0\)local maximum
\(D\lt0\)anysaddle point
\(D=0\)anytest fails: check by hand

In words: positive discriminant = the surface bends the same way in every direction (bowl or dome; \(f_{xx}\) says which); negative = up one way, down another (saddle).

Ready answer · when \(D=0\) (beyond the handout)
  1. Line of stationary points (\(f_x\) and \(f_y\) are multiples of the same factor): write \(f\) as a square, e.g. \(x^2+y^2-2xy=(x-y)^2\ge0\): every point of \(y=x\) is a (non-strict) local minimum.
  2. Sign of \(f(a+h,b+k)-f(a,b)\): always \(\ge0\) near the point → minimum; always \(\le0\) → maximum.
  3. Saddle: two paths through the point with opposite signs. Try \(y=0\), \(x=0\), then \(y=mx\): \(f\) becomes one-variable. A leading odd power (\(-x^3\)) changes sign by itself.

In words: when the quadratic part is flat in some direction, look past it: walk along a few lines through the point and see whether \(f\) goes up, down, or both.

Ready answer · \(f\) defined by an integral (Dec 2018, Dec 2021)

For \(f(x,y)=\displaystyle\int_0^1\big(E(x,y,u)\big)^2du\) with \(E\) linear in \(x,y\):

\[\begin{aligned}\pd fx&=\int_0^12E\,\pd Ex\,du,\\ \pd fy&=\int_0^12E\,\pd Ey\,du,\end{aligned}\]

two linear equations in \(x,y\); and \(f_{xx}=2\int_0^1E_x^2\,du\gt0\), so the single stationary point is always a minimum.

In words: \(f\) measures how badly a straight line in \(u\) fits a curve on \([0,1]\); the stationary point is the best-fitting line (least squares), so it must be a minimum.

“Consider \(f(x,y,z)=\dots\). (i) Find the gradient of \(f\) at \(P\). (ii) Find the directional derivative at \(P\) along \(\vb v\). (iii) Find the tangent plane to the surface \(f(x,y,z)=c\) at \(P\).”
Which method?
  • “Gradient at \(P\)” → \(f_x,f_y,f_z\) in general, then substitute \(P\).
  • “Directional derivative along \(\vb v\)” → normalise first: \(D_{\vb u}f\) \(=\nabla f\cdot\frac{\vb v}{|\vb v|}\).
  • “Maximum directional derivative” / “direction of most rapid increase” → \(|\nabla f(P)|\), in the direction \(\nabla f(P)\). “Is \(\vb v\) the direction of maximum change?” → is \(\vb v\) a positive multiple of \(\nabla f\)?
  • Surface \(F(x,y,z)=c\) → normal \(\nabla F(P)\); plane \(\nabla F(P)\cdot(\vb r-\vb r_0)=0\). Check \(F(P)=c\) first.
  • Surface \(z=f(x,y)\) → \(z_0=f(x_0,y_0)\); plane \(z-z_0=f_x(x-x_0)+f_y(y-y_0)\) (same as \(F=f-z\)).
  • General point, “show the plane is …” (ellipsoid) → same formula with \((x_0,y_0,z_0)\), then use that \(P\) lies on the surface to simplify the constant.
Do
1. Partials in general (one line each). 2. Substitute \(P\): \(\nabla f(P)\). 3. Unit vector, dot product. 4. Plane: \(\nabla F(P)\cdot(x-x_0,y-y_0,z-z_0)=0\), expand to \(Ax+By+Cz=D\).
Watch
The unnormalised \(\nabla f\cdot\vb v\) is the most common lost mark. Also check which point each part uses (Dec 2014 and May 2015 switch points between parts).

Gradient, directional derivative and tangent line on a contour map

\(\nabla f(P)\) is perpendicular to the level curve through \(P\) and points towards higher values. For a unit vector \(\vb u\) at angle \(\theta\) to it, \(D_{\vb u}f=|\nabla f|\cos\theta\): largest (\(|\nabla f|\)) when \(\theta=0\), zero along the tangent line. In 3D the level curves become level surfaces and the tangent line becomes the tangent plane.

Ready answer · gradient and directional derivative (not on the sheet) \[\begin{aligned}\nabla f&=f_x\,\ii+f_y\,\jj+f_z\,\kk,\\ D_{\vb u}f&=\nabla f\cdot\hat{\vb u},\quad\hat{\vb u}=\frac{\vb v}{|\vb v|},\\ \max_{\vb u}D_{\vb u}f&=|\nabla f|\ \text{ along }\ \frac{\nabla f}{|\nabla f|}.\end{aligned}\]

Minimum \(-|\nabla f|\) along \(-\nabla f\); zero in every direction perpendicular to \(\nabla f\).

In words: the rate of change of \(f\) in a direction = gradient · unit direction; the steepest climb is straight up the gradient, at a rate equal to its length.

Ready answer · tangent plane and normal line (not on the sheet)

Surface \(F(x,y,z)=c\), point \(P_0(x_0,y_0,z_0)\) on it:

\[\begin{aligned}&F_x(P_0)(x-x_0)+F_y(P_0)(y-y_0)\\&\quad+F_z(P_0)(z-z_0)=0,\\ &\text{normal line: }\vb r=\vb r_0+t\,\nabla F(P_0).\end{aligned}\]

Surface \(z=f(x,y)\), \(z_0=f(x_0,y_0)\):

\[\begin{aligned}z-z_0&=f_x(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0)\\&\quad+f_y(x_0,y_0)(y-y_0),\end{aligned}\]

with normal \(f_x\ii+f_y\jj-\kk\).

In words: (gradient at the point) · (position − point) = 0: the plane through the point that is perpendicular to the gradient.

C1.4Direct partial derivatives, implicit differentiation, linear approximation

5 parts · 5 sittings (May 2023, 2019, 2018, 2017, 2016; no December paper) · 41 marks · Learn it · note §2 linear approximation · note §4

Type card · predictable · which method?
Looks like
“Find the second partial derivatives …, hence evaluate their sum at a point”; “\(z\) is defined implicitly by …; find \(\pd zx,\pd zy\)”; “find the linear approximation of \(f\) at \(P_0\) and use it to estimate \(f(\dots)\)”.
Which method?
  • Composite \(f=S^2\), \(f=1/D\), \(f=\sin r\) → name the inside (\(S\), \(D\), \(r\)): \(f_x=2SS_x\), \(f_{xx}=2S_x^2+2SS_{xx}\); evaluate \(S,S_x,S_{xx}\) at the point before multiplying out.
  • Implicit \(F(x,y,z)=0\) → \(z_x=-F_x/F_z\), \(z_y=-F_y/F_z\) (ready answer).
  • Linear approximation → \(L=f(P_0)\) \(+f_x(P_0)(x-x_0)\) \(+f_y(P_0)(y-y_0)\), then substitute the new point.
  • \(f=g(r)\), \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), asked for \(f_{xx}+f_{yy}\) → the radial Laplacian \(g''+\frac1rg'\) (C1.1 ready answer) checks your answer.
Do
1. First partials, simplified. 2. Second partials by product/quotient rule. 3. Substitute the point. 4. Add up as asked.
Watch
\(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) are asked separately in May 2017: compute both (they agree, which is a check). In implicit differentiation, hold the other variable fixed and remember the minus sign.
Ready answer · linear approximation and implicit differentiation \[\begin{aligned}f(x,y)&\approx f(x_0,y_0)+f_x(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0)\\&\quad+f_y(x_0,y_0)(y-y_0);\\ F(x,y,z)=0\ &\Rightarrow\ \pd zx=-\frac{F_x}{F_z},\quad\pd zy=-\frac{F_y}{F_z}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: near a point, \(f\) is its value there plus slope-in-\(x\) × step in \(x\) plus slope-in-\(y\) × step in \(y\) (the tangent plane). For an implicit surface, the slope of \(z\) is minus the ratio of the partials of \(F\).

May 2016 Q1(a)8 marksMay format

Given \(f(x,y)=\sin\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). Assume the given function is continuous and all partial derivatives exist. Show that \(\pd fx\) \(=\dfrac{x\cos\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\) and \(\pd fy\) \(=\dfrac{y\cos\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\). Find the second partial derivatives \(\pd{^2f}{x^2},\pd{^2f}{y^2}\), hence evaluate \(\pd{^2f}{x^2}+\pd{^2f}{y^2}\) at \((x,y)\) where \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=2\pi\).

Hint

Write \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), \(r_x=\frac xr\). \(f_x=x\cdot\frac{\cos r}r\): product rule.

Solution
  1. \(f_x=\cos r\cdot r_x=\dfrac{x\cos r}r\); likewise \(f_y=\dfrac{y\cos r}r\). \(\blacksquare\)
  2. \(f_{xx}\) \(=\dfrac{\cos r}r+x\cdot\dfrac{d}{dr}\Big(\dfrac{\cos r}r\Big)\dfrac xr\) \(=\dfrac{\cos r}r-\dfrac{x^2\sin r}{r^2}-\dfrac{x^2\cos r}{r^3}\).
  3. \(f_{yy}\) \(=\dfrac{\cos r}r-\dfrac{y^2\sin r}{r^2}-\dfrac{y^2\cos r}{r^3}\).
  4. Add (\(x^2+y^2=r^2\)): \(f_{xx}+f_{yy}=\dfrac{\cos r}r-\sin r\). At \(r=2\pi\): \(\dfrac1{2\pi}-0\).

Check with the radial Laplacian \(g''+\frac1rg'\) for \(g=\sin r\): \(-\sin r+\frac{\cos r}r\), the same.

\[\begin{aligned}f_{xx}+f_{yy}&=\frac{\cos r}r-\sin r\\ &=\frac1{2\pi}\ \text{ at } r=2\pi\end{aligned}\]

◆Before the exam

  • Nothing from this chapter is on the formula sheet: know the chain rule, the polar derivatives, the second-derivative test and the tangent-plane formula by heart (the ready answers above).
  • Chain rule: draw the tree, write the rule in general form, then substitute. If \(x,y\) are given in the new variables, solve the 2×2 system for \(f_x,f_y\).
  • Second derivatives in new variables: apply the operator to \(f_x\), product rule on every coefficient, \(g_{uv}=g_{vu}\). Expect a first-derivative term (\(2g_u\), \(\frac1rg_r\)) to appear.
  • Polar Laplacian \(g_{rr}+\frac1rg_r+\frac1{r^2}g_{\theta\theta}\); radial case \(g''+\frac1rg'\); \(r_x^2+r_y^2=1\), \(r_{xx}+r_{yy}=\frac1r\).
  • Stationary points: factorise, case-split, never divide by a variable; check the count against any hint.
  • Classify with a table: \(f_{xx},f_{yy},f_{xy},D\). \(D\gt0\) needs the sign of \(f_{xx}\); \(D=0\) needs paths (\(y=0\), \(y=mx\)) or a square.
  • Directional derivative: normalise the direction first. Max rate = \(|\nabla f|\), along \(\nabla f\).
  • Tangent plane (every December!): check the point is on the surface, normal \(=\nabla F(P)\), plane \(\nabla F\cdot(\vb r-\vb r_0)=0\); for \(z=f(x,y)\) use \(z-z_0=f_x(x-x_0)+f_y(y-y_0)\).
  • Watch for a change of point between parts (Dec 2014, May 2015) and for a general gradient asked before the value at a point (Dec 2017, 2016).
  • Budget: about 1.8 min a mark, so 20 min for a 12-mark stationary-points part, 7 min for a 4-mark tangent plane.
