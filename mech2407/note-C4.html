1Periodic functions (handout §4.1, pp. 1–2) ~10 min

What is this? (periodic function)

Hold a note on a guitar and look at the sound wave on an oscilloscope: the same little wiggle repeats again and again. A periodic function is any signal like that, one fixed pattern copied end to end forever. The period is the length of one copy: how long you wait before the pattern starts over. This whole chapter is about building such repeating shapes out of pure tones, so first we pin down what "repeating" means.

Definition · periodic function, period

A function \(f(t)\) is periodic if there is a positive number \(T\) such that

\[f(t+T)=f(t)\qquad\forall t\]

and \(f(t)\) is defined for all real \(t\). The number \(T\) is called a period of \(f(t)\). Such a function is also said to be \(T\)-periodic.

In words: shift the graph sideways by \(T\) and it lands exactly on itself; the pattern repeats every \(T\) units of time. (The symbol \(\forall t\) means "for every value of \(t\)".)

Reading the formula
\(f(t)\)
the height of the wave at time \(t\).
\(f(t+T)\)
the height one waiting time \(T\) later.
\(=\)
you hear exactly the same thing then as now: the pattern has come round again.
\(\forall t\)
this must hold at every moment, not just at a few lucky points. One mismatch anywhere and \(T\) is not a period (this is the "biggest gap" in the figure below).

A periodic function, and the "shift test" for a period

Solid curve: \(f(t)\). Dashed curve: the shifted copy \(f(t+\text{shift})\). The shift is a period exactly when the dashed curve sits on top of the solid one everywhere, i.e. when the biggest gap (readout) is 0. The first function is a typical periodic wave like the one sketched in the course (marked at \(-2T,-T,0,T,2T\)); try shifts of 2, 4, 6. For \(\cos3t\) try \(2\pi/3\approx2.09\) and \(2\pi\approx6.28\). For \(\sin t+\sin2\pi t\) no shift ever gives exactly 0 (Example 4.1.3).

Periodic functions occur in many areas of engineering, for example the current and voltage in an AC (alternating current) circuit, and the motion of a piston in an internal combustion engine.

The fundamental period

If a pattern comes round again after time \(T\), it also comes round again after waiting \(T\) twice, three times, and so on. In symbols, if \(f(t)\) is \(T\)-periodic and \(n\) is any integer,

\[f(t+nT)=f(t)\]

and so \(2T,3T,4T,\dots\) are also periods of \(f(t)\) (shifting by \(T\) twice is shifting by \(2T\)). The smallest possible value of the period \(T\) is called the fundamental period, or simply the period, of \(f(t)\).

In words: if a pattern repeats every 2 seconds it also repeats every 4, 6, 8 seconds; "the period" means the shortest repeat time.

What is this? (pure tones and harmonics)

The simplest repeating signals are the pure tones \(\cos t\) and \(\sin t\): one smooth up-and-down swing, like a tuning fork. Squeeze the swing so that it happens 2, 3, … times as often and you get the harmonics, the higher notes that a guitar string sounds along with its main note. The key fact for the whole chapter: every harmonic fits a whole number of its swings into the main period, so all of them come back into step together at the end of each period.

Example 4.1.1
  1. \(\cos t\) and \(\sin t\) have the fundamental period \(2\pi\).
  2. \(\cos nt\) and \(\sin nt\) have fundamental period \(\dfrac{2\pi}n\) for \(n\in\{1,2,3,\dots\}\). \(2\pi\) is also a period of these functions. Why: \(\cos n\big(t+\frac{2\pi}n\big)=\cos(nt+2\pi)=\cos nt\); the wave \(\cos nt\) squeezes \(n\) complete oscillations into a length \(2\pi\), so \(2\pi=n\times\frac{2\pi}{n}\) is a whole number of its periods.
  3. \(f(t)=\) constant is periodic but has no fundamental period: every positive \(T\) is a period, and there is no smallest positive number. (A steady hum with no wiggle "repeats" after any wait at all.)
  4. \(\cos\omega t\) and \(\sin\omega t\) have a fundamental period of \(T=\dfrac{2\pi}\omega\). The harmonics (frequency \(n\omega\), \(n\gt1\) integer) \(\cos n\omega t\) and \(\sin n\omega t\) also have \(T\) as a period.
Plain-language terms · angular frequency, harmonics

In \(\cos\omega t\), the number \(\omega\) (omega) is the angular frequency: how many radians the angle \(\omega t\) turns through per unit time. One full oscillation takes \(T=2\pi/\omega\) (the period); the ordinary frequency, in oscillations per unit time, is \(1/T=\omega/2\pi\). A harmonic of \(\omega\) is a wave with angular frequency \(2\omega,3\omega,\dots\): it oscillates exactly 2, 3, … times as fast, so it fits a whole number of oscillations into one period \(T\).

Reading the formula
\(\omega\)
how fast the tone swings: a bigger \(\omega\) is a higher pitch.
\(T=\dfrac{2\pi}{\omega}\)
one full swing is one full turn of the angle (\(2\pi\) radians), and at speed \(\omega\) that takes time \(2\pi/\omega\). Faster swing, shorter period.
\(\cos n\omega t\)
the \(n\)th harmonic: \(n\) swings in the time the main tone makes one, so it is back in step at time \(T\).

Adding periodic functions

What is this? (mixing tones)

Play two tones that both repeat every \(T\) seconds at the same time: the mixture also repeats every \(T\) seconds, because both parts restart together. Turning one tone up or down (multiplying it by a constant) does not change when it restarts. The numbers \(\alpha,\beta\) below are these volume knobs. The condition that matters is the same period: if the tones do not restart together (Example 4.1.3), the mixture may never repeat.

If \(f(t)\) and \(g(t)\) are periodic with the same period \(T\), then any linear combination

\[h(t)=\alpha f(t)+\beta g(t)\]

is also periodic with period \(T\) (where \(\alpha,\beta\) are constants), since \(h(t+T)\) \(=\alpha f(t+T)+\beta g(t+T)\) \(=\alpha f(t)+\beta g(t)\) \(=h(t)\).

In words: adding or scaling functions that repeat every \(T\) gives something that also repeats every \(T\).

Example 4.1.2 \[\begin{aligned}f(t)=\ &a_0+a_1\cos t+a_2\cos2t+\dots\\&+a_n\cos nt+\dots\\&+b_1\sin t+b_2\sin2t+\dots\\&+b_n\sin nt+\dots\end{aligned}\]

has period \(2\pi\): by Example 4.1.1, every term has \(2\pi\) as a period, so (by the linear-combination rule) the whole sum does. This is exactly the shape of a Fourier series.Every ingredient is a harmonic of \(\cos t\), so all of them are back in step after \(2\pi\); the numbers \(a_n,b_n\) are only volume knobs and do not affect that.

However, the sum of periodic functions is not necessarily periodic!

Example 4.1.3

Consider

\[g(t)=\sin t+\sin2\pi t.\]

Notice that \(\sin t\) has fundamental period \(2\pi\) while \(\sin2\pi t\) has fundamental period \(1\). Here, \(g(t)\) is not periodic. (Check!)

The reason: a common period would have to be a whole number of \(2\pi\)'s and a whole number of 1's at the same time, \(T=2\pi k=m\), which would make \(\pi=\frac m{2k}\) a fraction. But \(\pi\) is irrational, so no such \(T\) exists. In the picture: the two tones are never back in step at the same moment, so the mixture never exactly repeats. The tab below turns this into a proof.

Show the calculation: why \(g(t)=\sin t+\sin2\pi t\) is not periodic
  1. Suppose, to get a contradiction, that \(g(t+T)=g(t)\) for all \(t\), with some \(T\gt0\).
  2. Differentiating an identity that holds for all \(t\) keeps it true, so \(g''(t+T)=g''(t)\) too. Here \(g''(t)=-\sin t-4\pi^2\sin2\pi t\).
  3. Combine to kill the second wave: \(g''+4\pi^2g=(4\pi^2-1)\sin t\). Why: \(-4\pi^2\sin2\pi t+4\pi^2\sin2\pi t=0\). Since the left side is \(T\)-periodic, so is \(\sin t\): \(T\) must be a multiple of \(2\pi\), say \(T=2\pi k\) with \(k\) a positive integer.
  4. Combine to kill the first wave: \(g''+g=(1-4\pi^2)\sin2\pi t\). So \(\sin2\pi t\) is \(T\)-periodic, and \(T\) must be a whole number \(m\) (its period is 1).
  5. Then \(2\pi k=m\), so \(\pi=\dfrac m{2k}\) would be a fraction, which is false (\(\pi\) is irrational). So no period \(T\) exists: \(g\) is not periodic. ∎

Notice in the figure that a shift of \(T=44\) comes very close (\(7\times2\pi=43.98\)), so \(g\) "nearly repeats", but never exactly.

Beyond the handouts · the period of a sum

When the separate periods have a rational ratio, the sum is periodic, and its period is the least common multiple. For example \(\sin3t+\cos2t\): periods \(\frac{2\pi}3\) and \(\pi\); the smallest length that is a whole number of both is \(2\pi\) (\(=3\times\frac{2\pi}3=2\times\pi\)). Exam questions often ask you to "state the fundamental period" of the function you are given: read it off from the interval on which it is defined (e.g. \(-2\lt t\lt2\) repeated: \(T=4\)).

How to find the period of a sum of tones:

  1. Do: Find each term's own period, \(2\pi/\omega\) for \(\cos\omega t\) or \(\sin\omega t\). This is how long each tone takes to come back to its start.
  2. Do: List whole multiples of each period and find the smallest number on every list (if the ratio of two periods is irrational, stop: there is none and the sum is not periodic). The mixture repeats only when every tone is back in step at the same moment.
  3. Do: Check by shifting: does \(t\mapsto t+T\) leave every term unchanged? For \(\sin3t+\cos2t\), a shift of \(\pi\) turns \(\sin3t\) into \(-\sin3t\), so \(\pi\) fails and \(2\pi\) is the answer. This is the shift test of the figure, done on paper.

You can now: say what a period and the fundamental period are, and test a candidate period by shifting; find the period of \(\cos\omega t\) and of its harmonics; and decide whether a sum of tones repeats, and with what period.

2Fourier series representation (handout §4.2, pp. 2–7) ~19 min

What is this? (Fourier series)

A musical note that repeats is a mixture of pure tones: a steady level, the main tone, and its harmonics (2, 3, 4, … times as fast). A Fourier series writes any repeating shape, even a square wave with sharp corners, as such a mixture. The list of numbers that says how loud each pure tone is, is the recipe; this section shows how to read that recipe off the shape.

Let \(f(t)\) be a periodic function of period \(2\pi\) which we wish to represent by the trigonometric series (a sum of a constant, cosines and sines), equation (4.1):

\[f(t)=\frac12a_0+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\big(a_n\cos nt+b_n\sin nt\big)\]

In words: \(f\) = half of \(a_0\) + (amount \(a_n\) of the wave \(\cos nt\)) + (amount \(b_n\) of the wave \(\sin nt\)), added up over \(n=1,2,3,\dots\)

Reading the formula
\(\frac12a_0\)
the steady level the wave wobbles about (its average height); the reason for the half appears below.
\(\cos nt,\ \sin nt\)
the pure tones: the \(n\)th harmonic, swinging \(n\) times per period \(2\pi\). Cosine and sine of the same \(n\) are the same pitch, starting at a different point of the swing.
\(a_n,\ b_n\)
the volume knobs: how much of each tone is in the mix (0 means that tone is absent; a negative value means it is flipped upside down).
\(\sum_{n=1}^\infty\)
play all the harmonics together. A shape with sharp corners needs infinitely many of them.
What is this? (finding the coefficients by "listening")

To find how loud one tone is inside a mixture, play that same tone alongside it and multiply the two, moment by moment, then average over a whole period. A different tone keeps drifting in and out of step with your test tone, so the product is positive half the time and negative half the time and averages to zero. The matching tone is always in step with itself (the product is a square, never negative), so it alone survives. That is the whole trick behind the formulas below: multiply by one wave, integrate over one period, and every other ingredient falls silent.

How do we determine the coefficients \(a_n\) and \(b_n\)? The trick is the same each time: multiply both sides by one particular wave and integrate over one period. Almost every term then integrates to zero, leaving one coefficient alone.

Finding \(a_0\)

Integrate both sides of (4.1) over the interval \([-\pi,\pi]\). Assuming term-by-term integration is possible (integrating an infinite sum one term at a time; this is allowed for the functions in this course), we have

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\,dt=\ &\frac12a_0\int_{-\pi}^\pi1\,dt +\sum_{n=1}^\infty\Big(a_n\int_{-\pi}^\pi\cos nt\,dt +b_n\int_{-\pi}^\pi\sin nt\,dt\Big)\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\,dt=\ &\frac12a_0\int_{-\pi}^\pi1\,dt\\&+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\Big(a_n\int_{-\pi}^\pi\cos nt\,dt\\&\qquad+b_n\int_{-\pi}^\pi\sin nt\,dt\Big)\end{aligned}\]

Now,

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-\pi}^\pi dt&=2\pi,\\ \int_{-\pi}^\pi\cos nt\,dt&=\Big[\frac{\sin nt}n\Big]_{-\pi}^\pi=0,\\ \int_{-\pi}^\pi\sin nt\,dt&=\Big[\frac{-\cos nt}n\Big]_{-\pi}^\pi\\&=-\frac1n\big(\cos n\pi-\cos(-n\pi)\big)=0,\end{aligned}\]

(since \(\sin(\pm n\pi)=0\) and \(\cos(-n\pi)=\cos n\pi\)) giving \(\int_{-\pi}^\pi f\,dt=\frac12a_0\cdot2\pi=\pi a_0\), i.e.

\[a_0=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\,dt.\]

In words: every wave has as much area above the axis as below over a full period, so integrating kills all of them and leaves only the constant. So \(\frac12a_0=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f\,dt\) is the average value of \(f\) over a period.

Reading the formula
\(\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\,dt\)
"listen" with the flat test tone 1: add up the whole signal over one period. Every swinging tone cancels itself out, so only the steady level is left.
\(\frac1\pi\)
the period has length \(2\pi\), so this gives twice the average: \(a_0\) is twice the steady level, and the series uses \(\frac12a_0\).

Finding \(a_n\)

Multiply both sides of (4.1) by \(\cos mt\) (\(m\) a fixed positive integer) and integrate over \([-\pi,\pi]\), giving equation (4.2):

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\cos mt\,dt=\ &\frac12a_0\int_{-\pi}^\pi\cos mt\,dt\\&+\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\int_{-\pi}^\pi\cos nt\cos mt\,dt\\&+\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n\int_{-\pi}^\pi\sin nt\cos mt\,dt.\end{aligned}\]

Now \(\int_{-\pi}^\pi\cos mt\,dt=0\) (as above), and for \(n\ne m\)

\[\begin{aligned}&\int_{-\pi}^\pi\cos nt\cos mt\,dt\\&=\frac12\int_{-\pi}^\pi\big[\cos(n+m)t+\cos(n-m)t\big]dt\\&=\frac12\Big[\frac{\sin(n+m)t}{n+m}+\frac{\sin(n-m)t}{n-m}\Big]_{-\pi}^\pi=0\end{aligned}\]

since the sine of an integer multiple of \(\pi\) is zero. If \(m=n\ (\ne0)\), the above result is incorrect due to the division by \(n-m=0\). In this case

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-\pi}^\pi\cos mt\cos mt\,dt&=\frac12\int_{-\pi}^\pi(1+\cos2mt)\,dt\\&=\frac12\Big[t+\frac{\sin2mt}{2m}\Big]_{-\pi}^\pi=\pi.\end{aligned}\]

Also,

\[\begin{aligned}&\int_{-\pi}^\pi\sin nt\cos mt\,dt\\&=\frac12\int_{-\pi}^\pi\big[\sin(n+m)t+\sin(n-m)t\big]dt\\&=\frac12\Big[\frac{-\cos(n+m)t}{n+m}-\frac{\cos(n-m)t}{n-m}\Big]_{-\pi}^\pi\\&=0\qquad(m\ne n)\end{aligned}\]

and if \(m=n\ (\ne0)\)

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-\pi}^\pi\sin mt\cos mt\,dt&=\frac12\int_{-\pi}^\pi\sin2mt\,dt\\&=\frac12\Big[\frac{-\cos2mt}{2m}\Big]_{-\pi}^\pi=0.\end{aligned}\]
Show the calculation: the product-to-sum identities and why each bracket vanishes
  1. Identities (from the addition formulas \(\cos(A\pm B)=\cos A\cos B\mp\sin A\sin B\), \(\sin(A\pm B)=\sin A\cos B\pm\cos A\sin B\), adding or subtracting the two versions): \(\cos A\cos B\) \(=\tfrac12[\cos(A+B)+\cos(A-B)]\), \(\sin A\cos B\) \(=\tfrac12[\sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B)]\), \(\sin A\sin B\) \(=\tfrac12[\cos(A-B)-\cos(A+B)]\).
  2. \(\cos nt\cos mt\), \(n\ne m\): \(n+m\) and \(n-m\) are nonzero integers \(p\). \(\big[\frac{\sin pt}{p}\big]_{-\pi}^{\pi}=\frac{\sin p\pi-\sin(-p\pi)}{p}=0-0=0\). Why: \(\sin\) of any whole multiple of \(\pi\) is 0.
  3. \(\cos^2mt\): \(\cos^2A=\tfrac12(1+\cos2A)\). The 1 integrates to \(2\pi\), the \(\cos2mt\) to 0, so the result is \(\tfrac12\cdot2\pi=\pi\).
  4. \(\sin nt\cos mt\), \(n\ne m\): \(\big[\frac{-\cos pt}{p}\big]_{-\pi}^{\pi}=\frac{-\cos p\pi+\cos(-p\pi)}{p}=0\). Why: \(\cos\) is even, \(\cos(-p\pi)=\cos p\pi\). (If \(n-m\) is negative the formula still works: \(p\) is just a negative integer.)
  5. \(\sin mt\cos mt=\tfrac12\sin2mt\), which integrates to 0 over a full period.
  6. \(\sin nt\sin mt\) (used for \(b_n\)): \(\tfrac12[\cos(n-m)t-\cos(n+m)t]\) integrates to 0 if \(n\ne m\); if \(n=m\), \(\sin^2mt=\tfrac12(1-\cos2mt)\) integrates to \(\pi\). (All these integrals were checked in sympy for \(n,m=0,\dots,6\).)

The only integral that is non-zero on the right-hand side of (4.2) is the coefficient of \(a_m\), which is \(\pi\). So the left side equals \(\pi a_m\), giving (renaming \(m\) as \(n\))

\[a_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\cos nt\,dt,\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots\]

We now see the reason for choosing \(\frac12a_0\) as the constant term: the formula for \(a_0\) is the same as for all the \(a_n\) terms (note that \(\cos0t\equiv1\)).

Reading the formula
\(f(t)\cos nt\)
play the test tone \(\cos nt\) against the signal: the product is large and positive where they swing together.
\(\int_{-\pi}^\pi\cdots dt\)
listen for one whole period. Every other tone drifts in and out of step and adds up to 0 (the zero integrals above); only the \(\cos nt\) part of \(f\) is left, giving \(a_n\times\pi\).
\(\frac1\pi\)
divide by how loud the test tone sounds against itself, \(\int\cos^2nt\,dt=\pi\), to turn the reading into the volume knob \(a_n\).
why the \(\frac12\) on \(a_0\)
the flat tone 1 sounds twice as loud against itself (\(\int1\cdot1\,dt=2\pi\), not \(\pi\)). Writing the constant as \(\frac12a_0\) absorbs that factor 2, so one formula serves every \(n\).

Finding \(b_n\)

Multiply both sides of (4.1) by \(\sin mt\) and integrate over \([-\pi,\pi]\). Now,

\[\int_{-\pi}^\pi\sin nt\sin mt\,dt=\begin{cases}0&m\ne n\\ \pi&m=n\ (\ne0)\end{cases}\]

and hence all the terms on the right-hand side are zero except the coefficient of \(b_m\), which is \(\pi\), giving

\[b_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\sin nt\,dt,\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots\]

In words: the same listening trick with the test tone \(\sin nt\). There is no \(b_0\): \(\sin0t=0\) is silence, so there is nothing to measure.

Result · the Fourier series of a \(2\pi\)-periodic function \[f(t)=\frac{a_0}2+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\big(a_n\cos nt+b_n\sin nt\big)\]

where

\[\begin{aligned}a_n&=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\cos nt\,dt,\quad n=0,1,2,3,\dots\\ b_n&=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\sin nt\,dt,\quad n=1,2,3,\dots\end{aligned}\]

In words: to find how much of the wave \(\cos nt\) (or \(\sin nt\)) is in \(f\), multiply \(f\) by that wave, integrate over one period, and divide by \(\pi\). The constant term is half of \(a_0\).

Reading the formula
\(f(t)\)
the repeating shape you want to copy.
multiply by \(\cos nt\) or \(\sin nt\)
play one test tone against it.
\(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\)
listen for one full period, so that every non-matching tone cancels out.
\(\frac1\pi\)
scale the reading into a volume knob.
\(\frac{a_0}2+\sum(\cdots)\)
play all the tones back at the volumes you measured: you rebuild \(f\).

This is known as the Fourier series representation of \(f(t)\), and the coefficients \(a_n,b_n\) are known as the Fourier coefficients of \(f(t)\).

What is this? (orthogonality)

Two tones are orthogonal when, played against each other over a full period, they cancel completely: the product averages to zero. Every pair of different tones in our list does this, which is exactly why "listening" with one tone cannot hear any of the others. It is the same idea as perpendicular arrows in space (see the analogy below): measuring along one direction ignores the others completely.

We note that it was possible to obtain formulae for the coefficients easily (they are sometimes known as Euler's formulae) because the functions \(1,\cos t,\cos2t,\cos3t,\dots,\sin t,\sin2t,\sin3t,\dots\) are orthogonal over the interval \([-\pi,\pi]\). That is, the product of any two different functions from this list integrates to zero over this interval. Since all these functions are \(2\pi\)-periodic, any interval of length \(2\pi\) will do for the integration, for example \([0,2\pi]\).

Analogy · components of a vector

To find the \(x\)-component of a vector \(\vb v=v_1\ii+v_2\jj+v_3\kk\) you take a dot product: \(\vb v\cdot\ii=v_1\), because \(\ii\cdot\jj=\ii\cdot\kk=0\) (perpendicular, "orthogonal") and \(\ii\cdot\ii=1\). For functions, "dot product" means \(\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)g(t)\,dt\). The waves \(1,\cos nt,\sin nt\) are like infinitely many mutually perpendicular axes, and \(a_n\) is the component of \(f\) along the \(\cos nt\) axis: "dot" \(f\) with \(\cos nt\), divide by the "length squared" \(\int\cos^2nt\,dt=\pi\).

Orthogonality: the area under a product of two waves

Thin curves: the two waves. Thick curve: their product, with the area above the axis shaded teal and below shaded red. Over \([-\pi,\pi]\) the two areas cancel exactly (integral 0) unless both waves are the same; then the product is a square, never negative, and the integral is \(\pi\) (or \(2\pi\) for \(\cos0t\cdot\cos0t=1\)).

Beyond the handouts · a quick listening test

Take a signal whose recipe we already know: \(f(t)=3+2\cos t+5\sin2t\). Listening with each tone gives back exactly the knobs we put in: \(a_0=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f\,dt=6\) (so the steady level is \(\frac12a_0=3\)), \(a_1=2\), \(b_2=5\), and every other coefficient (\(a_2,b_1,b_3,\dots\)) is 0 (checked in sympy). The formulas do not invent tones that are not there.

How to find the Fourier series of a \(2\pi\)-periodic function:

  1. Do: Sketch \(f\) over one period and write it piece by piece (e.g. 0 on \((-\pi,0)\), 1 on \((0,\pi)\)). You need to know the shape you are listening to; each piece becomes a separate integral.
  2. Do: Find \(a_0=\frac1\pi\int f\,dt\) over one period, and check that \(\frac12a_0\) matches the average height of your sketch. This measures the steady level; the sketch gives an instant check.
  3. Do: Find \(a_n=\frac1\pi\int f(t)\cos nt\,dt\) for general \(n\), integrating each piece (by parts if \(f\) contains \(t\) or \(e^t\)). This listens for each cosine tone in turn; a general \(n\) does all of them at once.
  4. Do: Find \(b_n=\frac1\pi\int f(t)\sin nt\,dt\) the same way. The same listening, with the sine tones.
  5. Do: Simplify with \(\sin n\pi=0\), \(\cos n\pi=(-1)^n\) (table at the end of this section). If your \(a_n\) has \(n\) in a denominator, find \(a_0\) separately (step 2), not by putting \(n=0\). The endpoints \(\pm\pi\) are whole multiples of \(\pi\), so the answer is a pattern in \(n\), such as "only odd tones".
  6. Do: Write \(f(t)=\frac12a_0+\sum_{n=1}^\infty(a_n\cos nt+b_n\sin nt)\) with your coefficients, and list the first few non-zero terms. This plays the tones back at the measured volumes: the rebuilt signal.

Find the Fourier series representation of the square wave

\[f(t)=\begin{cases}0&-\pi\lt t\le0\\1&0\lt t\lt\pi\end{cases},\qquad f(t+2\pi)=f(t).\]

(Graph: height 0 on \((-\pi,0]\) and height 1 on \((0,\pi)\), repeated every \(2\pi\): flat steps of height 1 over \((0,\pi)\), \((2\pi,3\pi)\), \((-2\pi,-\pi)\), …; see the figure below.)

No cosine tone is heard at all: over \((0,\pi)\) each \(\cos nt\) has as much area above the axis as below.

and

\[a_0=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\,dt=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi1\,dt=1.\]

Found separately because the \(a_n\) working divided by \(n\). Check: \(\frac12a_0=\frac12\), and the wave sits at 1 for half the time and 0 for the other half, so its average height is indeed \(\frac12\).

Also

so that

\[b_n=\frac1{n\pi}\big[1-\cos n\pi\big]=\frac1{n\pi}\big[1-(-1)^n\big].\]

\[\begin{aligned}&\therefore\ b_{2n}=0\ \text{ and } \quad b_{2n+1}=\frac2{(2n+1)\pi},\quad n=0,1,2,\dots\\ &\therefore\ f(t)=\frac12+\frac2\pi\Big[\frac{\sin t}1+\frac{\sin3t}3 +\frac{\sin5t}5+\cdots\Big]\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}&\therefore\ b_{2n}=0\ \text{ and }\\&\quad b_{2n+1}=\frac2{(2n+1)\pi},\quad n=0,1,2,\dots\\ &\therefore\ f(t)=\frac12+\frac2\pi\Big[\frac{\sin t}1+\frac{\sin3t}3\\&\qquad+\frac{\sin5t}5+\cdots\Big]\end{aligned}\]

or

\[f(t)=\frac12+\frac2\pi\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{\sin(2n+1)t}{2n+1}.\]

In words: the square wave = its average height \(\frac12\) + odd sine harmonics with amplitudes \(\frac{2}{\pi},\frac{2}{3\pi},\frac{2}{5\pi},\dots\)

Show the calculation: the two integrals \(\int e^t\cos nt\,dt\) and \(\int e^t\sin nt\,dt\)
  1. Let \(C=\int e^t\cos nt\,dt\) and \(S=\int e^t\sin nt\,dt\) (indefinite). Integrate \(C\) by parts with \(u=\cos nt\), \(dv=e^t\,dt\): \(C\) \(=e^t\cos nt+n\int e^t\sin nt\,dt\) \(=e^t\cos nt+nS\). Why: \(du=-n\sin nt\,dt\), and minus times minus gives plus.

  3. Substitute the second into the first: \(C=e^t\cos nt+ne^t\sin nt-n^2C\), so \((1+n^2)C=e^t(\cos nt+n\sin nt)\). Why: the integral we want reappears on the right; move it to the left (the "integrate by parts twice" trick).
  4. Hence \(C=\dfrac{e^t(\cos nt+n\sin nt)}{1+n^2}\) and then \(S=\dfrac{e^t(\sin nt-n\cos nt)}{1+n^2}\). (Check by differentiating; the December formula sheet's reduction formula for \(\int e^{\beta x}\cos^n\alpha x\,dx\) gives the same.)

3Convergence of Fourier series (incl. series sums) (handout §4.3, pp. 7–10) ~16 min

What is this? (convergence)

In §2 we built a shape out of pure tones and said "keep adding tones and the sum becomes the shape". This section checks that promise. Picture adding one more tone at a time and watching the height of the sum at one fixed moment \(t\): if that height settles down to a definite number, the series converges there, and the number it settles on is what the infinite series "equals" at that \(t\). The surprise is at a sudden jump in the shape: smooth tones cannot draw a vertical cliff, so the sum settles exactly halfway up it.

A series converges at a point \(t\) if its partial sums \(s_k(t)\) (the sum of the first \(k\) terms, i.e. the mix after \(k\) tones) settle down to a definite number as \(k\to\infty\); that number is the "value" of the infinite series there. The question of this section: for which functions, and to which values, does a Fourier series converge?

The class of functions which may be represented by a Fourier series is quite large, larger than the class of functions which have a Taylor series. A Taylor series requires infinite differentiability at a point (you need \(f'(0),f''(0),f'''(0),\dots\)), while a Fourier series can be applied to some discontinuous functions, such as the square wave. The approximation error using a truncated Taylor series (a Taylor polynomial) may be very large at a point far away from where the Taylor series is computed, while the truncated Fourier series has its error distributed more evenly along the domain of approximation. The reason is in how each is built: a Taylor polynomial copies the function's height and slopes at one point, while each Fourier coefficient is found by "listening" over the whole period (multiply and average), so every part of the interval has an equal say.

What is this? (piecewise continuous)

A piecewise continuous function is one you can draw with a pen that you lift only a few times. Between lifts the line is unbroken; at each lift the pen stops at a definite height and starts again at a definite height (possibly a different one), so the graph has a finite step there. What is not allowed is a piece that shoots off to infinity, because then there is no height to stop at. These are exactly the shapes that a mix of tones can copy.

Definition 4.3.1 · piecewise continuous

A real-valued function \(f\) is said to be piecewise continuous on an interval \([a,b]\) if

  1. \(f\) is defined and is continuous at all but a finite number of points in \([a,b]\);
  2. the right- and left-hand limits \[\begin{aligned}f(t_0^+)&=\lim_{h\to0}f(t_0+h)\quad(h\gt0)\\ f(t_0^-)&=\lim_{h\to0}f(t_0-h)\quad(h\gt0)\end{aligned}\] exist for all \(t_0\in(a,b)\), and the right-hand limit exists at \(t_0=a\) and the left-hand limit exists at \(t_0=b\).

In words: the graph is made of finitely many unbroken pieces, and at every break you can see a definite height approaching from the right (\(f(t_0^+)\)) and a definite height approaching from the left (\(f(t_0^-)\)); the graph never shoots off to infinity. A finite jump is allowed; a vertical asymptote such as \(1/t\) at 0 is not.

Reading the formula

a moment a little to the right (later) of \(t_0\): you are on the piece of graph just after the break.
\(t_0-h\) with \(h\gt0\)
a moment a little to the left (earlier): you are on the piece just before the break.
\(\lim_{h\to0}\)
slide towards the break without landing on it: the height you head for is where the pen stopped (left) or restarts (right). \(f(t_0)\) itself plays no part.

What is this? (one-sided derivatives)

At a break the ordinary slope makes no sense, but each piece still has its own slope as it arrives at the break. The left-hand derivative is the slope of the piece coming in from the left; the right-hand derivative is the slope of the piece leaving to the right. The convergence theorem needs each piece to arrive with a definite slope.

In a similar way to part 2 of Definition 4.3.1, we may define the right- and left-hand derivatives of \(f(t)\) at \(t_0\) by

\[\begin{aligned}f'(t_0^+)&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(t_0+h)-f(t_0^+)}{h}\quad(h\gt0)\\ f'(t_0^-)&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(t_0-h)-f(t_0^-)}{-h}\quad(h\gt0)\end{aligned}\]

respectively. Whenever \(f'(t_0)\) exists, \(f'(t_0^+)=f'(t_0^-)=f'(t_0)\).

In words: the right-hand derivative is the slope of the piece of graph just to the right of \(t_0\) (measured from the height that piece approaches); the left-hand derivative is the slope of the piece just to the left. At a jump they can exist even though the ordinary derivative does not.

What is this? (the convergence theorem)

Every tone in the mix is a smooth wave, so no finite mix can jump; near a cliff in the shape the sum has to climb steeply instead. As you add more tones the climb gets steeper and steeper, and at the cliff itself the sum settles on the halfway height, the average of the height just before and just after. That is natural, because every coefficient was found by averaging: at a jump, the series cannot "prefer" the top or the bottom. Wherever the shape is unbroken, the sum settles on the shape itself.

Theorem 4.3.1 (Convergence Theorem)

Let \(f(t)\) be a piecewise continuous function on \([-\pi,\pi]\) and have right- and left-hand derivatives on \((-\pi,\pi)\), with \(f'(-\pi^+)\) and \(f'(\pi^-)\) existing. Then,

  1. for \(-\pi\lt t_0\lt\pi\), the Fourier series converges to \(\frac12\big[f(t_0^-)+f(t_0^+)\big]\);
  2. at \(t_0=-\pi\) and \(t_0=\pi\), the Fourier series converges to \(\frac12\big[f(-\pi^+)+f(\pi^-)\big]\).

In words: the series converges to the average of the left and right limits, "the point midway between the gap in the graph".

Reading the formula
\(f(t_0^-)\), \(f(t_0^+)\)
the height of the shape just before and just after the moment \(t_0\): the bottom and top of the cliff (or the same height if there is no cliff).
\(\frac12\big[\ \cdot\ +\ \cdot\ \big]\)
the halfway height. The value the function was given at \(t_0\) does not appear: the tones never see a single point, only averages.
\(f(\pi^-)\) and \(f(-\pi^+)\) together
the series repeats with period \(2\pi\), so the end of one copy (\(f(\pi^-)\)) is glued to the start of the next (\(f(-\pi^+)\)). The two ends of the interval are one and the same joint.

The conclusion of this theorem may be stated as:

  1. At a point where \(f(t)\) is continuous, the Fourier series converges to \(f(t)\) (left and right limits are equal, so their average is \(f(t)\)).
  2. At a point where \(f(t)\) is discontinuous, the Fourier series converges to the point midway between the gap in the graph.
  3. At the end points, the Fourier series converges to the average of the two end-point limit values. Due to the periodicity of the Fourier series, this is just a special case of 2: the periodic extension joins the right end \(f(\pi^-)\) of one copy to the left end \(f(-\pi^+)\) of the next, so \(t=\pi\) is an ordinary (possible) jump.

What is this? (summing a number series)

A Fourier series is a recipe that holds at every moment \(t\). Freeze the recipe at a well-chosen moment, such as \(t=0\), \(\frac\pi2\) or \(\pi\), and every tone becomes a plain number (1, −1 or 0), so the infinite sum of tones turns into an ordinary number series. Its total must be the height the convergence theorem predicts at that moment, which we can read off the graph. So one recipe gives you the value of a number series for free.

By choosing special values for \(t\) in the Fourier series, we may often obtain results concerning sums of infinite series. For example, put \(t=\frac\pi2\) in the above series. Here \(f\) is continuous with \(f(\frac\pi2)=1\), so we know (from the convergence theorem) that the Fourier series converges to 1. Since \(\sin\frac{(2n+1)\pi}2=1,-1,1,-1,\dots\) for \(n=0,1,2,3,\dots\), this gives

\(t=\frac\pi2\) is chosen because it is the middle of the top step (no cliff, so the target value is simply 1) and because there every tone \(\sin(2n+1)t\) is at a crest or a trough, giving alternating \(\pm1\).

\[1=\frac12+\frac2\pi\Big(\frac11-\frac13+\frac15-\frac17+\cdots\Big)\]

or

\[\frac\pi4=1-\frac13+\frac15-\frac17+\cdots\]

Rearranging: subtract \(\frac12\), then multiply by \(\frac\pi2\). The left side is the height we read off the graph; the bracket is the number series we wanted.

In words: the alternating sum of the reciprocals of the odd numbers is \(\pi/4\) (Leibniz's series), read off from the square wave at its middle.

Look closely at a partial sum next to a jump: it always overshoots, by about 9% of the height of the jump, and the overshoot does not shrink as you add terms; it only gets narrower and moves closer to the jump. 4Even and odd functions (handout §4.4, pp. 11–13) ~12 min

What is this? (even and odd functions)

Put a mirror along the vertical axis. A shape that looks the same in the mirror is even: what happens at time \(-t\) is exactly what happens at time \(t\). A shape that looks the same after a half-turn about the origin is odd: what happens at \(-t\) is the upside-down of what happens at \(t\). Among our pure tones, the cosines (and the constant) are all mirror-symmetric, and the sines are all half-turn-symmetric. That is the whole point of this section: a mirror-symmetric shape can be built from cosine tones alone, a half-turn-symmetric one from sine tones alone.

Definition 4.4.1
  • \(f(t)\) is even if \(f(-t)=f(t)\quad\forall t\);
  • \(f(t)\) is odd if \(f(-t)=-f(t)\quad\forall t\).

In words: an even graph is a mirror image of itself in the vertical axis; an odd graph is unchanged by a half-turn (180° rotation) about the origin. Most functions are neither (e.g. \(e^t\), \(t+1\)).

Properties

What is this? (the area short cuts)

An integral over \((-\pi,\pi)\) is the signed area under the graph, left half plus right half. For an even shape the left half of the area is a mirror copy of the right half, so the total is twice the right half. For an odd shape the left half is an upside-down copy, so it is the same area with the opposite sign and the two halves cancel to zero. The algebra below just carries out this mirror (the substitution \(x=-t\)).

  1. For an even function, \[\begin{aligned}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\,dt&=\int_{-\pi}^0f(t)\,dt+\int_0^\pi f(t)\,dt\\&=\int_\pi^0f(-x)(-dx)+\int_0^\pi f(t)\,dt\\&=\int_0^\pi f(t)\,dt+\int_0^\pi f(t)\,dt\\&=2\int_0^\pi f(t)\,dt\end{aligned}\] (let \(x=-t\) in the first integral).
  2. For an odd function, \[\begin{aligned}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\,dt&=\int_{-\pi}^0f(t)\,dt+\int_0^\pi f(t)\,dt\\&=\int_\pi^0f(-x)(-dx)+\int_0^\pi f(t)\,dt\\&=-\int_0^\pi f(x)\,dx+\int_0^\pi f(t)\,dt\\&=0.\end{aligned}\]
Show the calculation: the substitution \(x=-t\), line by line
  1. In \(\int_{-\pi}^0f(t)\,dt\) put \(t=-x\): then \(dt=-dx\); when \(t=-\pi\), \(x=\pi\); when \(t=0\), \(x=0\). So \(\int_{-\pi}^0f(t)\,dt=\int_\pi^0f(-x)(-dx)\).
  2. Flipping the limits changes the sign again: \(\int_\pi^0f(-x)(-dx)=\int_0^\pi f(-x)\,dx\).
  3. Even \(f\): \(f(-x)=f(x)\), so this is \(\int_0^\pi f(x)\,dx\), the same as the right half (the name of the variable does not matter). Total: twice the right half.
  4. Odd \(f\): \(f(-x)=-f(x)\), so this is \(-\int_0^\pi f(x)\,dx\), which cancels the right half exactly. Total: 0.

In words: products follow the sign rule for multiplying ± numbers, with even = "+" and odd = "−": even × even = even, odd × odd = even, odd × even = odd.

Fourier series of even and odd functions

What is this? (cosine and sine series)

Recall from §2 that each coefficient is found by "listening" for one tone: multiply the shape by that tone and average over a period. If the shape is mirror-symmetric and you listen with a sine tone (half-turn-symmetric), the product is half-turn-symmetric, so its average is zero: there is no sine tone in the mix at all. Listening with a cosine tone gives a mirror-symmetric product, so you only need to listen over the right half and double. For a half-turn-symmetric shape it is the other way round.

Now consider finding the Fourier series of an even function \(f(t)\):

\[a_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\cos nt\,dt=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f(t)\cos nt\,dt\]

since the product \(f(t)\cos nt\) is even (property 1), and

\[b_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\sin nt\,dt=0\]

since the product \(f(t)\sin nt\) is odd (property 2). Hence the Fourier series of an even function \(f(t)\) is

Fourier cosine series (even \(f\), period \(2\pi\)) \[\begin{aligned}f(t)&=\frac12a_0+\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\cos nt,\\ a_n&=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f(t)\cos nt\,dt.\end{aligned}\]

In words: an even function needs only the even waves (the cosines and the constant); integrate over the right half only and double.

Reading the formula
\(f(t)\cos nt\)
listening for the \(n\)th cosine tone: mirror-symmetric times mirror-symmetric is mirror-symmetric, so the left half repeats the right half.
\(\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\)
listen over the right half only; the 2 counts the left half, which is a copy (the usual \(\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\) becomes \(\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\)).
no \(b_n\) terms
listening with a sine gives a half-turn-symmetric product whose area cancels: no sine tone is present.
\(\frac12a_0\)
the average height (the \(n=0\) case of the same formula, then halved).

This is called a Fourier cosine series. You may of course continue to use the full form of the Fourier series when \(f(t)\) is even, but the above form requires less work and you are less likely to make an error in the integration! As expected, an even function \(f(t)\) requires only even terms (the even functions \(1,\cos nt\)) in its representation.

Similarly, if \(f(t)\) is an odd function, all the coefficients \(a_n\) are zero (including \(a_0\)) and we find

Fourier sine series (odd \(f\), period \(2\pi\)) \[\begin{aligned}f(t)&=\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n\sin nt,\\ b_n&=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f(t)\sin nt\,dt.\end{aligned}\]

In words: an odd function is built from the odd waves \(\sin nt\) only; no constant, no cosines.

Reading the formula
\(f(t)\sin nt\)
odd times odd is even, so this product is mirror-symmetric: listen over the right half and double.
no \(a_n\), no \(a_0\)
odd times a cosine is odd, so every cosine "listen" cancels to zero; \(a_0=0\) says the average height of a half-turn-symmetric shape is 0.

This is a Fourier sine series, consisting of only odd terms (the odd functions \(\sin nt\)). Note: "even/odd terms" here means even/odd functions, not even/odd values of \(n\): the square wave of Example 4.2.1 contained only odd harmonics \(n=1,3,5,\dots\), which is a different matter.

Procedure · using the even/odd short cuts
  1. Do: test the symmetry: work out \(f(-t)\) (or reflect the sketch in the vertical axis) and compare with \(f(t)\) and \(-f(t)\). This checks whether the shape survives the mirror (even), the half-turn (odd) or neither.
  2. Do: if neither, try subtracting the average height (Example 4.4.3), or use the full formulas. A shape can be half-turn-symmetric about its own middle level; removing that constant tone can reveal the symmetry.
  3. Do: write the zero coefficients straight down with a reason: even \(\Rightarrow b_n=0\); odd \(\Rightarrow a_n=0\) for all \(n\ge0\). Listening with a tone of the opposite symmetry gives a product whose areas cancel; stating "the integrand is odd" earns the mark.
  4. Do: compute the surviving coefficients with \(\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\), using only the formula of \(f\) for \(t\gt0\). The left half is a copy, so half the interval is enough; often \(f\) has a simpler formula there (e.g. \(|\sin t|=\sin t\)).
  5. Do: treat \(a_0\) and any \(n\) that makes a denominator zero (such as \(n=1\) with \(1-n\)) separately. The general formula was derived by dividing by that quantity, so it says nothing about that tone; listen for it directly.
  6. Do: assemble \(\frac12a_0+\sum a_n\cos nt\) or \(\sum b_n\sin nt\), keeping only the non-zero terms. The recipe lists only the tones actually present in the mix.

The arches are mirror images about \(t=0\) (\(|\sin(-t)|=|\sin t|\)), so no sine tone is present and we listen over \((0,\pi)\) only, where \(|\sin t|=\sin t\).

5Half-range series (handout §4.5, pp. 14–15) ~9 min

What is this? (half-range series)

A guitar string only exists between its two fixed ends, so its shape is known on \((0,\pi)\) and nowhere else. Our tone recipe, though, needs a full repeating pattern of length \(2\pi\). So we invent the missing half: reflect the given piece in a mirror (an even shape, built from cosine tones only) or give it a half-turn (an odd shape, built from sine tones only), then repeat. Either invented pattern agrees with the string on \((0,\pi)\), which is all we care about, and §4's short cuts mean we only ever integrate over the half we were given.

In various kinds of applications (such as the solution of partial differential equations by the separation of variables method, Chapter 5), it is necessary to express a function \(f(t)\) defined only on \([0,\pi]\) as a series of cosines or sines. (Think of the initial shape of a guitar string fixed at both ends: it only exists between the ends.) Since we only care about \(0\lt t\lt\pi\), we are free to invent the function on \((-\pi,0)\) any way we like, then repeat it with period \(2\pi\). Two inventions are useful.

Definition · even and odd periodic extensions

We may construct an even function \(f_1(t)\) by

\[f_1(t)=\begin{cases}f(t)&0\lt t\lt\pi\\f(-t)&-\pi\lt t\lt0\end{cases}\]

and \(f_1(t+2\pi)=f_1(t)\), known as the even periodic extension of \(f(t)\). This has a Fourier cosine series which converges to \(f(t)\) on \((0,\pi)\).

Similarly we may construct an odd function \(f_2(t)\) by

\[f_2(t)=\begin{cases}f(t)&0\lt t\lt\pi\\-f(-t)&-\pi\lt t\lt0\end{cases}\]

and \(f_2(t+2\pi)=f_2(t)\), known as the odd periodic extension of \(f(t)\). This has a Fourier sine series which converges to \(f(t)\) on \((0,\pi)\).

Reading the formula
\(f(t)\) for \(0\lt t\lt\pi\)
the part we were given: both extensions keep it untouched.
\(f(-t)\) for \(-\pi\lt t\lt0\)
the invented left half for the even extension: the height at \(-t\) is copied from \(+t\), a mirror image.
\(-f(-t)\)
the invented left half for the odd extension: the mirror image turned upside down, i.e. a half-turn of the given piece.
\(f_{1,2}(t+2\pi)=f_{1,2}(t)\)
repeat the completed pattern forever, so it is a shape the tones can build.

In words: even extension = reflect the graph in the vertical axis, then repeat; odd extension = rotate it by a half-turn about the origin, then repeat. Then use the §4 short cuts: \(a_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f\cos nt\,dt\) (cosine series) or \(b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f\sin nt\,dt\) (sine series). These only use \(f\) on \([0,\pi]\), which is all we are given.

Procedure · building a half-range cosine or sine series
  1. Do: read which series is asked for: "cosine series" (or "even extension") or "sine series" (or "odd extension"). This decides how you invent the missing half: mirror for cosines, half-turn for sines.
  2. Do: sketch the extension on about \(-2\pi\le t\le2\pi\) (exams often ask for it). The sketch shows the invented pattern the tones are copying, and where its jumps are, which you need for convergence values.
  3. Do: cosine series: \(a_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f(t)\cos nt\,dt\), with \(a_0\) computed separately; sine series: \(b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f(t)\sin nt\,dt\). The extension is even (or odd), so §4 applies: the opposite tones are absent and listening over the given half, doubled, is enough.
  4. Do: simplify with \(\sin n\pi=0\) and \(\cos n\pi=(-1)^n\); split odd and even \(n\) if the pattern \((-1)^n-1\) appears.   5. Do: write the series and, if asked, a value or a number sum at a chosen \(t\) using the convergence theorem on the extension. The series copies the invented pattern, so at the ends \(0\) and \(\pi\) it follows the extension's halfway rule, not the given \(f\).

Show the calculation: integration by parts for \(a_n\)
  1. Parts with \(u=t\), \(dv=\cos nt\,dt\): \(du=dt\), \(v=\frac{\sin nt}n\). So \(\int_0^\pi t\cos nt\,dt=\big[\frac{t\sin nt}n\big]_0^\pi-\int_0^\pi\frac{\sin nt}n\,dt\).
  2. Boundary term: \(\frac{\pi\sin n\pi}{n}-0=0\) because \(\sin n\pi=0\).
  3. Remaining: \(-\frac1n\int_0^\pi\sin nt\,dt\) \(=-\frac1n\big[-\frac{\cos nt}{n}\big]_0^\pi\) \(=\big[\frac{\cos nt}{n^2}\big]_0^\pi\).
  4. \(=\frac{\cos n\pi-1}{n^2}=\frac{(-1)^n-1}{n^2}\). Times \(\frac2\pi\): \(a_n=\frac{2[(-1)^n-1]}{n^2\pi}\): \(-\frac{4}{n^2\pi}\) for odd \(n\), 0 for even \(n\).

Therefore,

\[\begin{aligned}f(t)&=\frac\pi2-\frac2\pi\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1-(-1)^n}{n^2}\cos nt\\&=\frac\pi2-\frac4\pi\cos t-\frac{4}{9\pi}\cos3t -\frac{4}{25\pi}\cos5t-\cdots\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}f(t)&=\frac\pi2-\frac2\pi\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1-(-1)^n}{n^2}\cos nt\\&=\frac\pi2-\frac4\pi\cos t-\frac{4}{9\pi}\cos3t\\&\quad-\frac{4}{25\pi}\cos5t-\cdots\end{aligned}\]

The coefficients fall like \(\frac1{n^2}\) and survive only for odd \(n\), so this series is the natural one for a sum of odd squares, \(1+\frac19+\frac1{25}+\cdots\); at \(t=0\) every \(\cos nt\) equals 1.

Putting \(t=0\) in this series (the triangular wave is continuous there, with value 0) gives

\[\begin{aligned}0&=\frac\pi2-\frac4\pi\Big(1+\frac19+\frac1{25}+\cdots\Big)\\ \frac{\pi^2}8&=1+\frac19+\frac1{25}+\cdots=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{(2n-1)^2}.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the sum of the reciprocals of the odd squares is \(\pi^2/8\), read off from the bottom corner of the triangular wave.

2. Sine series. The odd periodic extension of \(f(t)\) corresponds to the "saw-tooth" wave: \(t\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\), repeated; straight lines of slope 1 from \(-\pi\) up to \(\pi\), dropping back at every odd multiple of \(\pi\). Then

The half-turn of the line \(t\) about the origin is the line itself, so the odd extension is \(t\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\), repeated; it is odd, so only sine tones are needed.

\[f(t)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n\sin nt\]

where

\[\begin{aligned}b_n&=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi t\sin nt\,dt\\&=\frac2\pi\Big\{\Big[-t\frac{\cos nt}n\Big]_0^\pi-\int_0^\pi\frac{-\cos nt}n\,dt\Big\}\\&=\frac{-2(-1)^n}n.\end{aligned}\]
Show the calculation: integration by parts for \(b_n\)
  1. Parts with \(u=t\), \(dv=\sin nt\,dt\): \(v=-\frac{\cos nt}n\). So \(\int_0^\pi t\sin nt\,dt\) \(=\big[-\frac{t\cos nt}n\big]_0^\pi+\frac1n\int_0^\pi\cos nt\,dt\).
  2. Boundary term: \(-\frac{\pi\cos n\pi}n-0=-\frac{\pi(-1)^n}n\).
  3. Remaining integral: \(\frac1n\big[\frac{\sin nt}n\big]_0^\pi=0\).
  4. Times \(\frac2\pi\): \(b_n=-\frac{2(-1)^n}{n}=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}n\): \(2,-1,\frac23,-\frac12,\dots\)

Therefore,

\[\begin{aligned}f(t)&=-2\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n}n\sin nt\\&=2\Big(\sin t-\frac{\sin2t}2+\frac{\sin3t}3-\cdots\Big).\end{aligned}\]

The two answers look different because they copy different invented patterns; on \((0,\pi)\), the only part that was given, both patterns are the line \(t\).

In words: on \(0\lt t\lt\pi\), the same function \(t\) has two different series: a cosine series (from the triangle wave) and a sine series (from the saw-tooth). Both are correct there.

Beyond the handouts · half-range series on \([0,L]\), and which one is better

Any length \(L\). Exams give \(f\) on \([0,L]\) (e.g. \(L=1\), \(2\) or \(D\)). The extensions then have period \(T=2L\), and (with the formulas of §6)

\[\begin{aligned}\text{cosine: }&f=\frac{a_0}2+\sum a_n\cos\frac{n\pi t}L,\\&a_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf(t)\cos\frac{n\pi t}L\,dt;\\ \text{sine: }&f=\sum b_n\sin\frac{n\pi t}L,\\&b_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf(t)\sin\frac{n\pi t}L\,dt.\end{aligned}\]

In words: replace \(\pi\) by \(L\) in the limits and \(nt\) by \(\frac{n\pi t}{L}\) in the waves. With \(L=\pi\) these are the formulas above.

Which series approximates better for a fixed number of terms? The one whose extension has no jumps. The even extension is always continuous at \(t=0\) and \(t=L\) (it is reflected, so the two sides meet). The odd extension has a jump at \(t=0\) unless \(f(0)=0\), and at \(t=L\) unless \(f(L)=0\). Jumps make the coefficients shrink only like \(\frac1n\) (slow, with Gibbs overshoot), while a continuous extension with corners gives \(\frac1{n^2}\) (fast). For \(f=t\) above: cosine coefficients \(\sim\frac{4}{\pi n^2}\), sine coefficients \(\sim\frac2n\). Where the series converge at the ends: the sine series is always 0 at \(t=0\) and \(t=L\) (the average across the jump of an odd function); the cosine series gives \(f(0^+)\) and \(f(L^-)\).

What is this? (best copy with N tones)

Suppose you may only use a bass tone plus the first \(N\) pure tones to copy a sound, and you choose how loud each one is. Different choices give different copies, and some are closer to the real sound than others. This part asks which choice gives the closest copy, and the answer is neat: the loudness settings found by "listening" with each tone (the Fourier coefficients of §2) are already the best ones. The best copy using \(N\) tones is the Fourier copy.

In practice we can only add up finitely many terms. Is a partial sum of the Fourier series the best we can do with that many waves? Suppose we approximate \(f(t)\) by a finite trigonometric series with coefficients \(\alpha_0,\alpha_n,\beta_n\) that we are free to choose,

\[S_N(t)=\frac12\alpha_0+\sum_{n=1}^N\big(\alpha_n\cos nt+\beta_n\sin nt\big),\]

such that the square error

\[E=\int_{-\pi}^\pi\big[f(t)-S_N(t)\big]^2dt\]

is minimised.

In words: \(E\) adds up the squared gap between \(f\) and the approximation over a whole period; squaring stops positive and negative gaps from cancelling. We want the coefficients that make the total squared gap as small as possible ("least squares").

Reading the formula
\(S_N(t)\)
your copy of the sound: a bass level \(\frac12\alpha_0\) plus \(N\) tones whose loudness settings \(\alpha_n,\beta_n\) you are free to turn.
\(f(t)-S_N(t)\)
the mistake of the copy at the moment \(t\).
\(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}[\ \cdot\ ]^2dt\)
square every mistake (so all count as positive) and add them over one period: one score \(E\) for the copy, smaller is better.

To find the best settings, expand the square. Every term of \(S_N\) is one tone (the bass level counts as the tone "\(1\)"), so squaring \(S_N\) gives each tone times itself and each tone times a different tone.

\[\begin{aligned}E=\ &\int_{-\pi}^\pi\Big[f(t)-\frac12\alpha_0-\sum_{n=1}^N(\alpha_n\cos nt+\beta_n\sin nt)\Big]^2dt\\=\ &\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2\,dt+\frac14\alpha_0^2\int_{-\pi}^\pi1^2\,dt+\sum_{n=1}^N\Big(\alpha_n^2\int_{-\pi}^\pi\cos^2nt\,dt+\beta_n^2\int_{-\pi}^\pi\sin^2nt\,dt\Big)\\&-\alpha_0\int_{-\pi}^\pi f\,dt-2\sum_{n=1}^N\Big(\alpha_n\int_{-\pi}^\pi f\cos nt\,dt+\beta_n\int_{-\pi}^\pi f\sin nt\,dt\Big)\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}E=\ &\int_{-\pi}^\pi\left[\begin{aligned}&f(t)-\frac12\alpha_0\\&-\sum_{n=1}^N(\alpha_n\cos nt+\beta_n\sin nt)\end{aligned}\right]^2dt\\=\ &\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2\,dt+\frac14\alpha_0^2\int_{-\pi}^\pi1^2\,dt\\&+\sum_{n=1}^N\left(\begin{aligned}&\alpha_n^2\int_{-\pi}^\pi\cos^2nt\,dt\\&+\beta_n^2\int_{-\pi}^\pi\sin^2nt\,dt\end{aligned}\right)\\&-\alpha_0\int_{-\pi}^\pi f\,dt\\&-2\sum_{n=1}^N\left(\begin{aligned}&\alpha_n\int_{-\pi}^\pi f\cos nt\,dt\\&+\beta_n\int_{-\pi}^\pi f\sin nt\,dt\end{aligned}\right)\end{aligned}\]

(all other cross terms are zero due to the orthogonality of \(1,\cos nt,\sin nt\)).

Reading the formula
\(\int f^2\,dt\)
the size of the real sound itself; no setting can change it.
\(\alpha_n^2\int\cos^2nt\,dt\), …
each tone times itself: that tone's loudness squared in your copy.
\(-2\alpha_n\int f\cos nt\,dt\), …
the real sound times one tone: the "listening" integral of §2, so the true \(a_n\) appears here.
missing terms
a tone times a different tone averages to zero (orthogonality), so no product like \(\alpha_1\beta_2\) is left and each setting can be tuned on its own.

Using \(\int1\,dt=2\pi\), \(\int\cos^2nt=\int\sin^2nt=\pi\), and \(\int f\,dt=\pi a_0\), \(\int f\cos nt\,dt=\pi a_n\), \(\int f\sin nt\,dt=\pi b_n\) (the Fourier coefficients of §2),

\[\begin{aligned}E=\ &\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2(t)\,dt+\frac\pi2\alpha_0^2 +\pi\sum_{n=1}^N\big(\alpha_n^2+\beta_n^2\big) -\alpha_0\pi a_0-2\sum_{n=1}^N\big(\alpha_n\pi a_n+\beta_n\pi b_n\big).\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}E=\ &\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2(t)\,dt+\frac\pi2\alpha_0^2\\&+\pi\sum_{n=1}^N\big(\alpha_n^2+\beta_n^2\big)\\&-\alpha_0\pi a_0-2\sum_{n=1}^N\big(\alpha_n\pi a_n+\beta_n\pi b_n\big).\end{aligned}\]
Beyond the handouts · the same result by completing the square

Grouping the terms of each setting and completing the square gives

\[\begin{aligned}E&=E_{\min}+\frac\pi2(\alpha_0-a_0)^2 +\pi\sum_{n=1}^N(\alpha_n-a_n)^2 +\pi\sum_{n=1}^N(\beta_n-b_n)^2,\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}E&=E_{\min}+\frac\pi2(\alpha_0-a_0)^2\\&\quad+\pi\sum_{n=1}^N(\alpha_n-a_n)^2\\&\quad+\pi\sum_{n=1}^N(\beta_n-b_n)^2,\end{aligned}\]

with \(E_{\min}\) as below. Each bracket is "how far that tone's setting is from its Fourier value", squared: turning any knob away from its Fourier value can only make the copy worse. (Checked in sympy for \(N=2\), \(f=t+t^2\).)

Show the calculation: expanding the square
  1. Write \(S_N=\frac12\alpha_0\cdot1+\sum(\alpha_n\cos nt+\beta_n\sin nt)\). Then \((f-S_N)^2=f^2-2fS_N+S_N^2\).
  2. \(\int-2fS_N\,dt\) \(=-\alpha_0\int f\,dt\) \(-\:2\sum\big(\alpha_n\int f\cos nt+\beta_n\int f\sin nt\big)\). Why: \(2\times\frac12\alpha_0=\alpha_0\).
  3. \(S_N^2\) is a sum of squares of each term plus twice every product of two different terms. Every product of two different waves from \(1,\cos nt,\sin nt\) integrates to 0 (orthogonality, §2), so only the squares remain: \(\frac14\alpha_0^2\int1+\sum(\alpha_n^2\int\cos^2nt+\beta_n^2\int\sin^2nt)\).
  4. Insert the values: \(\frac14\alpha_0^2\cdot2\pi=\frac\pi2\alpha_0^2\); \(\alpha_n^2\pi+\beta_n^2\pi\).
  5. Insert \(\int f=\pi a_0\) etc.: \(-\alpha_0\pi a_0-2\sum(\alpha_n\pi a_n+\beta_n\pi b_n)\). (Checked in sympy for \(N=2\), \(f=t+t^2\): the direct integral and this formula agree.)

Because no cross terms survive, each setting appears in its own small upward parabola, so we can tune one tone at a time. For \(E\) to be a minimum, its partial derivative (rate of change, Chapter 1 §2) with respect to each coefficient must be zero, that is, turning any single knob a little must not lower the error:

\[\begin{aligned}0&=\pd E{\alpha_0}=\pi\alpha_0-\pi a_0&&\Rightarrow\ \alpha_0=a_0,\\ 0&=\pd E{\alpha_n}=2\pi\alpha_n-2\pi a_n&&\Rightarrow\ \alpha_n=a_n,\\ 0&=\pd E{\beta_n}=2\pi\beta_n-2\pi b_n&&\Rightarrow\ \beta_n=b_n,\end{aligned}\]

for \(n=1,2,\dots,N\). That is, the coefficients \(\alpha_0,\alpha_n,\beta_n\) of the finite series are exactly the coefficients taken from the Fourier series. Thus a truncated Fourier series is the best approximation to \(f(t)\) in the sense that it minimises the square error. (It is a minimum, not a maximum: \(E\) is a sum of upward-opening parabolas in the \(\alpha\)'s and \(\beta\)'s.)

In words: with \(N\) waves available, you cannot beat the Fourier coefficients; adding more waves never changes the earlier coefficients, it just adds new ones.

What is this? (Bessel and Parseval: energy bookkeeping)

Think of the "energy" of a sound as its average loudness squared. Because different tones do not interfere on average, the energy of a sum of tones is just the sum of the energies of the tones. A copy made of \(N\) tones holds part of the real sound's energy, never more than all of it (Bessel's inequality); with all the tones, nothing is missing and the two totals are equal (Parseval's theorem).

Now, for this choice of the coefficients,

\[E\to E_{\min}=\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2(t)\,dt-\frac\pi2a_0^2-\pi\sum_{n=1}^N\big(a_n^2+b_n^2\big)\]

or, since we must have \(E_{\min}\ge0\) (it is an integral of a square),

\[\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2(t)\,dt\ \ge\ \frac14a_0^2+\frac12\sum_{n=1}^N\big(a_n^2+b_n^2\big)\]

which is known as Bessel's inequality.

In words: the mean square of \(f\) is at least the part of it captured by the first \(N\) waves.

In the picture, \(E_{\min}\) is the energy still missing from the best \(N\)-tone copy; each extra tone takes its share off it.

As \(N\to\infty\), we have

\[\frac14a_0^2+\frac12\sum_{n=1}^\infty\big(a_n^2+b_n^2\big)\ \le\ \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2(t)\,dt\]

but in fact the \(\le\) sign should be replaced by an \(=\) sign, since (squaring the whole Fourier series and integrating, using orthogonality)

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2(t)\,dt&=\int_{-\pi}^\pi\Big[\frac12a_0 +\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\cos nt+b_n\sin nt\Big]^2dt\\&=\frac14a_0^2\cdot2\pi+\pi\sum_{n=1}^\infty\big(a_n^2+b_n^2\big).\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2(t)\,dt&=\int_{-\pi}^\pi\Big[\frac12a_0\\&\quad+\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\cos nt+b_n\sin nt\Big]^2dt\\&=\frac14a_0^2\cdot2\pi+\pi\sum_{n=1}^\infty\big(a_n^2+b_n^2\big).\end{aligned}\]
Parseval's theorem (4.3) \[\begin{aligned}&\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2(t)\,dt\\&=\frac14a_0^2+\frac12\sum_{n=1}^\infty\big(a_n^2+b_n^2\big)\end{aligned}\]

In words: mean square of the signal over a period = (constant term)\(^2\) + half the sum of the squared amplitudes of all the waves. (A wave \(a\cos nt\) has mean square \(\frac12a^2\).)

Reading the formula
\(\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f^2\,dt\)
the total energy of the signal: the average of its square over one period.
\(\frac14a_0^2=\big(\frac12a_0\big)^2\)
energy in the bass level (the constant term): a constant \(c\) has mean square \(c^2\).
\(\frac12(a_n^2+b_n^2)\)
energy in tone \(n\): a wave spends time near 0 as well as near its peaks, so its mean square is half its peak squared. Adding all of them gives the whole energy.

This result is known as Parseval's theorem. If \(f(t)\) is an electrical signal, \(\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2(t)\,dt\) is proportional to the power of the signal (power in a resistor is proportional to voltage squared). This result may be interpreted as "the mean power of a signal equals the sum of the powers in each of the frequency components of that signal".

Beyond the handouts · using Parseval to sum a series (exam type C4.4), worked example

How to use Parseval to sum a series:

  1. Do: find (or quote) the Fourier series of \(f\) on one period. You need to know how loud each tone is before you can add up the energies.
  2. Do: compute the left side, the mean square \(\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f^2\,dt\) (or \(\frac1T\int f^2\) over one period). This is the total energy, worked out directly from the shape of the signal.
  3. Do: square the coefficients and write \(\frac14a_0^2+\frac12\sum(a_n^2+b_n^2)\); only the nonzero ones matter. This is the same total energy, added up tone by tone.
  4. Do: set the two equal and rearrange to isolate the sum you want. Two ways of counting the same energy must agree; the unknown sum is hidden in the tone-by-tone count.

Use the saw-tooth series \(t=2\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n\sin nt\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\) (Example 4.5.1) to find \(\sum\frac1{n^2}\).

  1. Left side: \(\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi t^2\,dt=\frac1{2\pi}\cdot\frac{2\pi^3}3=\frac{\pi^2}3\). Total energy of the saw-tooth, from its shape.
  2. Right side: \(a_0=a_n=0\), \(b_n^2=\frac4{n^2}\), so \(\frac12\sum\frac4{n^2}=2\sum\frac1{n^2}\). The saw-tooth is odd, so only sine tones are present; tone \(n\) carries energy \(\frac12\cdot\frac4{n^2}\).
  3. Parseval: \(\frac{\pi^2}3=2\sum\frac1{n^2}\), so \(\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}6}\).

Other periods. For a \(T\)-periodic \(f\) (formulas below) Parseval reads \(\dfrac1T\displaystyle\int_a^{a+T}f^2\,dt=\frac14a_0^2+\frac12\sum(a_n^2+b_n^2)\): "mean square over one period" on the left. In the May-sheet convention (constant \(A_0=\frac12a_0\)) the first term is \(A_0^2\). Squares of coefficients like \(\frac1{n^2}\) give sums of \(\frac1{n^4}\): this is how exam questions get \(\sum\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{90}\).

Further properties

What is this? (differentiating and integrating tone by tone)

Can you find the slope or the running total of a signal by doing it to each tone separately? Integrating divides tone \(n\) by \(n\) (high tones get quieter), so it is safe. Differentiating multiplies by \(n\) (high tones get louder), so it works only if the signal has no jumps.

1. Differentiation and integration of Fourier series. Under certain conditions the Fourier series of \(f(t)\) may be differentiated or integrated term by term to give \(f'(t)\) and \(\int f(t)\,dt\), respectively.

Beyond the handouts · what "certain conditions" means in practice
  • Integrating term by term is safe for any piecewise continuous \(f\) (the terms get smaller: \(\sin nt\to-\frac{\cos nt}n\)). If \(a_0\ne0\) the constant integrates to \(\frac12a_0t\), which is not periodic, so integrate \(f-\frac12a_0\) instead.
  • Differentiating term by term needs the periodic extension to be continuous (no jumps) and \(f'\) piecewise smooth, because it multiplies each term by \(n\). Example: the triangular wave \(\frac\pi2-\frac4\pi\sum\frac{\cos(2k-1)t}{(2k-1)^2}\) (continuous) differentiates to \(\frac4\pi\sum\frac{\sin(2k-1)t}{2k-1}\), which is exactly the series of its slope, \(-1\) on \((-\pi,0)\) and \(+1\) on \((0,\pi)\). But the saw-tooth (which jumps) gives \(2\sum(-1)^{n+1}\cos nt\), which does not converge at all.
What is this? (change of interval)

Real signals repeat every \(T\) seconds, not every \(2\pi\). It is the same tune played at a different speed: stretch the time axis so that one period of length \(T\) fits exactly one period of the basic tone. The tones are still "the basic tone and its whole-number multiples"; only the basic tone's frequency \(\omega=2\pi/T\) changes.

2. Change of interval. Although we have only considered functions \(f(t)\) of period \(2\pi\), we may find the Fourier series of a function with period \(T\), defined on the interval \([a,a+T]\), by a change of variable. The result is

Fourier series of a \(T\)-periodic function (on the December formula sheet) \[\begin{aligned}f(t)&=\frac12a_0+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\big(a_n\cos n\omega t+b_n\sin n\omega t\big),\\ a_n&=\frac2T\int_a^{a+T}f(t)\cos n\omega t\,dt,\quad n=0,1,2,\dots\\ b_n&=\frac2T\int_a^{a+T}f(t)\sin n\omega t\,dt,\quad n=1,2,3,\dots\end{aligned}\]

where \(\omega=\dfrac{2\pi}T\) is the fundamental frequency.

In words: same recipe, but the waves are \(\cos n\omega t,\sin n\omega t\) (which fit \(n\) oscillations into one period \(T\)), the integral runs over any one period, and the factor in front is \(\frac2T\) (which is \(\frac1\pi\) when \(T=2\pi\)).

Reading the formula
\(\omega=\frac{2\pi}T\)
the basic tone: one full oscillation per period \(T\); tone \(n\) fits \(n\) oscillations.
\(\int_a^{a+T}f\cos n\omega t\,dt\)
"listening" with tone \(n\) over one full period; any starting point \(a\) works because everything repeats.
\(\frac2T\)
turns the listening integral into a loudness: \(\frac1T\) averages over the period, and the extra 2 undoes the \(\frac12\) mean square of a tone.
Show the calculation: where the formulas come from (substitution \(s=\omega t\))
  1. Let \(s=\omega t=\frac{2\pi t}{T}\) and \(g(s)=f\big(\frac s\omega\big)=f(t)\). When \(t\) increases by \(T\), \(s\) increases by \(2\pi\), so \(g\) is \(2\pi\)-periodic.
  2. Apply §2 to \(g\) over the period \(s\in[\omega a,\omega a+2\pi]\): \(g(s)=\frac12a_0+\sum(a_n\cos ns+b_n\sin ns)\) with \(a_n=\frac1\pi\int g(s)\cos ns\,ds\).
  3. Change back: \(ns=n\omega t\), \(ds=\omega\,dt=\frac{2\pi}T\,dt\), limits \(t=a\) to \(a+T\). So \(a_n\) \(=\frac1\pi\cdot\frac{2\pi}T\int_a^{a+T}f(t)\cos n\omega t\,dt\) \(=\frac2T\int_a^{a+T}f\cos n\omega t\,dt\). Same for \(b_n\).
  4. Orthogonality also carries over: \(\frac2T\int_a^{a+T}\cos n\omega t\cos m\omega t\,dt\) is 0 for \(n\ne m\) and 1 for \(n=m\ne0\) (checked for sample values in sympy).

Find the Fourier series of the 2-periodic function with \(f(t)=t\) for \(0\lt t\lt2\).

\(T=2\), \(\omega=\pi\) (one basic oscillation every 2 time units), and the formula-sheet interval \([a,a+T]=[0,2]\) is the natural choice (no need to rewrite \(f\) on \([-1,1]\)).

\[\begin{aligned}a_0&=\frac22\int_0^2t\,dt=2,\\ a_n&=\int_0^2t\cos n\pi t\,dt=0,\\ b_n&=\int_0^2t\sin n\pi t\,dt=-\frac2{n\pi}.\end{aligned}\]
Show the calculation: \(a_n\) and \(b_n\)
  1. \(a_n\): parts with \(u=t\), \(v=\frac{\sin n\pi t}{n\pi}\): \(\big[\frac{t\sin n\pi t}{n\pi}\big]_0^2+\big[\frac{\cos n\pi t}{(n\pi)^2}\big]_0^2\) \(=0+\frac{\cos2n\pi-1}{(n\pi)^2}\) \(=0\). Why: \(\sin2n\pi=0\) and \(\cos2n\pi=1\).
  2. \(b_n\): \(\big[-\frac{t\cos n\pi t}{n\pi}\big]_0^2+\big[\frac{\sin n\pi t}{(n\pi)^2}\big]_0^2\) \(=-\frac{2\cos2n\pi}{n\pi}+0\) \(=-\frac2{n\pi}\).
\[f(t)=1-\frac2\pi\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{\sin n\pi t}n.\]

In words: a saw-tooth of average height 1, built from \(\sin\pi t,\sin2\pi t,\dots\); at the jumps \(t=0,\pm2,\dots\) it converges to 1, the midpoint of 0 and 2.

What is this? (frequency spectrum)

The spectrum is the recipe card of the sound: a bar chart with one bar per tone, whose height says how much of that tone is in the mix, like the sliders on a graphic equaliser. A repeating signal only contains the basic tone and its whole-number multiples, so the bars stand at evenly spaced places with nothing in between.

3. Frequency spectrum of a signal \(f(t)\).

  1. The first term in the Fourier series, \[\frac12a_0=\frac1T\int_a^{a+T}f(t)\,dt,\] is the average value of \(f(t)\). It is time independent and is known as the D.C. (direct current) term. In the picture: the integral adds up the signal over one period and \(\frac1T\) divides by the length, so this is the steady level the tones wobble around (the bar at frequency 0).
  2. \(\cos\omega t\) and \(\sin\omega t\) are simple harmonic (single frequency) oscillations with angular frequency \(\omega\), \(\omega\) being the fundamental frequency of \(f(t)\); \(a_1\) and \(b_1\) are the amplitudes of these terms.
  3. \(\cos n\omega t\) and \(\sin n\omega t\) (\(n=2,3,\dots\)) are the harmonic terms, with frequency an integer multiple of the fundamental frequency, with amplitudes \(a_n\) and \(b_n\).
  4. Plots of the coefficients \(a_n\) and \(b_n\) as functions of the frequency \(n\omega\) form the frequency spectrum of \(f(t)\). For a periodic function only the frequencies \(n\omega\) occur, and hence the spectrum consists of discrete lines spaced \(\omega\) apart. It is a discrete or line spectrum.

In words: the spectrum is a bar chart of "how much of each frequency" the signal contains: a bar at 0 for the average, then bars at \(\omega,2\omega,3\omega,\dots\) and nothing in between.

How to read a spectrum:

  1. Do: read the bar at frequency 0. It is the D.C. term \(\frac12a_0\), the average level of the signal; zero means the signal spends as much time below the axis as above.
  2. Do: note which lines are missing, for example all \(a_n\), all \(b_n\), or all even \(n\). Missing tones reveal symmetry: no cosines means the signal (minus its average) is odd, no sines means it is even, and the square wave has only odd tones.
  3. Do: look at how fast the bars shrink as \(n\) grows. Bars falling like \(\frac1n\) mean the signal has jumps (sharp edges need many high tones); bars falling like \(\frac1{n^2}\) mean it is continuous with corners only.
  4. Do: to compare strengths, square the heights: tone \(n\) carries energy \(\frac12(a_n^2+b_n^2)\). This is Parseval: adding these energies gives the total mean square, which is what the readout below checks.

Line spectra, and Parseval's power count

Horizontal axis: frequency \(n\omega\) (here \(\omega=1\)). Teal bars: \(a_n\) (the bar at 0 shows the D.C. term \(\frac12a_0\)); orange bars: \(b_n\), drawn slightly to the right of the teal ones. The second option shows the amplitude \(c_n=\sqrt{a_n^2+b_n^2}\) of the combined wave \(a_n\cos nt+b_n\sin nt=c_n\cos(nt-\phi_n)\) (Beyond the handouts; engineers usually plot this). The readout checks Parseval: the power in the first 10 lines, \(\frac14a_0^2+\frac12\sum_{n\le10}(a_n^2+b_n^2)\), against the total mean square \(\frac1{2\pi}\int f^2\). Signals with jumps (square, saw-tooth, \(e^t\)) have spectra that die away slowly; continuous ones (triangle, \(|\sin t|\)) die away fast.

◆Find the mistake

Written for these notes. Each "solution" has one error. Find it before opening the answer.

1. The constant term

"For the square wave (0 on \((-\pi,0]\), 1 on \((0,\pi)\)): \(a_0=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi1\,dt=1\), \(a_n=0\), \(b_n=\frac{1-(-1)^n}{n\pi}\). So \(f(t)=1+\frac2\pi\big(\sin t+\frac{\sin3t}3+\cdots\big)\)."

Answer

The constant term is \(\frac12a_0=\frac12\), not \(a_0\). The written series would have average 1, but the square wave spends half its time at 0, so its average is \(\frac12\). Correct: \(f=\frac12+\frac2\pi(\sin t+\frac{\sin3t}3+\cdots)\).

2. A short cut too far

"\(f(t)=t+1\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\), period \(2\pi\). \(t\) is odd, so we use the sine series: \(b_n\) \(=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi(t+1)\sin nt\,dt\) \(=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}n+\frac{2[1-(-1)^n]}{n\pi}\), and \(a_n=0\) for all \(n\)."

Answer

\(t+1\) is neither even nor odd, so the half-range formula does not apply (and \(a_0\ne0\)). Split instead: \(t\) is odd, \(1\) is even. Then \(a_0=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi(t+1)\,dt=2\) (constant term 1), \(a_n=0\) for \(n\ge1\), \(b_n=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}n\). So \(f=1+2\sum\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n\sin nt\). The extra \(\frac{2[1-(-1)^n]}{n\pi}\) is the sine series of the constant 1 on \((0,\pi)\), which is not what \(1\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\) is.

3. Summing at a jump

"Put \(t=\pi\) in the series of Example 4.2.2: since \(f(t)=e^t\), the series converges to \(e^\pi\), so \(e^\pi=\frac{\sinh\pi}\pi+\frac{2\sinh\pi}\pi\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n(-1)^n}{1+n^2}\)."

Answer

\(t=\pi\) is a jump of the periodic extension: the left limit is \(e^\pi\), the right limit (start of the next copy) is \(e^{-\pi}\). The series converges to the average \(\frac{e^\pi+e^{-\pi}}2=\cosh\pi\). Correct: \(\cosh\pi=\frac{\sinh\pi}\pi\big(1+2\sum\frac1{1+n^2}\big)\), so \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{1+n^2}=\frac{\pi\coth\pi-1}2\approx1.077\) (checked numerically for these notes).

Written for these notes.

1. What is the fundamental period of \(\sin2t+\sin3t\)? Is \(\pi\) a period?

Answer

\(2\pi\) (periods \(\pi\) and \(\frac{2\pi}3\); smallest common multiple \(2\pi\)). \(\pi\) is not a period: \(\sin3(t+\pi)=-\sin3t\).

2. To what values does the square-wave series of Example 4.2.1 converge at \(t=0\) and at \(t=\frac{3\pi}2\)?

Answer

\(\frac12\) at \(t=0\) (a jump from 0 to 1). At \(\frac{3\pi}2\) the function is continuous with \(f(\frac{3\pi}2)=f(-\frac\pi2)=0\), so the series gives 0.

3. Without integrating, which coefficients are zero for (a) \(t^3\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\); (b) \(|t|\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\); (c) \(e^t\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\)?

Answer

(a) odd: all \(a_n\) (including \(a_0\)). (b) even: all \(b_n\). (c) neither: in general none (Example 4.2.2 has all \(a_n,b_n\ne0\)).

4. \(f(t)=3+2\cos t-\sin4t\). Write down its Fourier coefficients without integrating.

Answer

It already is a Fourier series: \(\frac12a_0=3\), so \(a_0=6\) (in the May-sheet convention \(A_0=3\)); \(a_1=2\); \(b_4=-1\); all others 0. (Uniqueness: the coefficient formulas would just give these back.)

5. Use Parseval to find the mean square \(\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2\,dt\) of \(f\) in check 4.

Answer

\(\frac14\cdot36+\frac12(2^2+1^2)=9+\frac52=\frac{23}2\) (checked by direct integration).

6. For \(f(t)=t\) on \((0,\pi)\) (Example 4.5.1), what do the sine series and the cosine series converge to at \(t=\pi\)?

Answer

Sine series: 0 (the saw-tooth jumps from \(\pi\) to \(-\pi\) there; average 0). Cosine series: \(\pi\) (the triangle wave is continuous at its peak).

7. Why does the square wave have a Fourier series but no Taylor series about \(t=0\)?

Answer

A Taylor series needs all derivatives at the point, and the square wave is not even continuous at 0. A Fourier series only needs \(f\) to be piecewise continuous with one-sided derivatives (Theorem 4.3.1).

Even extension to \((-2,2)\): period \(T=4\), \(L=2\), \(\omega=\frac\pi2\). \(b_n=0\), \(a_n\) \(=\frac22\int_0^2f\cos\frac{n\pi t}2\,dt\) \(=\int_0^1(1-t)\cos\frac{n\pi t}2\,dt\).

Answer

Sketch: triangles of height 1 on \([-1,1]\), \([3,5]\), \([-5,-3]\), … (peaks at \(t=0,\pm4\)), zero in between:

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}f(t)&=\frac14+\frac4{\pi^2}\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1-\cos(n\pi/2)}{n^2}\\&\qquad\times\cos\frac{n\pi t}2\end{aligned}}\] (constant \(\frac12a_0=\frac14\); first terms \(\frac4{\pi^2}\cos\frac{\pi t}2+\frac2{\pi^2}\cos\pi t+\frac4{9\pi^2}\cos\frac{3\pi t}2+\cdots\)).

Consider a periodic function \(f(x)=x^2\), \(-1\le x\le1\) with the period \(T=2\), and the Fourier series

\[f(x)=\frac13+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{4(-1)^n}{n^2\pi^2}\cos(n\pi x).\]

With the help of the given Fourier series, use Parseval's identity to find the sum of the series \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^4}\).

Hint

Here the constant term is \(\frac13\) (May-sheet \(A_0\)), so Parseval reads \(\frac1{2}\int_{-1}^1x^4\,dx=A_0^2+\frac12\sum a_n^2\).

Answer

\(\frac15=\frac19+\frac12\cdot\frac{16}{\pi^4}\sum\frac1{n^4}\Rightarrow\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{90}}\).
