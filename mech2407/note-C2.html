Starting point: the definite integral as a Riemann sum

What is this? (the integral as a sum)

Before going to two variables, recall what an ordinary integral really does. To find the area under a curve, cut it into thin vertical strips, pretend each strip is a rectangle (its height read off the curve at one point), and add up the rectangles. The thinner the strips, the less the curve can change across one strip, so the error shrinks to nothing. Everything in this chapter is this same idea: chop into tiny pieces, measure each piece roughly, add, and let the pieces shrink.

For a function \(f(x)\) of one variable, chop the interval \([a,b]\) into \(n\) pieces at points

\[a=x_0\lt x_1\lt x_2\lt\dots\lt x_n=b.\]

In each piece \([x_{k-1},x_k]\) pick any sample point \(c_k\). Build a rectangle on that piece with height \(f(c_k)\) and width \(x_k-x_{k-1}\). The definite integral of \(f\) from \(a\) to \(b\) is the limit of the total of these rectangles:

\[\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(c_k)\,(x_k-x_{k-1}),\]

taken so that \(\max\{x_k-x_{k-1}\}\to0\) as \(n\to\infty\) (every piece shrinks, not just most of them).

In words: integral = limit of the sum of (height × width) over thinner and thinner strips.

Reading the formula
\(f(c_k)\)
the height of the \(k\)-th strip, read off the curve at one sample point inside it.
\(x_k-x_{k-1}\)
the width of that strip.
\(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\)
add the rectangles from the left end to the right end.
\(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\), widths \(\to0\)
make every strip thinner, so the curve is almost flat across each one and the rectangles fit it exactly in the limit. This is why it does not matter which sample point you pick.
Definitions
  • The sum \(\sum f(c_k)(x_k-x_{k-1})\) is called a Riemann sum, and an integral defined as its limit is a Riemann integral.
  • Geometrically, \(\int_a^b f\,dx\) is the area between the graph and the \(x\)-axis, between the lines \(x=a\) and \(x=b\). Rectangles below the axis (where \(f(c_k)\lt0\)) count as negative area, so strictly it is a signed area.

Riemann sum for one variable

The \(k\)-th rectangle stands on \([x_{k-1},x_k]\) with height \(f(c_k)\), where \(c_k\) is the red dot (here the midpoint). Rectangles above the axis (teal) add area; those below (orange) subtract it. As \(n\) grows the sum settles on the integral.

From area under a curve to volume under a surface

What is this? (double integral)

Picture a field \(R\) covered in snow whose depth changes from place to place. To find the total amount of snow, divide the field into small square tiles. On each tile the depth is nearly constant, so the snow on it is about (depth × tile area). Add up every tile, then use smaller and smaller tiles: the total you approach is the double integral of the depth over the field. It is the one-variable strip idea again, with tiles of a field instead of strips of a line.

A function \(f(x,y)\) of two variables defines a surface \(z=f(x,y)\) (see Chapter 1 §1); in the picture, \(f(x,y)\) is the snow depth at the point \((x,y)\). Take a region \(R\) of the \(xy\)-plane (the "floor", our field) and stand a vertical wall on its boundary, like a cookie cutter pushed straight up. The surface, the wall and the floor enclose a solid. We want its volume.

Divide \(R\) into \(n\) small rectangles. The \(k\)-th one has sides \(\Delta x_k\), \(\Delta y_k\), area \(\Delta A_k=\Delta x_k\,\Delta y_k\), and contains a chosen point \((x_k,y_k)\). A column standing on it with height \(f(x_k,y_k)\) has volume \(f(x_k,y_k)\,\Delta A_k\). Add up all the columns and let them shrink:

Definition (double integral) \[\begin{aligned}\iint_R f(x,y)\,dR&=\iint_R f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\\&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(x_k,y_k)\,\Delta A_k,\end{aligned}\]

provided that \(\max\{\Delta A_k\}\to0\) (strictly, every little piece must shrink in all directions).

In words: double integral = limit of the sum of (height of surface × area of little floor tile) over all tiles; for \(f\ge0\) it is the volume of the solid between the floor region \(R\) and the surface.

The little-area symbol is written in several ways that all mean the same: \(dR\), \(dA\), \(dS\), \(dx\,dy\). Such an integral is sometimes called an iterated or repeated integral, because (as we shall see) it is evaluated as two ordinary definite integrals, one inside the other.

Reading the formula
\(f(x_k,y_k)\)
the snow depth (surface height) at a sample point of the \(k\)-th tile.
\(\Delta A_k\)
the floor area of that tile; depth × area is the snow on the tile (the volume of one column).
\(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\)
add the columns over every tile of the field \(R\).
\(\max\{\Delta A_k\}\to0\)
shrink every tile in both directions, so the depth cannot vary across any one tile and the columns fit the surface exactly. In the limit, \(dA=dx\,dy\) is "one tiny tile".

Columns under a surface: the double Riemann sum

Surface \(z=2xy+y^2\) over the rectangle \(R:\ 1\le x\le2,\ 0\le y\le1\) (the surface of Example 2.1.2 below). Each column has base \(\Delta A_k=\Delta x\,\Delta y\) and height \(f(x_k,y_k)\) at the centre \((x_k,y_k)\) of its tile. The total column volume approaches the exact value \(11/6\approx1.8333\).

For a region that is not a rectangle, the same idea works: lay a grid over \(R\) and keep only the small rectangles that lie inside \(R\). As the grid gets finer, the kept rectangles fill \(R\) more and more completely.

Example 2.1.1

With \(f(x,y)\equiv1\) (the constant function 1),

\[\iint_S dS=\text{area of the region }S.\]

In words: a solid of height 1 standing on \(S\) has volume = area of \(S\) × 1, so integrating 1 gives the area. Rectangular regions: slicing into slabs (Fubini's theorem)

What is this? (slicing, Fubini's theorem)

Adding millions of tiny columns one by one is hopeless by hand. Instead, cut the solid like a loaf of bread into thin slices. Each slice is flat, so its face area is an ordinary one-variable integral (the area under one curve). Slice volume = face area × thickness, and adding the slices is a second ordinary integral. You may slice the loaf front-to-back or left-to-right: it is the same loaf, so you get the same total. That is Fubini's theorem.

Let \(S\) be the rectangle \(a\le x\le b,\ c\le y\le d\). The region can be cut into thin strips in two ways:

  • horizontal strips: \(y\) is fixed along the strip, \(x\) runs from \(a\) to \(b\); the strip has width \(\Delta y\);
  • vertical strips: \(x\) is fixed along the strip, \(y\) runs from \(c\) to \(d\); the strip has width \(\Delta x\).

Cutting the rectangle into horizontal or vertical strips

The rectangle \(a\le x\le b,\ c\le y\le d\). A horizontal strip at height \(y\) has thickness \(\Delta y\) and runs from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\). A vertical strip at position \(x\) has thickness \(\Delta x\) and runs from \(y=c\) to \(y=d\). The green dot is a typical point \((x,y)\) of the strip.

Each strip carries a thin slab (one slice of the loaf) of the solid under \(z=f(x,y)\). If the strip is horizontal (aligned with the \(x\)-direction, at height \(y\)), the face of the slab is the region under the curve \(z=f(x,y)\) with \(y\) frozen, and its area is

\[A(y)=\int_{x=a}^{b}f(x,y)\,dx.\]

In words: cross-sectional area of the slab = ordinary integral along the strip, treating \(y\) as a constant.

The slab has thickness \(\Delta y\), so its volume is about \(A(y)\,\Delta y\). Summing over slabs of infinitesimal width:

Fubini's theorem (rectangles) \[\begin{aligned}\iint_S f\,dx\,dy&=\int_{y=c}^{d}\left[\int_{x=a}^{b}f(x,y)\,dx\right]dy \qquad(x\text{-direction first})\\&=\int_{x=a}^{b}\left[\int_{y=c}^{d}f(x,y)\,dy\right]dx \qquad(y\text{-direction first})\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}\iint_S f\,dx\,dy&=\int_{y=c}^{d}\left[\int_{x=a}^{b}f(x,y)\,dx\right]dy\\&\qquad(x\text{-direction first})\\&=\int_{x=a}^{b}\left[\int_{y=c}^{d}f(x,y)\,dy\right]dx\\&\qquad(y\text{-direction first})\end{aligned}\]

In words: do the inner integral (with the other variable held constant), then integrate the answer over the outer variable. For a rectangle, either order gives the same answer, and the limits are just constants.

\(dy\)
the thickness of that slice; \(A(y)\,dy\) is the slice's volume.
\(\displaystyle\int_{y=c}^{d}\)

A slab and its cross-section area (Example 2.1.2)

Surface \(z=2xy+y^2\) over \(1\le x\le2,\ 0\le y\le1\). The orange panel is the face of a slab; its area is \(A(y)=\int_1^2(2xy+y^2)\,dx=3y+y^2\) (or \(A(x)=\int_0^1(2xy+y^2)\,dy=x+\tfrac13\) for the other direction). The floor strip under it is drawn in red.

Example 2.1.2

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_S(2xy+y^2)\,dS\) where \(S\) is the rectangle \(1\le x\le2,\ 0\le y\le1\).

Integrating in \(x\) first (\(y\) held constant inside):The inner integral is the face area of the slice at height \(y\); the result \(3y+y^2\) is the \(A(y)\) shown on the orange panel above. The outer integral then adds the slices from \(y=0\) to \(y=1\).

\[\begin{aligned}&\int_{y=0}^{1}\left[\int_{x=1}^{2}(2xy+y^2)\,dx\right]dy\\&=\int_{y=0}^{1}\Big[x^2y+xy^2\Big]_{x=1}^{2}dy\\&=\int_{0}^{1}(4y+2y^2-y-y^2)\,dy\\&=\int_0^1(3y+y^2)\,dy\\&=\left[\frac{3y^2}{2}+\frac{y^3}{3}\right]_0^1=\frac{11}{6}.\end{aligned}\]

Or integrating in \(y\) first (\(x\) held constant inside):Now the loaf is sliced the other way: each slice has face area \(A(x)=x+\tfrac13\), and the slices are added from \(x=1\) to \(x=2\).

\[\begin{aligned}&\int_{x=1}^{2}\left[\int_{y=0}^{1}(2xy+y^2)\,dy\right]dx\\&=\int_{x=1}^{2}\left[xy^2+\frac{y^3}{3}\right]_{y=0}^{1}dx\\&=\int_{1}^{2}\left(x+\frac13\right)dx\\&=\left[\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac x3\right]_1^2=\frac{11}{6}.\end{aligned}\]

Both orders give \(11/6\), as Fubini's theorem promises: the same volume as the columns in the first 3D figure approach.

Non-rectangular regions

What is this? (slicing a curved region)

Now the field has a curved edge. Slice it as before, but each slice is a different length: a slice near the middle of a round field is long, one near the edge is short. So where a slice starts and stops depends on where the slice is, and the inner limits become functions of the outer variable. The method still works as long as every slice is one unbroken piece (it enters the field once and leaves once); a region where this is true is called simple in that direction.

Definition (y-simple region)

A region \(R\) is \(y\)-simple if every line in the \(y\)-direction (vertical line) meets \(R\) in a single interval (or a single point, or not at all). Such a region can be described by

\[a\le x\le b,\qquad g_1(x)\le y\le g_2(x).\]

In words: the region lies between a bottom curve \(y=g_1(x)\) and a top curve \(y=g_2(x)\), for \(x\) from \(a\) to \(b\). (Many books call this "type I".)

In this case the vertical slab at \(x\) has cross-section area \(A(x)=\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}f\,dy\), and

\[\begin{aligned}\iint_R f(x,y)\,dR&=\int_{x=a}^{b}A(x)\,dx\\&=\int_{x=a}^{b}\left[\int_{y=g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}f(x,y)\,dy\right]dx.\end{aligned}\]
Reading the formula
\(g_1(x)\)
where the vertical slice at position \(x\) enters the field (the bottom curve).
\(g_2(x)\)
where that slice leaves the field (the top curve). Both depend on \(x\) because slices at different places have different lengths.
\(\displaystyle\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}f\,dy\)
the face area \(A(x)\) of that slice, adding the depth along the slice only where it is inside the field.
\(a,\ b\)
the positions of the first and last slice: always constants, because the outer integral sweeps the slice across the whole field once.
Definition (x-simple region)

A region \(S\) is \(x\)-simple if every line in the \(x\)-direction (horizontal line) meets \(S\) in a single interval (or point, or not at all). Such a region can be described by

\[c\le y\le d,\qquad h_1(y)\le x\le h_2(y),\]

and then

\[\begin{aligned}\iint_R f(x,y)\,dR&=\int_{y=c}^{d}A(y)\,dy\\&=\int_{y=c}^{d}\left[\int_{x=h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)}f(x,y)\,dx\right]dy.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the region lies between a left curve \(x=h_1(y)\) and a right curve \(x=h_2(y)\), for \(y\) from \(c\) to \(d\). (Many books call this "type II".)

A region is called simple if it is both \(x\)-simple and \(y\)-simple (for example a disc or the inside of an ellipse). Then either formula can be used, whichever is more convenient.

How to set up the limits of a double integral over a region:

  1. Do: sketch the region on the \(xy\)-plane, labelling every boundary curve and every corner point (solve pairs of boundary equations to find the corners). All the limits are read off this picture of the field; you do not need to picture the surface above it.
  2. Do: choose a strip direction: vertical (\(y\) inside, \(dy\,dx\)) or horizontal (\(x\) inside, \(dx\,dy\)). Prefer the direction in which every strip enters through the same curve and leaves through the same curve, or the one that makes the inner integral possible. If the entry or exit curve changes partway across, one formula cannot describe all the slices, and you must split the field (see the Beyond box below).
  3. Do: draw one typical strip. Its inner limits are the curves where it enters and leaves the region, written in terms of the outer variable (solve each boundary equation for the inner variable). These are where the slice starts and stops, and they move as the slice moves.
  4. Do: the outer limits are the constant positions of the first and last strip. Sweeping the strip between them covers the whole field exactly once.
  5. Do: integrate the inner integral with the outer variable held constant, then integrate the result over the outer variable. First the face area of one slice, then add up the slices.
Example 2.1.3

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_S xy^2\,dS\) where \(S\) is the region bounded by \(y=0\), \(y=x^2\) and \(x=1\).

A sketch of the region \(S\) is important, but it is not necessary to be able to visualise the surface \(z=f(x,y)\). The three curves enclose the curved triangle with corners \((0,0)\), \((1,0)\) and \((1,1)\): the \(x\)-axis along the bottom, the line \(x=1\) on the right, and the parabola \(y=x^2\) on the upper left. Two integration orders can be chosen, corresponding to slicing in the \(y\)- or \(x\)-direction (see the figure below).

Vertical strips (integrate along the \(y\)-direction first). At a fixed \(x\) between 0 and 1, the strip runs from the bottom \(y=0\) up to the parabola \(y=x^2\):Every vertical strip enters through the \(x\)-axis and leaves through the parabola, so one pair of inner limits works; the outer limits 0 and 1 are the first and last strip.

\[\begin{aligned}\iint_S xy^2\,dS&=\int_{x=0}^{1}\int_{y=0}^{x^2}xy^2\,dy\,dx\\&=\int_{x=0}^{1}\left[\frac{xy^3}{3}\right]_{0}^{x^2}dx\\&=\int_{x=0}^{1}\frac{x^7}{3}\,dx\\&=\left[\frac{x^8}{24}\right]_0^1=\frac1{24}.\end{aligned}\]

Horizontal strips (integrate along the \(x\)-direction first). At a fixed \(y\) between 0 and 1, the strip runs from the parabola on the left (solve \(y=x^2\) for \(x\): \(x=\sqrt y\)) to the line \(x=1\) on the right:A horizontal strip's ends are \(x\)-values, so the parabola must be rewritten as "\(x=\) something in \(y\)" before it can serve as a limit.

\[\begin{aligned}\iint_S xy^2\,dS&=\int_{y=0}^{1}\int_{x=\sqrt y}^{1}xy^2\,dx\,dy\\&=\int_{y=0}^{1}\left[\frac{x^2y^2}{2}\right]_{\sqrt y}^{1}dy\\&=\int_{y=0}^{1}\left(\frac{y^2}{2}-\frac{y^3}{2}\right)dy\\&=\left[\frac{y^3}{6}-\frac{y^4}{8}\right]_0^1\\&=\frac16-\frac18=\frac1{24}.\end{aligned}\]

Beyond the handouts · reversing the order of integration

Exam questions often give an iterated integral that cannot be done in the given order (the inner integral has no elementary antiderivative, like \(\int e^{y^3}dy\)) and ask you to change the order. In the loaf picture: the given order slices the solid one way and the slices are impossible to measure, so slice it the other way. The volume is the same; only the description of the field changes. The recipe:

  1. Do: read the region off the given limits: the outer limits give the range of the outer variable; the inner limits give the two boundary curves. The limits are a coded description of the field; decode it before re-slicing.
  2. Do: sketch the region, labelling every boundary curve and every corner point. The new limits can only be read from the picture, not from the old limits by algebra alone.
  3. Do: draw a strip in the other direction. Read off its new inner limits (where it enters and leaves) and the new constant outer limits (the extreme values of the new outer variable). This is the field sliced the other way: new slice ends, new first and last slice.
  4. Do: if the new strips enter or leave through different curves in different parts of the region, split the region into pieces and add the integrals. Each piece is a part of the field where one formula describes every slice.

Worked example (written for these notes). \(\displaystyle\int_{x=0}^{4}\int_{y=\sqrt x}^{2}e^{y^3}\,dy\,dx\). The inner integral is impossible as it stands. The region is \(0\le x\le4,\ \sqrt x\le y\le2\): above the curve \(y=\sqrt x\) (that is, \(x=y^2\)) and below \(y=2\), with corners \((0,0),(0,2),(4,2)\). A horizontal strip at height \(y\) runs from \(x=0\) to \(x=y^2\), for \(0\le y\le2\).With horizontal slices the inner integral is in \(x\), and \(e^{y^3}\) is a constant for it; the slice length \(y^2\) then supplies exactly the factor needed to integrate \(e^{y^3}\).

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{0}^{4}\!\int_{\sqrt x}^{2}e^{y^3}dy\,dx&=\int_{0}^{2}\!\int_{0}^{y^2}e^{y^3}dx\,dy\\&=\int_0^2y^2e^{y^3}dy\\&=\left[\tfrac13e^{y^3}\right]_0^2=\frac{e^8-1}{3}.\end{aligned}\]

When a split is needed. The region bounded by \(y=\sqrt x\), \(y=2-x\) and \(y=0\) has corners \((0,0),(2,0),(1,1)\). Horizontal strips (from \(x=y^2\) to \(x=2-y\), for \(0\le y\le1\)) need one integral:

\[\text{Area}=\int_{0}^{1}\big(2-y-y^2\big)\,dy=\frac76.\]

Vertical strips have a top curve that changes at \(x=1\) (the strip meets \(y=\sqrt x\) for \(x\lt1\) but \(y=2-x\) for \(x\gt1\)), so that order needs two pieces:The two pieces are the parts of the field left and right of the corner \((1,1)\); in each, every slice leaves through the same curve.

\[\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{\sqrt x}dy\,dx+\int_1^2\!\!\int_0^{2-x}dy\,dx=\frac23+\frac12=\frac76.\]

Reversing the order: the two regions of the Beyond box

The readout gives the strip's limits. In the second region, move a vertical strip past \(x=1\) (dashed line) and watch its top boundary change from \(y=\sqrt x\) to \(y=2-x\): that is why the \(dy\,dx\) order needs two integrals.

You can now: explain a double integral as adding (height × tile area) over a region; set up and evaluate it as two ordinary integrals by slicing, with the limits read off a sketch; reverse the order of integration, splitting the region when the slice ends change.

2Change of variables in double integrals (handout §2.2, pp. 7–9) ~17 min

What is this? (change of variables)

Some fields are awkward to tile with ordinary squares: a slanted parallelogram, a round pond. The fix is to draw a new grid that fits the shape: lines parallel to the parallelogram's sides, or circles and rays for a disc. Think of a square grid printed on a rubber sheet, then stretched and laid over the field so that its lines follow the edges. On the rubber sheet the region is a plain rectangle with easy limits. The price: stretching changes the size of each tile, so every tile must be multiplied by how much it was stretched. That stretch factor is the Jacobian.

In first-year calculus, a substitution \(x=g(u)\) turns \(\int f(x)\,dx\) into \(\int f(g(u))\,g'(u)\,du\): the factor \(g'(u)\) says how much a small step \(du\) is stretched into a step \(dx\). Double integrals have the same idea. A clever choice of new variables can turn an awkward region (a slanted parallelogram, a disc) into a rectangle, or an awkward integrand into a simple one; the Jacobian plays the part of \(g'(u)\), stretching areas instead of lengths.

Start from the double integral

\[I=\iint_S f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.\]
Change of variables formula

The change of variables \(x=x(u,v),\ y=y(u,v)\) gives

\[\begin{aligned}I=\iint_{S^*}&f\big(x(u,v),\,y(u,v)\big) \times\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|du\,dv,\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}I=\iint_{S^*}&f\big(x(u,v),\,y(u,v)\big)\\&\times\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|du\,dv,\end{aligned}\]

where

\[J=\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=\det\begin{pmatrix}\pd xu&\pd xv\\[4pt]\pd yu&\pd yv\end{pmatrix}\]

is called the Jacobian of the transformation. \(S^*\) is the region in the \(uv\)-plane that corresponds to the region \(S\) in the \(xy\)-plane (every point of \(S^*\) is sent by the formulas to a point of \(S\), and every point of \(S\) comes from exactly one point of \(S^*\)). The Jacobian is assumed to be nonzero except at isolated points, and the partial derivatives are assumed continuous.

In words: to change variables, (1) rewrite the integrand in \(u,v\); (2) replace the region \(S\) by its image \(S^*\); (3) replace \(dx\,dy\) by \(|J|\,du\,dv\), where \(|J|\) = (area of a little piece in the \(xy\)-plane) ÷ (area of the matching piece in the \(uv\)-plane). Note the absolute value: areas are never negative.

Reading the formula
\(f\big(x(u,v),y(u,v)\big)\)
the height (snow depth) at the point of the field that the new grid labels \((u,v)\): the same function, just addressed by new grid labels.
\(S^*\)
the region drawn on the unstretched rubber sheet; with a good choice it is a rectangle, so the limits are constants.
\(du\,dv\)
the area of one tiny tile on the unstretched sheet.
\(\left|\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|\)
how many times bigger that tile becomes once stretched onto the field. So \(|J|\,du\,dv\) is the true area of the tile, and the sum is still (height × true tile area), as in §1.

The determinant \(\det\begin{pmatrix}p&q\\r&s\end{pmatrix}=ps-qr\), so written out,

\[J=\pd xu\pd yv-\pd xv\pd yu.\]

In words: Jacobian = (top-left × bottom-right) − (top-right × bottom-left) of the table of first partial derivatives; the rows are \(x\) and \(y\), the columns are \(u\) and \(v\).

Reading the formula
\(\left(\pd xu,\ \pd yu\right)\)
the first column: where on the field a tiny step in the \(u\)-direction of the sheet takes you. It is one side of the stretched tile.
\(\left(\pd xv,\ \pd yv\right)\)
the second column: the other side of the stretched tile, from a tiny step in \(v\).
\(\pd xu\pd yv-\pd xv\pd yu\)

Beyond the handouts · why the factor is |J|

Near \((u_0,v_0)\), a small step \(\Delta u\) (with \(v\) fixed) moves the point \((x,y)\) by the vector \(\vb a=\left(\pd xu,\pd yu\right)\Delta u\), and a small step \(\Delta v\) moves it by \(\vb b=\left(\pd xv,\pd yv\right)\Delta v\). So the little \(\Delta u\times\Delta v\) rectangle is carried to (almost) a parallelogram with sides \(\vb a\) and \(\vb b\). The area of a parallelogram with sides \((a_1,a_2)\) and \((b_1,b_2)\) is \(|a_1b_2-a_2b_1|\) (the size of the 2D cross product), which gives

\[\begin{aligned}\Delta A&\approx\left|\pd xu\pd yv-\pd xv\pd yu\right|\Delta u\,\Delta v\\&=|J|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v.\end{aligned}\]

In words: the Jacobian is the area magnification factor of the map at that point.

How to change variables in a double integral:

  1. Do: choose \(u\) and \(v\) from the shape of the region (or the integrand): if the boundary is two pairs of lines like \(x-2y=0,4\) and \(x+y=1,4\), take \(u=x-2y\), \(v=x+y\); for circles, take polar coordinates. You are choosing the new grid: its lines should run along the edges of the field, so that the field becomes a rectangle on the sheet.
  2. Do: map every boundary curve of \(S\) into the \(uv\)-plane to find \(S^*\) and its limits. This is the region drawn on the unstretched sheet.
  3. Do: find the Jacobian \(\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\) (directly, or with the reciprocal rule in the next box) and take its absolute value. This is the stretch factor that turns a sheet tile \(du\,dv\) into its true area on the field.
  4. Do: rewrite the integrand in \(u,v\), multiply by \(|J|\), and integrate over \(S^*\). Height × true tile area, added over the field, exactly as in §1.
Beyond the handouts · the exam routine for a u, v substitution

In the exam the substitution is usually given the "wrong way round", as \(u=u(x,y),\ v=v(x,y)\), and the boundaries of \(S\) are lines like \(x+y=1\). Two facts make this quick:

  1. Do: use the reciprocal rule: \(\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=1\Big/\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\). So you may compute the easy Jacobian of \(u,v\) with respect to \(x,y\) and flip it, without solving for \(x\) and \(y\). (It works because the two maps undo each other, and the determinant of a product of matrices is the product of the determinants.) If going from sheet to field enlarges tiles 3 times, going back from field to sheet must shrink them to one third.
  2. Do: map the region: map every boundary curve, not just the corners: a boundary like \(x+y=1\) becomes \(v=1\) if \(v=x+y\). For a linear map, straight edges stay straight, so mapping the corners is enough; for a non-linear map, edges can bend, so map each edge (for example, \(y=0\) under \(u=2xy,\ v=x^2-y^2\) becomes \(u=0,\ v\ge0\)). The edge of the field must become the edge of the region on the sheet; a curved grid can bend a straight edge, so corners alone do not show its shape.

Non-linear example (the type in Dec 2018 and Dec 2023): for \(u=2xy,\ v=x^2-y^2\),

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}&=\det\begin{pmatrix}2y&2x\\2x&-2y\end{pmatrix}\\&=-4(x^2+y^2),\end{aligned}\]

and since \(u^2+v^2=(x^2+y^2)^2\),

\[\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{u^2+v^2}}.\]
Show the calculation: why \(u^2+v^2=(x^2+y^2)^2\), and the flip
  1. Square and add: \(u^2+v^2\) \(=(2xy)^2+(x^2-y^2)^2\) \(=4x^2y^2+x^4-2x^2y^2+y^4\).
  2. Collect: \(x^4+2x^2y^2+y^4=(x^2+y^2)^2\) (a perfect square, \((A+B)^2=A^2+2AB+B^2\) with \(A=x^2,\ B=y^2\)).
  3. Take the square root: \(x^2+y^2=\sqrt{u^2+v^2}\) (positive root, since \(x^2+y^2\ge0\)).
  4. Reciprocal rule: \(\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\) \(=\dfrac{1}{-4(x^2+y^2)}\) \(=-\dfrac{1}{4\sqrt{u^2+v^2}}\). In the integral use the absolute value, \(\dfrac{1}{4\sqrt{u^2+v^2}}\).

The point of this step: the integral is now written entirely in \(u\) and \(v\), with no \(x\) or \(y\) left over.

Polar coordinates

What is this? (polar coordinates)

For round fields, use a dartboard grid: circles around the centre and rays out from it. A point is then given by its distance \(r\) from the centre and its angle \(\theta\). The dartboard's tiles are not all the same size: between the same two rays, a tile far out is wider than one near the bullseye, because the arc between two rays grows with distance. A tile at distance \(r\) is \(r\) times as wide along the arc, so its area carries an extra factor \(r\): that is the polar Jacobian.

Example 2.2.1

Find the moment of inertia of a hollow circular cylinder of inner radius \(a\), outer radius \(b\), height \(h\) and constant density \(\rho\) about the axis of the cylinder.

The moment of inertia about an axis measures how hard it is to spin a body about that axis: each bit of mass \(\Delta m\) at distance \(d\) from the axis contributes \(d^2\,\Delta m\), so mass far from the axis counts more. The cross-section of the cylinder (cut perpendicular to its axis) is an annulus, a ring between the circles of radius \(a\) and \(b\) centred on the axis (figure below).A ring is awkward in \(x,y\) (it is not simple in either direction) but on a dartboard grid it is just "\(r\) from \(a\) to \(b\), any angle": a rectangle with constant limits.

Change to polar coordinates, where \(r\) is the distance from the origin and \(\theta\) the angle measured anticlockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis:

\[x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta.\]

The Jacobian is

\[\begin{aligned}J=\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}&=\det\begin{pmatrix}\pd xr&\pd x\theta\\[4pt]\pd yr&\pd y\theta\end{pmatrix}\\&=\det\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\\&=r\cos^2\theta+r\sin^2\theta=r.\end{aligned}\]

In other words, \(dx\,dy\to r\,dr\,d\theta\). This corresponds to the elemental area in the \(r\theta\)-plane: a small Cartesian element is a rectangle with area \(\delta S=\delta x\,\delta y\), while a small polar element is a piece of a ring with sides \(\delta r\) (along the radius) and \(r\,\delta\theta\) (along the arc), so \(\delta S\approx r\,\delta r\,\delta\theta\).

In words: polar area element = (radial width) × (arc length) = \(\delta r\times r\,\delta\theta\). The factor \(r\) appears because arcs are longer further from the origin.

Reading the formula
\(\delta r\)
the thickness of the dartboard ring the tile sits in.
\(r\,\delta\theta\)
the length of arc between two neighbouring rays at distance \(r\): an angle in radians times the radius.
\(r\) (the Jacobian)
the stretch factor: with the same \(\delta r\) and \(\delta\theta\), a tile at \(r=2\) has twice the area of a tile at \(r=1\), and a tile at the centre has almost none.

The region of integration is now a rectangle (simple) in the \(r\theta\)-plane: \(0\le\theta\lt2\pi,\ a\le r\le b\). Each element of the cross-section, extended along the full height \(h\) of the cylinder, is a thin rod of mass \(\rho h\,r\,dr\,d\theta\) at distance \(r\) from the axis, contributing \(r^2\times\rho h\,r\,dr\,d\theta\). Thus the moment of inertia isHere the "height" being added over the ring is \(\rho h r^2\) (mass per unit floor area × distance²); the extra \(r\) is the tile-size factor, not part of the physics.

\[\begin{aligned}I&=\int_{r=a}^{b}\int_{\theta=0}^{2\pi}\rho h r^2\,r\,dr\,d\theta\\&=\rho h\big[\theta\big]_0^{2\pi}\left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_a^b\\&=\rho h\,2\pi\,\frac{b^4-a^4}{4}\\&=\frac M2\,(a^2+b^2),\end{aligned}\]

where the mass \(M\) is equal to \(\pi\rho h(b^2-a^2)\) (density × volume of the hollow cylinder). The last step uses \(b^4-a^4=(b^2-a^2)(b^2+a^2)\).

In words: moment of inertia of a pipe = half its mass × (inner radius² + outer radius²). Check: a solid cylinder (\(a=0\)) gives the familiar \(\tfrac12Mb^2\).

Example 2.2.2

Evaluate \(\displaystyle I=\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx\).

The function \(e^{-x^2}\) has no elementary antiderivative, so no first-year method works. The trick is to square \(I\) and turn it into a double integral. Since the name of the integration variable does not matter, \(I=\int_0^\infty e^{-y^2}dy\) as well, andSquaring turns a one-variable problem into the volume under a round bell over a quarter of the plane, and a round shape is exactly what the dartboard grid handles well.

\[\begin{aligned}I^2&=\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}dx\int_0^\infty e^{-y^2}dy\\&=\iint e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy,\end{aligned}\]

where the region of integration is the whole first quadrant \(x\ge0,\ y\ge0\). In polar coordinates the first quadrant is \(0\le r\lt\infty,\ 0\le\theta\le\pi/2\), and \(x^2+y^2=r^2\), soThe tile-size factor \(r\) is what rescues the calculation: \(r\) is (half) the derivative of \(r^2\), so \(re^{-r^2}\) has a simple antiderivative.

\[\begin{aligned}I^2&=\iint e^{-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta\\&=\frac\pi2\left[-\frac{e^{-r^2}}{2}\right]_0^\infty=\frac\pi4.\end{aligned}\]

Hence \(I=\sqrt\pi/2\) (positive, since the integrand is positive).

Show the calculation: each step explained
  1. Product → double integral. \(\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}dx\cdot\int_0^\infty e^{-y^2}dy\): the second integral is a constant as far as \(x\) is concerned, so it can be moved inside the first. That gives \(\int_0^\infty\!\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}e^{-y^2}\,dy\,dx\), and \(e^{-x^2}e^{-y^2}=e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\) (add the exponents). This is Fubini's theorem used backwards, over the "rectangle" \(x\ge0,\ y\ge0\).
  2. To polar. \(x^2+y^2=r^2\); \(dx\,dy\to r\,dr\,d\theta\) (Jacobian \(r\)); the first quadrant is \(0\le\theta\le\pi/2\), \(0\le r\lt\infty\) (every ray at an angle between 0 and \(\pi/2\) runs out to infinity).
  3. Separate. The integrand \(re^{-r^2}\) does not depend on \(\theta\), so \(I^2\) \(=\int_0^{\pi/2}d\theta\cdot\int_0^\infty re^{-r^2}dr\) \(=\frac\pi2\int_0^\infty re^{-r^2}dr\).
  4. The \(r\)-integral. Substitute \(w=r^2\), \(dw=2r\,dr\): \(\int_0^\infty re^{-r^2}dr\) \(=\frac12\int_0^\infty e^{-w}dw\) \(=\frac12\big[-e^{-w}\big]_0^\infty\) \(=\frac12(0-(-1))\) \(=\frac12\). (Equivalently, the antiderivative is \(-\frac12e^{-r^2}\), which tends to 0 as \(r\to\infty\) and equals \(-\frac12\) at \(r=0\).)
  5. Combine. \(I^2=\frac\pi2\cdot\frac12=\frac\pi4\), and \(I=\sqrt{\pi/4}=\sqrt\pi/2\).

Strictly, an integral to \(\infty\) means a limit: integrate over the quarter disc \(r\le R\) and let \(R\to\infty\), which is what the figure below shows.

In words: the extra \(r\) from the Jacobian is exactly what makes \(e^{-r^2}\) integrable: \(\int re^{-r^2}dr=-\tfrac12e^{-r^2}\).

  3. Do: the outer limits are the extreme angles: the first and last rays that meet the region. Turning the ray between them sweeps over the whole region once.

3Triple integrals (handout §2.3, pp. 10–13) ~13 min

What is this?

A double integral added up tiny floor tiles. A triple integral adds up tiny boxes that fill a solid. Picture a fruit loaf with raisins packed more tightly in some places: cut it into tiny cubes, find the raisins in each cube (raisins per cubic centimetre × size of the cube) and add up all the cubes to get the raisins in the whole loaf. A triple integral turns a density ("amount per unit volume") into a total.

Suppose that a scalar field \(f(x,y,z)\) (a rule giving one number at every point, such as temperature or density) is defined at all points within a region \(D\) of three-dimensional space. Divide \(D\) into \(n\) small subregions; the \(k\)-th has volume \(\Delta V_k\) and contains a point \((x_k,y_k,z_k)\). Multiply the value of \(f\) there by the little volume, add up, and let the pieces shrink:

Definition (triple integral) \[\begin{aligned}\iiint_D f(x,y,z)\,dV&=\iiint_D f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dy\,dz\\&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(x_k,y_k,z_k)\,\Delta V_k,\end{aligned}\]

provided that \(\max\{\Delta V_k\}\to0\) as \(n\to\infty\). Such an integral is often called a volume integral.

In words: triple integral = limit of the sum of (value of \(f\) at a point × volume of the little box around it) over all the boxes filling \(D\). There is no "height" picture any more (that would need 4 dimensions); think of \(f\) as a density and the integral as the total amount.

Reading the formula
\(f(x_k,y_k,z_k)\)
the density measured at one point inside the \(k\)-th little box (raisins per cubic centimetre there)
\(\Delta V_k\)
the size (volume) of that little box
\(f(x_k,y_k,z_k)\,\Delta V_k\)
the amount inside that one box: density × volume
\(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\)
add the amounts of all the boxes that fill the solid
\(\lim\), \(\max\{\Delta V_k\}\to0\)
cut ever smaller boxes, so the density hardly changes inside any one box and "density × volume" becomes exact

One small box \(\Delta V_k=\Delta x_k\,\Delta y_k\,\Delta z_k\) inside a solid region \(D\)

The region \(D\) (a rounded blob, drawn see-through, with a quarter wedge cut away so the inside is visible). The red box, sitting in the cut-away corner, has sides \(\Delta x_k,\Delta y_k,\Delta z_k\) and contains the sample point \((x_k,y_k,z_k)\) (dot). Filling \(D\) with such boxes and adding \(f(x_k,y_k,z_k)\,\Delta V_k\) gives the Riemann sum.

Example 2.3.1 (what triple integrals measure)
  1. If \(f(x,y,z)\equiv1\), then \(\displaystyle\iiint_D dV=\) volume of the region \(D\).
  2. If \(f(x,y,z)=\rho\), and this scalar field gives the (mass) density at a point, then \(\displaystyle\iiint_D\rho\,dV=\) mass of the region \(D\).
  3. If \(f(x,y,z)=\rho d^2\), where \(d\) is the perpendicular distance of a point in the region \(D\) to a fixed axis, then \(\displaystyle\iiint_D\rho d^2\,dV=\) moment of inertia of the region \(D\) about the given axis. (For the \(z\)-axis, \(d^2=x^2+y^2\).)
  4. If \(f(x,y,z)=\rho\) is the charge density at a point, then \(\displaystyle\iiint_D\rho\,dV=\) total charge within the region \(D\).

In words: integrate "amount per unit volume" over a solid to get the total amount.

Rectangular regions

What is this?

For a brick-shaped loaf, add in an orderly way: push a skewer straight down and add up everything along that thin column; line the columns up into a slice; stack the slices into the whole loaf. Each stage is an ordinary one-variable integral.

For a box \(x_1\le x\le x_2,\ y_1\le y\le y_2,\ z_1\le z\le z_2\), all six limits are constants, and the triple integral is three ordinary integrals done one inside the other (in any order), each time holding the other variables constant.

  1. Do: read the ranges from the six faces, then integrate the innermost variable (say \(z\)), treating \(x\) and \(y\) as constants. This adds up the density along one vertical column standing at the fixed floor point \((x,y)\).
  2. Do: integrate the result over the middle variable (\(y\)), treating \(x\) as a constant. This lines up all the columns along one row to make a slice at fixed \(x\).
  3. Do: integrate over the outer variable (\(x\)). This stacks the slices from one end of the box to the other: the whole solid.
Example 2.3.2 (rectangular region)

The density of a rectangular block bounded by the planes \(x=1\), \(x=2\), \(y=0\), \(y=3\), \(z=-1\) and \(z=0\) is given by the scalar function \(\rho(x,y,z)=x(y+1)-z\). Find the mass of the block.

The block is \(1\le x\le2,\ 0\le y\le3,\ -1\le z\le0\): length 1 in \(x\), 3 in \(y\), and it hangs 1 unit below the \(xy\)-plane (figure below). The total mass is

\[\begin{aligned}M&=\iiint\big(x(y+1)-z\big)\,dz\,dy\,dx\\&=\int_{x=1}^{2}\!\left[\int_{y=0}^{3}\!\left[\int_{z=-1}^{0}\big(x(y+1)-z\big)dz\right]dy\right]dx\\&=\int_{1}^{2}\!\left[\int_{0}^{3}\left[x(y+1)z-\frac{z^2}{2}\right]_{-1}^{0}dy\right]dx\\&=\int_{1}^{2}\!\left[\int_{0}^{3}\left(x(y+1)+\frac12\right)dy\right]dx\\&=\int_{1}^{2}\left[\frac{xy^2}{2}+xy+\frac y2\right]_0^3dx\\&=\int_{1}^{2}\left(\frac92x+3x+\frac32\right)dx\\&=\left[\frac{15}{4}x^2+\frac32x\right]_1^2=\frac{51}{4}.\end{aligned}\]

(In the third line: at \(z=0\) the bracket is 0; at \(z=-1\) it is \(-x(y+1)-\tfrac12\); subtracting gives \(x(y+1)+\tfrac12\).)

  1. Lines 3–4, the \(z\)-integral: \(x(y+1)+\tfrac12\) is the mass of the column of height 1 at \((x,y)\). One skewer pushed down through the block, from \(z=-1\) up to \(z=0\).
  2. Lines 5–6, the \(y\)-integral: \(\tfrac{15}{2}x+\tfrac32\) is the mass of the slice at \(x\). All the columns along the row \(0\le y\le3\) added together.
  3. Line 7, the \(x\)-integral: \(\tfrac{51}{4}\). The slices from \(x=1\) to \(x=2\) stacked into the whole block.

Sense check. Volume 3, so the average density is \(\tfrac{51}{4}\div3=4.25\), which (the density being linear in each variable) equals the density at the centre \((1.5,1.5,-0.5)\). ✓

Non-rectangular regions

What is this?

Most solids are not boxes: think of a loaf with a curved, risen top and a dented bottom. The skewer idea still works, but each column now starts at the bottom crust and stops at the top crust, so its length depends on where you push it in. Shine a torch straight down and the loaf casts a shadow on the table: the columns stand on that shadow, so the shadow tells you where the columns are, exactly like the floor region of a double integral.

Example 2.3.3 (non-rectangular regions)

Suppose \(D\) is bounded above by the surface \(z=\Psi_2(x,y)\) and below by \(z=\Psi_1(x,y)\), and its projection onto the \(xy\)-plane (its "shadow" when lit from straight above) is described by

\[a_1\le x\le a_2,\qquad\varphi_1(x)\le y\le\varphi_2(x).\]

Then,

\[\begin{aligned}\iiint_D f\,dV=\int_{x=a_1}^{a_2}&\int_{y=\varphi_1(x)}^{\varphi_2(x)}\int_{z=\Psi_1(x,y)}^{\Psi_2(x,y)}\\&f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx.\end{aligned}\]

In words: innermost, run up a vertical column from the bottom surface to the top surface; then sweep the column across a strip of the shadow; then sweep the strip across the whole shadow. Limits get "more constant" as you move outward: the \(z\)-limits may depend on \(x\) and \(y\), the \(y\)-limits only on \(x\), the \(x\)-limits are numbers.

Example 2.3.4

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iiint_D\frac{1}{(x+y+2z+1)^3}\,dV\) where \(D\) is the region enclosed by the planes \(x=0\), \(y=0\), \(z=0\) and \(x+y+z=1\).

\(D\) is a tetrahedron (a solid with four triangular faces) with corners \((0,0,0)\), \((1,0,0)\), \((0,1,0)\), \((0,0,1)\). Its top face is the slanted plane \(z=1-x-y\); its shadow on the \(xy\)-plane is the triangle under the line \(y=1-x\) (where the slanted plane meets \(z=0\)). So

\[\begin{gathered}0\le x\le1,\quad0\le y\le1-x,\\0\le z\le1-x-y.\end{gathered}\]

In the picture: each column stands on the floor and stops at the slanted roof; each strip crosses the triangular shadow up to the edge where the roof meets the floor.

We have

\[\begin{aligned}&\int_{x=0}^{1}\!\int_{y=0}^{1-x}\!\int_{z=0}^{1-x-y}\frac{dz\,dy\,dx}{(x+y+2z+1)^3}\\&=\frac14(\ln3-1)\approx0.0247.\end{aligned}\]
Show the calculation: the three integrations, each step explained

Step 1: the \(z\)-integral (\(x\) and \(y\) are constants). This adds the integrand up one column, from the floor to the slanted roof. Let \(w=x+y+2z+1\), so \(dw=2\,dz\). Then

\[\int\frac{dz}{w^3}=\frac12\int w^{-3}\,dw=\frac12\cdot\frac{w^{-2}}{-2}=-\frac{1}{4w^2}.\]

Limits: at \(z=0\), \(w=x+y+1\); at \(z=1-x-y\), \(w\) \(=x+y+2(1-x-y)+1\) \(=3-x-y\). So

\[\begin{aligned}\Big[-\frac{1}{4w^2}\Big]_{z=0}^{1-x-y}&=\frac{1}{4(x+y+1)^2} -\frac{1}{4(3-x-y)^2}.\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}\Big[-\frac{1}{4w^2}\Big]_{z=0}^{1-x-y}&=\frac{1}{4(x+y+1)^2}\\&\quad-\frac{1}{4(3-x-y)^2}.\end{aligned}\]

Step 2: the \(y\)-integral (\(x\) is a constant). This adds the column totals along one strip of the triangular shadow. Both terms are of the form \(1/(\text{linear in }y)^2\):

  • \(\int\frac{dy}{(x+y+1)^2}=-\frac{1}{x+y+1}\) (power rule; the inside has derivative \(+1\)).
  • \(\int\frac{dy}{(3-x-y)^2}=+\frac{1}{3-x-y}\) (the inside has derivative \(-1\), which flips the sign).
\[\begin{aligned}&\int_0^{1-x}\Big(\frac{1}{4(x+y+1)^2}-\frac{1}{4(3-x-y)^2}\Big)dy\\&=\Big[-\frac{1}{4(x+y+1)}-\frac{1}{4(3-x-y)}\Big]_{y=0}^{1-x}.\end{aligned}\]

At \(y=1-x\): \(x+y+1=2\) and \(3-x-y=2\), giving \(-\frac18-\frac18=-\frac14\). At \(y=0\): \(-\frac{1}{4(x+1)}-\frac{1}{4(3-x)}\). Top minus bottom:

\[-\frac14+\frac{1}{4(x+1)}+\frac{1}{4(3-x)}.\]

Step 3: the \(x\)-integral. This adds the strips across the whole shadow, from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\). \(\int\frac{dx}{x+1}=\ln(x+1)\) and \(\int\frac{dx}{3-x}=-\ln(3-x)\) (again the inside has derivative \(-1\)):

\[\begin{aligned}&\Big[-\frac x4+\frac14\ln(x+1)-\frac14\ln(3-x)\Big]_0^1\\&=\Big(-\frac14+\frac14\ln2-\frac14\ln2\Big) -\Big(0+0-\frac14\ln3\Big)=\frac14(\ln3-1).\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}&\Big[-\frac x4+\frac14\ln(x+1)-\frac14\ln(3-x)\Big]_0^1\\&=\Big(-\frac14+\frac14\ln2-\frac14\ln2\Big)\\&\quad-\Big(0+0-\frac14\ln3\Big)=\frac14(\ln3-1).\end{aligned}\]

(\(\ln\) is the natural logarithm, written \(\log\) in some books.)

Beyond the handouts · setting limits for any solid, and changing the order

The May papers ask you to "sketch the region and evaluate" (May 2015, 2016, 2017, 2022, 2024, 2025) and once to rewrite a triple integral in a different order (May 2021). The routine:

  1. Do: find the corners. For planes, set two of \(x,y,z\) to zero in each equation to find where it meets the axes (\(x+2y+z=1\) meets them at \(x=1\), \(y=\tfrac12\), \(z=1\)). The corners let you sketch the loaf, so you can see its crusts and its shadow.
  2. Do: choose the innermost variable whose bottom and top surfaces are easy (usually \(z\)). Write them as \(z=\Psi_1(x,y)\) and \(z=\Psi_2(x,y)\). This picks the direction of the skewer: each column must enter through one surface and leave through one other.
  3. Do: find the shadow on the plane of the other two variables: set \(\Psi_1=\Psi_2\) (where the top meets the bottom) to get its boundary. Sketch it. Where the top and bottom crusts meet, the column has shrunk to zero length: that is the edge of the shadow.
  4. Do: set the shadow's limits exactly as for a double integral (§1). The columns stand on the shadow, so placing them is a 2D problem you have already solved.
  5. Do: to change the order, project onto a different coordinate plane and repeat. Pushing the skewer in from a different side gives different columns and a different shadow, but the same loaf.

Example of a change of order. \(\int_0^1\!\int_0^{1-x}\!\int_0^{2-2z}f\,dy\,dz\,dx\) describes \(0\) \(\le x\) \(\le1,\ 0\) \(\le z\) \(\le1-x,\ 0\) \(\le y\) \(\le2-2z\). The solid is bounded by \(x+z=1\), \(y+2z=2\) and the coordinate planes. With \(z\) innermost: \(z\) runs from 0 to the lower of the two planes, \(\min\!\big(1-x,\ 1-\tfrac y2\big)\), so the \(xy\)-shadow (the rectangle \(0\le x\le1,\ 0\le y\le2\)) must be split along \(y=2x\) where the two planes cross.

Symmetry shortcut: if the region is symmetric under \(x\to-x\) and the integrand is odd in \(x\) (changes sign), the integral is zero, as in May 2025 Q3 (see the exam taster). In the picture, every box on one side has a partner box on the other side with the opposite amount, so the pairs cancel.

You can now: explain a triple integral as density × little volume added over a solid; set up the limits of a triple integral as column, strip and shadow, and evaluate it one integral at a time; rewrite a triple integral in a different order by projecting onto a different coordinate plane.

4Change of variables in triple integrals: cylindrical and spherical coordinates (handout §2.4, pp. 13–17) ~18 min

What is this?

So far we cut the solid into little rectangular boxes. For a round cake or a ball that is clumsy, because the edges are curved. Instead, cut along a curved grid that fits the shape (rings, wedges and layers for a cake; shells and cones for a ball). Each little piece of the new grid is a curved brick, and its volume is not simply "step × step × step": the Jacobian is the factor that says how much the coordinate grid stretches a tiny box into that brick.

Everything in §2 carries over to three dimensions, with a \(3\times3\) Jacobian. If

\[I=\iiint_D f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dy\,dz,\]

then the change of variables \(x=x(u,v,w),\ y=y(u,v,w),\ z=z(u,v,w)\) gives

Change of variables in a triple integral \[\begin{aligned}I=\iiint_{D^*}&f\big(x(u,v,w),y(u,v,w),z(u,v,w)\big) \times\left|\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)}\right|du\,dv\,dw,\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}I=\iiint_{D^*}&f\big(x(u,v,w),y(u,v,w),z(u,v,w)\big)\\&\times\left|\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)}\right|du\,dv\,dw,\end{aligned}\]

where

\[J=\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)}=\det\begin{pmatrix}\pd xu&\pd xv&\pd xw\\[4pt]\pd yu&\pd yv&\pd yw\\[4pt]\pd zu&\pd zv&\pd zw\end{pmatrix}\]

is the Jacobian of the transformation. \(D^*\) is the region in \(uvw\)-space corresponding to the region \(D\) in \(xyz\)-space. The Jacobian is assumed nonzero except at isolated points, and the partial derivatives are continuous.

In words: as in 2D, rewrite the integrand, replace the region by its image, and replace \(dx\,dy\,dz\) by \(|J|\,du\,dv\,dw\); now \(|J|\) is the volume magnification factor.

Reading the formula
\(f\big(x(u,v,w),\dots\big)\)
the density, looked up at the real point that the new labels \((u,v,w)\) stand for
\(du\,dv\,dw\)
a tiny box in the world of the new labels
\(\left|\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)}\right|\)
how many times bigger that tiny box is once it is placed in real space as a curved brick (volume stretch)
\(D^*\)
the same solid described in the new labels; a good choice makes it a plain box with constant limits
  1. Do: choose new coordinates that match the shape (cylindrical for things round about an axis, spherical for balls and cones from the origin). You want the curved grid whose bricks fit the solid, so the limits become constants.
  2. Do: write \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) in terms of the new variables and rewrite the integrand. The density must be looked up at the same real point, just under its new name.
  3. Do: describe the region by the new variables to get the limits of \(D^*\). This lists which bricks of the new grid make up the solid.
  4. Do: replace \(dx\,dy\,dz\) by \(|J|\,du\,dv\,dw\) (\(r\) for cylindrical, \(\rho^2\sin\phi\) for spherical). Each brick's true volume is the tiny box times the stretch factor; leaving it out counts every brick as the same size.
  5. Do: integrate one variable at a time, as in §3. The adding itself is unchanged: column, then strip, then the whole solid.

Cylindrical polar coordinates

What is this? (cylindrical coordinates)

Think of a round cake on a turntable with a skewer through its centre (the \(z\)-axis). To say where a raisin is, give how far it is from the skewer, which way it lies (a compass direction around the skewer), and how high it is. That is polar coordinates on the floor plus the ordinary height. Cutting the cake along these coordinates gives rings, wedges and layers, which fit a round cake perfectly.

Cylindrical coordinates are polar coordinates in the \(xy\)-plane plus the ordinary height \(z\). A point \(P\) is located by: \(r\), its distance from the \(z\)-axis; \(\theta\), the angle from the positive \(x\)-axis to the vertical plane through \(P\) and the \(z\)-axis (measured anticlockwise seen from above); and \(z\), its height above the \(xy\)-plane. Drop a perpendicular from \(P\) to the point \((x,y,0)\) on the floor: that floor point has polar coordinates \((r,\theta)\).

The transformation between rectangular coordinates and cylindrical polar coordinates is given by

\[x=r\cos\theta,\quad y=r\sin\theta,\quad z=z,\]

with

\[r\ge0,\quad0\le\theta\lt2\pi,\quad-\infty\lt z\lt\infty.\]

In words: \(r\) = distance from the \(z\)-axis, \(\theta\) = compass direction around the axis, \(z\) = height. Note \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), so \(x^2+y^2\) becomes \(r^2\).

Reading the formula
\(r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta\)
walk a distance \(r\) out from the skewer in direction \(\theta\); these are the east and north parts of that walk
\(z=z\)
the height is not changed at all
\(0\le\theta\lt2\pi\)
one full turn round the skewer covers every direction once

(expanding along the last row leaves exactly the \(2\times2\) polar Jacobian of §2). Thus we have

\[dV=dx\,dy\,dz\;\to\;r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz.\]

In words: a cylindrical volume element = polar area element \(r\,dr\,d\theta\) × height \(dz\): a small curved brick with sides \(dr\), \(r\,d\theta\) and \(dz\).

Setting up a cylindrical integral.

  1. Do: replace every \(x^2+y^2\) by \(r^2\) (and \(x\), \(y\) by \(r\cos\theta\), \(r\sin\theta\)). Distance from the skewer is what round shapes care about.
  2. Do: \(z\)-limits: bottom surface to top surface, written in \(r\) and \(\theta\). This is the column of §3, from the bottom crust to the top crust.
  3. Do: \(r\) and \(\theta\) limits: describe the shadow on the floor in polar form (a disc is \(0\le r\le a\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\)). The columns stand on the shadow, and polar coordinates make a round shadow a rectangle.
  4. Do: multiply the integrand by \(r\), then integrate. The outer bricks are bigger than the inner ones, and \(r\) accounts for that.

Find the volume between the surface \(x^2+y^2=2\), \(z=x^2+y^2+2\) and the \(z=0\) plane.

In Cartesian coordinates we have (the shadow is the disc \(x^2+y^2\le2\))

\[\begin{aligned}\iiint_V1\,dV=\int_{x=-\sqrt2}^{\sqrt2}&\int_{y=-\sqrt{2-x^2}}^{\sqrt{2-x^2}}\\&\int_{z=0}^{x^2+y^2+2}1\,dz\,dy\,dx,\end{aligned}\]

which is unpleasant because of the square-root limits (the shadow is round, but the strips are straight). In cylindrical polar coordinates the disc is simply \(0\le r\le\sqrt2,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\), and the top is \(z=r^2+2\):

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Volume}&=\int_{r=0}^{\sqrt2}\int_{\theta=0}^{2\pi}\int_{z=0}^{r^2+2}r\,dz\,d\theta\,dr\\&=\int_{r=0}^{\sqrt2}\int_{\theta=0}^{2\pi}(r^3+2r)\,d\theta\,dr\\&=2\pi\left[\frac{r^4}{4}+r^2\right]_0^{\sqrt2}=2\pi(1+2)\\&=6\pi.\end{aligned}\]
  1. The \(z\)-integral of \(r\) from 0 to \(r^2+2\) gives \(r^3+2r\). One column of height \(r^2+2\), standing on a floor piece whose size carries the factor \(r\).
  2. The \(\theta\)-integral just multiplies by \(2\pi\). The solid looks the same in every direction, so a full turn round the axis collects the same amount from every wedge.
  3. The \(r\)-integral from 0 to \(\sqrt2\). This adds the rings from the axis out to the cylinder wall.

Spherical polar coordinates

What is this? (spherical coordinates)

Think of a globe with its centre at the origin. To say where a point is, give how far it is from the centre, how far down it is from the North Pole (an angle), and its longitude (the angle round the axis). Cutting along these coordinates gives onion-like shells, cones and wedges, which fit balls and cones perfectly, just as rings and wedges fit a round cake.

Spherical coordinates suit balls, spherical shells and cones with their tip at the origin. A point \(P\) is located by: \(\rho\) (Greek "rho"), its distance from the origin \(O\); \(\phi\) ("phi"), the angle between the positive \(z\)-axis and the line \(OP\) (like latitude, but measured down from the North Pole); and \(\theta\), the same angle as in cylindrical coordinates (like longitude). Dropping a perpendicular from \(P\) to the floor gives the foot at horizontal distance \(r=\rho\sin\phi\) from the origin; the height of \(P\) is \(z=\rho\cos\phi\).

The transformation between rectangular coordinates and spherical polar coordinates is given by

\[\begin{gathered}x=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\quad y=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\\ z=\rho\cos\phi,\end{gathered}\]

with

\[\rho\ge0,\quad0\le\phi\le\pi,\quad0\le\theta\lt2\pi.\]

In words: first go up the \(z\)-axis a distance \(\rho\), tip down by angle \(\phi\), then swing round by angle \(\theta\). Note \(\phi\) only runs from 0 (straight up) to \(\pi\) (straight down), not to \(2\pi\); and \(x^2+y^2+z^2=\rho^2\).

\(\rho\sin\phi\)

\(\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\ \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta\)
that floor distance split into east and north parts, exactly as in cylindrical coordinates
\(\rho\cos\phi\)
the height: a point tipped by \(\phi\) from the pole rises \(\rho\cos\phi\)

The Jacobian of the transformation is given by

\[\begin{aligned}J&=\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(\rho,\phi,\theta)}\\&=\begin{vmatrix}\sin\phi\cos\theta&\rho\cos\phi\cos\theta&-\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta\\ \sin\phi\sin\theta&\rho\cos\phi\sin\theta&\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta\\ \cos\phi&-\rho\sin\phi&0\end{vmatrix}\\&=\rho^2\sin\phi.\end{aligned}\]
The determinant worked out (expanding along the bottom row)

The bottom row is \((\cos\phi,\ -\rho\sin\phi,\ 0)\). The cofactor of \(\cos\phi\) is

\[\begin{aligned}&+\begin{vmatrix}\rho\cos\phi\cos\theta&-\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta\\ \rho\cos\phi\sin\theta&\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta\end{vmatrix}\\&=\rho^2\sin\phi\cos\phi\,(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)\\&=\rho^2\sin\phi\cos\phi.\end{aligned}\]

The cofactor of \(-\rho\sin\phi\) (position row 3, column 2, sign \(-\)) is

\[\begin{aligned}&-\begin{vmatrix}\sin\phi\cos\theta&-\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta\\ \sin\phi\sin\theta&\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta\end{vmatrix}\\&=-\rho\sin^2\phi\,(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)=-\rho\sin^2\phi.\end{aligned}\]

So

\[\begin{aligned}J&=\cos\phi\cdot\rho^2\sin\phi\cos\phi+(-\rho\sin\phi)(-\rho\sin^2\phi)\\&=\rho^2\sin\phi\,(\cos^2\phi+\sin^2\phi)=\rho^2\sin\phi.\end{aligned}\]

Thus we have

\[dV=dx\,dy\,dz\;\to\;\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.\]

In words: a spherical volume element is a small curved brick with sides \(d\rho\) (outward), \(\rho\,d\phi\) (along a line of longitude) and \(\rho\sin\phi\,d\theta\) (along a circle of latitude, whose radius is \(\rho\sin\phi\)); multiply the three. Since \(0\le\phi\le\pi\), \(\sin\phi\ge0\) and no absolute value is needed.

Reading the formula
\(\rho\sin\phi\,d\theta\)
the east–west side: an arc of angle \(d\theta\) on a circle of latitude, whose radius \(\rho\sin\phi\) shrinks to 0 at the poles
\(\rho^2\sin\phi\)
the two stretched sides multiplied: bricks far out and near the equator are big, bricks near the centre or a pole are small

Quick check. For a ball of radius \(a\): the \(\rho\)-integral \(\int_0^a\rho^2\,d\rho=\frac{a^3}{3}\), the \(\phi\)-integral \(\int_0^\pi\sin\phi\,d\phi=2\) and the \(\theta\)-integral \(2\pi\) multiply to \(\frac43\pi a^3\), the familiar volume. ✓

Setting up a spherical integral.

  1. Do: replace \(x^2+y^2+z^2\) by \(\rho^2\), and \(z\) by \(\rho\cos\phi\). Distance from the centre is what balls care about.
  2. Do: \(\rho\)-limits: from the inner surface to the outer one (0 to \(a\) for a solid ball). This is the "column" now: a ray from the centre outwards through the shells.
  3. Do: \(\phi\)-limits: 0 to the edge angle (\(\pi/2\) for the upper half, \(\phi_0\) inside the cone \(\phi=\phi_0\), \(\pi\) for all). This chooses how far down from the North Pole the rays sweep.
  4. Do: \(\theta\)-limits: 0 to \(2\pi\) for a full turn, less for a wedge. This swings the fan of rays round the axis.
  5. Do: multiply the integrand by \(\rho^2\sin\phi\), then integrate. The bricks have different sizes; this is their volume factor.

The spherical volume element (Beyond the handouts: where \(\rho^2\sin\phi\) comes from)

The brick \(\rho_0\) \(\le\rho\) \(\le\rho_0+\Delta\rho,\ \phi_0\) \(\le\phi\) \(\le\phi_0+\Delta\phi,\ \theta_0\) \(\le\theta\) \(\le\theta_0+\Delta\theta\) (red), drawn on a faint sphere. Its volume is close to \(\rho^2\sin\phi\,\Delta\rho\,\Delta\phi\,\Delta\theta\) evaluated at its centre (readout), because its edges have lengths about \(\Delta\rho\), \(\rho\,\Delta\phi\) and \(\rho\sin\phi\,\Delta\theta\). Move \(\phi\) towards the pole (\(\phi\to0\)): the brick gets thinner because circles of latitude shrink, which is the \(\sin\phi\) factor.

Example 2.4.2

In a simple model of the charge distribution around the positively charged (\(Q\)) nucleus of the hydrogen atom, the charge density in the electron cloud at a distance \(\rho\) from the atom's centre is

\[\sigma(\rho)=-\frac{Q}{\pi a^3}e^{-2\rho/a},\]

where \(a\) is the Bohr radius. Determine the total charge in the electron cloud.

The density depends only on the distance \(\rho\), so spherical coordinates are natural. "All space" is \(0\le\rho\lt\infty,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\), and the integrand separates into a function of \(\rho\) times one of \(\phi\) times one of \(\theta\), so the triple integral is a product of three single integrals:

\[\begin{aligned}q&=\iiint_V\sigma\,dV\quad(V\text{ is all space})\\&=-\frac{Q}{\pi a^3}\int_{\theta=0}^{2\pi}\!\int_{\phi=0}^{\pi}\!\int_{\rho=0}^{\infty} \qquad\quad e^{-2\rho/a}\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\\&=-\frac{Q}{\pi a^3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^2e^{-2\rho/a}\,d\rho \times\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin\phi\,d\phi\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\\&=-\frac{Q}{\pi a^3}\cdot\frac{a^3}{4}\cdot2\cdot2\pi=-Q.\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}q&=\iiint_V\sigma\,dV\quad(V\text{ is all space})\\&=-\frac{Q}{\pi a^3}\int_{\theta=0}^{2\pi}\!\int_{\phi=0}^{\pi}\!\int_{\rho=0}^{\infty}\\&\qquad\quad e^{-2\rho/a}\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\\&=-\frac{Q}{\pi a^3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^2e^{-2\rho/a}\,d\rho\\&\qquad\quad\times\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin\phi\,d\phi\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\\&=-\frac{Q}{\pi a^3}\cdot\frac{a^3}{4}\cdot2\cdot2\pi=-Q.\end{aligned}\]
Show the calculation: the two angle integrals and the radial integral (integration by parts twice)

Why it splits. The limits are all constants and the integrand is (function of \(\rho\)) × (function of \(\phi\)) × 1, so the triple integral is a product of three single integrals.

Angle integrals. \(\int_0^\pi\sin\phi\,d\phi\) \(=[-\cos\phi]_0^\pi\) \(=-(-1)-(-1)\) \(=2\) and \(\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta=2\pi\).

Radial integral. Write \(c=2/a\) to keep the algebra short, so the integral is \(\int_0^\infty\rho^2e^{-c\rho}\,d\rho\). Integration by parts is \(\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du\).

  1. First time: \(u=\rho^2\) (gets simpler when differentiated), \(dv=e^{-c\rho}d\rho\), so \(du=2\rho\,d\rho\), \(v=-\frac1ce^{-c\rho}\): \[\int\rho^2e^{-c\rho}d\rho=-\frac{\rho^2}{c}e^{-c\rho}+\frac2c\int\rho\,e^{-c\rho}d\rho.\]
  2. Second time, on the new integral: \(u=\rho\), \(dv=e^{-c\rho}d\rho\): \[\begin{aligned}\int\rho\,e^{-c\rho}d\rho&=-\frac{\rho}{c}e^{-c\rho}+\frac1c\int e^{-c\rho}d\rho\\&=-\frac{\rho}{c}e^{-c\rho}-\frac{1}{c^2}e^{-c\rho}.\end{aligned}\]
  3. Put step 2 into step 1 and factor out \(-e^{-c\rho}\): \[\int\rho^2e^{-c\rho}d\rho=-e^{-c\rho}\Big(\frac{\rho^2}{c}+\frac{2\rho}{c^2}+\frac{2}{c^3}\Big).\]
  4. Back to \(a\): \(\frac1c=\frac a2,\ \frac1{c^2}=\frac{a^2}{4},\ \frac1{c^3}=\frac{a^3}{8}\), so the antiderivative is \[-\frac a2\,e^{-2\rho/a}\Big(\rho^2+a\rho+\frac{a^2}{2}\Big).\]
  5. Limits: as \(\rho\to\infty\) it tends to 0 (an exponential decay beats any polynomial); at \(\rho=0\) it equals \(-\frac a2\cdot\frac{a^2}{2}=-\frac{a^3}{4}\). So \(\int_0^\infty\rho^2e^{-2\rho/a}d\rho=0-\left(-\frac{a^3}{4}\right)=\frac{a^3}{4}\).

Combine. \(q\) \(=-\frac{Q}{\pi a^3}\cdot\frac{a^3}{4}\cdot2\cdot2\pi\) \(=-\frac{Q}{\pi a^3}\cdot\pi a^3\) \(=-Q\).

  1. The \(\rho\)-integral \(\int_0^\infty\rho^2e^{-2\rho/a}d\rho=\frac{a^3}{4}\). This adds the shells from the centre outwards; the \(\rho^2\) is there because outer shells hold more volume.
  2. The angle integrals give \(2\cdot2\pi=4\pi\). The density is the same in every direction, so the angles only count the full surface of each shell (\(4\pi\rho^2\) for radius \(\rho\)).

In words: the electron cloud carries total charge \(-Q\), exactly cancelling the nucleus: the atom is neutral, as it should be.

Example 2.4.2: how the charge is spread out with distance

Horizontal axis: distance \(\rho/a\). Teal: the charge in a thin shell, proportional to \(\rho^2e^{-2\rho/a}\) (the \(\rho^2\) is the Jacobian at work: shells further out are bigger); it peaks at \(\rho=a\), the Bohr radius. Orange: the fraction of the cloud's charge inside radius \(R\), equal to \(1-e^{-2R/a}\big(1+\frac{2R}{a}+\frac{2R^2}{a^2}\big)\), which climbs to 1 (the full \(-Q\)).

◆Common mistakes

✗Forgetting the Jacobian: writing \(dx\,dy=dr\,d\theta\) or \(dV=d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\). Fix: \(r\,dr\,d\theta\), \(r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz\), \(\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\); check with the area of a unit disc (\(\pi\), not \(2\pi\)). ✗Using \(\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\) where \(\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\) is needed. Fix: \(dx\,dy\) needs "\(x,y\) on top"; if you computed the other one, take its reciprocal. ✗Using a negative Jacobian as it is. Fix: take the absolute value \(|J|\) in the integral (but give \(J\) with its sign if the question asks for the Jacobian). ✗Outer limits that depend on a variable, e.g. \(\int_0^{x}\!\int_0^1\dots dy\,dx\). Fix: the outermost limits must be numbers; each inner limit may only involve variables still to be integrated outside it. ✗Reversing the order by swapping the limits as they stand. Fix: sketch the region, then read the new limits from a strip in the other direction; split the region if the strip's ends change curve. ✗Mapping only the corners of a region under a non-linear transformation. Fix: map each boundary curve; edges may bend. ✗Letting \(\phi\) run from 0 to \(2\pi\) in spherical coordinates. Fix: \(0\le\phi\le\pi\) covers the whole sphere; \(\theta\) runs from 0 to \(2\pi\). ✗Forgetting to rewrite the integrand: leaving \(x^2+y^2\) instead of \(r^2\), or \(x-y\) instead of \(u\). Fix: after substituting, no \(x\) or \(y\) may remain. ✓Always sketch the region (markers give marks for it), label every boundary curve and corner, and draw the strip you are using.

◆Find the mistake

Written for these notes. Each "solution" has one error. Find it before opening the answer.

1. Polar coordinates

"\(\displaystyle \iint_{x^2+y^2\le1}(x^2+y^2)\,dA\) \(\displaystyle =\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^1r^2\,dr\,d\theta\) \(\displaystyle =2\pi\cdot\frac13\) \(\displaystyle =\frac{2\pi}3.\)"

Answer

The Jacobian \(r\) is missing: \(dA=r\,dr\,d\theta\). Correct: \(\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^1r^3\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\cdot\frac14=\frac\pi2\).

2. Reversing the order

"\(\displaystyle \int_{x=0}^{1}\int_{y=x^2}^{1}x\,dy\,dx\) \(\displaystyle =\int_{y=0}^{1}\int_{x=0}^{y^2}x\,dx\,dy\) \(\displaystyle =\int_0^1\frac{y^4}{2}dy\) \(\displaystyle =\frac1{10}.\)"

Answer

The region is \(0\le x\le1,\ x^2\le y\le1\) (above the parabola, below \(y=1\); shaded above). A horizontal strip at height \(y\) runs from \(x=0\) to the parabola, where \(x=\sqrt y\), not \(y^2\). Correct: \(\int_0^1\!\int_0^{\sqrt y}x\,dx\,dy=\int_0^1\frac y2dy=\frac14\) (the same as the original order: \(\int_0^1x(1-x^2)\,dx=\frac14\)).

3. The Jacobian the wrong way round

"Area of the region \(R\) between \(x+y=0\), \(x+y=2\), \(x-y=0\), \(x-y=1\): let \(u=x+y,\ v=x-y\), so \(R^*\) is \(0\le u\le2,\ 0\le v\le1\). \(\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}=-2\), so the area is \(\int_0^2\!\int_0^1|{-2}|\,dv\,du=4\)."

Answer

The factor needed is \(\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|=\frac1{|-2|}=\frac12\). Correct area: \(\frac12\times2\times1=1\). Sanity check: \(R\) is a rectangle (rotated by 45°) with sides \(\sqrt2\) and \(\frac1{\sqrt2}\), area 1.

◆Concept checks

Written for these notes.

1. A student computes the area of the unit disc as \(\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^1dr\,d\theta\). What do they get, and what went wrong?

Answer

\(2\pi\), but the area is \(\pi\). They forgot the Jacobian \(r\): \(\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^1r\,dr\,d\theta=\pi\).

2. Can \(\iint_Rf\,dA\) be negative? What does it mean then?

Answer

Yes, if \(f\lt0\) on (most of) \(R\). It is then a signed volume: volume above the floor counts positive, volume below counts negative.

3. Without integrating, find \(\displaystyle\iint_{x^2+y^2\le1}xy^2\,dA\).

Answer

0. The disc is symmetric under \(x\to-x\) and \(xy^2\) changes sign, so the contributions from left and right halves cancel.

4. Why does \(\int_0^1\!\int_y^1e^{x^2}dx\,dy\) have to be done in the other order?

Answer

\(e^{x^2}\) has no elementary antiderivative in \(x\). In the other order the inner integral is in \(y\) (trivial), and it produces a factor \(x\) that makes \(\int xe^{x^2}dx\) easy.

5. Which coordinates suit the solid cone \(x^2+y^2\le z^2,\ 0\le z\le1\)? Find its volume.

Answer

Cylindrical: \(0\le r\le z\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\), \(0\le z\le1\). Volume \(=\int_0^1\!\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^zr\,dr\,d\theta\,dz=\int_0^1\pi z^2dz=\frac\pi3\) (spherical with \(\phi\le\frac\pi4\) also works).

6. In spherical coordinates, why is there no absolute value on \(\rho^2\sin\phi\)?

Answer

Because \(0\le\phi\le\pi\), so \(\sin\phi\ge0\), and \(\rho^2\ge0\). The Jacobian is never negative on the allowed ranges.

Answer

\(\displaystyle I\) \(\displaystyle =\int_{-\sqrt2}^{\sqrt2}\!\int_0^{2-x^2}\!\int_0^{y/2}x\,dz\,dy\,dx\) \(\displaystyle =\int_{-\sqrt2}^{\sqrt2}\frac{x(2-x^2)^2}{4}\,dx\) \(\displaystyle =\boxed{0}\), because \(D\) is symmetric about the plane \(x=0\) and the integrand \(x\) is odd. (The volume of \(D\) is \(\frac{16\sqrt2}{15}\), a useful check of the limits.)

Show the calculation (outline)
  1. \(z\)-integral (\(x,y\) constant): \(\int_0^{y/2}x\,dz=\frac{xy}{2}\).
  2. \(y\)-integral (\(x\) constant): \(\int_0^{2-x^2}\frac{xy}{2}\,dy=\frac x2\cdot\frac{(2-x^2)^2}{2}=\frac{x(2-x^2)^2}{4}\).
  3. \(x\)-integral: \(\frac{x(2-x^2)^2}{4}\) is odd (replacing \(x\) by \(-x\) changes its sign) and the interval \([-\sqrt2,\sqrt2]\) is symmetric about 0, so the left and right halves cancel: \(I=0\).
  4. Volume check: the same limits with integrand 1 give \(\int_{-\sqrt2}^{\sqrt2}\frac{(2-x^2)^2}{4}dx=\frac{16\sqrt2}{15}\), positive, so the limits describe a real solid.
May 2017 Q2(a)6 marksMay formatGuide C2.1 · Cartesian double integrals

Consider the double integral \(\displaystyle\int_{y=0}^{1}\int_{x=\sin^{-1}y}^{\pi/2}e^{\cos x}\,dx\,dy\) over the region in the \(xy\)-plane.

(i) Sketch the region of integration in the \(xy\)-plane. (ii) Change the order of integration, hence, or otherwise, evaluate the double integral. (iii) Is the answer in part (ii) equal to the area of the region of integration? Justify your answer by finding the area of that region.

Hint

\(x\ge\sin^{-1}y\) with \(0\le y\le1\) means \(y\le\sin x\): the region lies under the arch \(y=\sin x\) for \(0\le x\le\pi/2\). Vertical strips: \(0\le y\le\sin x\).

Answer

(ii) \(\displaystyle \int_0^{\pi/2}\!\int_0^{\sin x}e^{\cos x}\,dy\,dx\) \(\displaystyle =\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{\cos x}\sin x\,dx\) \(\displaystyle =\boxed{e-1}\) \(\displaystyle \approx1.718\).

(iii) No: the area is \(\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin x\,dx=1\). The integral equals the area only when the integrand is 1; here it is \(e^{\cos x}\ne1\).
