1Conservative fields and scalar potentials ~11 min

What is this? (conservative field)

Walking on a hillside, however you wander from the car park to the summit, the total height you gain is the same: summit height minus car-park height. A conservative field is a vector field that behaves like this: the work it does on you depends only on where you start and where you finish, never on the route, because the field is secretly just the "uphill arrow" of some height function.

In Chapter 3b §4 we met fields for which the work integral \(\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) between two points \(A\) and \(B\) gives the same value for every path \(C\) from \(A\) to \(B\) (the integral is path independent). This happens exactly when \(\vb F\) is the gradient of a scalar function.

Definition (conservative field, scalar potential)

A vector field \(\vb F\) whose work integrals are path independent is called a conservative field. Equivalently, \(\vb F=\nabla\phi\) for some scalar function \(\phi(x,y,z)\), called a scalar potential (or potential function) of \(\vb F\). Then for any path \(C\) from \(A\) to \(B\),

\[\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r=\phi(B)-\phi(A).\]

In words: work done by a conservative field = (potential at the end) − (potential at the start), whatever route is taken.

Reading the formula
\(\phi\)
the height function of the hill: one number at every point.
\(\vb F=\nabla\phi\)
at each point the field is the uphill arrow: it points the steepest way up, and is longer where the hill is steeper.
\(\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\)
add up, step by step along your route, how much each step goes uphill: the total climb along \(C\).
\(\phi(B)-\phi(A)\)
height at the finish minus height at the start. No \(C\) appears on this side, which is the whole point: the route has dropped out.

The gravitational field and the electrostatic field are conservative: the work gravity does on a mass moving from \(A\) to \(B\) depends only on the change in height (more precisely, in gravitational potential), not on the route. "Conservative" refers to conservation of energy: a particle carried round a closed loop by such a field gains no net energy.

Analogy

Think of \(\phi\) as height on a hillside: your total climb from car park to summit is (summit height) − (car-park height), zig-zag path or direct. The field \(\nabla\phi\) is the "uphill" arrow at each point, and the work integral adds up the climbing done along your route.

Path independence does not apply to the other kind of line integral, \(\int_C f(x,y,z)\,ds\) (the integral with respect to arc length, Chapter 3b §2), in general. That integral depends on the whole curve (a longer path usually gives a bigger value, like the distance you walk rather than the height you gain), and, as we have seen, it does not involve orientation (the direction of travel) at all.

How to find a potential in 2D (rebuilding the hill from its slopes), for \(\vb F=M\ii+N\jj\):

  1. Do: Write the two conditions: the slope in the \(x\)-direction of \(\phi\) is \(M\), and the slope in the \(y\)-direction is \(N\), i.e. \(\pd{\phi}{x}=M\), \(\pd{\phi}{y}=N\). \(\vb F\) is meant to be the uphill arrow, so its components are the east-slope and north-slope of the unknown height.
  2. Do: Integrate \(M\) with respect to \(x\), holding \(y\) fixed, and add an unknown function \(f(y)\) instead of a constant. Knowing only the east-slope fixes each east–west profile of the hill up to a starting height, and that starting height may differ from one north line to the next.
  3. Do: Differentiate the result with respect to \(y\) and set it equal to \(N\); solve for \(f'(y)\), then integrate. The north-slope is the second piece of information; it decides how the starting heights change from line to line.
  4. Do: Drop the final constant (take \(c=0\)) and work out \(\phi(B)-\phi(A)\). Raising the whole hill by \(c\) changes no slope and no climb, so \(c\) always cancels.

If step 3 gives an \(f'(y)\) that still contains \(x\), no such hill exists and the field is not conservative (the curl test below detects this in advance).

Example 3.7.1

Show that \(\vb F=y^2\,\ii+2xy\,\jj\) is a conservative field and hence evaluate \(\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) along any path \(C\) from \((0,0)\) to \((4,2)\).

\(\vb F\) is conservative if \(\vb F=\nabla\phi\), that is

\[y^2=\pd{\phi}{x}\quad\text{and}\quad 2xy=\pd{\phi}{y}.\]

Thus, integrating the first equation with respect to \(x\) (holding \(y\) fixed), we first haveThis rebuilds each east–west profile of the hill from its east-slope \(y^2\).

\[\begin{aligned}\phi&=y^2x+f(y),\\ \pd{\phi}{y}&=2yx+f'(y),\end{aligned}\]

where \(f(y)\) is an arbitrary function of \(y\). (It plays the role of the "constant of integration": anything that does not depend on \(x\) disappears when we take \(\partial/\partial x\).) Since \(2xy=\pd{\phi}{y}\), this implies \(f'(y)=0\) and \(f(y)=c\) is a constant function.The north-slope already matches with nothing extra, so the profiles need no extra lifting from line to line: the hill is complete. Finally, we have

\[\phi=y^2x+c.\]

Hence,Only the two end heights are needed; the route \(C\) never enters the calculation.

\[\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r=\phi(4,2)-\phi(0,0)=16.\]

In words: find \(\phi\) by undoing one partial derivative at a time, then the work is just \(\phi\) at the end minus \(\phi\) at the start. The constant \(c\) cancels, so take \(c=0\).

A necessary condition: curl F = 0

What is this? (no swirl on a hill)

Drop a tiny paddle wheel into the field (Chapter 3a §5): if the arrows push harder on one side of it than the other, it spins, and the curl measures that spin. Uphill arrows on a real hill can never do this. If they swirled round a point, you could walk a small circle with the field always helping you uphill and arrive back where you started higher than you left, an impossible staircase. So a conservative field must have zero curl everywhere.

Before trying to build a potential (which may simply fail), we would like a quick way to decide whether a given field \(\vb F\) is conservative at all.

To answer this question, we start from the fact that if \(\vb F\) is conservative, that is \(\vb F=\nabla f\), then (by the identity "curl grad = 0" of Chapter 3a §6) we have

\[\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\nabla\times\vb F=\nabla\times\nabla f=\vb 0.\]

In words: a gradient field has no swirl. So if \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F\ne\vb 0\) anywhere, \(\vb F\) is certainly not conservative.

Reading the formula
\(\nabla\times\nabla f\)
the spin of a tiny paddle wheel placed in the uphill arrows of the hill \(f\).
\(=\vb 0\)
no paddle wheel spins anywhere: a hill has no impossible staircases. Read backwards, a spinning wheel anywhere proves there is no hill.

It is natural to ask if the converse of this statement is also true. That is, does \(\nabla\times\vb F=\vb 0\) imply \(\vb F\) is conservative? Unfortunately, the answer is NO unless the domain of definition of \(\vb F\) (the region where \(\vb F\) is defined) falls into a certain category: it must be "simply connected" (§2).

Going further · finding a potential in 3D (the most examined skill of this chapter)

The example above is in 2D, but exams (type C3c.1) mostly use 3D fields \(\vb F=M\ii+N\jj+P\kk\). The same idea works, one variable at a time:

  1. Do: Check \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\vb 0\) (§2), or, if the field has unknown constants, solve \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\vb 0\) for them. Make sure a hill can exist before trying to build it; a spinning paddle wheel means it cannot.
  2. Do: Integrate \(M\) with respect to \(x\): \(\phi=\int M\,dx+g(y,z)\). The "constant" is now a function of the other two variables. The east-slope fixes the height along each east–west line, but each such line (labelled by its \(y\) and \(z\)) may start at its own height.
  3. Do: Differentiate with respect to \(y\) and set equal to \(N\): this gives \(\partial g/\partial y\); integrate it to get \(g=\ldots+h(z)\). The north-slope ties the lines together within each horizontal layer; only a height per layer (a function of \(z\)) is still free.
  4. Do: Differentiate with respect to \(z\) and set equal to \(P\): this gives \(h'(z)\), hence \(h\). The vertical slope stacks the layers at the right heights relative to each other.
  5. Do: Check: \(\nabla\phi=\vb F\). Then work = \(\phi(B)-\phi(A)\). The uphill arrows of your rebuilt hill must be the field you were given; then only the two end heights matter.

Worked example. Show that \(\vb F=(2xy+z^3)\,\ii+x^2\,\jj+3xz^2\,\kk\) is conservative, find a potential, and find the work done by \(\vb F\) from \((1,-2,1)\) to \((3,1,4)\).

\(\operatorname{curl}\vb F\) \(=(0-0)\ii+(3z^2-3z^2)\jj+(2x-2x)\kk\) \(=\vb 0\), and \(\vb F\) is defined on all of space, which is simply connected (§2), so \(\vb F\) is conservative.No paddle wheel spins and there is no hole for swirl to hide in, so a hill exists. Then

\[\begin{aligned}\phi&=x^2y+xz^3,\\ \text{work}&=\phi(3,1,4)-\phi(1,-2,1)\\&=201-(-1)=202.\end{aligned}\]
Show the calculation: building \(\phi\) step by step
  1. Curl: \(\ii\): \(\partial_y(3xz^2)-\partial_z(x^2)=0\); \(\jj\): \(\partial_z(2xy+z^3)-\partial_x(3xz^2)\) \(=3z^2-3z^2\) \(=0\); \(\kk\): \(\partial_x(x^2)-\partial_y(2xy+z^3)=2x-2x=0\).
  2. \(\phi\) \(=\int(2xy+z^3)\,dx\) \(=x^2y+xz^3+g(y,z)\). Why: \(y,z\) are held fixed, so anything built from them alone is a "constant".
  3. \(\pd{\phi}{y}=x^2+\pd gy\) must equal \(N=x^2\), so \(\pd gy=0\) and \(g=h(z)\).
  4. \(\pd{\phi}{z}=3xz^2+h'(z)\) must equal \(P=3xz^2\), so \(h'(z)=0\), \(h=c\). Take \(c=0\): \(\phi=x^2y+xz^3\).
  5. Check: \(\nabla\phi=(2xy+z^3,\ x^2,\ 3xz^2)=\vb F\). ✓
  6. \(\phi(3,1,4)=9\cdot1+3\cdot64=201\); \(\phi(1,-2,1)=-2+1=-1\); work \(=202\).

In words: each new integration can only add a function of the variables not yet used; the next partial derivative pins that function down.

You can now: explain why the work done by a conservative field depends only on the end points; rebuild a potential \(\phi\) from a field one variable at a time (in 2D or 3D) and use \(\phi(B)-\phi(A)\) for the work; and use a nonzero curl to show at once that a field is not conservative.

2The curl test and simply connected regions ~13 min

What is this? (simply connected)

Zero curl says no paddle wheel spins at any point of the region. But swirl can still hide in a place that is not in the region, such as a hole, the way water circles a plughole: every bit of water round the drain may flow without spinning, yet the water as a whole goes round. So whether "no spin anywhere" is enough depends on whether the region has holes that a loop can wrap round. A simply connected region is one with no such holes: every loop of string in it can be pulled tight to a point.

Whether \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\vb 0\) guarantees a potential depends on the shape of the region on which \(\vb F\) is defined. Two words describe that shape.

Definitions (connected, simply connected)

A region \(\mathcal D\) is connected if any two of its points can be joined by a path lying inside \(\mathcal D\) (it is "all in one piece").

A region \(\mathcal D\) in the plane or in space is simply connected if it is connected and all closed curves in \(\mathcal D\) can be continuously shrunk down to a point in \(\mathcal D\) while staying within \(\mathcal D\).

In words: lasso test. Throw a loop of string anywhere in the region and pull it tight. If it can always be pulled down to a single point without leaving the region, the region is simply connected. A hole that the string can wrap round (like a post) spoils this.

Connected or not, simply connected or not

"Blob with a hole" is connected but not simply connected: the red loop goes round the hole, and as you shrink it towards a point it must cross the hole, i.e. leave the region. "Blob without a hole" is simply connected. Right (drag to rotate): 3D space is simply connected. Remove the \(z\)-axis (dashed) and a loop round the axis can no longer be shrunk: not simply connected. But remove only the origin and every loop can still be slid off the missing point and shrunk: still simply connected.

In the plane, for a connected domain to be simply connected is the same thing as "no holes" within the domain, but in space this is not true: space with one point (or a solid ball) removed has a "hole" yet is simply connected, since a loop can slide round the missing point; space with a whole line removed is not. We have the following result.

What is this? (the curl test)

Shrink any loop to a point, as in the lasso test: it sweeps out a surface, and the circulation round the loop (the net push of the field as you go round) equals the total spin of the tiny paddle wheels on that surface (this is Stokes' theorem, §6). If every paddle wheel is still, every loop has zero circulation, and zero circulation round every loop is exactly what "conservative" means. The loop must shrink without leaving the region: hence "simply connected".

Theorem (the curl test)

Let \(\vb F\) be a vector field defined on a simply connected domain in the plane or space (with continuous partial derivatives). Then

\[\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\vb 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\vb F\ \text{is conservative}.\]

In words: on a region without "wrap-around" holes, no swirl anywhere means a potential exists.

Reading the formula
simply connected domain
every loop can be pulled tight without leaving the region, so there is no hole where swirl could hide.
\(\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\vb 0\)
no paddle wheel spins at any point of the region.
\(\Rightarrow\)
one way only: together these two facts guarantee a hill. Drop "simply connected" and the arrow can fail (the vortex field below).
Example 3.7.2

Suppose \(\vb F=(2x-3)\,\ii-z\,\jj+\cos z\,\kk\) is defined for every point in space. Show that \(\vb F\) is not conservative.

\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{curl}\vb F&=\nabla\times\vb F\\&=\begin{vmatrix}\ii&\jj&\kk\\[2pt]\pd{}{x}&\pd{}{y}&\pd{}{z}\\[4pt]2x-3&-z&\cos z\end{vmatrix}\\&=\ii\ne\vb 0.\end{aligned}\]

Since \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F\ne\vb 0\), hence \(\vb F\) is not conservative.The \(\ii\)-component 1 says a paddle wheel with its axle along \(x\) spins: the \(\jj\)-part \(-z\) changes as you move up, so it pushes unevenly on the wheel's two sides. A spinning wheel means no hill.

Show the calculation: the three components
  1. \(\ii\): \(\pd{}{y}(\cos z)-\pd{}{z}(-z)=0-(-1)=1\). Why: \(\partial_yP-\partial_zN\); \(\cos z\) has no \(y\).
  2. \(\jj\): \(\pd{}{z}(2x-3)-\pd{}{x}(\cos z)=0-0=0\). Why: \(\partial_zM-\partial_xP\).
  3. \(\kk\): \(\pd{}{x}(-z)-\pd{}{y}(2x-3)=0-0=0\). Why: \(\partial_xN-\partial_yM\).

In words: one nonzero component of the curl is enough to rule out a potential; no need to try (and fail) to build \(\phi\).

The test written out in components

The test \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\vb 0\) can be rewritten in a differential form. Suppose \(\vb F=M\ii+N\jj+P\kk\); then

\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{curl}\vb F=&\Big(\pd Py-\pd Nz\Big)\ii-\Big(\pd Px-\pd Mz\Big)\jj +\Big(\pd Nx-\pd My\Big)\kk=\vb 0\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{curl}\vb F=&\Big(\pd Py-\pd Nz\Big)\ii-\Big(\pd Px-\pd Mz\Big)\jj\\&+\Big(\pd Nx-\pd My\Big)\kk=\vb 0\end{aligned}\]

if and only if

\[\pd Py=\pd Nz,\qquad\pd Px=\pd Mz,\qquad\pd Nx=\pd My.\]

In words: the "cross" derivatives must match in pairs. For a 2D field \(M\ii+N\jj\) only the last one matters: \(\partial N/\partial x=\partial M/\partial y\). (These are the conditions you solve when a question asks for the constants that make a field conservative.)

Reading the formula
\(\pd Nx=\pd My\)
a paddle wheel lying flat (axle along \(z\)): how fast the north push grows as you move east must equal how fast the east push grows as you move north. Then the two turning effects cancel and the wheel stays still.
\(\pd Py=\pd Nz\)
the same balance for a wheel with its axle along \(x\).
\(\pd Px=\pd Mz\)
the same balance for a wheel with its axle along \(y\).

Why they are needed: if \(\vb F=\nabla f\), then \(M=f_x\), \(N=f_y\), \(P=f_z\), and each pair is a pair of mixed second partial derivatives of \(f\), e.g. \(\partial N/\partial x=f_{yx}=f_{xy}=\partial M/\partial y\), which are equal (Chapter 1 §2).

How to apply the curl test to \(\vb F=M\ii+N\jj+P\kk\):

  1. Do: Name the components \(M,N,P\) and write down the region where \(\vb F\) is defined (look for points where a denominator vanishes or a root or log is undefined). The region decides whether there may be a hole for swirl to hide in.
  2. Do: Compute the three cross pairs \(\partial P/\partial y\) vs \(\partial N/\partial z\), \(\partial P/\partial x\) vs \(\partial M/\partial z\), \(\partial N/\partial x\) vs \(\partial M/\partial y\) (only the last for a 2D field). Each pair is the balance for a paddle wheel on one axis.
  3. Do: If any pair differs at any point, stop: \(\vb F\) is not conservative. A spinning wheel anywhere rules out a hill (§1).
  4. Do: If all pairs match and the region is simply connected, \(\vb F\) is conservative: go and build \(\phi\) (§1). No spin and no hidden hole: every loop has zero circulation.
  5. Do: If all pairs match but the region has a hole a loop can wrap round, the test gives no answer: compute \(\oint\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) round one loop that encircles the hole, or try to build \(\phi\). The only place swirl can hide is in the hole, so a loop round it is the one that can catch it.
Summary of the foregoing results \[\begin{aligned}&\vb F\text{ conservative on }\mathcal D\\ \Leftrightarrow\ &\vb F=\nabla f\text{ on }\mathcal D\\ \Leftrightarrow\ &\oint_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r=0\text{ over any}\\&\text{closed path }C\text{ in }\mathcal D,\end{aligned}\]

and these imply \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\nabla\times\vb F=\vb 0\). Also,

\[\begin{aligned}&\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\vb 0\text{ with }\mathcal D\text{ simply connected}\\ \Rightarrow\ &\vb F\text{ is conservative on }\mathcal D.\end{aligned}\]

Finally, it is important to realise that on a domain \(\mathcal D\) which is not simply connected, \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\vb 0\) does not provide a definite answer for the conservativeness of \(\vb F\): the field may or may not be conservative.

In words: conservative = has a potential = zero work round every closed loop. Zero curl always follows; zero curl implies the rest only on a region without holes.

Show the calculation: why "zero round every loop" is the same as "path independent"
  1. Take two paths \(C_1,C_2\) from \(A\) to \(B\). Go out along \(C_1\) and come back along \(C_2\) reversed: this is a closed loop \(C\). Why: joining the paths end to end.
  2. \(\oint_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r=\int_{C_1}\vb F\cdot d\vb r-\int_{C_2}\vb F\cdot d\vb r\). Why: reversing a path changes the sign of a work integral (Chapter 3b §3).
  3. So the loop integral is 0 exactly when the two path integrals are equal. Every loop through \(A\) and \(B\) arises this way, so "all loop integrals 0" ⇔ "all paths give the same work".
Going further · a field with a hole at the origin (exam type C3c.2)

The standard example of the final warning is the vortex field (the magnetic field outside a wire, Chapter 3a §5; the plughole picture above)

\[\vb F=\frac{-y\,\ii+x\,\jj}{x^2+y^2},\]

defined on the plane with the origin removed, \(\R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}\). That region is connected but not simply connected (a loop round the origin cannot be shrunk without passing through the missing point).

  • \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\vb 0\) at every point where \(\vb F\) is defined.
  • Yet round the unit circle anticlockwise, \(\oint_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r=2\pi\ne0\). So \(\vb F\) is not conservative on \(\R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}\).
  • Round any loop that does not enclose the origin, the integral is 0; anticlockwise round any loop that encloses the origin once, it is \(2\pi\).
  • On a smaller, simply connected region that avoids wrapping round the origin (e.g. the half-plane \(x\gt0\)) it is conservative, with potential \(\phi=\tan^{-1}(y/x)=\theta\), the polar angle. On the whole punctured plane \(\theta\) cannot be defined continuously: going once round the origin it increases by \(2\pi\), which is exactly the circulation.
  • Other fields on the same punctured plane, such as \(\dfrac{x\ii+y\jj}{x^2+y^2}\), have zero curl and are conservative there (a potential is \(\tfrac12\ln(x^2+y^2)\), defined everywhere except the origin). That is what "no definite answer" means.
Show the calculation: curl and circulation of the vortex field
  1. \(M=-\dfrac{y}{x^2+y^2}\), \(N=\dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2}\). Quotient rule: \(\pd Nx=\dfrac{(x^2+y^2)-2x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}=\dfrac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\).
  2. \(\pd My\) \(=-\dfrac{(x^2+y^2)-2y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\) \(=\dfrac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\). So \(\pd Nx-\pd My=0\) (and the \(\ii,\jj\) components are 0 since nothing depends on \(z\) and \(P=0\)).
  3. Unit circle anticlockwise: \(x=\cos t\), \(y=\sin t\), \(0\le t\le2\pi\); \(dx=-\sin t\,dt\), \(dy=\cos t\,dt\), \(x^2+y^2=1\).
  4. \(\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) \(=\dfrac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}\) \(=(\sin^2t+\cos^2t)\,dt\) \(=dt\). Why: substitute; \(\sin^2+\cos^2=1\).
  5. \(\oint_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r=\int_0^{2\pi}dt=2\pi\).
  6. Potential on \(x\gt0\): \(\pd{}{x}\tan^{-1}\dfrac yx\) \(=\dfrac{-y/x^2}{1+y^2/x^2}\) \(=\dfrac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\) \(=M\) and \(\pd{}{y}\tan^{-1}\dfrac yx\) \(=\dfrac{1/x}{1+y^2/x^2}\) \(=\dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2}\) \(=N\). ✓

In 3D the same formula (with \(P=0\)) defines a field circling the \(z\)-axis, on space without the \(z\)-axis: the region of the right-hand picture above. A horizontal loop round the axis gives \(2\pi\) at any height.

In words: zero curl means "no swirl at each point", but the hole is where all the swirl is hidden. A loop that goes round the hole picks it up; a loop that doesn't, doesn't.

The vortex field: zero curl, but circulation 2π round the origin

Arrows: \(\vb F=(-y\ii+x\jj)/(x^2+y^2)\), circling the origin anticlockwise and weakening with distance (the origin, an open circle, is not in the domain). What is this? (surface integral)

Picture a fishing net stretched into a curved shape, or a thin curved metal sheet, whose thickness (mass per square metre) changes from place to place. To find its total mass, cut it into tiny patches, multiply each patch's area by the density there, and add. That is a surface integral: the double-integral idea of Chapter 2 (adding up tiny tiles) carried over from a flat floor to a curved sheet.

A line integral generalises the ordinary definite integral (from a straight interval to a curve); in a similar way, a surface integral generalises a double integral (from a flat region to a curved surface).

Let \(\Sigma\) (capital sigma, the usual name for a surface) be a surface \(h(x,y,z)=0\) in space with a projection onto the \(xy\)-plane corresponding to the region \(R\) (the "shadow" of \(\Sigma\) if light shines straight down). We assume that \(\Sigma\) has a continuous gradient \(\nabla h\) which never vanishes on \(\Sigma\). This is sufficient to guarantee that \(\Sigma\) is smooth (it has a well-defined tangent plane, and so a normal direction, at every point: no corners or creases).

Suppose at each point on the surface a quantity is determined according to the continuous scalar function \(g(x,y,z)\). For example, \(g(x,y,z)\) may represent the mass density of the surface (mass per unit area of a thin curved sheet) and our task is to find the mass of the surface. Cut \(\Sigma\) into \(n\) small patches of areas \(\Delta S_1,\dots,\Delta S_n\), and pick a point \((x_i,y_i,z_i)\) on the \(i\)-th patch. The mass can be approximated by

\[\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(x_i,y_i,z_i)\,\Delta S_i.\]

In words: (density at a point of the patch) × (area of the patch), added over all patches.

A small patch \(\Delta S_i\) of the surface and its shadow in \(R\)

As before, we let \(n\to\infty\) with all \(\Delta S_i\) (and hence their projected subregions in \(R\)) shrink to zero; then we have

\[\iint_\Sigma g(x,y,z)\,dS=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(x_i,y_i,z_i)\,\Delta S_i.\]

In words: the surface integral of \(g\) over \(\Sigma\) = the limit of (value × patch area) summed over ever finer patches. \(dS\) stands for "a tiny piece of surface area".

What is this? (the stretch factor)

We know how to add up over a flat floor region (Chapter 2), so we work on the sheet's shadow instead. The catch is that a tilted roof tile is bigger than the shadow it casts on the floor, and the steeper the roof, the bigger the difference. So each tiny floor tile must be multiplied by a "stretch factor": how many times bigger the piece of sheet above it is. A flat, level sheet has factor 1; the steeper the sheet, the larger the factor.

Notice that if \(h(x,y,z)=0=z-f(x,y)\) (the surface is the graph of a function), a tiny patch \(dS\) sits above a tiny rectangle \(dx\,dy\) of \(R\), as in the picture below (a magnified version of the prism above).

The top face is tilted so that its unit normal \(\vb n\) makes the angle \(\gamma\) with \(\kk\). The tilt of a patch is measured by the angle \(\gamma\) between its normal (the arrow sticking straight out of it) and the vertical. We have

\[dx\,dy=dR=|\cos\gamma|\,dS\]

(absolute value is added since \(\gamma\) may be greater than \(\pi/2\), if \(\vb n\) points downward). It can be easily seen that

\[\vb n\cdot\kk=\cos\gamma,\]

where \(\vb n\) is the unit normal at \((x_k,y_k,z_k)\) (dot product of two unit vectors = cosine of the angle between them). The unit normal \(\vb n\) is given by (the gradient is normal to a level surface, Chapter 1 §6)

\[\vb n=\frac{\nabla h}{|\nabla h|}=\frac{-\pd fx\ii-\pd fy\jj+\kk}{\sqrt{\Big(\pd fx\Big)^2+\Big(\pd fy\Big)^2+1}}.\]

This implies that

\[\vb n\cdot\kk=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Big(\pd fx\Big)^2+\Big(\pd fy\Big)^2+1}}=\cos\gamma\]

and thus

\[\begin{aligned}dS&=\frac{1}{|\cos\gamma|}\,dx\,dy\\&=\sqrt{\Big(\pd fx\Big)^2+\Big(\pd fy\Big)^2+1}\;dx\,dy.\end{aligned}\]

Surface integral over \(z=f(x,y)\) (on the formula sheet) \[\begin{aligned}&\iint_\Sigma g(x,y,z)\,dS\\&=\iint_R g\big(x,y,f(x,y)\big)\sqrt{f_x^2+f_y^2+1}\;dx\,dy.\end{aligned}\]

In words: to integrate over the surface, integrate over its shadow \(R\) instead: replace \(z\) by \(f(x,y)\) in \(g\), and multiply by the "stretch factor" \(\sqrt{f_x^2+f_y^2+1}\) (how much bigger each piece of surface is than its shadow).

Show the calculation: from \(\nabla h\) to the stretch factor
  1. \(h=z-f(x,y)\), so \(\nabla h=-f_x\,\ii-f_y\,\jj+1\,\kk\). Why: \(\partial h/\partial z=1\), and \(\partial h/\partial x=-f_x\), \(\partial h/\partial y=-f_y\).
  2. \(|\nabla h|=\sqrt{f_x^2+f_y^2+1}\), never 0, so \(\vb n=\nabla h/|\nabla h|\) is defined. Its \(\kk\)-component is positive: it points upward.
  3. \(\vb n\cdot\kk\) picks out the \(\kk\)-component: \(\dfrac1{\sqrt{f_x^2+f_y^2+1}}\). Why: \(\ii\cdot\kk=\jj\cdot\kk=0\), \(\kk\cdot\kk=1\).
  4. Since \(|\vb n|=|\kk|=1\), \(\vb n\cdot\kk=\cos\gamma\). Why: \(\vb a\cdot\vb b=|\vb a||\vb b|\cos(\text{angle})\).
  5. Projection: tilt a rectangle of sides \(\ell_1,\ell_2\) about the side \(\ell_2\) by angle \(\gamma\); its shadow has sides \(\ell_1\cos\gamma,\ \ell_2\). So shadow area = \(|\cos\gamma|\times\) area, i.e. \(dx\,dy=|\cos\gamma|\,dS\). Any small patch can be cut into such rectangles.
  6. Divide: \(dS=\dfrac{dx\,dy}{|\cos\gamma|}=\sqrt{f_x^2+f_y^2+1}\,dx\,dy\).

How to set up a surface integral by projection onto the \(xy\)-plane:

  1. Do: Write the surface as a height over the floor, \(z=f(x,y)\) (choose the right branch, e.g. the upper half of a cone or sphere), or as \(h(x,y,z)=0\) if \(z\) cannot be isolated. The method works on the shadow, so each point of the sheet must sit above exactly one floor point.
  2. Do: Find the shadow \(R\): project the edges of \(\Sigma\) straight down (e.g. set \(z\) equal to the bounding heights and read off the curves in \(x,y\)). Sketch it. \(R\) is the floor region you will actually integrate over.
  3. Do: Compute \(f_x\), \(f_y\) and the stretch factor \(\sqrt{f_x^2+f_y^2+1}\) (or \(|\nabla h|/|h_z|\)), and simplify it using the surface's equation. This converts each floor tile into the area of the sheet above it.
  4. Do: In \(g\), replace \(z\) by \(f(x,y)\). You only ever evaluate \(g\) on the sheet, where the height is \(f(x,y)\).
  5. Do: Evaluate the double integral over \(R\), choosing coordinates that suit its shape (polar for discs and rings, Chapter 2 §2). From here on it is an ordinary Chapter 2 problem: adding up over flat tiles.

4Orientation and flux integrals ~18 min

We have seen earlier (Chapter 3b) that the line integral \(\int_Cf(x,y,z)\,ds\) of a function \(f\) over a curve \(C\) was first defined; then another type of line integral relating to a vector field \(\vb F\), given by \(\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\), was subsequently defined. Now that we have defined a form of surface integral \(\iint_\Sigma g(x,y,z)\,dS\) of a function \(g\) over a surface, we shall define a type of surface integral relating to a vector field. In order to do that, we need to introduce the idea of orientable surfaces: just as a work integral needs a direction of travel along the curve, a flux integral needs a chosen "positive side" of the surface.

What is this? (orientation)

Picture a fishing net stretched across a river. Before you can say "this much water goes through the net each second", you must agree which way counts as positive: downstream through the net, or upstream. Choosing an orientation means painting one face of the net red and declaring "water coming out of the red face counts as plus". A surface is orientable if this painting can be done without the red paint ever having to jump to the other face.

Definition (orientable surface)

We call a smooth surface \(\Sigma\) orientable or two-sided if it is possible to define a field of unit normal vectors \(\vb n\) on \(\Sigma\) that varies continuously with position.

In words: you can stick a unit arrow out of every point, all on "the same side", with no sudden flips. Such a surface has two choices of normal field (one for each side).

Two-sided surfaces: either side can be chosen

Drag to rotate. A wavy sheet with its unit normals (red) all on the upper side, or all on the lower side (blue): either choice varies continuously, so the sheet is orientable. The folded sheet has a nonsmooth boundary between its two faces (the purple crease, where there is no tangent plane and so no normal); on each flat face the normals are constant, and they can still be chosen consistently on one side of the whole sheet, so it is orientable too (piecewise smooth).

The little arrows are the paint in the picture: at every point of the net the unit normal \(\vb n\) sticks out of the red face, at right angles to the net. Spheres and other smooth closed surfaces in space (smooth surfaces that enclose solids) are orientable. By convention, we choose \(\vb n\) on a closed surface to point outward (for a closed bag, "positive" means "leaving the bag"). Once \(\vb n\) has been chosen, we say that we have oriented the surface. The vector \(\vb n\) at any point is called the positive direction at that point.

How do you actually get the normal you want? The exam procedure:

  1. Do: read off which side the question wants: "upward", "outward", "away from the \(z\)-axis", or, for a closed surface with nothing said, outward. This is deciding which face of the net is painted red, so that "through the net" has a sign.
  2. Do: write the surface as a level surface \(h(x,y,z)=0\) (for \(z=f(x,y)\) take \(h=z-f(x,y)\), or \(h=f(x,y)-z\)). The gradient of \(h\) is at right angles to its level surfaces (Chapter 1), so it gives an arrow sticking straight out of the net.
  3. Do: compute \(\nabla h\) and divide by its length: \(\vb n=\nabla h/|\nabla h|\). Only the direction matters for "straight through"; dividing by the length makes the arrow one unit long, so it measures direction and nothing else.
  4. Do: test the sign at one convenient point (for "upward", is the \(\kk\)-component positive? for "outward", does the arrow point away from the solid?). If it is the wrong side, use \(-\vb n\). \(\nabla h\) and \(-\nabla h\) are both perpendicular to the net; this step makes sure the arrows stick out of the red face, not the blue one.

Shapes worth remembering: on a sphere of radius \(a\) centred at the origin, the outward normal is \((x\ii+y\jj+z\kk)/a\); on the side of the cylinder \(x^2+y^2=a^2\) it is \((x\ii+y\jj)/a\); on a horizontal disc it is \(\pm\kk\).

What is this? (one-sided surface)

Some surfaces cannot be painted on one face only. If you start painting red and keep going, you come back to where you started on the "other" face, and the whole surface ends up red. Such a surface has no "through", so no flux can be defined for it.

A famous non-orientable or one-sided surface is given by the Möbius strip: take a long strip of paper, give one end a half twist, and glue the ends together.

The orientation of the boundary curve

What is this? (boundary orientation)

An open net has a rim, like the hoop of a butterfly net. Once you have painted the red face, there is one natural direction to walk round the rim: walk with your head out of the red face and the net on your left. Fixing the rim direction from the red face (and the red face from the rim direction) is what lets Stokes' theorem in §6 compare "swirl round the rim" with "swirl over the net".

We have discussed the orientation of a closed surface above (outward). For an open surface \(\Sigma\) (one with an edge) with boundary curve \(C\) (the edge), we introduce a convention which makes the direction of \(C\) consistent with the choice of \(\vb n\). The following rule is generally used:

Convention (positive orientation of the boundary)

Imagine walking along the curve \(C\) in the direction of its orientation, but standing perpendicular to the surface (head in the direction of \(\vb n\)) and on the side of the surface such that nearby points on the surface are on your left.

In words (right-hand rule): curl the fingers of your right hand along \(C\); your thumb points to the side of \(\Sigma\) where \(\vb n\) is. For a surface with upward normal, \(C\) runs anticlockwise when seen from above.

  1. Do: choose (or read off) the normal \(\vb n\) on \(\Sigma\). The red face has to be fixed first; the rim direction is derived from it.
  2. Do: point your right thumb along \(\vb n\) and let your fingers curl; that is the direction of \(C\). Equivalently, stand on the red face at the rim and walk with the net on your left. Both are the same agreement between face and rim; use whichever you find easier to picture.
  3. Do: check with a picture from the end of \(\vb n\): looking down onto the red face, \(C\) should run anticlockwise. If the normal is reversed, reverse \(C\). Turning the net over swaps red and blue, so the rim must be walked the other way to stay consistent.

Flux integrals

What is this? (flux)

Back to the net in the river. The flux is how much water passes through the net per second, counted positive when it comes out of the red face. Only the part of the current that goes straight through the net matters: water sliding along the net never gets through, and a net held edge-on to the current catches nothing. So at each little square of mesh you ask "how fast is the water going straight through here?", multiply by the size of the square, and add up over the whole net.

Definition (flux)

If \(\vb F\) is a continuous vector field defined over an oriented surface \(\Sigma\), we call the integral over \(\Sigma\) of \(\vb F\cdot\vb n\), the scalar component of \(\vb F\) in the direction of \(\vb n\), the flux of \(\vb F\) across \(\Sigma\) in the positive direction. That is,

\[\text{flux}=\iint_\Sigma\vb F\cdot\vb n\,dS.\]

In words: at each point, keep only the part of \(\vb F\) that goes straight through the surface (\(\vb F\cdot\vb n\)), and add it up over the surface. Flow along the surface contributes nothing; flow against \(\vb n\) counts negative.

Reading the formula
\(\vb F\)
the current of the river at a point of the net (speed and direction).
\(\vb n\)
the unit arrow sticking straight out of the red face there.
\(\vb F\cdot\vb n\)
the part of the current going straight through the net: positive if out of the red face, zero if the water slides along, negative if it goes back through.
\(dS\)
the area of one tiny square of mesh, so \(\vb F\cdot\vb n\,dS\) is the water through that square per second.
\(\iint_\Sigma\)
add over every square of the net: the net flow through the whole surface.

If \(\vb F\) is the velocity field of a 3D fluid flow, then the flux is the net rate at which the fluid is crossing \(\Sigma\) in the positive direction (volume per unit time).

Show the calculation: why \(\vb v\cdot\vb n\,dS\) is the volume crossing a patch per unit time
  1. In a short time \(\delta t\), the fluid that crosses a small flat patch of area \(dS\) is the fluid that was within distance \(|\vb v|\delta t\) behind it, along \(\vb v\): a slanted box (a parallelepiped) with base \(dS\) and slant edge \(\vb v\,\delta t\).
  2. Its volume = base × perpendicular height = \(dS\times(\vb v\,\delta t\cdot\vb n)\). Why: the height measured perpendicular to the base is the component of the slant edge along the unit normal.
  3. Per unit time: \(\vb v\cdot\vb n\,dS\). It is negative when the flow crosses against \(\vb n\), and zero when \(\vb v\) is tangent to the patch (fluid slides along it).
  4. Adding over all patches gives \(\iint_\Sigma\vb v\cdot\vb n\,dS\) = net volume per unit time. With density \(\rho\), \(\iint\rho\vb v\cdot\vb n\,dS\) = mass per unit time.

How to compute a flux integral over a surface given as \(z=f(x,y)\) (the common exam case):

  1. Do: if \(\Sigma\) is a closed surface and \(\operatorname{div}\vb F\) is simple, stop and use Gauss' theorem (§5) instead. For a closed bag, counting what leaves through the skin can be swapped for adding up the taps inside, which is usually far easier.
  2. Do: find the unit normal \(\vb n\) on the side asked for (procedure above). This fixes which face of the net is red, i.e. which way through counts as plus.
  3. Do: compute \(\vb F\cdot\vb n\) and replace \(z\) by \(f(x,y)\). This is "how fast the water goes straight through" at each mesh square; on the net, \(z\) is not free but is the height of the net.
  4. Do: write \(dS=\sqrt{f_x^2+f_y^2+1}\,dx\,dy\) (§3); the square root cancels with the one in \(\vb n\). A tilted mesh square is bigger than its shadow on the floor; the root is that stretch factor, and the same tilt appears in \(\vb n\), which is why they cancel.
  5. Do: find the shadow \(R\) of \(\Sigma\) in the \(xy\)-plane and integrate over it (polar coordinates if \(R\) is a disc). Adding over every mesh square of the net is the same as adding over every point of its shadow.
  6. Do: read the sign. Positive: more water leaves the red face than enters it. Negative: the net flow is the other way.
Example 3.8.2

Suppose \(\Sigma\) is the part of the paraboloid \(z=1-x^2-y^2\) that lies above the \(xy\)-plane and is oriented by the normal directed upward. Assume that the velocity of a fluid with constant density \(\rho\) is given by

\[\vb v(x,y,z)=x\,\ii+y\,\jj+2z\,\kk.\]

Determine the rate of mass flow through \(\Sigma\) in the direction of \(\vb n\).

The required level surface is given by \(h(x,y,z)=z+x^2+y^2-1=0\).Writing the dome as a level surface lets the gradient supply the arrow straight out of the net.

The total flux that passes through the parabolic surface is given by

\[\iint_\Sigma\rho\,\vb v\cdot\vb n\,dS,\]

where

\[\vb n=\frac{\nabla h}{|\nabla h|}=\frac{2x\,\ii+2y\,\jj+\kk}{\sqrt{(2x)^2+(2y)^2+1}}.\]

This is the required upward unit normal (its \(\kk\)-component is positive), thus the total flux is given byThe sign check: upward means the red face is the top of the dome, and a positive \(\kk\)-part confirms the arrows stick out of it.

\[\begin{aligned}&\rho\iint_\Sigma\frac{2x^2+2y^2+2z}{\sqrt{4x^2+4y^2+1}}\,dS\\&=\rho\iint_\Sigma\frac{2}{\sqrt{4x^2+4y^2+1}}\,dS\\&=2\rho\iint_{\Sigma^*}dx\,dy=2\pi\rho,\end{aligned}\]

where \(\Sigma^*\) is the projected region (the unit circle with centre at the origin, i.e. the disc \(x^2+y^2\le1\), of area \(\pi\)).On the dome the straight-through flow per unit area of shadow is the constant 2, so the total is just 2 times the area of the shadow.

Show the calculation: the three equalities
  1. \(\vb v\cdot\nabla h\) \(=x(2x)+y(2y)+2z(1)\) \(=2x^2+2y^2+2z\).
  2. On \(\Sigma\), \(z=1-x^2-y^2\), so \(2x^2+2y^2+2z\) \(=2x^2+2y^2+2-2x^2-2y^2\) \(=2\). Why: we may use the equation of the surface inside a surface integral.
  3. \(dS=\sqrt{f_x^2+f_y^2+1}\,dx\,dy\) with \(f=1-x^2-y^2\): \(f_x=-2x\), \(f_y=-2y\), so \(dS=\sqrt{4x^2+4y^2+1}\,dx\,dy\) (§3).
  4. The square roots cancel: \(\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{4x^2+4y^2+1}}\) \(\cdot\sqrt{4x^2+4y^2+1}\,dx\,dy\) \(=2\,dx\,dy\).
  5. The shadow of \(\Sigma\) (where \(z\ge0\)) is \(x^2+y^2\le1\), area \(\pi\): flux \(=\rho\cdot2\cdot\pi=2\pi\rho\).

In words: mass per unit time through the dome = density × (net volume per unit time) = \(2\pi\rho\).

Flux in physics

The following are further examples relating to the flux integral; in each, \(\Sigma\) is a surface with chosen normal \(\vb n\).

  1. If \(\vb E\) is the electric field, then over a closed surface \(\Sigma\) \[\iint_\Sigma\vb E\cdot\vb n\,dS=\frac{q}{\epsilon_0}\] gives the total charge \(q\) within \(\Sigma\), where \(\epsilon_0\) is the permittivity of empty space (Gauss' law of electrostatics). In words: electric flux out of a closed surface = enclosed charge ÷ \(\epsilon_0\).
  2. If \(\vb v\) is the velocity field of a fluid, \(\iint_\Sigma\vb v\cdot\vb n\,dS\) represents the total volume of fluid crossing \(\Sigma\) in a unit time. Replacing \(\vb v\) by \(\rho\vb v\), where \(\rho\) is the density, gives the mass flux (mass per unit time).
  3. If \(\vb J\) is an electric current density vector (current per unit cross-sectional area), \(\iint_\Sigma\vb J\cdot\vb n\,dS\) represents the rate at which electric charge crosses \(\Sigma\) (the current through \(\Sigma\)).
  4. If \(\vb q\) is a heat conduction vector (heat flow per unit area per unit time), \(\iint_\Sigma\vb q\cdot\vb n\,dS\) represents the rate at which heat flows through \(\Sigma\).

In passing, we remark that in certain texts the surface integral may be written as

\[\iint_\Sigma\vb F\cdot d\vb S,\]

where \(d\vb S=\vb n\,dS\) is a vector differential with magnitude \(dS\) and direction normal to the element of surface \(dS\). In the picture, \(d\vb S\) is one mesh square carried as an arrow: its length is the square's area and it points out of the red face.

5Gauss' divergence theorem ~14 min

What is this? (Gauss' theorem)

Think of a closed bag held under water, with a few taps (sources) and drains (sinks) inside it. There are two ways to find how much water leaves the bag each second: stand outside and count what comes out through every bit of the skin, or go inside and add up what every tap pours in minus what every drain swallows. Water cannot pile up or vanish, so the two counts must agree. Gauss' theorem says exactly this: the flux out through a closed surface equals the total divergence (tap strength) inside.

Consider a solid region \(\mathcal D\) with no "holes" or "bubbles" in it. It has an interior and an exterior separated by a boundary surface \(\Sigma\), which is part of the region (a closed surface, like the skin of a potato). By convention, the orientation of the surface \(\Sigma\) is chosen such that its normals are directed outward from \(\mathcal D\).

For a given vector field \(\vb F\), we would like to relate the total flux across \(\Sigma\),

\[\iint_\Sigma\vb F\cdot\vb n\,dS,\]

with the divergence of \(\vb F\) at all the points within \(\mathcal D\). Recall from Chapter 3a §4 that \(\operatorname{div}\vb F\) at a point is the net outflow per unit volume from a tiny box around that point. Let \(\vb F=M\ii+N\jj+P\kk\).

Theorem 3.9.1 (Divergence (Gauss') Theorem) \[\begin{aligned}&\iint_\Sigma\vb F\cdot\vb n\,dS\\&=\iint_\Sigma(M\ii+N\jj+P\kk)\cdot\vb n\,dS\\&=\iiint_{\mathcal D}\Big(\pd Mx+\pd Ny+\pd Pz\Big)dV\\&=\iiint_{\mathcal D}\operatorname{div}\vb F\,dV.\end{aligned}\]

In words: total outflow through the closed skin = total of the sources inside (divergence added up over the volume). Notice that the divergence theorem relates a flux integral (over a surface) with a divergence integral (over a volume). It is on the December formula sheet.

Reading the formula
\(\Sigma\) closed, \(\vb n\) outward
the whole skin of the bag, with "leaving the bag" counted as plus.
\(\iint_\Sigma\vb F\cdot\vb n\,dS\)
the outside count: water leaving through every patch of skin per second.
\(\operatorname{div}\vb F\)
tap strength at a point: water poured in per second per unit volume (negative at a drain).
\(\iiint_{\mathcal D}\dots\,dV\)
the inside count: add the tap strength over every tiny box of the solid.
\(=\)
nothing piles up inside, so what the taps pour in is what leaves through the skin.

How to use Gauss to replace a surface integral (the exam procedure):

  1. Do: check that \(\Sigma\) is closed (it encloses a solid \(\mathcal D\)) and that the normals are outward; if the question wants inward normals, change the sign at the end. If \(\Sigma\) is open, close it first (procedure in the Beyond box at the end of this section). The theorem compares the skin of a sealed bag with its inside; a bag with a hole lets water escape uncounted.
  2. Do: check that \(\vb F\) is smooth everywhere inside \(\mathcal D\) (no division by zero, no \(\tan\) blowing up). A hidden point source inside, where the field is undefined, would pour in water that the divergence never records.
  3. Do: compute \(\operatorname{div}\vb F=\pd Mx+\pd Ny+\pd Pz\). Awkward terms often drop out. This is the tap strength at each point; a term that does not contain the variable you differentiate by adds no tap.
  4. Do: set up \(\iiint_{\mathcal D}\operatorname{div}\vb F\,dV\) in the coordinates that fit the solid (cylindrical for cylinders, spherical for balls, Chapter 2). If the divergence is a constant \(c\), the answer is \(c\times\) volume. This is the inside count: add the taps over every tiny box of the solid.
  5. Do: evaluate; the result is the outward flux. By the theorem, the inside count equals the outside count.

Solids with holes

What is this? (solids with holes)

Now the bag has an air bubble sealed inside it, so the solid is the water between the outer skin and the bubble. The solid's boundary is both skins. Water leaving the solid can escape through the outer skin into the world, or through the bubble's skin into the bubble; both count as "leaving", so on the bubble the normal points into the bubble.

We have explicitly assumed earlier that the region \(\mathcal D\) has no holes in it. In fact, it can be shown that the theorem also holds for a solid with holes, like a chunk of Swiss cheese, provided we always require \(\vb n\) to point away from the interior of the solid, i.e. on the surface of a hole, \(\vb n\) points into the hole.

Going further · why Gauss' theorem is true (the cancelling-faces argument)

Think of the bag as built from many tiny bags glued together.

Chop \(\mathcal D\) into many tiny boxes. For each box, the net outflow \(\approx\operatorname{div}\vb F\times(\text{box volume})\) (that is what divergence means). Add up the outflows of all the boxes. Every inside face is shared by two neighbouring boxes, whose outward normals on it are opposite, so the flow out of one is flow into the other: these contributions cancel. Only the faces on the outer skin \(\Sigma\) survive. So

\[\begin{aligned}\iint_\Sigma\vb F\cdot\vb n\,dS&=\sum_{\text{boxes}}(\text{outflow})\\&\approx\sum\operatorname{div}\vb F\,\Delta V\to\iiint_{\mathcal D}\operatorname{div}\vb F\,dV.\end{aligned}\]

6Stokes' theorem ~11 min

What is this? (Stokes' theorem)

Hold a butterfly net on a round hoop in flowing water. Imagine a tiny paddle wheel in every patch of the net, each lying flat in the net, and add up how hard the water spins them all. Stokes' theorem says this total equals the push the water gives you along your path as you walk once round the hoop. Inside the net, neighbouring paddle wheels push opposite ways along their shared edges and cancel, so only the swirl along the rim is left. That is why the bag of the net can be any shape (flat, domed, baggy): only the hoop matters.

The precise setting. Let \(\Sigma\) (the net) be an oriented surface with normal \(\vb n\) and finite area. Assume that \(\Sigma\) is bounded by a closed, piecewise smooth curve \(C\) (the hoop) (smooth except possibly at finitely many corners) with orientation consistent with the surface normals (the walking rule of §4: \(\vb n\) is your head, the surface is on your left). Let \(\vb F=M\ii+N\jj+P\kk\) be a continuous vector field defined on \(\Sigma\) and suppose \(\vb F\) has continuous partial derivatives at each nonboundary point of \(\Sigma\).

Theorem 3.10.1 (Stokes' Theorem) \[\iint_\Sigma\operatorname{curl}\vb F\cdot\vb n\,dS=\oint_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r.\]

In words: the flux of the curl through the surface = the circulation of \(\vb F\) round its edge. Notice that Stokes' Theorem relates a flux integral (of \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F\), over a surface) with a line integral (of \(\vb F\), round a closed curve). It is on the December formula sheet.

Reading the formula
\(\operatorname{curl}\vb F\cdot\vb n\)
how fast a tiny paddle wheel lying in the net (axle along \(\vb n\)) is spun: the swirl at that spot, measured across the net.
\(dS\)
the area of that patch. Spin per unit area × area = the circulation round the patch's own tiny loop.
\(\iint_\Sigma\)
add over every patch of the net; the shared inside edges cancel.
\(\oint_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\)
walk once round the hoop and total the push of the flow along your path (the circulation of Chapter 3b).
\(\vb n\) and the direction of \(C\)
tied by the right-hand rule: thumb along \(\vb n\), fingers curl the way you walk. Reverse either one and the answer changes sign.

Why it is plausible: \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F\cdot\vb n\) is the circulation per unit area round a tiny loop on the surface (Chapter 3a §5). Cover \(\Sigma\) with tiny loops all turning the same way: each inside edge is travelled twice in opposite directions and cancels (as for Gauss' theorem in §5), leaving only \(C\). A striking consequence: any two surfaces with the same boundary \(C\) (and matching orientation) have the same flux of curl.

How to use Stokes' theorem (the exam procedure):

  1. Do: decide which side to compute. A line integral round an awkward curve (an intersection of two surfaces, or many pieces), or "use Stokes": compute the surface side. A flux of curl through a curvy surface: compute the line side, or swap to an easier surface with the same edge. Both sides are the same number; you only choose between walking round the hoop and adding up paddle-wheel spins over a net.
  2. Do: find the edge \(C\) exactly. If it is an intersection, solve the two equations together and look at its shadow on the \(xy\)-plane. Everything hangs on the hoop: the net you choose must end exactly on it.
  3. Do: pick a surface \(\Sigma\) whose edge is \(C\), as flat as possible: a piece of a plane, a disc, or a graph \(z=f(x,y)\). Every net on the same hoop catches the same total spin, so take the one whose normal and shadow are simplest.
  4. Do: orient with the right-hand rule: thumb along \(\vb n\), fingers curl in the direction of \(C\). If no direction is given, choose one and say so. A paddle wheel counts as positive when it turns the way your fingers curl; flipping the net or the walk flips the sign.
  5. Do: compute \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F\), then \(\vb n\,dS\): for \(z=f(x,y)\) with upward normal, \((-f_x\ii-f_y\jj+\kk)\,dx\,dy\); for a horizontal flat piece, \(\kk\,dx\,dy\). The curl is the spin axis and rate at each point; \(\vb n\,dS\) is the patch's own axis times its area. Their dot product is how much of the spin that patch catches.
  6. Do: take the dot product and integrate over the shadow \(R\) (polar coordinates for circles); look for symmetry that makes the answer 0. This is adding up the spins of all the patches of the net.
  7. Do: check the conditions: \(\vb F\) and its partial derivatives are continuous on the whole of \(\Sigma\). A point where \(\vb F\) blows up is a hole in the net where the spin cannot be counted (see the last box of this section).

Why the curl test of §2 works. If \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\vb 0\) on a simply connected region, every closed curve \(C\) in it can be shrunk to a point, sweeping out a surface \(\Sigma\) inside the region with edge \(C\). By Stokes, \(\oint_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r=\iint_\Sigma\vb 0\cdot\vb n\,dS=0\), so all loop integrals vanish and \(\vb F\) is conservative.

"Does this contradict Stokes' theorem?" For the vortex field \(\vb F=\dfrac{-y\ii+x\jj}{x^2+y^2}\) of §2, \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\vb 0\) but \(\oint\vb F\cdot d\vb r=2\pi\) round the unit circle. No contradiction: every surface with the unit circle as its edge (the flat disc, a dome, …) must cross the \(z\)-axis, and in particular the flat disc contains the origin, where \(\vb F\) is not defined (not continuous, no partial derivatives). The hypotheses of Stokes' theorem fail, so it does not apply. 7Application: Ampère's law and Maxwell's equations ~7 min

What is this? (Ampère's law)

An electric current in a wire makes a magnetic field that circles round the wire, like water swirling round a plughole. Ampère's law is Stokes' theorem in physics clothes. Walk round any loop and total the push of the magnetic field along your path: you get a fixed constant times the current flowing through any net stretched on that loop. The current is the "water through the fishing net" (a flux, §4), and the magnetic swirl round the hoop measures it.

In electromagnetic theory, Stokes' Theorem gives Ampère's Law which states that, in the absence of electric field (more precisely: when no electric field is changing with time, e.g. steady currents),

\[\oint_C\vb B\cdot d\vb r=\frac{1}{c^2\epsilon_0}\iint_\Sigma\vb J\cdot\vb n\,dS,\]

where \(\vb B\) is the magnetic field, \(\vb J\) is the current density (current per unit cross-sectional area, a vector pointing along the flow of charge), \(c\) is the speed of light and \(\epsilon_0\) the permittivity of empty space; \(\Sigma\) is any surface whose edge is the loop \(C\), oriented to match \(C\). (The constant \(\frac1{c^2\epsilon_0}\) is the permeability of empty space, usually written \(\mu_0\).)

In words: the circulation of the magnetic field round a loop = \(\mu_0\) × the electric current passing through the loop (the flux of \(\vb J\), §4).

Reading the formula
\(\oint_C\vb B\cdot d\vb r\)
the swirl of the magnetic field round the hoop \(C\): its total push along your walk.
\(\iint_\Sigma\vb J\cdot\vb n\,dS\)
the current through the net: how much charge crosses it per second (flux of the current density).
\(\dfrac1{c^2\epsilon_0}=\mu_0\)
a constant of nature converting "current through" into "swirl round".
any \(\Sigma\) with edge \(C\)
as in Stokes, only the hoop matters: every net on it catches the same current.
What is this? (Maxwell's equation for curl B)

Now shrink the hoop to a tiny loop round one point. The law about a whole loop becomes a law about a single point: a tiny paddle wheel placed in the magnetic field spins only where current is flowing, and spins faster where the current is denser. This point-by-point version is one of Maxwell's equations.

In fact, one of Maxwell's equations (the four equations of electromagnetism) is given by

\[\operatorname{curl}\vb B=\frac{1}{c^2\epsilon_0}\,\vb J.\]

In words: at each point, the swirl of the magnetic field is proportional to the current density there; where no current flows, \(\vb B\) is irrotational.

Reading the formula
\(\operatorname{curl}\vb B\)
the paddle-wheel spin of the magnetic field at one point (axis and rate).
\(\vb J\)
the current density at that point: which way charge flows there, and how densely.
proportional

Show the calculation: from Ampère's law to \(\operatorname{curl}\vb B=\vb J/(c^2\epsilon_0)\) using Stokes
  1. Do: apply Stokes to the left side of Ampère's law: \(\oint_C\vb B\cdot d\vb r=\iint_\Sigma\operatorname{curl}\vb B\cdot\vb n\,dS\). Swirl round the hoop = total paddle-wheel spin over the net.
  2. Do: conclude \(\iint_\Sigma\Big(\operatorname{curl}\vb B-\dfrac{1}{c^2\epsilon_0}\vb J\Big)\cdot\vb n\,dS=0\) for every surface \(\Sigma\) (every loop \(C\)). Subtract the right side of Ampère's law: the "spin minus current" adds up to nothing over every possible net.
  3. Do: take \(\Sigma\) a tiny flat disc at a point \(P\) with normal \(\vb n\). The integral ≈ \(\Big(\operatorname{curl}\vb B-\dfrac{\vb J}{c^2\epsilon_0}\Big)\cdot\vb n\times(\text{area})=0\), so the component of \(\operatorname{curl}\vb B-\vb J/(c^2\epsilon_0)\) along \(\vb n\) is 0 at \(P\). On a tiny disc the continuous integrand is nearly constant.
  4. Do: choose \(\vb n=\ii,\jj,\kk\) in turn: all three components vanish: \(\operatorname{curl}\vb B=\vb J/(c^2\epsilon_0)\) at every point. A tiny paddle wheel can be turned to face any way; if it shows zero difference for every axis, the two vectors are equal.

Going further · the other Maxwell equations, and Gauss' law

Gauss' law of electrostatics (§4) says that for every closed surface, \(\iint_\Sigma\vb E\cdot\vb n\,dS=q/\epsilon_0\), where \(q=\iiint_{\mathcal D}\rho_e\,dV\) is the charge inside (\(\rho_e\) = charge per unit volume). By Gauss' divergence theorem the left side is \(\iiint_{\mathcal D}\operatorname{div}\vb E\,dV\); as this holds for every region, exactly as in the tab above,

\[\operatorname{div}\vb E=\frac{\rho_e}{\epsilon_0}.\]

In words: electric field lines start on positive charge and end on negative charge (sources and sinks of \(\vb E\)).

The full set, in empty space with charges and currents, is: \(\operatorname{div}\vb E=\rho_e/\epsilon_0\); \(\operatorname{div}\vb B=0\) (no magnetic "charges"); \(\operatorname{curl}\vb E=-\partial\vb B/\partial t\) (Faraday's law of induction); \(\operatorname{curl}\vb B=\mu_0\vb J+\frac{1}{c^2}\partial\vb E/\partial t\) (Ampère's law with Maxwell's correction; the version above is the case of an unchanging \(\vb E\)). Not examined in MECH2407, but a good illustration of why div, curl, Gauss and Stokes matter.

What is this? (Green's theorem)

Lay the fishing net flat on the floor (the \(xy\)-plane), with the water flowing across the floor. Every tiny paddle wheel in it now has an upright axle, so its spin is just a number: the 2D curl. Green's theorem is Stokes' theorem for this flat net: add up the spins of all the paddle wheels in the region, and you get the swirl you feel walking once round its edge anticlockwise, with the region on your left.

Green's theorem (tangential form)

Let \(R\) be a region in the \(xy\)-plane bounded by a piecewise smooth simple closed curve \(C\) (a loop that does not cross itself), traversed anticlockwise (so that \(R\) is on your left). If \(M(x,y)\) and \(N(x,y)\) have continuous partial derivatives on \(R\) (including \(C\)), then

\[\oint_CM\,dx+N\,dy=\iint_R\Big(\pd Nx-\pd My\Big)\,dx\,dy.\]

In words: circulation round the edge of a flat region = the total of the 2D curl \(N_x-M_y\) over the region.

Reading the formula
\(M\,dx+N\,dy\)
the push of the flow along each small step of your walk round the edge (\(\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) with no \(dz\), since you stay on the floor).
\(\pd Nx\)
the northward flow is stronger on the east side of a paddle wheel than on its west side: this turns it anticlockwise.
\(-\pd My\)
the eastward flow is stronger on the north side than on the south side: this turns it clockwise, hence the minus.
\(dx\,dy\)
one floor tile; \(\iint_R\) adds the spins of all tiles.
anticlockwise
the right-hand rule with the thumb pointing up (\(\vb n=\kk\)).
Show the calculation: Green's theorem is Stokes' theorem with \(\Sigma=R\) and \(\vb n=\kk\)
  1. Take \(\vb F=M(x,y)\,\ii+N(x,y)\,\jj+0\,\kk\) and \(\Sigma=R\), a flat piece of the \(xy\)-plane with normal \(\vb n=\kk\), \(dS=dx\,dy\).
  2. \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F\) \(=\big(0-0\big)\ii+\big(0-0\big)\jj+\Big(\pd Nx-\pd My\Big)\kk\). Why: \(P=0\) and \(M,N\) do not depend on \(z\).
  3. \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F\cdot\kk\,dS=\Big(\pd Nx-\pd My\Big)dx\,dy\).
  4. \(\vb F\cdot d\vb r=M\,dx+N\,dy\) (as \(dz=0\) in the plane).
  5. The upward normal \(\kk\) matches anticlockwise travel (right-hand rule, §4). Stokes' theorem then reads exactly as Green's theorem.
Worked example

Verify Green's theorem for \(\oint_Cxy\,dx+x^2\,dy\), where \(C\) is the boundary of the unit square \(0\le x\le1\), \(0\le y\le1\), anticlockwise.

Double integral. \(M=xy\), \(N=x^2\): \(\pd Nx-\pd My=2x-x=x\), and \(\int_0^1\!\int_0^1x\,dx\,dy=\tfrac12\).This is the total paddle-wheel spin over the square: the wheels spin faster towards the east side, where \(x\) is larger.

Line integral, side by side (figure below):

  • Bottom, \(y=0\), \(x:0\to1\): \(dy=0\), \(M=0\): contributes 0.
  • Right, \(x=1\), \(y:0\to1\): \(dx=0\), \(N=1\): \(\int_0^11\,dy=1\).
  • Top, \(y=1\), \(x:1\to0\): \(dy=0\), \(M=x\): \(\int_1^0x\,dx=-\tfrac12\). Along the top you walk west, against the eastward flow, so this side pushes you backwards: the limits run from 1 to 0.
  • Left, \(x=0\), \(y:1\to0\): \(dx=0\), \(N=0\): contributes 0.

Total \(0+1-\tfrac12+0=\tfrac12\). ✓ Both sides agree.

In words: on horizontal sides only the \(dx\) term survives, on vertical sides only the \(dy\) term; keep the direction of travel in the limits.

Green's theorem: circulation round \(C\) = total 2D curl inside

Arrows: the field \(\vb F=M\ii+N\jj\). The region \(R\) is shaded by the value of the 2D curl \(N_x-M_y\) (darker = larger; white where it is near 0), and its boundary \(C\) runs anticlockwise (arrows). The readout computes both sides numerically. For the vortex field the curl is 0 wherever it is defined, so the shading is blank: the two sides agree (both 0) unless the origin is inside \(R\) (choose the disc), where the field is undefined, Green's theorem does not apply, and the circulation is \(2\pi\). (For the unit square the origin is a corner of \(C\) itself, so the vortex integral is not defined there.)

Two useful consequences

Area as a line integral. Choose \(M=-\tfrac12y\), \(N=\tfrac12x\), so that \(N_x-M_y=1\). Then

\[\text{Area of }R=\iint_R1\,dx\,dy=\frac12\oint_C(x\,dy-y\,dx).\]

Choose a field whose floor paddle wheels all spin at rate 1 everywhere: the total spin over \(R\) is then just the area of \(R\), and Green turns it into a walk round the edge.

In words: walking once round the edge, you can measure the area enclosed. For the ellipse \(x=a\cos t\), \(y=b\sin t\) this gives \(\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}(ab\cos^2t+ab\sin^2t)\,dt=\pi ab\).

Regions with holes. If \(R\) has holes, \(C\) consists of the outer boundary (anticlockwise) and the boundary of each hole traversed clockwise (still keeping \(R\) on your left). For the vortex field on the ring \(1\le x^2+y^2\le4\): outer circle \(+2\pi\), inner circle clockwise \(-2\pi\), total 0, which equals \(\iint_R0\,dx\,dy\): consistent, because the origin is not in \(R\).A hole in the region is a hole in the net: its rim is part of the edge, walked so that the net stays on your left, which is clockwise round the hole.

Normal (flux) form. May 2015 calls the version above the "tangential form". There is also a normal form, the 2D version of Gauss' theorem: \(\oint_CM\,dy-N\,dx=\iint_R\big(\pd Mx+\pd Ny\big)\,dx\,dy\) (outward flux across \(C\) = total divergence inside). This is the "count what leaves a closed bag" picture of Gauss (§5), squashed flat: the bag is the region and its skin is the curve \(C\).

Exam routine (type C3c.6). "Verify": compute both sides. "With the help of Green's theorem": compute only the double integral.

  1. Do: fix the direction of \(C\): anticlockwise, region on your left. If no direction is stated, take anticlockwise and say so. This is the right-hand rule with the thumb up; walking the other way flips the sign.
  2. Do: compute the 2D curl \(N_x-M_y\). Terms like \(e^{\sin x}\) in \(M\) or \(\sqrt{y^4+1}\) in \(N\) are there to make the line integral impossible, and they vanish here. This is the spin of each floor paddle wheel; a part of \(M\) that depends only on \(x\) (or of \(N\) only on \(y\)) pushes equally on opposite sides of a wheel and cannot spin it.
  3. Do: integrate it over \(R\), usually in polar coordinates for discs and sectors. For "with the help of Green's theorem", stop here. Adding up the spins over the whole floor region gives the swirl round its edge.
  4. Do ("verify" only): split \(C\) into pieces (lines and arcs), each with its own parametrisation and direction; on horizontal sides only the \(dx\) term survives, on vertical sides only the \(dy\) term. This is the walk round the edge, one stretch at a time; the limits follow the direction you walk.
  5. Do: check both sides agree, and that \(M\), \(N\) and their partial derivatives are continuous on all of \(R\). A point inside \(R\) where the field blows up (the vortex) is a hole in the net, and the theorem does not apply there.

You can now: state Green's theorem as Stokes' theorem for a flat region and read \(N_x-M_y\) as the spin of a floor paddle wheel; verify it or use it to evaluate a line integral (type C3c.6); find an area by a line integral and handle regions with holes.

◆Common mistakes

✗Finding \(\phi\) by adding \(\int M\,dx\) and \(\int N\,dy\) (and \(\int P\,dz\)). Terms common to both are counted twice. Fix: integrate one component, then differentiate and compare with the next one; check \(\nabla\phi=\vb F\) at the end. ✗Writing the "constant" after \(\int M\,dx\) as a number \(c\). Fix: it is a function \(g(y,z)\) of the other variables. ✗"curl \(\vb F=\vb 0\), so \(\vb F\) is conservative" on a region with a hole (e.g. the plane without the origin). Fix: state the region; the test needs a simply connected region. Fields like \((y\ii-x\jj)/(x^2+y^2)\) are the classic trap. ✗Replacing \(dS\) by \(dx\,dy\). Fix: \(dS=\sqrt{f_x^2+f_y^2+1}\,dx\,dy\) (or \(a^2\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta\), \(R\,d\theta\,dz\)); only a horizontal flat surface has \(dS=dx\,dy\). ✗Wrong normal direction: using the upward formula when the question wants downward or outward, or pointing the inner normal of a shell outward. Fix: decide \(\vb n\) first, and check its sign at one convenient point. ✗In "verify Gauss", forgetting part of the closed surface (the flat base of a hemisphere, the top or bottom of a cylinder). Fix: list all the pieces of \(\Sigma\) with their normals before integrating; a base on which \(\vb F\cdot\vb n=0\) still has to be mentioned. ✗Using Gauss on an open surface. Fix: Gauss needs a closed surface; close it with a lid and subtract the lid's flux. ✗In Stokes, traversing \(C\) the wrong way for the chosen normal, getting the answer with the wrong sign. Fix: right-hand rule; upward normal ↔ anticlockwise from above. ✗Forgetting the Jacobian \(r\) in polar or cylindrical coordinates (\(dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta\)). Fix: write \(dA\) or \(dV\) in full before substituting. ✓Before a long direct computation, ask: is \(\vb F\) conservative (use \(\phi\))? Is the surface closed and the divergence simple (Gauss)? Is it a curl flux or a circulation with an easier partner surface or curve (Stokes)?

◆Find the mistake

Each "solution" has one error. Find it before opening the answer.

1. A potential

"For \(\vb F=(2xy+1)\,\ii+(x^2+2y)\,\jj\): \(\int M\,dx=x^2y+x\) and \(\int N\,dy=x^2y+y^2\), so \(\phi=x^2y+x+x^2y+y^2=2x^2y+x+y^2\)."

Answer

The term \(x^2y\) appears in both integrals and has been counted twice: \(\pd{}{x}(2x^2y+x+y^2)=4xy+1\ne M\). Correct method: \(\phi=x^2y+x+g(y)\); \(\phi_y=x^2+g'(y)=x^2+2y\Rightarrow g=y^2\). So \(\phi=x^2y+x+y^2\).

2. A flux through a hemisphere

"Flux of \(\vb F=z\,\kk\) upward through the hemisphere \(z=\sqrt{a^2-x^2-y^2}\): \(\vb n=(x\ii+y\jj+z\kk)/a\), so \(\vb F\cdot\vb n=z^2/a\), and \(\iint\dfrac{z^2}a\,dS\) \(=\iint_R\dfrac{a^2-x^2-y^2}{a}\,dx\,dy\) \(=\dfrac1a\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^a(a^2-r^2)r\,dr\,d\theta\) \(=\dfrac{\pi a^3}2\)."

Answer

\(dS\) was replaced by \(dx\,dy\). On the hemisphere \(dS=\dfrac az\,dx\,dy\), so the integrand becomes \(\dfrac{z^2}{a}\cdot\dfrac az=z=\sqrt{a^2-r^2}\), and the flux is \(\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^a\sqrt{a^2-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta=\dfrac{2\pi a^3}{3}\) (the same as the formula-sheet route \(\iint_R(-Mf_x-Nf_y+P)=\iint_Rz\)).

3. "Stokes' theorem fails"

"For \(\vb F=-y\,\ii+x\,\jj\) on the disc \(x^2+y^2\le1\) with normal \(\kk\): \(\iint\operatorname{curl}\vb F\cdot\kk\,dS=2\pi\). Round the circle \(\vb r=\cos t\,\ii-\sin t\,\jj\), \(0\le t\le2\pi\): \(\oint\vb F\cdot d\vb r=\int_0^{2\pi}(-\sin^2t-\cos^2t)\,dt=-2\pi\). So Stokes' theorem fails."

Answer

The parametrisation \((\cos t,-\sin t)\) runs clockwise, which does not match the normal \(\kk\) (right-hand rule: anticlockwise). With \((\cos t,\sin t)\) the circulation is \(+2\pi\), in agreement. (Note: \(\vb F=\sin t\,\ii+\cos t\,\jj\) on this path, \(\vb r'=-\sin t\,\ii-\cos t\,\jj\).)

1. (§1) Find a potential for \(\vb F=(2x+y)\,\ii+(x+3y^2)\,\jj\) and the work it does from \((1,0)\) to \((2,1)\).

Hint

\(\phi=x^2+xy+g(y)\); match \(\phi_y\) with \(N\).

Answer

\(\phi=x^2+xy+y^3\); work \(=\phi(2,1)-\phi(1,0)=7-1=6\).

2. (§2) For which constant \(a\) is \(\vb F\) \(=(axy-z^3)\,\ii+(a-2)x^2\,\jj+(1-a)xz^2\,\kk\) conservative on \(\R^3\)? Find a potential for that \(a\).

Hint

Compute the three components of the curl and make each vanish for all \(x,y,z\).

Answer

\(\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\vb 0\cdot\ii+(a-4)z^2\,\jj+(a-4)x\,\kk\), so \(a=4\) (and \(\R^3\) is simply connected). Then \(\vb F=(4xy-z^3)\ii+2x^2\jj-3xz^2\kk\) and \(\phi=2x^2y-xz^3\).

Show the calculation: the curl
  1. \(\ii\): \(\partial_y\big((1-a)xz^2\big)-\partial_z\big((a-2)x^2\big)\) \(=0-0\) \(=0\).
  2. \(\jj\): \(\partial_z(axy-z^3)-\partial_x\big((1-a)xz^2\big)\) \(=-3z^2-(1-a)z^2\) \(=(a-4)z^2\).
  3. \(\kk\): \(\partial_x\big((a-2)x^2\big)-\partial_y(axy-z^3)\) \(=2(a-2)x-ax\) \(=(a-4)x\).
  4. Potential: \(\int(4xy-z^3)\,dx\) \(=2x^2y-xz^3+g(y,z)\); \(\phi_y=2x^2\Rightarrow g_y=0\); \(\phi_z=-3xz^2\Rightarrow g\) constant.

3. (§3) Evaluate \(\iint_\Sigma z\,dS\), where \(\Sigma\) is the hemisphere \(z=\sqrt{a^2-x^2-y^2}\).

Hint

\(dS=\dfrac az\,dx\,dy\): the \(z\) cancels.

Answer

\(\iint_Rz\cdot\dfrac az\,dx\,dy\) \(=a\times(\text{area of the disc})\) \(=\pi a^3\). (With spherical \(dS\): \(\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^{\pi/2}a\cos\phi\cdot a^2\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta=\pi a^3\).)

4. (§4) Find the upward flux of \(\vb F=z\,\kk\) through the hemisphere \(z=\sqrt{a^2-x^2-y^2}\).

Hint

Formula sheet: \(\iint_R(-Mf_x-Nf_y+P)\,dx\,dy\) with \(M=N=0\), \(P=z\).

Answer

\(\iint_R\sqrt{a^2-x^2-y^2}\,dx\,dy\) \(=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^a\sqrt{a^2-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta\) \(=2\pi\cdot\dfrac{a^3}3\) \(=\dfrac{2\pi a^3}{3}\).

5. (§5) Use Gauss' theorem to find the outward flux of \(\vb F=x^3\,\ii+y^3\,\jj+z^3\,\kk\) through the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2\).

Hint

\(\operatorname{div}\vb F=3(x^2+y^2+z^2)=3\rho^2\); use spherical coordinates, \(dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\).

Answer

\(\int_0^a3\rho^2\cdot4\pi\rho^2\,d\rho=\dfrac{12\pi a^5}{5}\).

6. (§6) Use Stokes' theorem to evaluate \(\oint_C-y^3\,dx+x^3\,dy-z^3\,dz\), where \(C\) is the intersection of the cylinder \(x^2+y^2=1\) and the plane \(x+y+z=1\), anticlockwise seen from above.

Hint

Take \(\Sigma\) = the flat piece of the plane inside the cylinder, \(z=1-x-y\), upward normal. Only the \(\kk\)-component of the curl matters.

Answer

\(\operatorname{curl}\vb F=3(x^2+y^2)\,\kk\); with \(\vb n\,dS=(\ii+\jj+\kk)\,dx\,dy\): \(\iint_{r\le1}3r^2\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta=\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\). (Checked against the line integral directly.)

7. (§7) A long straight wire carries a steady current \(I\). Assuming \(\vb B\) circles the wire with the same strength \(B\) at every point at distance \(r\) from it, use Ampère's law to find \(B\).

Hint

Take \(C\) = a circle of radius \(r\) round the wire; \(\vb B\) is tangent to it, so \(\oint\vb B\cdot d\vb r=B\cdot2\pi r\). The current through any surface spanning \(C\) is \(I\).

Answer

\(2\pi rB=\dfrac{I}{c^2\epsilon_0}\), so \(B=\dfrac{I}{2\pi c^2\epsilon_0\,r}=\dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\).

◆Concept checks

1. Is \(\int_Cf\,ds\) path independent (try \(f=1\))? Does reversing the direction of \(C\) change it?

Answer

No: \(\int_C1\,ds\) is the length of \(C\), which depends on the path. Reversing \(C\) does not change \(\int_Cf\,ds\) (no orientation), whereas it changes the sign of \(\int_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\).

2. A field has \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F=\vb 0\) everywhere except on the \(z\)-axis, where it is undefined. Can you conclude it is conservative?

Answer

No. Space without the \(z\)-axis is not simply connected, so the test gives no definite answer: \(\dfrac{-y\ii+x\jj}{x^2+y^2}\) is not conservative (circulation \(2\pi\) round the axis), but \(\dfrac{x\ii+y\jj}{x^2+y^2}=\nabla\big(\tfrac12\ln(x^2+y^2)\big)\) is.

3. Which are simply connected: (a) the plane without the origin; (b) space without the origin; (c) the solid region between two concentric spheres; (d) a solid ring doughnut?

Answer

(a) No. (b) Yes. (c) Yes (a loop can slide round the inner hole, like (b)). (d) No: a loop going round the ring cannot be shrunk.

4. Without computing, what is the flux of \(\operatorname{curl}\vb F\) out of the unit sphere for \(\vb F=yz^2\,\ii+x^3\,\jj+\sin(xy)\,\kk\)?

Answer

0. By Gauss it equals \(\iiint\operatorname{div}(\operatorname{curl}\vb F)\,dV=0\) (div curl = 0, Chapter 3a §6). Or by Stokes: cut the sphere at the equator into two hemispheres; their circulations round the equator are travelled in opposite directions and cancel.

5. What is the flux of a constant field \(\vb F=\vb c\) out of any closed surface? Why does that make physical sense?

Answer

0, since \(\operatorname{div}\vb c=0\). A uniform flow carries as much fluid in on one side as out on the other.

6. You reverse the normal of an oriented open surface. What happens to the flux, to the direction of the boundary curve, and to Stokes' theorem?

Answer

The flux changes sign; the boundary orientation reverses (right-hand rule), so the circulation also changes sign; Stokes' theorem still holds. On a Möbius strip no consistent choice exists, so flux is not defined at all.

Answer

(i) \(\operatorname{div}\vb F=2x+2y+2z\). (ii) Both sides equal \(\boxed{\pi R^2H^2}\).

Show the calculation: outline
  1. Volume: \[\begin{aligned}&\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^R\!\!\int_0^H2(r\cos\theta+r\sin\theta+z) \times r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta.\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}&\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^R\!\!\int_0^H2(r\cos\theta+r\sin\theta+z)\\&\qquad\times r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta.\end{aligned}\]The \(\cos\theta,\sin\theta\) terms vanish over \(0\) to \(2\pi\); the \(z\) term gives \(2\cdot\tfrac{H^2}2\cdot\tfrac{R^2}2\cdot2\pi=\pi R^2H^2\).
  2. Top \(z=H\), \(\vb n=\kk\): \(\vb F\cdot\vb n=H^2\), flux \(H^2\cdot\pi R^2\).
  3. Bottom \(z=0\), \(\vb n=-\kk\): \(\vb F\cdot\vb n=-z^2=0\), flux 0.
  4. Side: \(\vb F\cdot\vb n\) \(=\dfrac{x^3+y^3}{R}\) \(=R^2(\cos^3\theta+\sin^3\theta)\), \(dS=R\,d\theta\,dz\); \(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^3\theta\,d\theta=\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^3\theta\,d\theta=0\), flux 0.
  5. Total \(\pi R^2H^2\). ✓
Dec 2022 Q3(a)(i)–(iv)6 + 3 + 3 + 5 marksalso Dec 2018 Q4(b)Guide C3c.2 · fields with a hole

Consider the two vector fields

\[\begin{aligned}\vb F_1&=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,\ii+\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}\,\jj,\\ \vb F_2&=\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}\,\ii-\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,\jj\end{aligned}\]

defined over \(\R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}\) (that is, the whole \(XY\)-plane with \((0,0)\) removed).

(i) Find \(\operatorname{curl}(\vb F_1)\) and \(\operatorname{curl}(\vb F_2)\).

(ii) Find \(\phi_1(x,y)\) such that \(\nabla\phi_1=\vb F_1\) and determine the region where \(\phi_1(x,y)\) is defined.

(iii) Find \(\phi_2(x,y)\) such that \(\nabla\phi_2=\vb F_2\) and determine the region where \(\phi_2(x,y)\) is defined. (Hint: \(\int\frac{1}{1+u^2}du=\tan^{-1}u+k\) for some arbitrary constant \(k\).)

(iv) Evaluate \(\oint_C\vb F\cdot d\vb r\) for the cases with \(\vb F=\vb F_1\) and \(\vb F=\vb F_2\) where \(C\) is the unit circle oriented clockwise (\(C\) is parametrized by \((x(t),y(t))=(\cos t,-\sin t)\) for \(t\in[0,2\pi]\)).

Figure: left \(\vb F_1\) points straight away from the origin; right \(\vb F_2\) swirls clockwise round it. The dashed unit circle is traversed clockwise.

Hint

(ii) Integrate \(\dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2}\) in \(x\): a logarithm. (iii) Integrate \(\dfrac{y}{x^2+y^2}=\dfrac{1/y}{1+(x/y)^2}\) in \(x\) with \(u=x/y\). (iv) Which potential is defined all the way round the circle?

Answer

(i) Both curls are \(\vb 0\). (ii) \(\phi_1=\tfrac12\ln(x^2+y^2)\), defined on all of \(\R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}\). (iii) \(\phi_2=\tan^{-1}(x/y)\), defined only where \(y\ne0\) (each of the half-planes \(y\gt0\), \(y\lt0\)). (iv) \(\oint_C\vb F_1\cdot d\vb r=\boxed{0}\), \(\oint_C\vb F_2\cdot d\vb r=\boxed{2\pi}\).

Show the calculation: outline
  1. (i) For \(\vb F_2\): \(\partial_x\Big(\dfrac{-x}{x^2+y^2}\Big)=\dfrac{x^2-y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\) and \(\partial_y\Big(\dfrac{y}{x^2+y^2}\Big)=\dfrac{x^2-y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\); difference 0. Similarly for \(\vb F_1\) both cross derivatives are \(\dfrac{-2xy}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\).
  2. (ii) \(\int\dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2}dx\) \(=\tfrac12\ln(x^2+y^2)+g(y)\); \(\partial_y\) gives \(\dfrac{y}{x^2+y^2}+g'\Rightarrow g'=0\).
  3. (iii) \(\int\dfrac{y\,dx}{x^2+y^2}=\tan^{-1}\dfrac xy+g(y)\); \(\partial_y\tan^{-1}\dfrac xy\) \(=\dfrac{-x/y^2}{1+x^2/y^2}\) \(=\dfrac{-x}{x^2+y^2}\) \(\Rightarrow g'\) \(=0\). Undefined on \(y=0\).
  4. (iv) \(\vb F_1\): \(\phi_1\) is a potential on a region containing \(C\), and \(C\) is closed, so 0. \(\vb F_2\): \(x=\cos t\), \(y=-\sin t\), \(dx=-\sin t\,dt\), \(dy=-\cos t\,dt\): \(\dfrac{y\,dx-x\,dy}{1}=(\sin^2t+\cos^2t)\,dt\), integral \(2\pi\). No single potential for \(\vb F_2\) works all round \(C\) (it crosses \(y=0\)), and the region is not simply connected.
