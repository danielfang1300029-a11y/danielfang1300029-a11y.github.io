C2.1Double integrals in Cartesian form

3 parts · 3 sittings (May 2021, 2018, 2017) · 26 marks · Learn it · note §1

Type card · predictable · which order?
Looks like
An iterated integral with given limits, or a region bounded by two or three curves: “sketch the region”, “evaluate”, “change the order”, “is the answer the area?”.
Which order?
  • Inner integral has no elementary antiderivative (\(\int e^{\cos x}dx\), \(\int e^{x^2}dx\), \(\int\sin y^2dy\)) → reverse the order; the new inner integral is just a length and supplies the factor you need. (May 2017.)
  • Top and bottom boundaries are each one formula in \(x\) → vertical strips, \(dy\,dx\). (May 2021: between \(y=x^3\) and \(y=x^{1/3}\).)
  • Left and right boundaries are \(x=g(y)\) (a sideways parabola \(x=y^2-2y\)) → horizontal strips, \(dx\,dy\). The other order then needs splitting wherever the top or bottom formula changes. (May 2018.)
  • Region symmetric, integrand odd → 0 without integrating; integrand even → twice one half.
Do
1. Sketch every boundary; find the corners by solving the curves in pairs. 2. Shade the region and draw one strip. 3. Inner limits = where the strip enters and leaves; outer limits = first and last strip. 4. Integrate the inner integral first, holding the outer variable constant.
Watch
Changing the order is never “swap the limits”: redraw the strips the other way. And \(\iint f\,dA\) is the area only when \(f=1\).
Ready answer · reversing the order, and area \[\begin{aligned}&\int_{c}^{d}\!\!\int_{h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)}f\,dx\,dy\\&=\int_{a}^{b}\!\!\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}f\,dy\,dx\end{aligned}\]

when both describe the same region: horizontal strips from \(x=h_1(y)\) to \(x=h_2(y)\) for \(c\le y\le d\), or vertical strips from \(y=g_1(x)\) to \(y=g_2(x)\) for \(a\le x\le b\). If \(g_1\) or \(g_2\) changes formula at \(x=x_0\), split: \(\int_a^{x_0}+\int_{x_0}^b\).

\[\text{Area}(R)=\iint_R1\,dA=\int_a^b\big(g_2(x)-g_1(x)\big)\,dx.\]

In words: the inner limits are the two ends of a typical strip (curves), the outer limits are numbers; area is the double integral of 1, i.e. “top minus bottom” integrated.

May 2021 Q2(a)10 marksMay format

Let \(D\) be the region enclosed by \(y=x^3\) and \(x=y^3\). Sketch the region \(D\) and determine its area with the use of double integration.

Hint

Solve the two curves together: \(x=x^9\). The curves cross three times, so \(D\) has two pieces. \(x=y^3\) is \(y=x^{1/3}\).

Solution
  1. Intersections: put \(y=x^3\) into \(x=y^3\): \(x=x^9\), so \(x(x^8-1)=0\), \(x=0,\pm1\). Points \((0,0)\), \((1,1)\), \((-1,-1)\).
  2. For \(0\le x\le1\): \(x^{1/3}\ge x^3\), so the lobe is \(x^3\le y\le x^{1/3}\). For \(-1\le x\le0\) the order flips: \(x^{1/3}\le y\le x^3\). The two lobes are images of each other under \((x,y)\to(-x,-y)\), so they have equal area.

    \(D\): two lobes between \(y=x^3\) (blue) and \(y=x^{1/3}\), i.e. \(x=y^3\) (red), meeting at \((-1,-1)\), \((0,0)\), \((1,1)\); one vertical strip drawn.

  3. \[\begin{aligned}\text{Area}&=2\int_0^1\!\!\int_{x^3}^{x^{1/3}}dy\,dx\\&=2\int_0^1\big(x^{1/3}-x^3\big)\,dx\\&=2\Big(\tfrac34-\tfrac14\Big)\end{aligned}\]

Both lobes are “enclosed by” the curves; a first-quadrant-only answer of \(\frac12\) should say it is one lobe.

\(\displaystyle\text{Area}(D)=1\quad(\tfrac12\text{ for each lobe})\)
May 2018 Q2(a)10 marksMay format

Given \(\displaystyle\int_{y=-1}^{2}\int_{x=y^2-2y}^{x=2-y}(x+y)\,dx\,dy\) over a region in the \(xy\)-plane.

(i) Sketch the region of integration in the \(xy\)-plane. (ii) Calculate the double integral with the given order of integration. (iii) For the same region and integrand, write down the lower and upper bounds of the double integral for the integration order \(dy\,dx\); no need to calculate. (Hint: for the order \(dy\,dx\) the region needs to be separated into two sub-regions.) (iv) Is the answer of the given integral equal to the area of the region? Verify your answer by calculating the double integral for the area of the region.

Hint

Horizontal strips from the sideways parabola \(x=y^2-2y\) (vertex \((-1,1)\)) to the line \(x=2-y\); they meet at \(y=-1\) and \(y=2\). For \(dy\,dx\), solve the parabola for \(y\): \(y=1\pm\sqrt{1+x}\).

Solution
  1. (i) \(x=y^2-2y=(y-1)^2-1\): a parabola opening to the right, vertex \((-1,1)\). It meets \(x=2-y\) where \(y^2-y-2=0\), \(y=-1\) or \(2\): corners \((3,-1)\) and \((0,2)\). The region lies between them.

    Region between \(x=y^2-2y\) (red, left) and \(x=2-y\) (blue, right). Dashed \(x=0\): for \(dy\,dx\) the top changes from the parabola (\(x\lt0\)) to the line (\(x\gt0\)).

  2. (ii) Inner integral: \[\begin{aligned}&\Big[\tfrac{x^2}2+xy\Big]_{y^2-2y}^{2-y}\\&=\Big(2-\tfrac{y^2}2\Big)-\Big(\tfrac{y^4}2-y^3\Big).\end{aligned}\]
  3. \[\begin{aligned}&\int_{-1}^2\Big(2-\tfrac{y^2}2+y^3-\tfrac{y^4}2\Big)dy\\&=6-\tfrac32+\tfrac{15}4-\tfrac{33}{10}=\tfrac{99}{20}.\end{aligned}\]
  4. (iii) \(x\) runs from \(-1\) (vertex) to 3. Lower boundary: lower half of the parabola, \(y=1-\sqrt{1+x}\). Upper boundary: upper half of the parabola for \(-1\le x\le0\), the line \(y=2-x\) for \(0\le x\le3\): \[\begin{aligned}&\int_{-1}^{0}\!\int_{1-\sqrt{1+x}}^{1+\sqrt{1+x}}(x+y)\,dy\,dx\\&+\int_{0}^{3}\!\int_{1-\sqrt{1+x}}^{2-x}(x+y)\,dy\,dx.\end{aligned}\]
  5. (iv) No, the integrand is \(x+y\), not 1. \[\begin{aligned}\text{Area}&=\int_{-1}^2\big[(2-y)-(y^2-2y)\big]dy\\&=\int_{-1}^2(2+y-y^2)\,dy=\tfrac92.\end{aligned}\]

In (iii) the split at \(x=0\) (where \(1+\sqrt{1+x}=2-x\)) is what the 3 marks are for; show why on the sketch.

\(\displaystyle\begin{aligned}&\text{(ii) }\frac{99}{20}=4.95\\&\text{(iv) Area}=\frac92\ne\frac{99}{20}\end{aligned}\)
May 2017 Q2(a)6 marksMay format

Consider the double integral \(\displaystyle\int_{y=0}^{1}\int_{x=\sin^{-1}y}^{\pi/2}e^{\cos x}\,dx\,dy\) over a region in the \(xy\)-plane.

(i) Sketch the region of integration in the \(xy\)-plane. (ii) Change the order of integration, hence, or otherwise, evaluate the double integral. (iii) Is the answer in part (ii) equal to the area of the region of integration? Justify your answer by finding the area of that region.

Hint

\(\int e^{\cos x}dx\) cannot be done, so reverse. \(x\ge\sin^{-1}y\) means \(y\le\sin x\): vertical strips from \(y=0\) to \(y=\sin x\).

Solution
  1. (i) Horizontal strips run from \(x=\sin^{-1}y\) (the curve \(y=\sin x\)) to \(x=\frac\pi2\), for \(0\le y\le1\). So the region is under the arch \(y=\sin x\), above \(y=0\), for \(0\le x\le\frac\pi2\).

    Region under \(y=\sin x\), \(0\le x\le\frac\pi2\); corners \((0,0)\), \((\frac\pi2,0)\), \((\frac\pi2,1)\).

  2. (ii) Vertical strips: \(0\le y\le\sin x\), \(0\le x\le\frac\pi2\): \[\begin{aligned}I&=\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{\sin x}e^{\cos x}\,dy\,dx\\&=\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{\cos x}\sin x\,dx\\&=\Big[-e^{\cos x}\Big]_0^{\pi/2}=-1+e.\end{aligned}\]
  3. (iii) No. \(\text{Area}=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin x\,dx=1\). The integral equals the area only when the integrand is 1, and here it is \(e^{\cos x}\) (between 1 and \(e\)).

Say why you reverse (no antiderivative of \(e^{\cos x}\)) and show the new limits on the sketch.

\(\displaystyle I=e-1\approx1.718;\qquad\text{Area}=1\ne I\)

C2.2Change of variables \(u,v\) in double integrals

29 parts · 14 sittings (Dec 2025, 2024, 2023, 2022, 2021, 2016, 2015, 2014 + Dec 2018 repeat; May 2026, 2025, 2024, 2022, 2019, 2016 + May 2023 repeat) · 160 marks · Learn it · note §2

No fixed pattern: the map may be given either way round (\(x,y\) in terms of \(u,v\), or \(u,v\) in terms of \(x,y\)), linear or non-linear, and the region may be four lines, an ellipse, or curves. So this type gets a decision procedure rather than a card.

Decision procedure
  1. Map given as \(x=\dots,\ y=\dots\) → \(J=\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=x_uy_v-x_vy_u\) straight away. Then solve for \(u,v\) in terms of \(x,y\) (you need this to map the boundaries).
  2. Map given as \(u=\dots,\ v=\dots\) → compute \(\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\) and take its reciprocal (ready answer). If asked “in terms of \(u\) and \(v\)”, rewrite what is left (e.g. \(x^2+y^2=\sqrt{u^2+v^2}\)).
  3. No map given, region bounded by two pairs of parallel lines \(a_1x+b_1y=c_1,c_2\) and \(a_2x+b_2y=d_1,d_2\) → let \(u=a_1x+b_1y\), \(v=a_2x+b_2y\) (the left-hand sides). The region becomes the rectangle \(c_1\le u\le c_2\), \(d_1\le v\le d_2\); \(J=1/(a_1b_2-a_2b_1)\) (ready answer). Prefer the choice that also simplifies the integrand (\(x-y\), \(x+y\) appearing in it).
  4. Ellipse \(\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}\le1\) → \(x=ar\cos\theta\), \(y=br\sin\theta\), \(J=abr\), region \(0\le r\le1\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\) (ready answer). Tilted ellipse \(Ax^2+Bxy+Cy^2\le k\) → use the given linear map; it turns the quadratic into \(\lambda(u^2+v^2)\), a disc; then polar in the \(uv\)-plane.
  5. Curved boundaries (\(x^2-y^2=\pm1\), \(y=\sqrt x\), \(y=-x^2\)) → non-linear map. Substitute each boundary curve into the map; each should become \(u=\)const, \(v=\)const, or a simple curve. Use the sign conditions given (\(u\ge0\), \(v\ge0\)) to throw away extra roots.
  6. Map the region: for every boundary, substitute; track which corner goes where (corners → corners); for a non-linear map an edge can become a curve, so map whole edges, not just corners. Test one interior point to see which side is inside.
  7. Sketch both (even if only one is asked): \(xy\)-region with corners labelled, \(uv\)-region with the boundary equations.
  8. Integrate: \[\begin{aligned}&\iint_Rf\,dx\,dy\\&=\iint_{R^*}f\big(x(u,v),y(u,v)\big)\,|J|\,du\,dv.\end{aligned}\] Limits from the \(uv\)-sketch. Check: area of \(R=\iint_{R^*}|J|\,du\,dv\); symmetric region + odd integrand → 0.

Cues: “under the transformation”, “\(UV\)-plane”, “Jacobian”; four straight boundaries in two parallel pairs; an integrand with \(\frac{x-y}{x+y}\), \(x^2-y^2\), \(e^{x-y}\); an ellipse.

Traps:

  • Using \(\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\) where \(\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\) is needed (they are reciprocals: \(-3\) vs \(-\frac13\)).
  • Forgetting the absolute value: \(J=-\frac13\) contributes \(+\frac13\).
  • Mixing coordinates: limits in \(u,v\) but integrand still in \(x,y\), or vice versa.
  • Mapping only the corners of a non-linear map (Dec 2023: the edge \(y=1\) becomes a parabola).
Ready answer · Jacobian (not on the sheet) \[\begin{aligned}J=\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}&=\begin{vmatrix}x_u&x_v\\y_u&y_v\end{vmatrix}\\&=x_uy_v-x_vy_u,\end{aligned}\] \[\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=\frac{1}{\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)},\]\[dx\,dy=|J|\,du\,dv.\]

In words: the Jacobian is the factor by which a tiny \(du\,dv\) rectangle is stretched into an \(xy\) area; going the other way divides by it, and only its size (not its sign) enters the integral.

Ready answer · linear maps

If \(u=ax+by\), \(v=cx+dy\), then \(\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}=ad-bc\) and

\[\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=\frac1{ad-bc}.\]
Map\(\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\)Used in
\(u=x-y,\ v=x+y\)\(\frac12\)Dec 2025, 2014; May 2026, 2024
\(u=x+y,\ v=y-x\)\(\frac12\)Dec 2016
\(u=x+y,\ v=x-2y\)\(-\frac13\)Dec 2022
\(u=3x+2y,\ v=x+4y\)\(\frac1{10}\)May 2019
\(x=u+v,\ y=-2u+v\)\(3\)Dec 2015

In words: for a linear map the Jacobian is a constant, the determinant of the coefficients (or one over it, if the map is written the other way round); lines stay lines, so a parallelogram becomes a rectangle.

Ready answer · ellipse maps \[\begin{aligned}&x=ar\cos\theta,\ y=br\sin\theta:\\&J=abr,\quad\tfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\tfrac{y^2}{b^2}=r^2,\\&\text{ellipse}\to0\le r\le1,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi.\end{aligned}\]

Or \(x=au\), \(y=bv\): \(J=ab\), ellipse → unit disc. Area of the ellipse \(=\pi ab\).

In words: squash the ellipse into a unit circle; every area shrinks by the factor \(ab\), which the Jacobian puts back.

Dec 2025 Q1(b)(i)–(iii)5 + 4 + 6 marks

Let \(S\) be the region in the \(XY\)-plane bounded by the lines \(y=x\), \(y=x-1\), \(y=1-x\), \(y=2-x\).

(i) Sketch the transformed region of \(S\) in the \(UV\)-plane under the coordinate transformation \(x=\dfrac{u+v}{2}\), \(y=\dfrac{v-u}{2}\). (ii) Find the Jacobian of the transformation \(\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\). (iii) Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_S(x-y)\,dS\).

Hint

Procedure step 1: solve for \(u,v\): add and subtract the two equations. Each boundary line becomes \(u=\)const or \(v=\)const.

Solution
  1. \(x+y=v\) and \(x-y=u\).
  2. (i) \(y=x\to u=0\); \(y=x-1\to u=1\); \(y=1-x\to v=1\); \(y=2-x\to v=2\). So \(S^*\) is the square \(0\le u\le1\), \(1\le v\le2\). Corners: \((u,v)=(0,1),(1,1),(1,2),(0,2)\) ↔ \((x,y)=(\frac12,\frac12),(1,0),(\frac32,\frac12),(1,1)\).

    Left: \(S\) (a tilted square). Right: \(S^*\), \(0\le u\le1\), \(1\le v\le2\). Same colour = same edge.

  3. (ii) \[\begin{aligned}J&=\begin{vmatrix}\frac12&\frac12\\-\frac12&\frac12\end{vmatrix}=\frac14+\frac14=\frac12.\end{aligned}\]
  4. (iii) \(x-y=u\), \(dS=\frac12\,du\,dv\): \[\begin{aligned}\iint_S(x-y)\,dS&=\int_1^2\!\!\int_0^1u\cdot\tfrac12\,du\,dv\\&=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\cdot1.\end{aligned}\]

The sketch needs the four \(uv\) boundary equations and corner values on the axes, not just a square.

\(\displaystyle\begin{aligned}&S^*:0\le u\le1,\ 1\le v\le2\\&J=\tfrac12\\&\iint_S(x-y)\,dS=\tfrac14\end{aligned}\)
Dec 2024 Q1(b)(i)–(iii)4 + 4 + 5 marks

Let \(S\) be the region specified by the ellipse \(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}=1\) where \(a\gt0\) and \(b\gt0\).

(i) Describe the region in the \(R\Theta\)-plane under the coordinate transformation \((x,y)=(ar\cos\theta,\ br\sin\theta)\). (ii) Find the Jacobian of the transformation \(\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}\). (iii) Use the transformed coordinates to evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_S(x^2+y^2)\,dS\).

Hint

Procedure step 4. Substitute into \(\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}\): it becomes \(r^2\).

Solution
  1. (i) \(\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=r^2\cos^2\theta+r^2\sin^2\theta=r^2\), so inside the ellipse ⇔ \(r\le1\). One turn round the ellipse is \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\). The region is the rectangle \(0\le r\le1\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\) (\(r=1\) is the ellipse, \(r=0\) the centre, \(\theta=0\) and \(2\pi\) both the positive \(x\)-axis).

    Left: \(S\), drawn for \(a=2\), \(b=1.2\). Right: \(0\le r\le1\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\); the red edge \(r=1\) is the ellipse.

  2. (ii) \[\begin{aligned}J&=\begin{vmatrix}a\cos\theta&-ar\sin\theta\\b\sin\theta&br\cos\theta\end{vmatrix}\\&=abr(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)=abr.\end{aligned}\]
  3. (iii) \[\begin{aligned}I&=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1r^2(a^2\cos^2\theta+b^2\sin^2\theta)\\&\qquad\times abr\,dr\,d\theta\\&=\frac{ab}4\int_0^{2\pi}(a^2\cos^2\theta+b^2\sin^2\theta)\,d\theta\\&=\frac{ab}4\,(a^2\pi+b^2\pi).\end{aligned}\]

Check: \(a=b\) gives \(\frac{\pi a^4}2\), the disc result. Quote \(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta=\pi\).

\(\displaystyle\begin{aligned}&J=abr\\&\iint_S(x^2+y^2)\,dS=\frac{\pi ab(a^2+b^2)}4\end{aligned}\)
Dec 2023 Q2(b)(i)–(iii)4 + 4 + 4 marks

Also set: Dec 2018 Q2(a)(i)–(iii) (5 + 4 + 5 marks): (i) “show that \(J=-\frac{1}{4(x^2+y^2)}=-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{u^2+v^2}}\)”; (ii) the same triangle, given as “bounded by \(y=x\), \(y=-x\), \(y=1\)” with a figure; (iii) “evaluate” the same integral, with the hint “observe the symmetry”.

Consider the transformation \(u=2xy\), \(v=x^2-y^2\).

(i) Find the Jacobian of the transformation \(\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\) expressed in \(u\) and \(v\). (ii) Transform the region in the \(XY\)-plane given by \(0\le y\le1\), \(-y\le x\le y\) to the corresponding region in the \(UV\)-plane. (iii) Use the transformation, or otherwise, to show that

\[\int_0^1\!\!\int_{-y}^{y}xy(x^4-y^4)\sqrt{1+(x^2-y^2)^2}\,dx\,dy=0.\]
Hint

Procedure step 2: the map is \(u,v\) in terms of \(x,y\), so find \(\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\) and flip it. Note \(u^2+v^2=(x^2+y^2)^2\). For (ii) map each edge of the triangle.

Solution
  1. (i) \(\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}=\begin{vmatrix}2y&2x\\2x&-2y\end{vmatrix}=-4(x^2+y^2)\).
  2. \[\begin{aligned}u^2+v^2&=4x^2y^2+(x^2-y^2)^2\\&=(x^2+y^2)^2,\end{aligned}\] so \(x^2+y^2=\sqrt{u^2+v^2}\) and \[\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=-\frac1{4(x^2+y^2)}=-\frac1{4\sqrt{u^2+v^2}}.\]
  3. (ii) The region is the triangle \(O(0,0)\), \((-1,1)\), \((1,1)\). Edge \(y=x\), \(0\le x\le1\): \(u=2x^2\), \(v=0\) → segment \(v=0\), \(0\le u\le2\). Edge \(y=-x\): \(u=-2x^2\), \(v=0\) → \(v=0\), \(-2\le u\le0\). Edge \(y=1\), \(-1\le x\le1\): \(u=2x\), \(v=x^2-1\), so \(v=\frac{u^2}4-1\), a parabola from \((-2,0)\) through \((0,-1)\) to \((2,0)\). Interior test: \((0,\frac12)\to(0,-\frac14)\), inside.

Type card · predictable
Looks like
A disc, an off-centre circle, or a region cut out by an arc and straight lines; an integrand with \(x^2+y^2\) or \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\); “use polar coordinates to describe the region / evaluate / find the area”.
Do
1. Sketch. 2. Turn every boundary into polar form (ready answer): circle → \(r=\)const or \(r=2a\cos\theta\), \(2a\sin\theta\); line through \(O\) → \(\theta=\)const; vertical line \(x=a\) → \(r=a\sec\theta\). 3. Draw a ray from \(O\): \(r\) from where it enters to where it leaves (inner), \(\theta\) between the extreme rays (outer). 4. \(dA=r\,dr\,d\theta\), integrate \(r\) first.
Watch
The extra \(r\) in \(dA\). For a circle through the origin, \(\theta\) runs over a half-turn only (\(-\frac\pi2\) to \(\frac\pi2\) for \(r=2a\cos\theta\); 0 to \(\pi\) for \(r=2a\sin\theta\)).
Ready answer · polar coordinates (not on the sheet) \[\begin{aligned}&x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta,\ x^2+y^2=r^2,\\&dA=dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta\quad(J=r).\end{aligned}\]
CurvePolar form\(\theta\) range
\(x^2+y^2=a^2\)\(r=a\)\(0\) to \(2\pi\)
\((x-a)^2+y^2=a^2\)\(r=2a\cos\theta\)\(-\frac\pi2\) to \(\frac\pi2\)
\(x^2+(y-a)^2=a^2\)\(r=2a\sin\theta\)\(0\) to \(\pi\)
\(x=a\) / \(y=a\)\(r=a\sec\theta\) / \(r=a\csc\theta\)
\(y=mx\)\(\theta=\tan^{-1}m\)

Area inside \(r=f(\theta)\) between two rays: \(\displaystyle\int_\alpha^\beta\tfrac12f(\theta)^2\,d\theta\). Useful: \(\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^3\theta\,d\theta=\frac43\) (sheet's reduction formula).

The axis is the \(y\)-axis: polar in the \(xz\)-plane, \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(z=r\sin\theta\). Then \(r^2\le y\le4\), \(0\le r\le2\), \(0\le\theta\le\frac\pi2\).

Solution
  1. A paraboloid bowl opening along the \(y\)-axis, cut off by the plane \(y=4\) (where \(x^2+z^2=4\), radius 2); first octant keeps one quarter.
  2. \(\sqrt{x^2+z^2}=r\), \(dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dy\): \[\begin{aligned}I&=\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^2\!\!\int_{r^2}^4r\cdot r\,dy\,dr\,d\theta\\&=\frac\pi2\int_0^2r^2(4-r^2)\,dr\\&=\frac\pi2\Big(\frac{32}3-\frac{32}5\Big)=\frac\pi2\cdot\frac{64}{15}.\end{aligned}\]

Two factors of \(r\): one from the integrand, one from \(dV\).

\(\displaystyle I=\frac{32\pi}{15}\approx6.70\)

Consider \(\displaystyle\iiint_D\ln(x^2+y^2)\,dz\,dy\,dx\) over the solid region \(D\) bounded by the interior of the conical region \(x^2+y^2\le z^2\), \(0\le z\le1\). Directly write down the Jacobian of the coordinate transformation between cylindrical and Cartesian coordinates. Hence, evaluate the triple integral over \(D\) in cylindrical coordinates.

Printed “\(x^2+y^2+\le z^2\)”: the stray “+” is read as \(x^2+y^2\le z^2\).

Hint

\(J=r\). Cone: \(0\le r\le z\). Inner integral \(\int r\ln r^2\,dr=\int2r\ln r\,dr\) by parts.

Solution
  1. \(J=\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(r,\theta,z)}=r\). The cone \(x^2+y^2\le z^2\) is \(r\le z\); so \(0\le r\le z\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\), \(0\le z\le1\).
  2. \[\begin{aligned}\int_0^z2r\ln r\,dr&=\Big[r^2\ln r-\frac{r^2}2\Big]_0^z\\&=z^2\ln z-\frac{z^2}2\end{aligned}\] (\(r^2\ln r\to0\) as \(r\to0\)).
  3. \[\begin{aligned}I&=2\pi\int_0^1\Big(z^2\ln z-\frac{z^2}2\Big)dz\\&=2\pi\Big(-\frac19-\frac16\Big)=2\pi\cdot\Big(-\frac5{18}\Big).\end{aligned}\] (\(\int_0^1z^2\ln z\,dz=-\frac19\) by parts.)

A negative answer is right: \(\ln(x^2+y^2)\lt0\) everywhere inside (\(r\lt1\)).

\(\displaystyle J=r;\qquad I=-\frac{5\pi}9\approx-1.745\)
May 2018 Q2(b)10 marksMay format

Consider \(\displaystyle\iiint_D\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}\,e^{(x^2+y^2+z^2)}\,dz\,dy\,dx\) over the solid region \(D\) bounded by the two spherical surfaces \(x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2\) and \(x^2+y^2+z^2=b^2\), \(0\lt a\lt b\). Directly write down the Jacobian of the coordinate transformation between spherical coordinates \((\rho,\phi,\theta)\) and Cartesian \((x,y,z)\). Hence, calculate the triple integral in spherical coordinates. What is the value of the triple integral if \(a=1\), \(b=2\)?

Hint

\(J=\rho^2\sin\phi\); the shell is \(a\le\rho\le b\) with full angles. The radial integral \(\int\rho^3e^{\rho^2}d\rho\) needs \(w=\rho^2\), then parts.

Solution
  1. \(J=\rho^2\sin\phi\). \(D\): \(a\le\rho\le b\), \(0\le\phi\le\pi\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\). Integrand \(=\rho\,e^{\rho^2}\).
  2. \[\begin{aligned}I&=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\!\!\int_a^b\rho e^{\rho^2}\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\\&=4\pi\int_a^b\rho^3e^{\rho^2}\,d\rho.\end{aligned}\]
  3. \(w=\rho^2\), \(dw=2\rho\,d\rho\): \[\begin{aligned}\int\rho^3e^{\rho^2}d\rho&=\frac12\int we^w\,dw\\&=\frac12(w-1)e^w.\end{aligned}\] So \[I=2\pi\Big[(\rho^2-1)e^{\rho^2}\Big]_a^b.\]
  4. \(a=1\), \(b=2\): \(2\pi(3e^4-0)=6\pi e^4\).

The angular part \(\int_0^\pi\sin\phi\,d\phi\cdot\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta=4\pi\) should be shown once, then reused.

\(\displaystyle\begin{aligned}I&=2\pi\big[(b^2-1)e^{b^2}\\&\qquad-(a^2-1)e^{a^2}\big];\\a=1,\ b=2&:\ I=6\pi e^4\approx1029\end{aligned}\)

◆Before the exam

  • Learn the Jacobians by heart; the December sheet gives none: \(r\) (polar, cylindrical), \(\rho^2\sin\phi\) (spherical), \(abr\) (ellipse), \(\frac1{ad-bc}\) (linear map written as \(u,v\) in terms of \(x,y\)).
  • Read which way the map is written. \(x=\dots\) → \(J\) directly; \(u=\dots\) → compute \(\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\) and flip it (ready answer).
  • Use \(|J|\) in the integral, but report \(J\) with its sign when asked “find the Jacobian”.
  • Map every boundary, not just corners: substitute the line or curve into \(u=\dots\), \(v=\dots\). Four lines in two parallel pairs → a rectangle.
  • Always sketch both regions with boundary equations and corners labelled; a sketch request is 3–5 marks on its own.
  • Set limits from the sketch with a strip (2D) or floor → roof (3D): outer limits numbers, inner limits may depend on the outer variables.
  • Look for the zero: symmetric region + odd integrand (Dec 2023, May 2025) — but state the reason.
  • Polar circles through the origin: \(r=2a\cos\theta\) (\(-\frac\pi2\) to \(\frac\pi2\)), \(r=2a\sin\theta\) (0 to \(\pi\)); \(x=a\) is \(r=a\sec\theta\).
  • Spherical: the angle from the \(z\)-axis runs 0 to \(\pi\); check which letter the paper gives it.
  • Budget about 23 minutes for a 13-mark C2 part. Sanity checks: area \(=\iint|J|\,du\,dv\); volume with \(f=1\); a square integrand cannot give a negative answer.
